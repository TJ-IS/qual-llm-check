# Performing organizational learning with machine apprentices

Gregg S. Elofson

University of Miami, Miami, FL, USA

Benn R. Konsynski

Harvard University, Boston, MA, USA

Organizational learning typically focuses on implicit adaptation in the firm's internal environment. This paper discusses organizational learning in the extended enterprise: consisting of the turbulent competitive/political environment of threats and opportunities, and the organization's internal environment – traditionally consisting of people and their institutions – extended to include the information technology platform. Here, internal and external policies need to be modeled for an effective adaptation of the enterprise. Artificial intelligence and machine learning methods are applied to organizational design in monitoring and analyzing decisions based on the impact and structure of the organization – monitoring changes as part of the learning and adaptation cycle.

Keywords: Organizational learning; Artificial intelligence.

## 2. Motivations for organizational learning

While enhancement of performance is common to the many prescriptions for organizational learning, agreement on the methods and practices of organizational learning is largely uncommon. For example, organizations may learn proper congruence over organizational characteristics such as strategy, structure, and technology [4]. They may learn adaptation to their environment, choosing assessment and accommodation through appropriate organizational design features [9]. Organizations may learn by exercising error recognition and improved avoidance [2]. They may “improve actions through better knowledge and understanding” [12]. Organizational learning can offer the opportunity for improved decision-unit effectiveness [8], crisis prevention and coping with environmental change [3,21], as well as premise re-evaluation when previous beliefs are no longer sufficient for choosing appropriate organizational structures [6]. Successful organizational learning has been described as the acquisition of routines and procedures [18], and conversely, as the abandonment of routines and procedures [6]. Organizational learning is viewed to aid the firm through the refinement of knowledge [9], the acquisition of knowledge [16], as well as through the abandonment of knowledge [21].

Prescriptions for organizational learning also entail the construction or acquisition of an institutional memory. Duncan and Weiss [9] stress that organizational learning “is more than the simple aggregate of individual learning (...) [it] must involve the learning done by a given individual that can be shared, evaluated, and integrated with that done by others.” Jelinek [16] states that: “a means of capturing or impounding (...) insights is the first requirement for organizational learning.” The substance of the memory generally includes the interpretations of the organization in relation to the external environment. For example, Dery [7] suggests that: “if organizations are learning entities, then these entities must have an epistemology (‘mental maps’, ‘perceptual filters’) mediating between them and the welter of experience surrounding them.” In understanding the external environment, then, choices are made about what information to consider and how to interpret it. The institution’s memory reflects the experience acquired during this process. Unfortunately, as Levitt and March tell us [18]: “a good deal of experience goes unrecorded because the costs are too great.” The end result here is that “knowledge disappears from the organization’s active memory” [20].

## 3. Extending the enterprise

While the literature on organizational learning may include the notion of IS as a conduit of “sensory data,” and the need for both external and internal information is often stated $[8,9,12,14,5]$ , little attention has been given to the proposition that the IS sector of the organization be included among those structures or processes that learn. Levitt and March $[18]$ mention the idea of traditional IS acting as something of an organizations memory, but have strict reservations about its ability to enhance adaptive capabilities. Jelinek $[16]$ brought out the notion of IS capturing the priorities and procedures of an organization’s planning function. But overall, the bulk of organizational learning literature has focused on the aggregate of individuals reflecting and interpreting in a cycle of learning and adaptation.

With the increasing importance of an organization's technological platform becoming evident in the organization's efforts to meet their strategic goals, the ability of the IS function to respond to changes in the environment has become a salient issue in the organization's mission. For instance, Daft and Huber [4] encourage the proper alignment of the IS resource with decision-making scenarios, and Galbraith [13] forwards the notion of changing an organization's design as a way to cope with an overload of information.

When the question arises as to how best to design an organization for the purposes of learning, the IS component can also be considered. In the extended enterprise, the IS platform can be charged with greater responsibility in absorbing, translating, and refining the voluminous information intake of the organization. Through the reinterpretation of the learning organization to include the IS function, a history of interpretations, knowledge, and experience becomes possible. Previously, the history of an organization has largely been a history of transactions. Aside from financial statements, the firm's history has been expressed in terms of its myths and anecdotes. But myths and anecdotes must serve many masters. They must convey knowledge, values, role models, wisdom, and the latest goals of the firm. Accuracy is perhaps the least influential master in the making of these stories. Strategic decisions at the highest level have been remembered – however imperfectly. But there is no explicit organizational memory of decisions and the information used in making these decisions. The adapting organization typically loses its past wisdom in the organizational design equivalent of a paradigm change. Organizations appear to be either static and learn that change only brings risk of failure, or are dynamic, forgetting the past with each new metamorphosis. Hence, there is a lack of wise old corporations.

## 4. Scanning the environment

The organizational activity of interest here is environmental scanning, during which the organization becomes an interpreter of internal and external events. Briefly reviewed, environmental scanning was largely regarded as an informal activity in corporations during the sixties and early seventies, and was usually based on personal contacts to capture market/sales related information [1]. By the mid-seventies, work being produced in the area [23] reflected increased organizational awareness of environmental factors in strategic planning. Over the past ten years, though, vastly increasing amounts of publicly available information about the competitive environment – the sharp increase in online data bases, journals, information released under the Freedom of Information Act, etc. – has been accompanied by a rapidly growing interest in the organization's external environment. But, while research has been done to aid planners in enumerating potential threats and opportunities [19], analyzing the results of monitoring [22] and disseminating environmental scanning conclusions [15], less attention has been devoted to monitoring patterns of indicators in the external environment.

During monitoring, area specialists and intelligence analysts in well-developed environmental scanning units combine their efforts in monitoring and searching the environment [10]. Given the most current goals of the organization, the area specialists (experts in some aspect of the external environment such as political events, regulatory measures, competitor financial status, etc.) decide upon monitoring sets of qualitative indicators that might provide insight into various threats and opportunities to the organization. Once the indicators are chosen, the area specialists request estimates from the intelligence analysts of the indicators' values. The intelligence analyst has the role of locating and interpreting information that will shed light on the disposition of the indicators in question. In a typical information request, the area specialist generates a list of information requirements and sends them via electronic-mail to the intelligence analyst.

Also, when the information requested concerns business intelligence, requests will very likely be repeated. Once the sought-after information is made available to the area specialist, inferences and classifications over the data's content are made and the process begins again. The emphasis of monitoring is to identify patterns of activity that could indicate an opportunity or threat to the organization. Once such a pattern is detected, a concentrated search is then invoked to substantiate or disprove the pattern of indicators, and, if the threat of opportunity is substantiated, the intelligence process will continue through to a report to senior policy makers.

From an organizational learning point of view, the perceptual framework of the scanning unit is distributed across the various area specialists. They are responsible for translating strategic goals into questions and interpretations. As they leave the organization, so do the lessons of that organization's history. And, it is at this juncture that the extended enterprise can act to share and integrate the knowledge and learning of the area specialists. Here, the extended enterprise can capture the insights of the organization's members, acquire the mental maps that mediate between the specialists and the flux of information surrounding them.

## 5. Caching knowledge

From a technical perspective, traditional expert system technology is quick to be considered when problems of knowledge storage and inference are presented. However, both its product and process attributes make it an unsuitable alternative. The lengthy time requirements of knowledge acquisition, the changing nature of the organization's goals and objectives with its concomitant changes in potential threats and opportunities, and the monolithic structure of traditional knowledge bases makes the expert system architecture an unfeasible design alternative for keeping pace with the organization's learning requirements.

To capture the perceptual filters of the organization and form an institutional memory, a number of software engineering innovations have been made. The architecture and prototype developed acquires knowledge rapidly and incrementally; learns to improve its search strategy; succeeds through a large number of small knowledge bases instead of one large, monolithic one; and quickly adapts to the changing agenda of the organization [10].

This approach makes use of a collection of intelligent agents. Here, the agents act as “apprentices" to an area specialist, caching his knowledge as he monitors the environment. The apprentice's orientation is problem specific. For example, one apprentice would be concerned only with political violence problems while another would be concerned only with new technology threats. Instead of using electronic mail messages to communicate questions to an intelligence analyst, an area specialist will use a structured message backed by an intelligent agent – an apprentice. The apprentices make themselves available to all other area specialists, improve their search strategy over time, and explain their conclusions.

## 5.1. Operating an apprentice

The operational characteristics of an apprentice will be described by an organization doing a particular monitoring activity. For example, when an organization monitors the political climate of a foreign country, the attributes an area specialist considers include the following: Pro-regime and anti-regime sense of relative deprivation; pro-regime and anti-regime belief in violence; coercive force available to both pro-regime and anti-regime actors; and institutional support for both pro-regime and anti-regime actors [15]. In seeking attributes for these values, the area specialist initiates a request for information by creating a new apprentice. The message includes the following:

(a) attributes for which the area specialist requires values and the name of the apprentice – which corresponds to the threat or opportunity being monitored;

(b) an explanation or elucidation of the attributes to better clarify the nature of each being requested;

(c) scaling information specifying the values which are acceptable as answers to the attribute requests.

This structured message, this apprentice, is sent by the area specialist to the intelligence analyst, and the questions about the attribute values, together with the explanations and scaling information, are asked of the intelligence analyst. To each of the attribute questions the intelligence analyst responds with a value corresponding to one of the scaled values provided by the apprentice. Additionally, the intelligence analyst may provide a written explanation of why he chose a particular scaled value.

Once this is done, the apprentice carries its newfound information back to the area specialist. And, upon returning to the area specialist with its new information, the apprentice displays the answers to the specified questions and asks (in effect): "What does it mean?" To this, the area specialist may ask for an explanation of one of the intelligence analyst's answers, or give the apprentice an assessment, a classification, of the information provided. With the answer, the apprentice forms an initial concept – a concept that is represented as a collection of attribute-value pairs and a classification.

The next time the area specialist needs information about the same threat or opportunity, restating the questions, explanations, and scaling information is unnecessary. The area specialist need only send the apprentice as a pre-packaged mail message – it already has the questions and other requisite information. From that point, the apprentice proceeds to the intelligence analyst, as before, and asks the same questions and receives a new set of answers. With these new answers the apprentice returns to the area specialist, and, if the apprentice has a concept that matches the answers provided by the intelligence analyst, it reports its classification to the specialist. Otherwise, it again asks the area specialist: "What does it mean?" And the specialist provides another classification which the apprentice uses to augment its concepts and make further generalizations.

It will attempt to evaluate all attributes found and continue searching until it has either made a classification or exhausted its problem solving knowledge – in which case it returns to the area specialist with the attribute-values and asks for a classification. ## 5.2. Apprentice architecture

A prototype of the apprentice has been developed on a MAC II using Allegro Common Lisp. Architecturally, the three-layered apprentice uses a similarity-based learning algorithm to generate a knowledge base combining search heuristics with rules, which in turn is used by a knowledge-scheduling, opportunistically searching inference engine. The concept formation layer's central responsibility involves the gathering of groups of attribute-value pairs to form generalizations based on commonalities in those groups. The knowledge source layer receives knowledge from the output of the concept formation layer. That is, the tree generated by the concept formation layer is parsed to yield a distinct set of concepts which become the knowledge sources for the apprentice. The blackboard layer acts as the inference engine for the apprentice, using the search information provided by the knowledge sources to intelligently query the data source for specific values of chosen attributes.

## 5.3. The concept formation layer

The algorithm chosen for the concept formation layer was Unimem [17]. Unimem is an incremental conceptual clustering algorithm that creates a hierarchy of feature-vectors from inputs of labelled sets of attribute-value pairs. And with each new piece of data, Unimem updates its hierarchy.

Although space does not permit a full review of the Unimem algorithm, two points need review here. First, the classifications of the feature-vectors are identified by either a single label or a disjunction of labels. Within the hierarchy created by Unimem, those feature-vectors close to the root are more general (having fewer attribute-value pairs and more labels) than those near the leaves. And second, the arcs pointing to the nodes in Unimem's hierarchy have predictive attribute-values associated with them. These predictive attribute-values provide heuristic search information to the inference engine, as the node pointed to incorporates the predictive attribute-value pair while the other nodes probably do not. Unimem gathers its new inputs of classified attribute-value pairs from the intelligence analyst. The classification of this information, the judgement call made by the area specialists about the returned information, becomes the label of the attribute-value data.

## 5.4. Knowledge-source layer

The knowledge-source level is the result of parsing the hierarchy formed by Unimem. The result is a number of hypotheses that correspond to each of the hierarchy's nodes, together with the arc-labels pointing to them. Thus, a given hypothesis will contain information about both its predictive and predictable attribute-values, as well as the classification given to it. The actual representation would appear as follows:

(((a american-made)(c very-fast)) ((b medium-weight)(d expensive)) (Corvette ZR1))

Here, from left to right, there are three groups in this hypothesis: Predictive values, predictable values, and classification label. In this example, if the object in question is American made and very fast, it is likely that it is also medium-weight and expensive – more particularly a Corvette ZR1. The values “american-made” and “very-fast” are the predictive values – their presence suggests the presence of the predictable values “medium-weight” and “expensive”. Using this hypothesis, if all four attribute-value pairs are matched, then the conclusion is that the object is a Corvette ZR1.

## 5.5. The hybrid blackboard layer

Because the cost to acquire individual data values is non-trivial, attention must be paid to reducing the number of queries that an apprentice makes. While a hybrid of a blackboard architecture is used for the purpose of exhibiting global data to the various knowledge sources, neither multiple levels nor links are employed. The knowledge sources, or hypotheses, correspond directly to the concepts developed by the concept formation level of the apprentice. And the information needed to improve the search for the correct hypothesis is made available through the predictive values produced by the concept formation layer of the apprentice. With this predictive information, along with multiple levels of classification specificity for the knowledge sources of any given agent (the more classifications a concept will conclude, the less specific it is), an opportunistic search method is used.

In conducting the search for a classification, it is first necessary to begin with some piece of information. Therefore, a choice of a particular attribute for which to find a value must be made. In making this choice, the preferred result will be that it leads quickly to a conclusion, taking the search in a direction that requires fewer data requests of the intelligence analyst rather than more data requests. In achieving this end, two constraints are placed on the selection of an attribute to investigate: (1) it must be a predictive attribute as the presence of such an attribute value match tends to rapidly decrease the number of alternative hypotheses that may be considered;

Note here the implicit assumption that the apprentice will find a matching concept for the incoming values. That is, if it is assumed that the value being sought will most likely match one of the values found in the concepts, then the tree is most quickly pruned by choosing the most varying value. Conversely, if it assumed that the value being sought is only as likely to occur as any other possible value for the attribute in question, then the tree is most quickly pruned by choosing the least varying value.

Once an attribute value is chosen for processing, and a value is assigned to that attribute by the intelligence analyst, a list of candidate hypotheses are selected from the knowledge base. They are chosen simply by whether or not they have the attribute just identified and whether their value for that attribute matches the value supplied by the intelligence analyst. Next, given that more than one hypothesis becomes a candidate, the selection of which of these hypotheses is to be the focus of attention is made. This is done by selecting the competing hypothesis with the most specific classification. For example, if one of the competing hypotheses would make the classification A OR B, while another of the hypotheses would make the final classification of just A, then the hypothesis that was the more specific of the two, the one concluding A, would become the focus of attention. To further complicate matters in this process of conflict resolution, there is the problem of deciding over two competing hypotheses when both are most specific. That is, what does the system do when both competing hypotheses have only one conclusion? The answer here is indicative of a bias toward predictiveness. The hypothesis with fewer unanswered predictive values is opted for. Should these also be equal, the hypothesis with fewer unanswered predictable values will be opted for. And if these are also equal, the choice is simply arbitrary – the first hypothesis on the list is chosen.

Together, these three layers effect the required functionality of the apprentice. The concept formation layer is put to use when the apprentice ‘asks’ the area specialist for a classification of attribute value pairs. The classification given is used, along with the attribute-values provided by the intelligence analyst, to augment the concepts via the learning algorithm. The output of the learning algorithm, Unimem, is put into the knowledge sources, and is used by the blackboard to conduct a search/query of the intelligence analyst. Thus, different parts of the apprentice’s architecture are used according to the individual with which the apprentice is interacting. The blackboard and knowledge sources are used with the intelligence analyst, and the concept formation layer is used with the area specialist.

## 5.6. Supporting architecture

While apprentices are capable of learning to gather attribute-values from an intelligence analyst, as well as classifying that information, additional capabilities must be provided to make it possible for the system to deliver that classification to the area specialist requesting it. Moreover, though apprentices are capable of gathering the knowledge of the area specialist and categorizing it, more needs to be done to make that knowledge available to the area specialist's colleagues and the organization.

The primary components of this architecture are the files of apprentices and the ISO X.400 - electronic envelope - communications protocol (fig. 3). Each file holds the apprentices belonging to any given area specialist, and the files are open to all other specialists. This means that area specialists have access to another's apprentices:

## 5.7. Apprentice monitoring example

Concerning the latter example, while looking for marketing vulnerabilities in a competitor’s product line, an ‘area specialist’ monitored changes in the competitor’s distribution strategies, cash position, plant usage, and market share – while an apprentice captured knowledge about how to act in the absence of that same ‘area specialist’. Furthermore, if the information gathered from the information analyst was, for example, to match hypothesis A, the apprentice would assert that the competitor was either vulnerable to new product innovations or that the competitor had not substantially changed.

Had the area specialist been present, undoubtedly only one classification would have been provided. This fact supports what we would intuitively believe of an apprentice – that is, after ‘existing’ for only a short period of time, it is not going to be as good at making classifications as an established expert. Nevertheless, the apprentice would be able to provide a useful indication of what the pattern of qualitative data meant. Additionally, the strategy of an apprentice is conservative. It identifies only what it has already observed or some subset of what it has already observed. If it is confronted with a pattern completely unlike anything it has come across before, it defers judgement and asks for a classification.

Thus, besides gathering and classifying information and knowledge, acquiring the perceptual framework used in interpreting the environment, the apprentice distributes the information and knowledge, making it publicly available. The apprentice distributes the information by delivering it to the requestor. Moreover, in the absence of the area specialist who created a particular apprentice, that same apprentice can deliver/distribute both information and classifications to other area specialists requiring such assistance. For instance, an area specialist who is an expert in identifying new product developments may need to know about the cash position of a competitor, but doesn't have the expertise to make the assessment. That area specialist may fail to contact another area specialist whose sphere of expertise is in competitor financial matters. Thus, the objectives of strengthening monitoring capabilities and augmenting the organization's knowledge resources are addressed.

## 6. Conclusion

The traditional expert system, under this kind of scrutiny, has a fixed interpretation scheme. What the system considers in making its decisions does not change over time, even though an organization's strategies, as well as potential threats and opportunities, do change over time. As a model of an expert's memory and reasoning abilities, the traditional expert system represents a fixed memory with fixed expectations. Conversely, increased flexibility is possible by knowledge caching. An apprentice's concepts and search patterns can change over time. New apprentices can be created in accordance with the changing goals of the organization.

Finally, a Knowledge Cache provides the means to realize an explicit organizational memory, with knowledge and reasoning that may be distributed among the organization's members. It captures some of the perceptual filters, or mental maps, of the individuals involved in environmental ‘sense-making’. And, it does this with less system rigidity than earlier, traditional, knowledge-based approaches.
