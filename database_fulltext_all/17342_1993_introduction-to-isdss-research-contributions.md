# Introduction to ISDSS research contributions

Clyde W. Holsapple

University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY, USA

Andrew B. Whinston

University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX, USA

Over the past two decades, the decision support system (DSS) field has expanded from a few formative ideas to a realm of intense research and widespread practice. The field is concerned with the use of computer and communication technology to support decision making. The support could occur at various junctures in a decision-making process. The DSS could be supporting individual or multiparticipant decision makers, such as teams, groups, or organizations. The nature of the support possibilities is wide-ranging, including such capabilities as interpretation, coordination, adaptation, analytical modeling, inference, and other more mundane knowledge management techniques.

As research has advanced along theoretical, experimental, and implementational avenues, the multidisciplinary nature of the DSS field has become increasingly pronounced. It forms a meeting place for integrating such diverse disciplines as cognitive science, business computing, operations research/management science, and the various functional areas of management. Each of these can contribute to further DSS advances and each can benefit from DSS investigations. Ultimately, this multidisciplinary work aims at increasing the productivity and effectiveness of decision makers.

The advent of the Decision Support Systems journal signaled that the DSS field had achieved a definite critical mass. Since then, the field has evolved, grown, and matured to the point where an open society devoted to DSS research, practice, and education was founded. This International Society for Decision Support Systems (ISDSS) is a focal point for DSS discourse. One manifestation of this was the ISDSS Conference held September 11–14, 1990 at the IC2 Institute at The University of Texas, in Austin, Texas. Sponsored by The University of Texas at Austin, University of Kentucky, and the RGK Foundation, the conference included over 50 presentations by participants from over ten countries. These diverse presentations were concerned with DSS foundations, development, usage, evaluation, management, and education.

We encouraged those making invited presentations at the ISDSS Conference to submit manuscripts for review and possible publication in this special issue of Decision Support Systems. This process resulted in the eight articles that appear here. While an initial version of each was presented at the conference, these articles have been revised for this issue. They reflect some of the discussion engendered by their authors' conference presentations and subsequent developments by the authors, as well as the suggestions and observations of the reviewers who helped us in refereeing this issue.

The eight articles are representative of the variety of DSS research efforts aired at the conference, addressing such issues as knowledge representation, development methodology, use of artificial intelligence, support of multiparticipant decision makers, acquiring user knowledge, evaluating DSSs, development tools, and interface features to assist users. From among the various ways these articles could have been arranged, we have opted to organize them into two categories, one having a developer-orientation and the other emphasizing user features. Of course, those in the developer-oriented category do not ignore user concerns and those in the user-oriented category are cognizant of underlying development concerns. It is a matter of relative emphasis.

The developer-oriented articles begin with Bob Blanning's presentation of unified framework for managing a DSS's descriptive, procedural, and reasoning knowledge. Based on the algebra of relations and the new operation of pseudodivision, this framework gives DSS developers a way to devise knowledge systems adhering to the relational style, yet incorporating decision models and assertions in addition to data.

In their article on DSS development methodology, Sung Joo Park and Hyoung Do Kim introduce a rapid constraint-based prototyping approach. They argue that modeling environments can be viewed as constraint enforcement systems and show the types and structures of constraints developers could use to specify such environments. These specifications serve as the basis for generation of the target system. The approach is illustrated in the case of structured modeling.

In a very different examination of development methodology, Mike Shaw and Mark Fox are concerned with support of multiparticipant decision making in complex organizations. They use notions of distributed artificial intelligence to devise a framework for formalizing and designing multiparticipant DSSs. Particular attention is given to the issues of coordination and learning. Their approach is illustrated with two examples, one involving a network of expert systems and the other concerned with concurrent design decisions.

Charlie Gardner, Jim Marsden, and Dave Pingry outline a methodology for DSS developers to use in evaluating the performance of varying DSSs on an ex-ante basis. This is a marked contrast to ex-post evaluation which focuses on actual results of DSS implementations. They argue that the ex-ante approach can be more cost effective and offer competitive or strategic advantages. Their methodology employs induced-value experimentation. To illustrate it, they present an example experiment and discuss its implications with respect to DSS hypotheses.

The final four articles, while of interest for DSS development, have a special emphasis on various user-oriented features of DSSs. In the first of these, T. Kampke, Franz Radermacher and P. Wolf examine the treatment of a certain kind of user knowledge: user preferences. That is, how can a DSS elicit preferences of its users as a basis for issuing responses that account for those preferences? They introduce a formal approach to identifying and continually updating user preferences. This involves a declaration representation of such knowledge with respect to precoded attributes and scales. Machine scheduling and environmental risk assessment applications are outlined.

The topic of direct manipulation user interfaces for DSSs is addressed by Ron Lee. In particular, he explores the dynamics of this kind of interface for construction and manipulation of graph-based decision models. A generator for producing such user interfaces is presented. Models devised by users with a generated interface can be processed by Prolog routines or external solvers. In a somewhat related vein, Jim Courtney and David Paradice are concerned with DSSs for problem formulation. They cite several related studies, including graphics software to help users formulate problems and such extensions as problem diagnosis, advisory, discovery, and dialectical facilities.

In the final article, Harvey Greenberg is concerned with helping users on the opposite side of a DSSs knowledge production activities. Rather than studying how to facilitate a user's statement of problems, he concentrates on how the system can help a user understand its responses to problem statements. This involves a description of software for developing math-programming-oriented DSSs that are able to explain dual prices, diagnose infeasibilities, and reason about redundancy.

The articles in this issue are indicative of the rich interchange of DSS ideas fostered by ISDSS. At the conference, this interchange involved not only planned presentations and panel discussions, but also first-hand contacts among society members and other attendees.
