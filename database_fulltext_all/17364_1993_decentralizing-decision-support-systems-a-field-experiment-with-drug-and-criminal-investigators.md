# Decentralizing decision support systems

# A field experiment with drug and criminal investigators

Kenneth E. Kendall

Rutgers University, Camden, NJ, USA

Barbara A. Schuldt

University of Wisconsin, Oshkosh, WI, USA

Improving effectiveness in a highly decentralized organization can be elusive. In the case of largely autonomous drug and criminal investigators, working for a state patrol agency, it is extremely difficult. One possible solution was to develop and implement a case progression decision support system (DSS) for decision making support. This paper describes a field experiment undertaken to determine the effectiveness of using a decentralized DSS compared with a centralized one, as well as comparing the decentralized DSS with no formalized decision support system. The field experiment evaluated the investigators' attitudes toward the availability of information, accuracy of information, support in problem identification, perceived usefulness of the system, and its overall effectiveness. Results indicated that there is a significant difference between the participants' attitudes toward availability of information and support in identifying problems. Furthermore, the study showed that either the centralized or the decentralized DSS is considerably more effective than the current approach of not using a DSS at all.

Keywords: Decision support systems, Centralization, Decentralization, Drug enforcement, Law enforcement.

This study began by analyzing the Investigative Services Division (ISD) of the Nebraska State Patrol (NSP). Decision support was developed, but the unanswered question was how it should be implemented. Two options were possible: (1) the DSS would be centralized at the state patrol headquarters in Lincoln, Nebraska; or (2) the DSS would be decentralized throughout the state in each office of the investigative units.

Investigators in the ISD are autonomous in many ways. They manage their own time and take personal responsibility for their effectiveness. With this in mind, the implementation of a decentralized DSS was proposed. This paper describes a field experiment that considers the effects of varying the degree of centralization of a DSS in the Nebraska State Patrol.

## 2. Review of literature

Organizations that were moving from centralized computing facilities to decentralized, user-controlled computing facilities are returning to centralized information systems $[6]$ . For decision support systems supporting ad hoc decisions this move may not be warranted. Changes in the computing environment have facilitated variable structures and locations of computing facilities. These changes are reductions in computing costs relative to size, increased computer literacy among non-technical computer users and the availability of distributed databases. Yet the need to maintain control through efficient and effective management is encouraging organizations to centralize information systems. Rather than equivocating between centralized and decentralized as the current information system strategy dictates, organizations need to match their information systems to their organizational structures $[21]$ .

Many early MIS researchers have commented on the affects of computerization on an organization's structure. Whisler [32] felt that organizations would use computer technology to centralize decision making and reverse the trend towards decentralization (pp. 11–12). Burlingame [4] anticipated that computerization would provide timely and relevant information that would facilitate decentralized decision making. However two studies, one by Hofer [17] and the other by Hunt and Newell [18] suggested that the relationship between computer technology and organizational structure was unclear at that time and barely formed a weak relationship. Markus and Robey [22] conjectured that if the information system did not match the organization then the information system would have the ability to change the organizational structure. The relationship between the information system location and the organizational structure linkage to successful information systems has not been made clear [21].

It is difficult to find literature detailing an experiment that compares a centralized DSS to a decentralized DSS within a decentralized organization. In the last decade or so, researchers have used opinion surveys, case studies and surveys of 'successful' organizations [8,10,12,13,25,27–29,34]. Kendall [20] analyzed decentralized DSS effectiveness in a highly decentralized organization (a regional blood system) and determined that the decentralized DSS was particularly effective for professional objectives (e.g., using more appropriate blood products when they are called for) and social objectives (e.g. preventing blood from being wasted).

Organizations choose to decentralize for a variety of reasons. These include:

(1) Firms that chose to centralize or decentralize to meet internal and external environmental constraints will choose the appropriate information technology to match that structure, [26];

(2) Working in a complex global market place, decision makers require timely, usable, accurate information. These decision makers have not been able to get the desired information in a usable form and/or at the appropriate time from traditional computer facilities [31]; and

(3) The move to decentralize computing services mirrors a move in organizations to push decision making, planning, and control to the lowest possible level [11].

The degree of information system (IS) centralization is positively related to the degree of organizational centralization, and the less centralized the organization the greater the integration of the MIS function, according to Ein-Dor and Segev [10]. Still, decentralization has been limited in the past, primarily due to the cost and size of computing systems. The reduced cost for computer power has provided the stimulus to decentralize computing facilities [30]. Now, computing technology is able to parallel the structure of the organization.

The type of the decision, location of the decision maker, and the goal of the decision indicate the ideal placement of the DSS. The DSS should be close to the decision maker in order to facilitate the decision making process, but hardware/software constraints can effect the organization's ability to achieve this goal. A field experiment was run to compare the effectiveness of a centralized decision support system with a decentralized decision support system in a decentralized organization. The field experiment hypothesizes that the use of a decentralized decision support system in a decentralized organizational environment would improve the decision makers' effectiveness.

## 3. The case progression decision support system, the experimental setting

The organizational structure of the Nebraska State Patrol's Investigative Services Division is relatively decentralized. Investigators act as independent agents servicing the public. On the other hand strategic planning, establishment of policies, and the database for case and intelligence reports as well as accounting are centralized at the State Headquarters in Lincoln, Nebraska. Using Mintzberg's [23] classification scheme the investigative unit would be a professional bureaucracy.

The case progression DSS (CPDSS) was designed and used as an operational and tactical decision making tool for the Investigative Services sergeants at the Nebraska State Patrol. Operationally, the CPDSS was used to assist the sergeants in making ill-structured decisions about case assignments, termination of an investigation, and a variety of other decisions. The tactical aspect of the DSS concerned its use during quarterly case reviews.

## 3.1. Development of the CPDSS

Recognition of the need to track cases is not new, since good investigators do this already using their own heuristics. Case tracking involves knowing what has been done on the case and what can be done to further the investigation. Problems arise when a case is not actively worked for some time, when too much time is put in on an unsolvable case, or when the investigator needs additional personal to help solve the case. Given the large number of cases for which an investigator is responsible, it is important that a formal tracking system be in place to improve the sergeants supervision of cases and investigators, to remind the investigator to close out unproductive cases and to finalize the disposition of closed cases.

The CPDSS was developed by evaluating the investigative process, the Nebraska State Patrol's existing tracking forms $[1]$ , and a similar case tracking system used by the Orange County Sheriff's Department in Florida $[16]$ . The CPDSS required the decision makers (the sergeants) to use their subjective estimates about the time the investigators spent on each activity based on data from several existing reports (weekly time sheets, administrative data forms, and case prioritization forms). After the CPDSS report was generated the sergeant made decisions about the progression of active cases and the productivity of their investigative unit.

## 3.2. Measures of effectiveness

An important issue in this study was the assessment of the decision makers' effectiveness with the use of the CPDSS. One goal of a DSS is to improve the effectiveness of the decision maker. Keen and Scott Morton [19] define effectiveness as identifying what should be done and ensuring that the chosen criterion is relevant. The emphasis of the CPDSS is to improve the effectiveness of the sergeants. In improving the effectiveness of the sergeants' decision making about case progression and investigator productivity, the investigative unit's effectiveness should improve.

Drawing from the literature on police effectiveness, Needle [24] defines effectiveness as “the degree to which departmental objectives (or goals) are achieved successfully”. Butler [2] stresses that effectiveness cannot be measured unless you use a performance measure and states that the two concepts are the same. Commonly listed objectives for investigative divisions are to deter and prevent crime, uncovering the occurrence of crimes, identifying and apprehending criminal offenders, recovering stolen property, supporting the prosecution of arrested offenders, and maintaining public confidence in the police [15].

Traditional methods of measuring the effectiveness of law enforcement in meeting their goals are the use of clearance rates, arrest rates, federal Uniform Crime Reports (UCR), and number of cases worked. Clearance rates are the number of cases closed compared to the number of cases worked. The problem with clearance rates involve differences in definition by police administrators, regarding when an arrest is made, when an arrest can be made, if the prosecuting attorney will prosecute, or if the case is administratively closed due to a low probability of being solved. Arrest rates are highly inaccurate. A Rand study [6] found only three percent of all arrests were the result of the skill and training of the investigator [3]. UCR numbers are a self-reported, federally collected statistic that has inherent inaccuracies and fuzzy definition of terms. Additionally, using the number of cases worked leads to the problem the DEA experienced when their agents worked small nickel bag buy-busts to intentionally increase the numbers. These cases had questionable impact on their overall effectiveness [33]. Therefore, measuring the effectiveness of the investigative unit is essential in evaluating the success of the DSS.

In order to evaluate the DSS support in improving the decision maker's effectiveness the goal of the Nebraska State Patrol Investigative Service Division must be defined. Their goal is to solve targeted, complex, “quality cases” that facilitate the reduction of crime. “Quality cases”, as defined by the Nebraska State Patrol, are investigations involving correct legal procedures with concise documentation developed against higher quality defendants. Higher quality defendants are those identified as being part of the upper levels of the organized criminal activity or the perpetrator who places a severe burden on the average taxpayer [9]. By looking at solvability factors and key information about a crime, the investigators can target cases that have a high probability of being solved. This allows the investigators to develop more effective investigations that lead to an arrest, prosecution and conviction of a “quality” criminal. The case progression decision support system (CPDSS) was designed to help the sergeants improve the effectiveness of their investigative unit. It was hypothesized that the unit's effectiveness would improve since the sergeants would have better information about the cases (the quality of each case, the progression of each case towards closure, and resources needed to pursue each case). Changes in the decision maker's effectiveness will be measured using a self-administered questionnaire and an investigator effectiveness measure (IEM). These measures are explained in the next section.

## 4. The field experiment

The field experiment was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a decentralized decision maker using a decentralized case progression decision support system (CPDSS) compared to the decentralized decision maker's effectiveness using a centralized CPDSS. The objectives of the experiment were to evaluate the construct that a decentralized organization would have improved decision maker effectiveness with a decentralized DSS, compared to the use of a centralized DSS.

The field experiment divided the Investigative Services Division (ISD) into three groups: Decentralized, centralized and control. The organizational level at which the CPDSS report was generated was the manipulated independent variable in the field experiment. The decentralized group produced their own CPDSS reports, the centralized group received CPDSS reports generated by the ISD administration, and the control group did not use the CPDSS. Each group contained one rural and one urban troop area, and each troop area is composed of one drug and one criminal sergeant. By randomly selecting an urban and a rural troop area to be in each group, the affects of geographic location and demographics were minimized for each group.

Group 1 was the control group. They had summary reports distributed exactly as they had been in the past. These reports include a periodic summary report giving case statistics: Cases initiated, supplemental reports and cases cleared. It takes approximately two to three weeks for these reports to reach the troop area. No CPDSS was used by group 1.

Group 2 used a decentralized CPDSS generated by the troop area sergeants. The decentralized CPDSS allowed the sergeant to input their subjective evaluations concerning their troop area investigative unit. The two decentralized troop areas had access to the necessary computer hardware and software to run the CPDSS. They were trained on both the equipment and the system. The third group received the CPDSS reports centrally generated at headquarters but distributed to the investigative units on a timely basis. The Investigative Services Division Major and Captain acted as administrative decision makers who determined the subjective evaluations for the troop areas involved in the third group.

In order to assess the decision maker's effectiveness while using the CPDSS, two measurement scales were implemented. The first is a self-evaluation questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed and used by [3]. It was given to the twelve sergeants at the end of the study. The results from this questionnaire were used to support hypotheses one through four, to be discussed in the next section.

A second measure, the investigator effectiveness measure (IEM) was developed and used in the study to evaluate the percentage of time the investigative unit or the investigator spends on a case. It includes those activities that facilitate the investigative process. Only time applied to an investigation is used in the IEM. The time measures were obtained from a monthly summary of the investigator's weekly time reports.

The IEM determines the percentage of time that the sergeant's investigative unit spent investigating cases and other activities that did not directly involve the investigation of a case, but contributed to the solving of other investigator's cases. These activities included investigative services reports, intelligence reports, polygraphs, hypnosis, and ident (identification) kits. The weighting scheme was determined by using the Delphi technique with various groups of investigators (investigators, sergeants, and lieutenants). The investigators ranked the activities, then they were asked how valuable each activity is in the effectiveness of case investigations. At the end of the three month time period CPDSS evaluation questionnaires were distributed, completed and analyzed to obtain the user's assessment of the system. Each group's investigative effectiveness measure (IEM) was determined for a three month time period prior to the experiment and a three month time period during the experiment.

## 5. Hypotheses and rationale

The field experiment tests hypotheses that compare decision maker effectiveness of a decentralized DSS or a centralized DSS within a decentralized organizational structure. The first hypothesis concerns the decision maker's (sergeants') attitude towards the availability of information received from the system.

H1. There is a significant difference between the participant groups attitude towards availabil-

ity of information received from the system. The sergeants are the first line of supervision for the investigators, and therefore should know what information is available concerning the investigative unit's case progressions. Due to the time lag and potential data input errors at the centralized computer system in Lincoln, it is hypothesized that the decentralized sergeants' group will perceive information to be more readily available than will the centralized sergeants'.

The second hypothesis evaluates the sergeant's attitude towards the accuracy of data going into the system and information from the system.

H2. There is a significant difference between the participant groups attitude toward the accuracy of information put into and received from the system.

A key factor in improving the effectiveness of the sergeants' decision making is to mandate the accuracy of the data going into the system and the information coming from it. It is the sergeants' attitude towards the accuracy and dependability of the information and their ability to use the information that improves the decision making effectiveness.

The third hypothesis relates to the sergeants' ability to identify problems by using the CPDSS. The possible problems that need identification concern case progression towards closure, investigators that don't take a case to an arrest, cases that need additional personnel, etc.

H3. There is no significant difference between the decentralized and the centralized groups' evaluation of the systems support in problem identification.

The problem identification comes from the use of the case progression decision support system. The accuracy of information will vary but the use of the tool will be similar regardless of the location of the CPDSS.

The fourth hypothesis pertains to the sergeants' perception of the usefulness of the CPDSS information. Due to the sergeants' limited familiarity and use of the CPDSS reports it is hypothesized that there will be no significant differences in their perception of the usefulness of the generated information.

H4. There is no significant difference between the decentralized and the centralized groups' evaluation of the usefulness of the CPDSS information.

The fifth hypothesis concerns changes in the effectiveness of the investigative unit - the major goal of the CPDSS.

H5. The investigative effectiveness measure (IEM) will show significant differences between the various groups.

The investigative effectiveness measure evaluates the percentage of time the investigative unit spends working on prioritized cases. Cases are prioritized by solvability factors and the administration's and/or public's reaction to the case.

H5a. The IEM will show a significant difference between the decentralized and the centralized groups; and

H5b. The IEM will show a significant difference between the centralized and control groups.

In evaluating the hypotheses it was necessary to use a measurement scale. The first four hypotheses were evaluated using the self-assessment questionnaire. The fifth hypothesis was evaluated using the investigative effectiveness measure. The use of two unrelated measures addresses the lack of a valid and reliable effectiveness measurement scale in the IS field. The IEM is new to the criminal justice and information systems environment. The control group was used to address the validity and reliability concerns of this measure.

The CPDSS was designed for the sergeants of the Nebraska State Patrol. Although the number of participants is small this experiment utilizes the entire population of the system rather than a sample. Due to the unique features of the population, nonparametric statistics were applied.

The statistical test used for significance was the Kruskal–Wallis test, which is an extension of the Mann–Whitney test for three or more samples. These tests are also an extension of the Wilcoxon rank sum test for two independent samples. It has the same power properties relative to the analysis of variance F test. The independent groups met the assumptions of the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Hypotheses two (accuracy of information) and four (usefulness of information) tested without significance from the questionnaire data. Hypothesis one (availability of information) was significant at the 0.05 level. The third hypothesis (support in problem identification) was significant at the 0.15 level. The sergeants work with their investigators – reviewing their activities, correcting their paperwork and assisting in case management decisions – so they possess first-hand knowledge of investigative activities. Further examination of the results concerning the timeliness of the generated reports, revealed that the centralized report was generated after the centralized computing facility received the time summaries. The Investigative Services Divisional Major and Captain scheduled a time to evaluate the information and generate the report. The reports were then distributed to the appropriate troop areas. The decentralized group generated their own reports. Given time constrained sergeants and the uncertainty in the investigative units' environment, the generation of the report was placed as a low priority.

Other rationale for the results on the availability of the information can be derived from Mintzberg's [23] classification of NSP's Investigative Services Division as a professional bureaucracy and Leifer's [21] information systems match with the organizational classification. In accordance with the professional bureaucracy criterion the investigators are acting as semi-autonomous agents in a constant yet complex environment. Each investigator is responsible for their given territory and type of criminal activity. Their complex environment is the variegated world of criminal activities. Within the organization, a stable, military structure is followed. This structure provides the stability for the investigators. According to [21], the information systems required for a professional bureaucracy is a centralized system and localized personal systems. The localized systems are used to help the individual investigators tap into the specializations of others (external databases and expertise by other investigators). The centralized systems are used for coordination and control by the administration. This field experiment supports Leifer's system configuration match with the professional bureaucracy organizational structure.

The implementation of the CPDSS forced the investigative units to standardize the data being collected. Prior to the CPDSS the data definitions were limited and left to the interpretation of the individual units. The required consistent data definition was achieved by distributing a data dictionary with the system information. When the data is better managed as a resource, the greater the tendency of the organization to centralize the decision making $[14]$ . The results from the questionnaire would validate this issue.

Hypothesis one was to support the concept of an organization with a decentralized structure implying the use of a decentralized information system. Instead of the decentralized group's mean having a higher value, it was the centralized group that had a significantly higher value. Therefore, results suggest the use of a centrally generated performance report (the case progression decision support system report) for the Nebraska State Patrol.

There was no significant difference between the groups concerning the accuracy of information (H2). The directions on the questionnaire asked participants to evaluate the system in terms of their participation in the experiment. This implies that the sergeants evaluated as equal the accuracy of information from the administration in comparison to the information that they themselves developed. This result could be partially attributed to the semi-militaristic organizational

Hypothesis three stated that there would be no significant difference between the different groups for support in problem identification. The results are significant at the 0.15 level. The system was in place for a limited time, and hence, it did not allow the sergeants to use it to track any complicated, lengthy cases which are often worked for years before a criminal is apprehended. It is this type of case that would best utilize the system, thereby enabling the sergeants to set goals and track the case progression to ensure the stated goals are being met. Although this study found that the CPDSS supported some identification of problems, the form would benefit from modifications to allow sergeants to evaluate cases and individual investigators.

Hypothesis four evaluated the usefulness of information from the system. There was no significant difference between the various groups. We observed that during the system's implementation the sergeants did not experiment with the form to determine what information could be derived from the form. This system was not a high priority for the participants, since they were faced with real-world time constraints and pressure to solve cases. Several sergeants commented that if there was a computer terminal at their desk, then the form would be helpful and up to date.

The fifth hypothesis (concerning the differences in the investigative units effectiveness score) was supported from the statistical analysis. The post-system IEM determined the actual changes in effectiveness compared to the perceived changes in effectiveness assessed by the sergeants. The pre-system effectiveness measures showed no significant difference between the three groups (decentralized, centralized, and control) but the post-system effectiveness measure exhibited a significant difference between the three groups (see table 1). This analysis suggests that the changes in the effectiveness scores between groups are due to implementation of the case progression decision support systems.

## 7. Conclusion

This field experiment provided the rare opportunity to manipulate the degree of centralization of the decision support system in a real-world setting. Taking advantage of this opportunity has yielded a contribution to the body of knowledge in the information systems field.

In this field experiment two of the five hypotheses were supported. The experiment did not support the concept of a decentralized organization requiring a decentralized DSS. Closer examination of the Nebraska State Patrol structure may explain this result. The State Patrol is organized in a semi-military model. The troop area investigators appear to be decentralized but are in reality command controlled from the central administration.

The results of the experiment, although not directly applicable to every organization, can and should be used to evaluate alternative implementation location strategies in other organizations. In our study both the decentralized and the centralized DSS dramatically improved the decision making effectiveness of the investigative units when compared to effectiveness without a decision support system.

Consequently, organizations with limited resources to purchase computer hardware and software, may be able to fully utilize a centrally located system. This can be especially important to non-profit service organizations.

Additional knowledge pertinent to the function of a decision support system in an organization was obtained. First, all decentralized organizations do not prefer a decentralized DSS (e.g. the state patrol was still a semi-military organization even though it was decentralized). Second, a centralized DSS can improve organizational effectiveness by generating distributed reports in a decentralized environment compared to no DSS at all. Third, this field experiment contributed by testing and supporting part of the theoretical work of Leifer [21] and Goodhue, Quillard and Rockart [14].

And fourth, the state patrol benefited from the development of the CPDSS in the Investigative Services Division. The CPDSS formalized the method of tracking case progression throughout the state. The CPDSS helped even out the scarce resource allocations between troop areas. Prior to the implementation of the CPDSS, each troop area felt their cases were the highest priority and that they had the most urgent need for additional resources. The CPDSS gave the administration and each troop area a uniform way of comparing cases and investigative activities throughout the state. Specific cases that need special handling can be pinpointed (e.g. additional personnel and equipment, better supervision, etc.) rather than relying on emotional appeals from the investigator and/or the investigative unit. This promoted consistent resource allocation decisions based on statewide need and benefits. Another benefit was the identification of investigators that need additional training or coaching. The data collected from the CPDSS will be used in the future to document expanded resource requirements from state legislators. The investigative effectiveness measure is being used to evaluate procedural changes in policies and/or procedures in the Investigative Services Division.

The field experiment supported the use of a centralized DSS. This is a major benefit to the NSP Investigative Services Division, since they lack immediate funding to decentralize their computing facilities. Although creating additional work for the central administration, it nevertheless provided an objective basis to evaluate the troop areas for additional investigative resource allocations and funding requests.
