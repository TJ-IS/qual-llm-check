# Executive information systems – a special issue

E. Turban and J.G. Walls

California State University, Long Beach, CA 90840, USA

## 1. Introduction

During the last two decades the technology of decision support systems (DSS) has evolved and expanded to cover topics ranging from support of decisions made by a group to methodologies for justifying the technology or evaluating its usefulness. The basic objective of DSS is to support the solution of fairly complex problems. The technology is used primarily by analysts to assess the seriousness of problems, to evaluate proposed solutions or to help in selecting and implementing a particular course of action. The technology, however, was rarely used directly by senior or even functional managers simply because a DSS is not designed to support many of the tasks performed by these managers. Executive information systems (EIS) and its variants (executive support system, enterprise wide support system) are designed specifically to serve the needs of executives and other managers. The first generation of EIS started in the early 1980's [5] and it was developed primarily as a monitoring and control system. Typical capabilities were tracing the performance of key indicators, exception reporting, drill down (for details) and electronic mail. The basic objective of the system was to help in identifying problems and opportunities as early as possible. Most of the data were internal, but some systems monitored external data as well or allowed accessibility to external information. At that time it was recognized that finding the information needs of executives plays a critical role in designing EISs (e.g. see [3]). Researchers at that time were also interested in system development, capabilities and use.

The second generation of EIS started to develop in the late 1980's when Rockart and De-Long [6] introduced the concept of executive support systems (ESS). Their concept envisioned a comprehensive support system that goes beyond EIS to include communication, office automation and analysis support. At that time, there was a surge of tools to support the new concept. Several vendors provided integrated tools which allowed extensive analysis and communication to be performed together with monitoring and control (for a list see [7]). In addition, there was an increased interest by researchers in the topic. The annual DSS conferences started to include a section of EIS (e.g. [2]) and a minitrack was added at the Hawaii International Conference on System Science (HICSS).

Several books appeared on the market (e.g. [1,4,9]). Many introductory MIS texts added a special chapter on EIS, or included it in the DSS chapter and the topic received attention in most of the DSS texts.

Researchers started to pay attention to EIS/ESS. For example, the following list of research issues was proposed by Watson et al. [10]:

\- Is the organizational position and level of commitment of the executive sponsor related to EIS success?

\- What considerations are most important when selecting an operating sponsor?

\- How can the benefits of an EIS be assessed in advance?

\- How does the software used in building an EIS affect the development process and system success?

\- What level of staffing and organizational structure are best for the EIS builder/support staff?

\- What methods can be most effectively used to identify executive information requirements?

\- What are the major EIS data management problems and their solutions?

\- What impact does the inclusion of soft data have on EIS success?

\- What are the major problems associated with EIS ‘spread’ and its evolution?

\- How can EIS functionality be increased while maintaining ease of use?

\- What emerging technologies (e.g., voice, optical disc) can be effectively used with EIS?

\- What are the most effective screen presentation formats for an EIS?

The third generation of EIS is starting to emerge. These systems are LAN-based and they are attempting to enable executives and other managers to access both internal and external information rapidly from almost any location. The third generation adopted the point of view of Enterprise-wide systems. As a result, one should expect new emphasis in the research area. For example, one may be interested in distributed EIS in the impacts of the technology and its spread within the organization, in system integration, and the use of object-oriented paradigms and graphical user interface. Finally, the attempt to make EIS/ESS smarter, or more intelligent merit attention.

This special issue of Decision Support Systems includes seven papers which present both empirical and theoretical work related to the topic. The essentials of these papers are:

The first paper (by Elam and Leidner) presents the findings of a field study conducted in three organizations regarding the adaption, use and impact of executive support systems. The conclusions of this paper are provided as a set of five propositions which relate decision style, time pressure, reliance on subordinates, speed of making decisions and organization learning to the use of ESS.

While the first paper provides the results of observations regarding the utilization of EIS, the second paper by Bergeron, Raymond and Rivard attempts to identify the major determinants of EIS use. Using a behavioral model developed by Triandis, the authors conducted a field study in nine organizations in Canada. The following variables were investigated: experience with EIS, the influence of the work group (the top executive team), the users' satisfaction with the information provided by the EIS, the ease of use and assistance in case of need, the perceived consequences of the use of EIS, and the sophistication of and availability of help hotline. The study found that the most important determinants were the perceived consequences of using EIS, the users' satisfaction from the EIS, social factors, length of experience with the EIS and the presence of facilitating conditions.

The third paper (by Volonino, Watson and Robinson) deals with a second generation's issue of how to apply EIS to successfully solve complex business problems. Specifically, the paper discusses the EIS capabilities needed for the support of most current organizational strategies and the development of such capabilities using recent system development methodologies and tools. It is interesting to note that Comshare Inc., the largest vendor of EIS tools, is implementing similar ideas (i.e., developing systems for supporting total quality management and client's customization). Special attention is given to the flexibility of the EISs and their ability to respond to changing environmental conditions.

The fourth paper (by Frolick and Robichaux) presents an adaptation of a methodology for system development called strategic business objective (SBO) that adopts an enterprise-wide orientation [8]. This methodology intended to increase the accuracy of identifying the executives' information requirements. A failure to properly identify such requirements is one of the major reasons why EISs fail. The SBO is based on extensive communication between the builders and the users which can be improved by using group support system (GSS) technology. The paper aligns existing GSS tools with the various steps in the SBO methodology.

The development process of EIS can be lengthy and complex and it is one of the major success factors for EIS. Watson and Singh present, in the fifth paper, the results of a survey conducted in 43 organizations regarding how the initial version of their EIS has been developed. The major conclusion is that the first generation of EIS was primarily developed to provide easy and rapid access to internal information. This objective resulted in building the necessary features to support monitoring performance, accessibility to timely data (frequently from multiple databases) and friendly user interface (including multimedia type presentation). This situation underwent change and the second EIS generation includes extensive communication and analysis capabilities. The paper also identifies factors which are positively and negatively associated with the development of successful systems.

A major success factor in developing EIS is the use of an appropriate development process. The sixth paper (by Rainer and Watson) presents the results of 48 interviews which were conducted with users, developers, consultants and vendors of EIS. Key success factors were identified for both the development of EIS and its operation and maintenance. There was a fair amount of consensus among the interviewers with respect to the factors and their importance.

The special issue concludes with a futuristic paper (by Chi and Turban). The authors envision the integration of several intelligent agents into the EIS environment. Such an integration will enable the system to provide quick answers to complex executive queries without the need to conduct a manual drill down and a separate analysis. The proposal is based upon a model of distributed artificial intelligence which allow the intelligent agents to work collaboratively.

Efraim Turban (MBA, Ph.D., University of California at Berkeley) is professor of Information Systems at California State University, Long Beach. Prior to joining CSULB, Dr. Turban was a distinguished professor at Eastern Illinois University. Dr. Turban also taught at UCLA, USC, Florida International University and Lehigh University. Of his many books, the most known are DSS and Expert Systems (MACMILLAN) and Funda-

mentals of Management Science (R.D. IRWIN). His latest book is Applied Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems (MACMILLAN). Dr. Turban authored about 75 papers in journals such as Management Science, Operations Research, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Operations Management, Computers and Operations Research, and The Journal of MIS. Dr. Turban was worked several years in industry (General Electric) and he provided-consulting services to many corporations and governments. His research interests are in the applications of emerging technologies to Management Support Systems. His current work is in the areas of expert systems and multimedia, neural computing and expert systems and executive information systems.
