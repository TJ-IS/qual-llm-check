# Using EIS to respond to dynamic business conditions

Linda Volonino $^{a,*}$ , Hugh J. Watson $^{b}$ , Stephen Robinson $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management Information Systems, Canisius College, 2001 Main St., Buffalo, NY 14208, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

$^{c}$ Financial Institutions Consulting Practices, Ernst and Young, 277 Park Ave., New York, NY 10172, USA

## Abstract

Early research on executive information systems (EISs) focused on distinguishing EISs from other types of support systems and identifying the attributes of their successful design and implementation. Recent research has shifted to a broader, more profitable focus on examining how EISs can be applied to solve challenging business problems. The shift signifies the deemphasis of EISs as elitist applications and the positioning of EIS as a versatile technology for every managerial level. This paper extends that new perspective by investigating how EIS technology can be used to respond to major business problems being intensified by global recessionary and competitive forces – adaptability to changing customer requirements, quality improvement, and cost-containment. To do so, an EIS must be flexible enough to support diverse classes of business data (e.g., external, internal, structured, and unstructured) and distinct classes of users (i.e., executive as well as non-executive users). We examine the current problems confronting organizations and the changes in organizational structure and managerial activities that are being undertaken to mitigate those problems. Then we discuss how emerging technologies and software development tools can redefine the utility, desirability, and economic viability of EIS technology with a focus on the special needs of executives.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Organizational change; Business restructuring

## 1. Introduction

Obliviousness to the latent effects of growth led many organizations to excesses, putting them on a doomsday path to future competitive problems. During business upswings, there is a tendency to capitalize on increased demand by expanding capacity, personnel, and other resources. When an economic or competitive reversal slows growth, the waste, extravagance, and inefficiency of the past become evident on the bottom line [33].

A worldwide economic downturn, punctuated by massive financial losses and liquidity problems, therefore, is a key force driving business restructuring in a growing number of organizations. The vast majority of organizations that prospered during rather comfortably stable business conditions are downsizing to shed excess personnel, streamline business processes, and squeeze inefficiencies out of the organizational structure. In fact anything that adds cost or complexity without adding clear value is a candidate for reform or elimination. In the midst of these streamlining reforms, countervailing forces are pressuring firms to increase speed to market, “delight” customers, and improve quality. Consequently, organizational structures defined by such factors as the degree of centralization and formal communication patterns for coordinating the tasks of individuals and teams are being re-evaluated.

Few would dispute that achieving efficiency or eliminating wastes, without damaging the long-term vigor of the firm, and increasing flexibility requires aligning information resources with corporate business plans. To do so, business end users need systems that provide access to diverse types of information, offer a portfolio of tools and models to gain insight into the information, and enable them to share insights or communicate directives. Many organizations have chosen EIS technology to provide this degree of support.

EISs are relatively new components of the enterprise information architecture. EIS sits atop the information pyramid, illustrated in Figure 1, as the final building block that delivers information supportive of decision making, planning, and control, and presents it in easily interpretable formats. Because of the differences among the information systems shown in Table 1, it is often imperative that the other components of the infrastructure be in place, including the information gateways connecting the basic components, before the EIS can be deployed effectively. Each component is distinguished by a discrete set of characteristics that must be addressed by the technologies and tools used to construct and maintain them.

There is an on-going debate as to whether an EIS is an information system for executives or for everyone [14]. There are dangers and benefits associated with both sides of this argument. When EISs are identified as systems for use by executives (and relatively few others), budgetary cost-justifications can be difficult, thus bringing the cost-effectiveness of the system into question [35]. This disadvantage can warrant that the EIS be rolled out to other knowledge workers. In many instances, the EIS spreads to staff, lower organizational levels, or others whose performance is spotlighted by the EIS.

Even when an EIS spreads to lower organizational levels, it is important that designers remember who constitutes the primary client base and realize that this group of users requires specialized handling that differs considerably from non-executives. Consequently, the risk of identifying EIS as technology for all knowledge workers is that the nature and work requirements of executives can get clumped together with those of middle-level managers, line managers, sales personnel, etc.

We propose, therefore, that EIS be viewed as technology for information delivery to all business end users, one subset of which is the executive user, where the support provided is customized to the needs and nature of the class of user. Operationally, this is achieved by customizing interfaces and system capabilities (e.g., integrated decision support systems) to a common technology infrastructure that drives the EIS. With an integrated technology infrastructure (components of which include PCs, graphical user interfaces, and networks), EIS workstations have the same underlying software and hardware, but have different interface configurations and capabilities depending on the intended user's requirements.

When EISs support job responsibilities and workflows, they become integrated into business processes and tied to the corporate strategy. This provides the necessary link between the functionality of EIS applications and the current business vision of the institution, thus sustaining the life of the EIS and allowing the impact of the technology to grow [35]. When the EIS directly enhances the business' ability to accomplish its objectives, the EIS is immune to other common reasons for failure, namely loss of executive sponsor and lack of organizational focus [36]. An approach to increasing executives' acceptance and use, which further sustains the EIS, is to ensure that EIS applications and interfaces are tailored to the special needs of this unique class of users.

Using this EIS perspective, we examine the effects of dynamic business conditions and how organizational restructuring brings about changes in the nature and scope of management processes. We discuss several key changes supported by EIS which include a redistribution of authority, expanded lateral and vertical communication, improved collaboration and synchronization within and among work groups, and reduction in the dysfunctional features of common control mechanisms such as meetings, memos, and telephones. In a later section, we examine how emerging technologies and software development tools can redefine the utility, desirability, and economic viability of EIS technology, wherein the focus is on executives as the EIS client-base. Included in the discussion on emerging technologies are ways they can be applied to resolve one of the most challenging and critical components of an EIS – data accessibility – which often incurs the greatest time, expense, and delay in EIS development.

## 2. Theory, research and experience

## 2.1. Strategy and structure

Triggered by severe market and economic conditions, many organizations are rethinking their competitive strategies. Several researchers have substantiated that once strategic changes have been implemented, subsequent changes in organizational structure are inevitable $[1,6,11,20,23]$ .

![](/api/attachments/AGGE3DGH/fulltext/images/46c16075dca2dd1bdf24fda8b16faab0a702c669c51c58fe5e0c881857cf1f5e.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Dynamic adjustments initiated by strategic redirection.

Shifts in strategy instigate modifications in structure until it more effectively supports the new strategy. Based on Chandler's [12] "structure follows strategy" research, the model presented in Figure 2 shows the interrelated changes initiated by strategic redirection. The model is also based on the emergent perspective of causal agency, which refers to the assumptions about the identity of the initial (causal) change agent and the direction of causal influence [25]. The emergent perspective proposes that because of dynamic interactions between information technology and organizational constituents, organizational changes are both the cause and result of changes in information system (IS) support. Because of the recursive impacts of these changes, there tends to be mutually adjusting changes in organizational structures, business processes, and information requirements. By supporting information requirements, EIS can play a major role in helping executives and managers adjust to a dynamic business climate. Adjustments that many organizations are undergoing are discussed next.

## 2.2. Nature of change

Under stable conditions, businesses profited from emphasizing division of labor, economies of scale, and hierarchical control. The most widely used ideas about structural design were those developed by classical organizational theorists whose emphasis on economic and technical efficiency led them to recommend dividing up work by function or product [24,38]. In order to promote efficiency, organizations' lines of communication and authority were hierarchical, decision making was centralized, and command-and-control logic permeated management processes. It was presumed that coordination issues were best handled through the management hierarchy wherein subordinates followed the directions of their superiors, military style. Organizations institutionalized procedures for dealing with recurring situations to minimize decision making and maximize consistency, but at the eventual price of an often cumbersome, rigid bureaucracy.

Not surprisingly during the dynamic conditions of the last decade, many organizations learned that their hierarchical structures, business processes, and information systems were archaic and threatening their survival $[30]$ . That situation is attributed to a variety of factors. Designing organizations around functional or divisional boundaries causes fragmentation, which subsequently requires the use of management hierarchy as the integrative mechanism to coordinate efforts $[19]$ . Hierarchy not only leads to managerial levels whose primary role is to supervise people at lower levels, but it also proves to be an insufficient mechanism to achieve coordination, particularly when the objectives of departments or business units are parochial or inconsistent with those of the organization.

With hierarchical lines of communication and authority, each vertical layer acts as a relay which slows information flows and often distorts information [29]. Information flows rarely take the form of serial reproduction because data typically is summarized or interpreted as it moves upward and directives are operationalized as they move downward [3]. Without distortion-free information exchange and feedback, numerous opportunities for loss of control and coordination occur.

Organizations that are fragmented and structured hierarchically naturally have fragmented IS applications and databases dedicated to subobjectives, so they lack integrated, cross-functional systems [39]. Operational flexibility to be responsive to customer demands and increase the speed of doing business requires integrated efforts and ISs, which cannot be achieved without forcing territorial sacrifices from entrenched groups [37]. Largely due to the effects of fragmenting and cementing procedures, potent inertial forces constrain an organization's response to environmental changes [15]. In summary, traditional organizational structures and ISs designed to support them are not suited for a dynamic environment.

## 2.3. Environment and organizational structure

According to organizational (population) ecology theory, when the environment and organizational structure are misaligned, competitive forces drive changes toward structures that more closely match the conditions of the prevailing environment $[10,17]$ . Burns and Stalker $[8]$ investigated the relationship between structure and environment and identified significant structural differences between successful and unsuccessful firms based on whether the environment was stable or dynamic. In stable environments, successful firms had mechanistic characteristics defined as centralized decision making at high levels, many rules and procedures, narrow spans of supervisory control, and mainly hierarchical communication channels. In dynamic environments, the effective organizations had predominantly organic characteristics such as decentralization of decision making, few rules and regulations, wide spans of supervisory control, and both hierarchical and lateral communication channels. Therefore, the business processes being engineered today are toward organic characteristics.

## 2.4. Impacts of restructuring on management processes and information requirements

Drucker [13] describes the direction of restructuring as toward flatter and leaner organizations because of reductions in the vertical levels of management and size of the work force, with self-directed teams and distributed decision making becoming more prevalent. Organizations are becoming less formalized with decentralizing decision making, but with greater centralized control and accountability. Work processes are being redesigned from predominantly isolated or individual activities to activities that are becoming increasingly more integrated or cross-functional.

In flatter organizations, managers whose departments once worked independently now work collaboratively, relying on information systems to share critical information and expertise, eliminate paper-based time-delays, reduce redundancies, and maintain coordination and control. This reduces internal governance costs by reducing the numerous transactions necessary for coordination. Williamson [42] defines transactions as technologically-separable activities. That is, any activity that is involved in producing a product, or creating a report, or monitoring operations that is distinct from the activities preceding and following it because of discontinuities in technology-support constitutes a transaction. There is a direct relationship between the number of transactions and transaction costs, need for coordination, and difficulty in maintaining control.

It is instructive to analyze examples of the influence of EIS in supporting restructured activities. In the next section, EISs are shown to help executives and others meet business challenges through their influence on communication, coordination, control, and feedback.

## 2.5. Use of EIS in restructured organizations

The new work environment is characterized by three types of integration, each of which can be facilitated by an EIS: (1) vertical integration wherein individuals take on work activities that were formerly done by superiors, subordinates, or staff; (2) horizontal integration wherein individuals assume work activities formerly done by peers or colleagues; and (3) concentric integration wherein individuals add new activities to their current job responsibilities. As managers take on more responsibility, they become more dependent on technology such as EIS to help run their information-intensive, downsized organizations. The movement toward decentralization and distributed decision making also heightens the need to empower workers and install control mechanisms for monitoring their performance.

EISs can be used to clarify the purpose of goals, suggest paths to goals, and provide feedback on performance relative to goals $[21]$ . Information about objectives, job responsibilities, directives, viewpoints, budgets, quotas, forecasts, and comparisons with actual results help identify where attention is needed and what action to take. This feedback, or evaluative information, plays an important role in individual behavior and performance $[4]$ . Feedback can impact performance by motivating individuals and providing them with guidance and direction for future action, but only if the information is timely and detailed. Organizations often find that the time to gather, process, and disseminate information is totally inadequate to meet their business needs and consequently much needless uncertainty remains in business decision making $[22]$ . For example, even when activities are within established targets, detrimental patterns may be developing that go unnoticed without detailed data. Those patterns may be the first indication of a problem, which could become serious if left unattended $[18]$ . Consider sales revenues which remain stable, even though repeat orders are decreasing – a pattern that indicates a potentially serious problem. To manage at this level of subtlety requires very detailed data.

An EIS can widen an executive's span of control, thus making it possible to effectively handle more subordinates by using computer monitoring and control as substitutes for personal supervision. The executive support system at Xerox provides this level of detail by "empowering top executives to view critical information unfiltered by management levels" [16]. This ability helps power the shift toward transparency as shown in Figure 3, which enforces greater degrees of accountability on the part of subordinates.

As organizations grow in size and complexity, individuals rely less on firsthand knowledge and more on data that has been provided by others and processed by an information system $[26]$ . Therefore, much of what is known by managers and subordinates depends on what is available to them through their ISs.

By increasing the transparency of what is known to executives and subordinates, an EIS supports coordination and control processes and increases the capacity for self-management. The transparency area is extended to the right by improving information flow from subordinates to executives; it is extended downward by improving information flow from executives to subordinates. The better capture of internal and external information decreases the opaque area. The exchange of accurate, consistent information provides a more unified strategic focus throughout the organization and sets up conditions for maximizing everyone's productivity.

In summary, an EIS can eliminate delays between the occurrence of a serious discrepancy and a manager's ability to find out about it directly. EISs reduce top managers' dependency on staff to evaluate, advise, and alleviate informational burdens. Fewer people can accomplish the same amount of work because better coordination eliminates redundancy and reduces the amount of rework to correct defects or errors. When used this way, an EIS provides the opportunity to reduce internal governance costs by reducing the number of transactions and increasing the ability to manage and control. When an EIS functions as a managerial control system, it can reduce the dysfunctional features of control mechanisms such as meetings, telephones, and written mail. If the EIS is rolled out to lower management levels, it gives them broader access to relevant information for self-management and expedites the vertical and lateral communication that is critical for coordination.

In the next section, we discuss how the utility, desirability, and economic viability of EIS are being redefined at a rapid rate by the arrival and/or acceptance of existing and new technologies in the marketplace. Even though the next generation of executives is apt to be more willing EIS users, the nature of what executives do might not lend itself to heavy computer usage unless EISs are full-featured and integrated with other systems.

## 3. Emerging technologies

New technologies combined with advanced software development tools and methodologies such as computer-aided software engineering (CASE), rapid application development (RAD), and object-oriented programming (OOP) have facilitated the development of the specialized applications required to support executives and other knowledge workers. These advanced tools also incorporate a high level of customization and dynamism which are vital for acceptance and sustained usage. Some of the technologies or application categories that are currently being incorporated into EIS applications include: group decision support systems (GDSS); e-mail with text-annotation, voice-annotation; or voice-to-text capabilities; data analysis and reporting tools (DARTs); advanced spreadsheets; and expert system shells.

Many companies, particularly in highly competitive industries such as investment banking and consumer goods which tend to be early adopters, have successfully and widely deployed EIS using these technologies.

A major Wall Street investment bank has developed a network of specialized workstations that enable bankers to access and share mission-and time-critical information on market conditions directly from the trading floor, along with specific business development data on the firm's clients and prospects. Since these professionals engage in buying and selling activity on behalf of their clients, the efficient exchange of relevant data facilitates quick response to client requests and promotes the rapid matching of capital sources and investment opportunities. This application has been credited for the identification of several closed deals that might not have been surfaced in the past. This system was integrated using off-the-shelf components featuring Lotus Notes, the leading product in the groupware arena, and was implemented less than six months after proposal.

In another example, a Fortune-50 consumer package goods company has installed an EIS that allows marketing executives to track all product orders and shipments worldwide by market, geographic region, and delivered item in real, or near-real time. This has enabled the users to make more informed and timely decisions concerning production, promotion, pricing, and advertising in response to changing conditions in the marketplace. It also facilitates the analysis of this data using sophisticated methodologies unique to that industry, as well as the ability to formally present and share the results among groups of decision makers far more rapidly than was previously practical. This application was developed using the Data Interpretation System (DIS) product from Metaphor. DIS was the first widely available EIS generator to completely incorporate the principles of OOP.

The executive community, now having had several years to adjust to the idea of direct contact with information technology in their personal and professional lives, has recognized the necessity to deploy these technologies within their ranks to improve their efficiency and retention of competitive advantage. At the same time, the myth that information technology at the desktop is inconsistent with an executive image is dissolving. With more advanced interfaces displacing the keyboard – long a symbol of white collar rejection – knowledge workers can communicate in a far more natural fashion. As a result of that naturalness, the demand for these applications has become more widespread, creative, and specific, thus eclipsing developers' and vendors' tendency to create applications that fit their vision of the technology.

What follows is a review of these emerging technologies, and the manner in which current development methodologies and developing conditions in the marketplace are enhancing their utility. A common theme underlying these techniques is their ability to improve data accessibility.

## 3.1. Workstations

The most visible advances in technology have come in the workstation arena where research and intense competition continue to extend the price-performance horizon. The level of processing power, memory, and display capability that can be massively deployed at the desktop throughout the organization continues to grow geometrically. This has freed software developers to create ever more capable tools and applications that emphasize ease-of-use and versatility over efficiency.

One area where this is most evident is user interfaces. For input, the keyboard has given way to far more intuitive, but resource-intensive, modes such as graphical or iconic-pointer modes, and techniques such as pen-based and voice recognition. For output, multimedia technology has dramatically expanded the possibilities for user feedback by enabling the incorporation of photographic-quality full-motion video and high-fidelity audio. By adding multimedia such as voice and video annotations to data rather than merely graphics, the value content of data and the user-friendliness of the EIS are improved. Being able to see and hear voices from the EIS also reduces the unnatural quality of dealing with a computer. This has had the effect of increasing the amount of information that can be communicated back to the user in the same amount of time that is needed to read a textual response.

Another factor that has facilitated the acceptance of technology into the executive workplace has been the enhanced portability of delivered applications. This has been made possible by the miniaturization of hardware components like laptop and palm computing devices that fit in with the itinerant nature of executive life. Portability has been further advanced by the deployment of advanced wireless or cellular technologies that make this portability practical. It is now possible to deliver information using a sophisticated multimedia presentation in travel situations where power and communications connections are not available. Mobile technologies eliminate the common acceptance barrier that exists when information delivery is limited to the desktop from which executives are often trying to break away.

Portability promotes managing in near-real time and at any time which can lead to tangible improvements in productivity by enabling executives to determine when to be productive. The still prevalent use of paper and dictaphones will be further outmoded by the portability offered by wireless LANs, cellular modems and telephones, and notebook computers. Executives will be able to dictate to or query from an EIS directly with minimal physical limitations. A voice can be picked up through a microphone or telephone receiver and attached to a text file to be transferred over a network and annotated to documents, spreadsheets, or charts $[34]$ . Incorporating value-added features into EIS better satisfy executives' preference for verbal information.

As the convergence of EIS and groupware technologies occurs, there will be greater interdependence between managers and information flow across functional and geographical boundaries. Even though EISs typically have e-mail functions, few are currently integrated well enough to provide sufficient improvement in collaboration among work groups $[31]$ .

## 3.2. Information gateways

An entirely new class of software utilities known as information gateways has entered the marketplace, spearheaded by such products as EDA/SQL from Information Builders Inc. (IBI) and Database Gateway from Micro Decisionware Inc. (MDI). These products form a general-purpose data bridge between workstation networks, which often provide the optimal information delivery/user interface platform, and the mainframe-based legacy systems, which reside on more traditional platforms and house the central data resources of the enterprise. The central data resources have traditionally been inaccessible to information seekers in an ad hoc fashion because they were designed to optimize their throughput performance. Gateway products enable such access without the need to replicate and/or summarize the data in advance. The breakthrough is the ability to access non-relational data structures using relational tools like SQL, and SQL-generation tools that are available to end-users. The effect is that of making these institutional tools running on production mainframes appear to the user network as MIS data stores running on a dedicated server. In fact, if dedicated relational servers are available, data from these can be combined with data from non-relational production databases in a single query in a manner that is transparent to the users.

The significance of these capabilities is the opportunity to leverage the ability of the workstation platform to support office automation and analysis functions with the real-time or near real-time information from business operations, without disruption of those operations or use of redundant resources. These tools are designed for maximum compatibility and flexibility (openness) to fit with the majority of commonly used environments. EIS developers can now tap into the information resources that were once inaccessible because of economic and/or operational barriers.

## 3.3. Distributed databases

The advent of distributed database architectures offered by such vendors as Oracle and Sybase has given EIS developers the ability to tie together data stores, that must out of practical necessity be physically distributed over many sites, into one logical whole from which business intelligence can be gleaned. These tools eliminate the need to poll data from the field from a central site and periodically create a snapshot IS repository. Queries can now be processed over the network in real-time to provide up to the minute information in situations where timeliness and completeness are of equal value. These tools, combined with telecommunications and networking technologies, can tie geographically dispersed operations into a single informational view without the need for additional dedicated resources or delays in information delivery.

## 3.4. 32-Bit multitasking operating systems

EISs require a high level of integration, performance, and interface consistency. Integration is necessary to make the multitude of data acquisition, analysis, presentation, and communications functions endemic to the successful EIS into a single seamless application. High performance is fundamental to achieving the responsiveness that executives require, even when performing complex, resource-intensive operations. Intuitive, consistent interfaces are necessary to minimize the training tasks which can pose a barrier to system acceptance.

Until recently, the commercial availability of high performance processors such as the Motorola 68040, Intel 486, and those based on RISC architectures have been hampered by operating systems that either inhibited their performance with 16-bit data paths, or user interfaces that deterred all but the most technically oriented user. With the release of IBM's OS/2 (Rel 2.0) and graphical user interfaces (GUI) shells for UNIX such as Next Step, Motif, and New Wave, this is no longer the case. The Windows NT product from Microsoft (for which a system development kit is already available) will also have a large impact. In addition, the Macintosh platform continues to evolve with the release of System 7, and remains the platform of choice for a dedicated minority of installations. Although it will take some time for application developers and the general marketplace to establish clear platform standards, there has been a continuous flow of application development tools based on these technologies that are well suited for EIS development.

## 3.5. Advanced development tools / methodologies

Among the application development tools that have been developed to exploit these advanced platforms are several that are particularly well suited to EIS development. In particular, those that incorporate object-orientation and RAD have proven to be most successful. The most venerable of these is the Data Interpretation System (DIS) from Metaphor. Others include

PowerBuilder from Powersoft and Easel Workbench from Easel Inc. All of these are designed for the construction of applications which embrace the properties of object orientation that facilitate rapid application development and ease of maintenance, and are essential to meet the dynamic requirements of EIS applications. They all feature GUIs and some feature iconic, event-driven scripting capability without the use of textual or symbolic languages to support ease of use and maintenance. In many cases, customization functions can be assumed entirely by users or user support personnel with a limited technical background. So called data-driven applications, which automatically adapt their structures to changes in source data can practically eliminate the maintenance function.

CASE tools have been used for several years to improve programmer productivity in the development of traditional production/operations-oriented applications. The full benefit of this technology has not been directly available to EIS developers, however, who may be using RAD methodology or OOP development tools since the commonly available CASE products do not specifically support them. This is about to change as several of the major CASE vendors, including Oracle and Knowledgeware (ADW), have either implemented this support or have announced plans to do so in the near future.

This combination of development methodologies and tools, as they mature and gain wide acceptance, will prove to be a powerful enabler to EIS as it will leverage the data modeling and management capability of CASE, with the inherent flexibility and responsiveness of OOP and RAD. By their nature, EISs are heavily oriented toward data management and analysis which necessitate the use of sound data models. The process modeling features enhance the analyst's ability to fit the design of application functionality and user interfaces with the unique and often complex nature of executive work.

## 3.6. Software standards / open architectures

The evolution in the software industry away from proprietary standards and single-vendor solutions toward open architectures based on industry standards has also influenced trends in EIS development. It is now becoming quite common for implementors to first choose a platform and (often by implication) a set of standards to adopt, and then purchase a suite of applications and tools that can integrate seamlessly by virtue of their conformance to those standards. Examples of this include Dynamic Data Exchange (DDE) and Dynamic Linked Libraries (DLL) under Windows, Common User Access (CUA), High-level Application Programming Interface (HLAPI), and Advanced Program to Program Communications (APPC) in the IBM-OS/2 world. Often applications built in this fashion can be so seamless that the user is entirely ignorant of which program is in control of the session and work as a whole better than the old single-user solutions. Among the reasons for this is that the implementor now can choose from among many options for the various necessary system components and assemble an all-star team of components that best fit the needs of that application. Vendors, who have a strong economic incentive to be standards-compliant, seek the competitive advantage of interoperability between their own products and those of other vendors that offer complementary facilities. In recent months, a steady stream of development and marketing alliances were being formed to further this goal.

## 4. Concluding remarks

Today there is an urgent need for organizations to adapt to constantly changing business conditions. EIS workstations, particularly those that incorporate emerging technologies, can increase an organization's ability to react to changing circumstances, quickly and flexibly.

Several factors have served to enhance the demand for EIS. The most important is the increased IS awareness and sophistication of the executive community that has now had far more direct contact with ISs in their homes and schools, in addition to the fear of losing competitive advantage as word of other successful implementations spreads through the executive community.

The cost of the requisite technologies has decreased to the point where extensive hard-dollar justification becomes less necessary to demonstrate the economic value of the investment. IS management, as part of the downsizing trend in the industry, has embraced many of the enabling technologies instead of dogmatically fighting them in the name of tradition and familiarity. Many have gone so far as to retrain their development staffs in the use of the new tools and methods, which in turn has increased understanding of their capabilities within the organization. User organizations in these enterprises no longer have to proceed alone without the support of IS management in the introduction of the technologies that enable EIS. This has removed a formidable barrier to development in many organizations.

The most significant result of these changes is that the technology is no longer driving the viability and design of EIS applications. The determinant forces have become the nature and criticality of the business requirements, combined with the competence and creativity of the designers, developers, and implementors. Coupled with the increasing awareness that executive activities can be facilitated as much by technology as operational activities, this has made the user the focus of the development effort. The next few years will see more profound changes in organizational design and behavior at the top of the organization, influenced by technology in a manner comparable to the changes in the lives of blue-collar and executive support personnel in the past.
