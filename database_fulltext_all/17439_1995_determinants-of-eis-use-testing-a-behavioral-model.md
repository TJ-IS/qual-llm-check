# Determinants of EIS use: Testing a behavioral model $^{1,2}$

François Bergeron ${}^{a,*}$ , Louis Raymond ${}^{b}$ , Suzanne Rivard ${}^{c}$ , Marie-France Gara ${}^{d}$

$^{a}$ Département Systèmes d'information organisationnels, Faculté des sciences de l'administration, Université Laval, Québec, Québec G1K 7P4, Canada

$^{b}$ Département d'administration et d'économique, Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières, Trois-Rivières, Québec G9A 5H7, Canada $^{c}$ Méthodes quantitatives et systèmes d'information, École des Hautes Etudes Commerciales, 5255 Decelles, Montréal, Québec H3T 1V6, Canada $^{d}$ Département Systèmes d'information organisationnels, Faculté des sciences de l'administration, Université Laval, Québec, Québec G1K 7P4, Canada

## Abstract

An increasing number of organizations have implemented executive information systems (EIS), hoping to improve the effectiveness of senior managers and to facilitate their work. The use of such systems is thus a necessary condition through which executive performance can be affected. This study aims at understanding the various factors related to EIS utilization, using as theoretical foundation a model from organizational behaviour. This model, developed by Triandis, leads us to hypothesize that EIS use (behaviour) is determined by EIS experience (habits), work group influence (social factor), user satisfaction with information, system access and assistance (affect), perceived consequences (of EIS use), EIS sophistication and presence of a hotline (facilitating conditions). Field data obtained from 38 executives in nine organizations allowed us to test and confirm the appropriateness of the behavioral model through correlation and regression analyses. The results of the study are shown to have implications both for researchers and practitioners.

Keywords: Executive information systems; EIS use; Behavioral model; Utilization

## 1. Introduction

One of the key developments of the new information technology has been the widening use of executive information systems (EIS) by top-level managers whose decisions must be made in an increasingly competitive and uncertain environment. One can globally define EIS by referring to the use of a computer-based system, most often through direct access to a terminal or personal computer, for any business function. The users are either the CEO or members of the senior management team reporting directly to him or her [31]. An EIS may be implemented at the corporate or divisional level. Some figures help appreciate the explosion of the market for Executive Information Systems in the recent years. As of 1988, approximately 25% of large U.S. companies had senior executives who directly accessed such systems through workstations [7]. In Europe, the British, French and West German market grew by 72% in 1989 alone (Financial Times, May

15, 1990). EIS have also made inroads in the public sector [23] and in the small business sector [26]. In 1990, the software revenues of three executive information systems leaders (Comshare, Execucom and Pilot) was estimated at \$157 millions [16]. As such, EIS are likely to have a direct impact on top-management decision-making and, as a result, on organizational performance [33,39].

Executive information systems are designed to provide internal and external information suited to the unique requirements of top-managers (e.g. in terms of timeliness), and to provide support for executive functions such as planning, tracking and communicating $[11,38]$ . These systems must be comprehensive yet flexible because EIS users can have very different information acquisition behaviours $[13]$ . Top-managers use an EIS because they are often pressured for time and in response to the competitive environment $[12]$ . These systems are appreciated for the quality of the information output, the user interface, the benefits provided and the technical capabilities $[5]$ . Presently used EIS, however, must not be overestimated as there have been cases where systems have fallen far short of their stated goals, or have been outright failures $[7,15]$ . Thus, in view of the current and potential impact of EIS as an advanced information technology designed to enhance executive intelligence and decision making $[19]$ , a fundamental research aim is to understand the various factors related to the successful (or unsuccessful) use of such systems. In this regard, apart from case studies, very few results of systematic field investigations of EIS use have been reported in the literature.

In their review of utilization as a key dependent variable in most streams of IS research (traditional MIS, DSS, end-user computing), Trice and Treacy [38] noted that system use is the necessary condition through which information technology can affect performance. Utilization also becomes more important from a practical standpoint “in a computing environment that is increasingly driven by voluntary users”. These authors also asserted that, as a behaviour whose determinants are not well understood in IS research, system use can best be explained by referring to an appropriate reference theory.

This advice has in fact been followed in certain IS utilization studies $[2,8]$ , with Fishbein and Ajzen's $[14]$ theory of reasoned action as the conceptual framework of choice employed to link user beliefs and attitudes to behaviour. A more recent investigation $[35]$ has sought to explain personal computer usage by grounding its research model on a similar but richer theoretical framework, developed by Triandis $[36,37]$ . The present study on EIS use is in the same vein and employs the same approach.

Given the preceding considerations, the purpose of this study is to identify the behavioral factors which explain the use of an EIS by executives. Based on Triandis' comprehensive and recognized framework from the organizational behaviour discipline, an explanatory model of EIS usage was tested empirically. This model implies that system use will be influenced by a number of social, attitudinal and situational factors, through nineteen hypothesized relationships.

## 2. Triandis' model of values, attitudes and behaviour

In their attempts to explain user behaviours, some IS researchers relied on Ajzen and Fishbein's [1] model of beliefs, attitudes and behaviour [18,38]. While it is very useful, this model is somewhat incomplete in that it leaves aside factors which could also have an influence on behaviour intentions, and on behaviour itself [37]. In an attempt to encompass a larger number of relevant variables, Triandis proposed a “theoretical network of interrelated hypotheses around the constructs of attitude and behaviour, placing them in the broadest possible context” ([37], p. 196). As shown in Fig. 1, the model includes a large number of variables, and a larger number of hypotheses. Since the study reported here examined only a subset of Triandis’ model, it is not the objective of the paper to provide a detailed description of the model and of its 34 related hypotheses. Rather, a general presentation of the framework is made, followed by its operationalization in the context of EIS utilization. For a thorough discussion of the model, the reader should refer to Triandis [37].

Akin to Ajzen and Fishbein's framework, Triandis' model relates behaviour to intentions. However, Triandis claims that, in addition to intentions, other factors directly influence behaviour. These factors are 1) habits, 2) relevant arousal, and 3) facilitating conditions.

Triandis defines habits as “situation-behaviour sequences that are or have become automatic, so that they occur without self-instruction” (p. 204). Habits are closely related to an individual’s past experience and ability to perform a given act. The model suggests that in addition to intentions, the habitual nature of a behaviour will have an influence on the individual response to a given situation. Triandis even argues that for many behaviours, habit is more important than intention.

Relevant arousal is a physiological factor. Triandis states that “the physiological arousal of the organism that is relevant to the act facilitates the act, and increases its probability” (p. 205). The model suggests that relevant arousal directly influences behaviour and is influenced by genetic and biological factors, as well as by the social situation, that is, the behaviour setting.

According to Triandis, it may happen that an individual has the intention to do something, but is unable to do it because the environment prevents the act to be performed. Consequently, the level of facilitating conditions is an important factor in explaining an individual's behaviour, and must be taken into account. In turn, facilitating conditions are dependent on the social situation.

Intentions constitute another of the framework's constructs. They are influenced by social factors, affect, and the behaviour's consequences. Social factors consist in the internalization that people make of the subjective culture of the reference group to which they belong or with which they interact most frequently. Thus, in addition to influencing intentions, social factors are themselves dependent on the social situation, and on the individual's perception of subjective culture variables. Social factors are similar to Ajzen and Fishbein's social norms.

Fig. 1. Triandis' framework.

Affect relates to the individual's feelings of pleasure, displeasure, joy, delight or disgust, toward a given behaviour. Positive feelings will increase the intention toward a given behaviour, while negative feelings will decrease them. Affect is influenced by the individual's habits and by his/her perceptions of subjective culture variables.

The factor “consequences” actually consists in the expected value of a behaviour; it is a function of the perceived consequences of an act and of the value of each consequence. Perceived consequence refers to the probability that a given consequence will follow from performing the behaviour. The value of the consequence is the “affect attached to the consequence” (p. 203). The model hypothesizes that the higher the expected value of the act, the more likely the person will intend to perform it. As is the case for the social factors and affect variables, consequences are influenced by the individual’s perceptions of subjective culture variables. In addition to influencing behaviour, through intentions, consequences are influenced by it. More precisely, the objective consequences of the behaviour are interpreted by the individual, and, "as a result of these interpretations, the person feels reinforced" (p. 198). Reinforcement has two different impacts on the consequences of a behaviour. First, it changes the subjective probabilities that a behaviour will result in a given consequence. Second, it changes the value of the consequences.

While the previous variables of Triandis' model refer to aspects which are directly related to an individual, the other constructs are related to the individual's environment, and shape the individual's personality. These variables are history, ecology, and culture. Triandis states that personality is an outcome of situation-behaviour-reinforcement sequences and of the subjective culture of the individual. In turn, personality has an impact on the way people will interpret the objective consequences of their behaviour.

As this description of the model shows, Triandis' framework is not only very complete, it is also quite complex. For the purpose of the study reported here, it appeared more appropriate to focus on the subset of the model that included the variables that are the most relevant to explaining EIS utilization. The research model adopted for this study is depicted in Fig. 2. It includes six variables: habits, consequences, affect, social factors, facilitating conditions, and behaviour. As will be discussed in more detail in the following section, habits were operationalized here by assessing the individual's experience with the EIS. Consequences consist in the perceived consequences of EIS use on the executive's work. Affect is constituted by the person's satisfaction with the information provided by the system, with the ease of access and with the assistance the person receives. Social factors were operationalized by measuring the individual's work group influence. The availability of a hotline to help executive in their use of the EIS, as well as the degree of sophistication of the EIS constitute the facilitating conditions. Finally, behaviour was operationalized by measuring frequency of EIS use and internalization of use.

Some variables such as culture, social situation and genetic/biological factors were felt to be less relevant to the explanation of EIS use, and were not taken into account. The same variables were excluded by Thompson and al. [35] in their study of the utilization of personal computers. According to these authors, culture, social situation and genetic and biological factors are not germane to the understanding of personal use of information technology. In addition, the difficulty of measuring such complex variables precludes their inclusion in the present research model.

It should also be noticed that the model does not include behavioral intentions. This could be seen as a limitation of the study, since Triandis hypothesized relationships between behaviour and most of the other variables via intentions, rather than directly. However, similarly to the works by Thompson et al. [35], and Moore and Benbasat [24], intentions are not included in the research design. As indicated by Ajzen and Fishbein [1, pp. 47–52] it is important to measure intentions as close as possible to the behavioral observation to obtain an accurate prediction but it may not be feasible nor of practical value to do so. Indeed, fully testing the model would have required a longitudinal study. This approach was dismissed given the inherent difficulties of such a methodological approach in the context of this research and the probability of an internal validity problem due to historical events that could happen between the time intentions and behaviour are measured. Moreover, our use of the Triandis' model is not to predict behaviour but to explain it.

In line with Triandis' proposal, the following hypotheses are embedded in the research model, and were tested:

H1 The longer the user's experience with the EIS, the higher the satisfaction with:

a) its information contents,

b) its access mechanisms, and

c) the assistance provided.

Previous research has identified computer experience as a determinant of user attitudes toward information systems. In the present study, EIS experience is used as a proxy for habits. Executives who had been using organizational applications for a greater length of time were seen to have better attitudes in terms of user comprehension and participation [28]. In the same vein, length of use was positively related to user satisfaction with a DSS [32]. One should expect a similar phenomenon to occur in regard to EIS.

H2 EIS experience is positively related to: a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

According to Triandis, habits have a major contribution to the explanation of behaviour. While, in their study of the utilization of personal computers based on Triandis' model, Thompson et al. decided not to take into account the habit variable, the results they obtained led them to conclude that the inclusion of habits in their research design might have been appropriate. These authors argue that the amount of variance explained in personal computer utilization might have been increased by the inclusion of this construct.

H3 Perceived consequences of EIS utilization is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

Thompson et al. examined the relationships between the utilization of personal computers and their perceived consequences. Their study included two different operationalizations of perceived consequences: job fit and long-term consequences. In both cases, the variables were found to be positively related to utilization. In a study on system usage, Davis [8] found perceived usefulness to be significantly correlated with both self-reported current usage and self-predicted future usage. Davis' definition of perceived usefulness is closely related to the notion of perceived consequences. In the same line of thought, the current research model hypothesizes that perceived consequences of EIS use will be positively related to usage.

H4 Work group influence in regard to EIS use is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

Triandis, along with Azjen and Fishbein, states that social norms have an important influence on behaviour. In the context of end user computing, utilization of personal computers was found to be positively related to social norms [35].

H5 Satisfaction with EIS information contents is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

H6 Satisfaction with EIS access mechanisms is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

H7 Satisfaction with assistance in regard to EIS is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

Triandis' model hypothesizes that affect will be positively related to behaviour. That is, the more a behaviour is appealing, the more often it is likely to occur. In their test of Triandis' framework in the context of personal computer use, Thompson and al. found no significant relationship between these two variables. According to these authors, one explanation may lie in the way affect was operationalized. They further mention that improving the measure might lead to different results. For this reason, the present study assessed respondents' affect toward various facets of EIS information provided by the system, system access, and support provided.

H8 The sophistication of the EIS is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

H9 The availability of an EIS hot-line is positively related to:

a) the internalization of EIS use, and

b) the frequency of use.

According to Triandis, facilitating conditions will be positively related to behaviour. In the context of personal use of information technologies, facilitating conditions have often been seen as support provided to users $[4,6,35]$ . This view is also adopted in the present study. In addition, it was found that the various features offered by a given EIS is akin to the facilitating conditions surrounding its use. Consequently, it is hypothesized that the degree of sophistication of an EIS will be positively correlated with usage.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sample

The data collected for the purposes of this study were obtained from 38 EIS users in 9 enterprises, located in the province of Québec, Canada. An initial phone contact was made with IS directors to determine if their organization had in fact implemented an EIS, on the basis of the definition given in the introduction. When there was a willingness to cooperate, a letter of instruction and questionnaires were mailed, to be distributed to executive users within the organization. The sample consists of large organizations in sectors such as insurance, pulp and paper, utilities and government, whose mean number of employees is 1,160. These organizations have a mean annual revenue, IS budget and EIS budget of \$334,500,000, \$12,760,000 and \$156,000 respectively. A total of 33 out of the 38 sampled EIS users are top-level managers (CEO, vice-president, director of marketing, finance or production); the five others are middle managers whose presence confirms a trend for certain organizations to make their EIS available to all managers and staff personnel. Indeed, the term EIS is proving to be a misnomer since they are also finding a home on the desks of middle managers eager to analyze what is happening within the company [17,54,21].

The managers had been using an EIS for 13 months on average. Twenty-nine percent of the systems had been developed in-house while the others were either commercial or a mix of commercial and in-house software. The characteristics of the EIS are presented in Table 1. They correspond to the elements included in the EIS sophistication scale.

## 3.2. Measures

The measurement approach used for the variables in the research model is based on Triandis' suggested operationalizations of the constructs defined in his framework, as applied to the specific context of this study and guided by previous empirical results on system use. The following paragraphs describe the various measures used, and the Appendix presents the items used for each scale.

Habits: Triandis asserts that habits require learning which “is a function of its close contiguity in time”. He also indicates that this can be measured by the frequency of occurrence of a behaviour. Since it is one of the measures employed here for EIS use, this would be tautological [35]. For this reason, habits was measured by ascertaining the user's EIS experience, i.e. the number of months since he or she had first begun to use the system. In fact, IS research found early on that users learn experientially [22]. Previous research has also related computer experience to the successful implementation of end-user computing [30] and personal DSS applications [29].

Consequences: The perceived consequences of a behaviour (or subjective utility) result from the product of the individual's "beliefs" that such consequences will occur by the "value" attached to these consequences. Triandis concurs with Fishbein and Ajzen on this aspect. Such an attitudinal construct was measured here by evaluating user beliefs on the expected consequences of EIS use upon managerial and decisional performance (six 4-point Likert scales, 0: very uncertain, 3: very certain), and multiplying each score by a second score obtained by having users attach a value to these same consequences (six 7-point Likert scales, -3: very bad, +3: very good). The resulting score obtained by averaging on the six scales thus varies from -9 to +9. The scales were found to be reliable, with a Cronbach's alpha equal to 0.80 [25]. They were built following the steps suggested by Ajzen and Fishbein [1, pp. 261–263].

Social factors: The reference group's subjective culture influences the individual's notion of an appropriate or desirable behaviour. In the present context, this refers to the influence of the executive's work group (peers, superior, subordinates, IS director) upon his or her use of EIS. As for the previous variable, this was measured by obtaining the users' assessment of the influence of the work group upon their behaviour in general (four 4-point Likert scales, 0: very weak, 3: very strong), and multiplying by their evaluation of the probability that the work group does in fact want them to use the EIS (four 7-point Likert scales, -3: very improbable, +3: very probable). The scales were taken from Ajzen and Fishbein [1, pp. 74–75; pp. 272–273] and adapted to the EIS environment. The alpha for the resulting four scales was found to be at the 0.70 level.

Affect: This construct refers to a person's "feelings" (pleasant-unpleasant) associated with a particular behaviour. It was operationalized in this study through three variables. The first one measures the user's feelings of satisfaction with the information content of the EIS (timeliness, completeness, etc.), using an instrument developed by Bergeron [3] which consists of twelve 7-point semantic differential scales. The second measure looks at user satisfaction with the quality of the system's access mechanisms (ease of use, system reliability, response time, etc.), using thirteen 7-point semantic differential scales adapted from Swanson's [34] instrument. A third measure of user satisfaction with the level of assistance (quality of the support provided by the information systems personnel) was also adapted from Bergeron, Rivard and De Serre's [6] instrument (ten scales). The three measures were found reliable, with alpha equal to 0.89, 0.83 and 0.94 respectively.

Facilitating conditions: Being “out there” in the environment, certain objective factors can make an act easier to do. In terms of EIS use, the two facilitating conditions deemed to be immediately relevant were the sophistication of the EIS itself, and the availability of a hotline for user assistance. The first variable was measured by ascertaining the presence of various technical capabilities associated with commercial EIS products such as “tunnelling”, “traffic-lighting”, graphics and the capacity to acquire external information (seven 3-point scales, 1: no, 2: more or less, 3: yes). The alpha for these scales was equal to 0.61. The second variable was measured on a dichotomous scale (0: no, 1: yes), as used by Bergeron and Bérubé [4].

Internalization of EIS use: One of the fundamental aspects of behaviour which can be measured is its intensity, i.e. the degree to which it is “internalized” by the actor. In terms of system use, Trice and Treacy [38] identify three relevant aspects of this construct. The first one relates to the user’s level of “dependence” upon the system. The second one is the extent of system “ownership” felt by the user. The third aspect refers to the “routinization” of system usage. Following these suggestions, a measure of internalization of system use was designed for the purpose of the study. The measure consists in seven 5-point scales (1: fully disagree, 5: fully agree) which characterize the extent to which the system has become an integral part of the executive’s work activities. The alpha for this last measure was equal to 0.83.

Frequency of EIS use: Another aspect of behaviour which can be measured is its frequency. Triandis asserts that “it may be more useful to use the actor’s perspective” than the observer’s because the former includes past, present and future behaviour, whereas the latter includes “only” present behaviour (unless the study is longitudinal). Most of the studies reviewed by Trice and Treacy have in fact operationalized system utilization subjectively through self-report measures. In this study, frequency of use was obtained by asking executives to indicate the average number of terminal sessions per month they initiate in using the EIS.

## 4. Results and discussion

## 4.1. Bivariate analysis

Tables 3 and 4 present the results of testing the hypotheses embedded in the research model (Fig. 2). The results do not support the hypothesized relationship between EIS experience and satisfaction (H1). The fact that a negative relationship was found between EIS experience and one affect factor (satisfaction with assistance) may appear counter-intuitive. However, results of a prior empirical study tend to suggest such a relationship, as Raymond [27] found a negative correlation between organizational IS experience and user satisfaction. On one hand, the significant relationship between EIS experience and satisfaction with assistance would suggest that managers having more experience with EIS are generally less satisfied with the support they receive from the IS personnel in the organization. On the other hand, less experienced users are more satisfied with assistance. This might be due to the fact that less experienced users usually face simpler problems which are easily solved by IS personnel. These users therefore show a higher level of satisfaction with the answers obtained, i.e., with assistance. More knowledgeable users face more complex problems which can probably not be solved as easily by the support personnel.

As the research model assumes the effect of EIS experience on usage to be both direct and indirect, that is, through affect in the latter case, partial correlation coefficients were also calculated. When controlling for all three affect variables (satisfaction with information, with access and with assistance), the correlation between experience and internalization becomes non-significant $r = 0.10, p = 0.28$ , and the one between experience and frequency of use remains non significant $r = 0.07, p = 0.36$ . In regard to internalization, this added result would indicate that the effect of experience is mostly indirect, and that this variable is in fact more antecedent than simultaneous in its relationship with EIS use.

Another strongly supported relationship is the one between the perceived consequences of EIS and internalization of use (H3a). For the EIS to be used, it should therefore be seen not only as having the potential of producing results, but the potential of producing results valued by managers. This is consistent with the results of a survey of 52 EIS users [5] which concluded that system designers must focus on the features of EIS that provide actual benefits to users.

The significant relationship observed between the work group's influence in regard to EIS and internalization of use (H4a) calls for the organization to adequately prepare EIS implementation by focusing not only on individual users but also on their work units [20]. This result might in fact indicate the need for a new perspective in EIS implementation research. Whereas up to now only the individual executive user has been considered, future research should take a broader perspective by including the executive's group and even members of other organizations whom users consider as their peers.

Satisfaction with information content and access also showed significant relationships with internalization of EIS use (H5a, H6a). These two independent variables were also significantly related to frequency of use (H5b, H6b). This could be expected since quality of information output was found to be the most important attribute of EIS for top-managers [5]. In other information systems for which use is not mandatory, satisfaction with information and access has been recognized as a “sine qua non” condition to greater usage [34].

As a facilitating condition, EIS sophistication was also found to be correlated with internalization of EIS use (H8a). This signifies that EIS designers must increase the sophistication of their products by improving the technical capabilities, for instance, by providing increased availability of external information.

Finally, a significant but negative relationship was found between the presence of an EIS hotline and the internalization of use (H9a). This is in contradiction with earlier results of a study of 212 end-users [4], in which end-users were more satisfied with their microcomputing activities when a hotline was available. Accordingly, in this study, the availability of a hot-line as a facilitating condition should have shown a positive rather than negative relationship with behaviour. This might be explained by the fact that hot-line services are specifically offered to EIS users experimenting problems with their system. Generally speaking, an EIS may be sufficiently user-friendly and easy to use that assistance is not really required, once the initial training has been provided to the manager. It is only when there are serious problems that a hot-line is offered to placate the user. This suggests that in the case of EIS, a hot-line may be more of a resulting than a facilitating condition. Another explanation may be in the fact that EIS being a particular case of information system, a hot-line is not the truly appropriate support mechanism for users. It is possible that executives require support in a face-to-face fashion, rather than through a hot-line.

There are certain limitations to this study that must be mentioned. First, the size and nature of the sample evidently preclude an outright generalization of the results. However, these systems can provide other types of support, including office support (e.g. e-mail), and more importantly, executive team support with tools designed to facilitate teamwork (group communication, decision) and to enhance executives' mental models of the organisation's business environment [41]. Third, there is the ever-present dependent variable issue. EIS use per se is not necessarily successful use, and it is but one of a number of systems success categories [3], [10]. ## 5. Conclusion

To summarize, the results of this study show that the behaviour of EIS users, as measured by the internalization of use, is determined in order of importance by the perceived consequences of using EIS, by an affect component, by social factors (work group influence in regard to EIS), by habits (EIS experience) and by facilitating conditions (the sophistication of EIS products). While this research does have some limitations, it has nevertheless provided findings which contribute to a better understanding of EIS utilization factors.

These results also have implications for EIS research and practice. In light of preceding arguments, future investigations should aim for a cumulative tradition by continuing to employ Triandis' framework as a theoretical foundation to further understand the phenomenon of EIS use. For instance, such an investigation could include unobtrusive operationalizations of EIS use in addition to self-report measures. To increase the practical relevance of this line of research, one could also examine the complex linkages between utilization and performance.

EIS designers should be conscious of the greater importance of the information contents of the system (e.g. timeliness), compared to its access mechanisms (e.g. minimal keyboard use) or its technical capabilities (e.g. drill-down). In this sense, a good design should be focused first and foremost on the former criteria, even if the latter are more “marketable” when considering EIS as a commercial product. Those responsible for implementing an EIS should also recognize the importance of group influences on the utilization behaviour of executives. This should lead to a more global perspective, where the EIS implementation process is adapted to the interpersonal contingencies of the users’ intra and extra-organizational environment.
