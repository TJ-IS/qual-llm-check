# EIS information requirements determination: Using a group support system to enhance the strategic business objectives method

Mark N. Frolick ${}^{a,*}$ , Barry P. Robichaux ${}^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of Memphis, Management Information Systems, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, Memphis, Tennessee 38152, USA

$^{b}$ University of Houston, College of Business Administration, Melcher Hall 280G-MIS, Houston, Texas 77204, USA

## Abstract

Executive information systems (EIS) provide executives with easy access to high-level strategic information about a firm's current status. The potential of EIS is reflected in a growing list of success stories. Unfortunately, more EIS fail than succeed. Among the reasons for EIS failure is the inability to develop systems that meet executive needs. The strategic business objectives (SBO) method has previously been suggested to meet this challenge. The SBO method takes an organization-wide view of EIS information requirements by identifying and linking business objectives related to the strategic business objectives of the organization. The method relies on effective communication between EIS executive users and EIS developers. The SBO method can be enhanced by group support systems (GSS). GSS support a variety of group activities, including communication, planning, and decision making. Group activities within the SBO method that benefit from GSS support are identified and matched with GSS software tools and procedures. Advantages and disadvantages of this approach are discussed.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Group support systems; Information requirements; Strategic business objectives

## 1. Introduction

Executive information systems (EIS) have traditionally been defined as computer-based information systems (IS) that provide executives with easy access to high-level strategic information about a firm's current status [21,25]. EIS retrieve data from other functional systems and elsewhere in the organization and present this information in summary form to assist senior executives in their strategic decision-making. Recently, there has been considerable discussion in the literature regarding EIS use trickling down the organization hierarchy [e.g.,2,5,13]. This increased EIS usage by other organization personnel has led to EIS being referred to as “everyone information systems” or “enterprise-wide” EIS.

A growing list of success stories attest to EIS' potential [e.g.,8,16]. Unfortunately, more EIS fail than succeed [16]. Among the reasons for EIS failure is the inability to develop systems that meet executive needs [27]. Davis [3] lists three obstacles to successful information requirements determination: (1) constraints on humans as information processors and problem solvers, (2) variety and complexity of information requirements, and (3) complex patterns of interaction among users and analysts in defining requirements. Valusek and Fryback [20] label these obstacles as WITHIN (a user), AMONG (users), and BETWEEN (a user and a developer), respectively. These obstacles hinder communication between users and developers. These obstacles are more pronounced when executive information requirements are desired due to the less structured nature of much executive work. Executives may be unable to agree among themselves which components of an EIS are essential, or satisfactorily explain their information needs to system developers.

IS professionals are often unable to properly determine executives' information requirements. This is partly due to their approaching executive requirements determination as an extension of their prior experiences with traditional information systems [2]. Inexperienced analysts may not realize the extent to which actual information requirements are likely to differ from what executives say they are. Even when this is known, reliable procedures for obtaining actual requirements may not be available. Executives, on the other hand, may assume that analysts can develop useful systems even when information requirements are not fully specified. Yadav [29] refers to this dilemma as an "information no man's land." This problem can be lessened by using experienced EIS analysts having established relationships with the executives that they support. EIS consultants with proven track records of understanding executives' information requirements and delivering useful systems can also be used. Regardless of the principals involved, the difficulties in accurately determining executives' information requirements suggest that methods which enhance communication between executives and EIS developers should be employed.

The purpose of this paper is to suggest a method for determining executives' information requirements and supporting communication between executives and EIS developers. The strategic business objectives (SBO) method is proposed to help determine EIS information requirements. The procedure outlined by this method facilitates identification of information requirements related to the strategic business objectives of an organization and the underlying business processes aimed at meeting the objectives. Effective communication between executives and EIS developers is necessary to apply this method. As such, a procedure that improves communications should prove to be useful during the EIS information requirements determination process using the SBO method. Group support systems (GSS) provide automated support for group activities. This support includes software tools and procedures designed to improve group communication in meetings. The authors see the augmentation of the SBO method with GSS as a more effective way to determine and manage information requirements for EIS.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Prior research in information requirements determination is first reviewed, with specific regard to methods used for executive information requirements. One of these methods, the SBO method, is then discussed in greater detail. This is followed by a description of group support systems. Next, an approach for augmenting the SBO method with GSS is presented. Finally, the advantages and disadvantages of this approach are discussed.

## 2. Prior research

This section reviews prior research on information requirements determination, including work by Davis [3] in specifying levels of requirements and general strategies for their determination. The section then identifies a number of approaches geared toward executive information requirements. These include the critical success factors method, prototyping, and the SBO method.

Davis distinguishes between two levels of information requirements, organization-level and application-level [3]. Organization-level requirements often take the form of overall, or master, plans for information systems in an organization. Boundaries and interfaces for application subsystems are defined, but detailed requirements are not determined. Application-level requirements do include the detailed requirements associated with much system development. EIS rely on both levels of requirements, an overall plan for assessing the organization's performance and specific status indicators that allow for this assessment.

Davis also identifies four strategies for determining information requirements: (1) asking, (2) deriving from an existing information system, (3) synthesizing from characteristics of the utilizing system, and (4) discovering from experimentation with an evolving information system [3]. Specific methods have been proposed for each strategy, or for a combination of strategies. A number of such methods are listed in this section. Characteristics of the organization, the application, or both may dictate the appropriate method to be used. For EIS, existing systems may not be in place, and simply asking executives about their information requirements may yield few details. As indicated earlier, many executives may be unable to delineate their information requirements due to the unstructured nature of much of the work they do.

Watson and Frolick [25] suggest several methods for determining EIS information requirements. The EIS support staff can participate in strategic planning sessions, conduct informal discussions with executives, track executive activity, discuss needs with executive support personnel, examine computer and noncomputer generated information, attend executive meetings, and/or track EIS usage. Watson and Frolick further suggest that rather than using one specific method, organizations should use a portfolio of methods. Some methods may be better for determining information requirements in the initial version of an EIS; others may be more appropriate for changing requirements once executives have a chance to use the system.

Rockart and Treacy [15] proposed the critical success factor (CSF) method for information requirements determination. It helps executives to focus on those things that “must go right” for the organization to succeed. Key performance measures of CSFs are often financial data residing in computerized files already used for other applications in the organization. A potential problem with the CSF method is that it ignores executives’ need for soft information [7]. Soft information includes impressions, gossip, and news of recent or impending events. This information is not located in organization databases; it comes from publications or human sources both internal and external to the organization. EIS that do not incorporate soft information fail to provide much of the information that executives use.

Another problem with the CSF approach is that it may not be timely enough. Organizations using this approach must realize that CSFs change as executives' information requirements change. This trait of CSFs suggests an evolving approach to requirements determination consistent with the fourth strategy identified by Davis [3]. If CSFs are employed for EIS development they must be recognized as temporary indicators of organization status. System reviews are warranted periodically to determine if systems developed based on CSFs remain useful planning and decision-making aids.

Watson, Rainer, and Koh [28] suggest a prototyping approach to EIS development. Prototypes are used when it is difficult to determine information requirements in advance, as is often the case for EIS [17]. EIS have been called “eternal prototypes” due to their propensity toward change [23]. Prototyping allows EIS analysts to refine the EIS through quick iterations as information requirements become better known or change through executive use. A potential problem with prototyping is that the prototype may reflect the information needs of the executive sponsor and fail to build a broad base for EIS support. By emphasizing individual needs over organization needs the EIS does not become a part of the corporate culture [5]. If the executive sponsor leaves the organization, an EIS based on his information needs alone may be abandoned if it does not fulfil the information needs of other organizational personnel [2, 5, 23]. Thus, when prototyping, a conscious effort must be made to build organization-wide support for EIS [24].

Volonino and her colleagues [22,24] have documented an innovative approach for identifying EIS information requirements: the strategic business objectives (SBO) method. This method takes an organization-wide view of information requirements by identifying business objectives and linking them to the information needs of individuals throughout the organization. This approach allows the EIS to be developed as an enterprise-wide system. Specifically, the EIS will be developed with the needs of all organizational executives in mind, not just the needs of one or a few.

Fisher-Price was the first organization known to use the SBO method for determining EIS information requirements [22]. Fisher-Price's EIS has passed one test associated with many EIS failures, turnover of the executive sponsor. The system has been so successful that “even though the president and all of the vice-presidents have changed, the EIS continues to be an integral part of the management process at Fisher-Price” [24, p. 38]. The success of Fisher-Price may have influenced others to try the SBO method. Watson and Frolick [26] recently conducted a survey of EIS developers of which 43% stated that they use this method or something approximating it.

The SBO method can be augmented by the use of group support systems (GSS). Since this method relies on effective communication between EIS executive users and developers, any impediments to effective communication must be identified and removed. GSS software tools and procedures can be used to improve communication between these two groups and to capture the information used to develop an EIS. The next two sections describe, in turn, the SBO method and GSS.

## 3. The strategic business objectives (SBO) method

The SBO method suggests a six-step procedure to determine executive information requirements and promote EIS development based on an organization's strategic business objectives [24]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the method begins with determination of an organization's SBOs. For example, SBOs can include being the market leader, the low-cost provider, or the leader in a specific service.

Once the SBOs are identified, the next step is to identify related business processes. For example, if an SBO is to be the low-cost provider, then the purchasing, manufacturing, and distribution processes employed in advance of product sales are critical to meeting this objective. Identification of all related business processes is necessary for each SBO. This helps to pinpoint where much of the information required to monitor the SBOs can be found. The method's third step is to then prioritize the SBOs and their related business processes. This prioritized list of objectives allows the EIS development team to address the organization's most pressing information needs first.

Step four specifies determination of the information critical to each business process. One or more of the methods suggested by Watson and Frolick [25] can be used to help obtain this information. As this is the basis of requirements determination for EIS design, it is important that the correct information is collected and represented. While a number of methods might be used to obtain this information, the nature of executive work suggests that methods which leverage executives' time and focus their attention should be applied. Communication support may be most critical during this step of the process.

Information linkages are shared information needs across business processes. Step five of the SBO method requires identification of information linkages across the SBO business processes. Linkages help to identify all information relevant to any business process. For example, a production manager may need access to information about inventory levels, production potential, and delivery dates promised by salespeople. This information may reside in various databases supporting distinct business processes, but it is needed by the production manager to ensure a smooth production process. Identification of these information linkages is very important to EIS design. The sixth and final step of the SBO method is to plan for the development, implementation, and evolution of the system. Since EIS are evolving systems, it is important to revisit any and all steps necessary when executives' information requirements change.

The SBO method requires extensive coordination of communication between executive users and EIS developers. The developers must learn the organization's business, the threats and opportunities facing the organization, and how executive productivity might be improved with an EIS [24]. The evolving nature of EIS requires that this communication be ongoing. GSS can be used to support communication between executives and EIS developers. Information can be shared through the system and simultaneously recorded for later use in EIS development. GSS also provide software tools and procedures designed to overcome many shortcomings of traditional meetings and to provide support for planning, decision making, and other group activities. Its use in conjunction with the SBO method should make meetings between executives and EIS developers more productive and should improve communication and shared understanding between the two groups.

## 4. Group support systems (GSS)

Given the difficulty of information requirements determination, tools and procedures which help to overcome the obstacles to successful information requirement determination should be applied. Group support systems are suggested here for use in this capacity. GSS are designed to support and structure group interactions, including information sharing, planning, problem solving, decision making, and conflict resolution. GSS automate group meetings, providing group members with computer workstations, software tools, and procedures that allow them to enter their ideas, comments, and evaluations and view those of others. The principal advantages of GSS include [11]:

(1) Parallel communication, which promotes broader input into the meeting process and reduces the chance that only a few people will dominate the meeting;

(2) Anonymous input, which mitigates evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure so that issues are discussed candidly;

(3) Process support and structure, which helps to focus the group on key issues and to discourage irrelevant digressions and unproductive behaviours; and

(4) Task support and structure, which provides information and approaches to analyze it.

The primary venue for GSS use is the decision room, a technically-supported meeting room where groups gather to identify problems, discuss ideas and alternatives, and select a course of action $[11]$ . In this setting, groups are usually led by a meeting facilitator who leads the group through activities designed to help it achieve its goals (a facilitator may or may not be a member of the group). Parallel communication and anonymity result directly from the use of computer keyboards. Since group members type comments that are collected in electronic files, they do not have to share floor time or reveal their identity as speakers must. If desired, some GSS allow comments to be tagged with the authors' names, but anonymity is more common. Process and task support and structure result from many aspects of GSS use including involving all group members at all times, tying meeting agendas to GSS use, facilitators providing direction, and software tools which help the group to achieve its goals.

The facilitator selects specific process structuring software tools and procedures to help direct group activities toward the desired outcomes. When groups get off track or slowed down in the meeting, facilitators keep things moving by getting the group to refocus on their goals. Facilitators provide instruction as needed for tool use or for task support. When a facilitator is not a group member, it is best to have a recognized group leader to consult for issues that are task-related. Facilitators will typically hold a running conversation with the group, but verbal communication among group members can vary greatly, from nearly none to being the principal mode of communication. When verbal exchanges are common, a policy for recording these contributions should exist. The record of the meeting will be incomplete if important exchanges are not recorded.

It should be noted that meetings can employ facilitators and many of the procedures commonly used with GSS without using a GSS. However, problems with traditional meetings are well documented [e.g., 9] and suggest that facilitators and procedures alone are not enough. The advantages provided by GSS merit their consideration when organizations experience unproductive meetings. Many advantages result from software tools which provide process and task support and structure. A few software tools common to GSS are discussed next.

GSS offer an assortment of software tools which support a variety of group activities. For example, a group of executive users and support personnel meeting to identify the strategic business objectives of an organization would generate a list of SBOs. Generation tools within GSS support the generation of ideas, topics, alternatives, or objectives. They allow groups to generate items in a structured or unstructured manner, and are based on techniques used successfully in traditional meetings. If a group wishes to invite as many ideas as possible on a given topic, techniques such as brainstorming can be employed. Brainstorming suggests four rules to maximize idea generation [12, p. 156]:

(1) Criticism is ruled out - adverse judgment of ideas must be withheld until later;

(2) "Free-wheeling" is welcomed – the wilder the idea, the better; it is easier to tame down than to think up;

(3) Quantity is wanted - the greater the number of ideas, the greater the likelihood of useful ideas; and

(4) Combination and improvement are sought - beyond contributing ideas of their own, participants should suggest how ideas of others can be turned into better ideas, or how two or more ideas can be joined into still another idea.

Brainstorming procedures sacrifice structure in order to maximize the total number of ideas generated. For example, brainstorming on goals of the organization might yield such comments as:

\- Gain a 25% market share in the US and 10% globally,

\- Decrease manufacturing defects per 1000 to less than 2,

\- Reduce operating costs to achieve a 10% profit margin, and

\- Increase sales by 20% over the next fiscal year.

By generating and sharing these ideas electronically, a group can produce more ideas in a shorter amount of time.

Since this information is stored in electronic form, it can easily be edited, printed, or stored for future uses. Public screens can be used to display the information to everyone in the room as soon as it is collected. It is also possible that information located in electronic form elsewhere can be imported into the record of the current meeting to avoid unnecessary duplication of information. Group members may generate ideas at their convenience, store them, and copy them into the system during the meeting.

Once information is generated or collected, it is often organized in some way. Organizing tools let a group add structure to items generated or collected. For example, groups can combine similar ideas into representative concepts. Rather than consider several ideas separately, a group can use GSS to expedite the combination of the ideas into a more manageable list of major themes. Consider an executive group wishing to determine immediate threats to their organization's success. Several threats may be identified, each possibly falling into one of the following categories:

\- Price competition from existing competitors,

· Market contraction due to worsening economic conditions,

\- Increased costs for raw materials, and

\- Smaller margins due to increased government involvement.

A GSS allows the group to view all ideas generated and easily combine related ideas into categories. The group then references the ideas by category while retaining the ability to view specific ideas grouped into each category. For example, ten suppliers who are planning to increase their prices may be identified. A purchasing agent would be concerned with each contact with suppliers, but an executive group would likely view this as a single threat to organizational profit margins under a category name such as increased costs for raw materials. A GSS allows easy access and reference to this information at either level.

Limited resources require evaluation of alternatives and selection of one or a few. Evaluation tools let groups evaluate alternatives as individual members and then combine evaluations to calculate a group opinion. These tools use ranking (e.g., from best to worst), rating (e.g., from 1 to 10), point allocation (e.g., assign 10 points to 3 alternatives), or choice (e.g., yes/no or alternative receiving most votes) methods to judge alternatives. In the case of SBOs, a group would rank the list of strategic objectives from most to least important. Due to the computers ability to quickly and accurately process numerical information, A GSS can save a group much time during evaluation steps.

Computer technology lets group members contribute ideas, comments, and evaluations anonymously and simultaneously. This is achieved by typing these contributions via computer keyboards, possibly a disadvantage for unskilled typists. GSS may eventually all voice input to remedy this situation. For now, skilled typists can be employed to enter the contributions of unskilled typists. The next section describes GSS support for the SBO method.

## 5. GSS support of the SBO method

The SBO method details a procedure to determine an organization's strategic business objectives and the information required to achieve them. However, communication barriers can frustrate even the best formulated plans. GSS software tools and procedures can help to remove many communication barriers experienced by groups. This section steps the reader through an example of the SBO method and discusses how GSS might be applied to support this method. The reader should keep in mind that the choice of GSS used and specific software tools within the chosen system are less important than the features and capabilities provided by these choices. Any number of substitute products or tools may be equally applicable.

Problem solving or decision-making situations can be partitioned into a list of general activities. Each step in the SBO method, for example, can be described in terms generating, organizing, or evaluating information. For example, the SBO method begins with identification of an organization's strategic business objectives. This information would be generated, or collected, by a group of executives and their assistants. For each SBO method step, one possible GSS approach is illustrated. A versatile GSS such as System V (Ventana Corporation, Tucson, Arizona) offers several choices in software tools and their utilization.

Executives and EIS developers must first be introduced to GSS technology and its use in meetings. While most find GSS easy to use $[14]$ , some instruction is necessary to understand how the system works and how it supports groups. Users should understand the role of facilitators, the different tools available to support group activities, and how information is entered, processed, and shared within the system. Once this understanding is gained, the executive group and EIS developers are ready to proceed.

Rockart and De Long [16], citing prior managerial theory, suggest that an EIS changes or enhances how an executive thinks about the business, i.e. it improves his mental model of the organization. As the process of determining executive information requirements for an EIS begins with identification of the strategic objectives of the organization, it is useful to consider this as a process of documenting an organization model. Since the model is dynamic, it is important that it be easily alterable. A GSS aids the capture and representation of this model, and allows for its evolution. This is illustrated through the remainder of this section.

Step 1: Identify Strategic Business Objectives. Executives and their assistants (henceforth referred to as the executive group) may be able to identify the strategic business objectives of an organization with little effort. A list of SBOs may be a byproduct of strategic planning. Mission statements and planning documents can also be used as sources for strategic objectives. On the other hand, some organizations may not employ methods to identify and maintain a list of strategic objectives as part of their strategic planning process. Still others may not employ strategic planning. In any case, the list of SBOs which will lead ultimately to development of an EIS must be found or generated. If there are no known lists of these objectives, the executive group must compile such a list.

When SBO lists do not exist, less structured GSS tools might be employed to initiate communication between the executive group and EIS developers. System V's Electronic Brainstorming tool allows entry of information with little structure. The executive group and EIS developers can discuss the business of the organization, EIS features desired, information needs, or tactics for procuring information. Questions can be asked and answered to break the ice between executives and EIS developers. All comments are recorded within the Electronic Brainstorming tool and can be reviewed by participants at any time. While EIS developers may be able to attend strategic planning meetings to get some of this information, this approach is useful in initiating a working relationship between the executive group and EIS developers.

Once the group has established rapport and preliminary data has been collected and shared, the executive group is ready to identify specific strategic business objectives for the organization. This is best achieved by using an organizing tool such as System V's Idea Organizer. Information entered during brainstorming is analyzed to determine idea content. Idea Organizer facilitates this by allowing information to be grouped into categories. For example, if many comments are entered with respect to increasing market share, the category Increase Market Share can be created as an SBO. This approach will yield an evolving text model of the organization's SBOs.

Organizations that already maintain an SBO list do not need to brainstorm to determine these objectives. However, it may be useful for executive groups and EIS developers in these organizations to copy the SBOs into Idea Organizer and to discuss each of them. The SBOs provide a starting point for determining EIS information requirements, but it is essential that the executive group and EIS developers discuss and thoroughly understand these objectives.

When the group agrees that all strategic objectives are identified, they can move on to the next step of the SBO method. It should be noted, however, that new SBOs can be added as identified due to the dynamic nature of the model that is being built with the GSS. The text model can be updated to reflect any changes. While this may be difficult to manage in a manual environment, GSS provide group communication and text processing support to make this approach easier. These features in combination allow GSS to provide a structured approach to collecting, generating, and sharing information.

The facilitator must help the group to reach closure in this step. If there is general agreement on which objectives are strategic, this will be an easy task. When considerable disagreement exists, the group must resolve these matters before moving on to the second step of the SBO method. Voting procedures may be required in some cases. System V's evaluation tools can be employed to obtain a group position on the importance of any objective.

Step 2: Identify Critical Business Processes. Once an agreed upon SBO list is obtained, business processes related to each SBO must be identified. These processes may be difficult to identify and cut across multiple functional areas [24]. There may also be significant disagreement on what the key related business processes are for any given SBO. One task for the facilitator in this step is to bring the group to agreement on which processes are related to which SBOs.

Identifying critical business processes for each objective is an organizing task. System V's Topic Commenter tool can be used for this step. Topic Commenter allows topic windows for text entry to be created for each SBO. For example, for the SBO Increase Market Share, the executive group would create a list of critical business processes related to increasing market share. An open topic window allows the user to enter comments on each business process viewed by this person as critical to increasing market share.

Each meeting participant can enter any number of business processes into each topic window. Using this approach, there is likely to be quite a bit of redundancy in identifying business processes. For example, many group members might identify the advertising, marketing, and production business processes as being related to the Increase Market Share SBO. One useful feature of Topic Commenter is that it allows a user to view all business processes entered previously for each SBO. Executives can review the business processes already identified and add additional ones that they feel are related to the SBO. This helps reduce the redundancy that would occur otherwise.

Allowing each group member to provide their own list of critical business processes is also likely to result in some disagreement over which processes to include. It is important to list all business processes critical to the achievement of each SBO, but the determination of criticality is a subjective one. After ample time is given to identify all of the business processes for a given SBO, the facilitator guides the group through an exercise of considering each identified business process for inclusion in the final list. For example, if only one group member views the inventory management business process as related to the Increase Market Share SBO, the group is likely to decide that this process should be removed from the list. As noted in Step 1, the group must resolve any disagreement before it can continue to the next step. In most instances, a good GSS facilitator can help a group reach a reasonable solution. When disagreements persist, evaluation tools can be employed to quickly and anonymously obtain the group's position on any point of contention. The group should always employ a decision rule acceptable to all members in these situations.

The identified business processes represent an elaboration of the model begun in Step 1. The executive group has now identified the strategic business objectives of the organization and those business processes critical to achieving each of these objectives. The approach used to obtain this information emphasizes the sharing of information with EIS developers. Documenting SBOs and their related business processes as a group using a GSS allows for better information sharing and increased understanding between the executive group and EIS developers.

Step 3: Prioritize Strategic Business Objectives. The SBO list is prioritized to allow the development team to concentrate on those objectives that are deemed by the executive group to be the most important to the organization. Prioritization of the SBO list can be achieved with GSS evaluation tools. These solicit individual evaluations from each group member which are then combined to determine the group position. The average of the individual rankings determines the group ranking.

System V's Voting tool can be used to rank SBOs on the criterion of strategic importance to the organization. Most organizations would have many SBOs. For example, the Increase Market Share SBO and others could be ranked by group members. A useful feature of the Voting tool is that it allows all related business processes to be viewed for each SBO. If a group member wishes to review a given SBO's identified business processes before ranking its strategic importance to the organization, they may do so. Once comfortable with their understanding of the SBOs and the rank that they have assigned to it, group members cast their votes.

The group must decide if the voting procedure and results are satisfactory. The facilitator can guide the group in the use of the Voting tool, but only the group can accept the results of the rank vote. The SBO method calls for a simple prioritization of the SBOs, but a group could conceivably opt for alternative approaches. GSS evaluation tools also expedite rating and classification tasks. For example, groups might rate the SBOs from very important (e.g., a rating of 10) to not very important (e.g., a rating of 1) or classify each as very important, important, or less important.

If more elaborate prioritization methods are desired, System V's Alternative Evaluator tool might be used. It allows a group to specify multiple criteria on which to rate the SBOs. Different weights can be assigned to each criterion, resulting in a weighted average rating for each SBO. The SBO method calls for a simpler prioritization of objectives, however, GSS can greatly expand the SBO method by supporting alternate prioritization approaches.

It must be emphasized that the roles of the facilitator and the GSS are to expedite the prioritizing process, not to select a procedure for prioritizing the objectives. The executive group always retains the responsibility for prioritizing the list of SBOs.

Step 4: Identify Information Needed to Support Critical Business Processes The information needs of each critical business process must next be identified. As discussed earlier, many methods can be used to identify the information critical to these business processes [25]. GSS organizing tools can be helpful for identifying information requirements that support the business processes. In Topic Commenter, each critical business process would serve as a category under which its information requirements would be listed. For example, the executive group responsible for the marketing business process might get together and utilize Topic Commenter to identify EIS information requirements specific to this critical business process. Information identified in this step should result from ongoing business activities. For example, marketing business process information requirements might include such data as sales projections, customer demographics, seasonal trends, and economic forecasts and advertising information requirements might include budgets, promotions, media cost structures, and average industry costs.

Topic Commenter allows each group member to enter information requirements for each business process. It also allows group members to view the contributions of others to avoid redundancy. As was the case for identifying business processes critical to the achievement of the SBOs, the facilitator will help the group agree on which information requirements should be listed under each business process. The facilitator guides the group through an exercise of considering each identified information requirement for inclusion in the final list.

This step is valuable in illustrating the virtues of GSS support. To do a commendable job of identifying information requirements, the executive group and EIS developers would likely address each business process independently. They might break from the larger group and meet with individuals involved with each of the business processes. These individuals could add insight on the information requirements for these processes. Once executive group members and EIS developers have gathered the information requirements for each business process, the group can reconvene and compare notes. As a central repository for documenting these information requirements, the GSS stores this information and allows the group to update it going forward.

Step 5: Identify Information Linkages Among Critical Business Processes. Information requirements generated for each business process are compared across business processes for similar entries. These shared information needs are known as information linkages, and are critical for EIS design using the SBO method. This sharing of information between business process is inherent in any business. For example, advertising promotions, which are a part of the advertising business process, are likely to utilize customer demographic information, which is a part of the marketing business process.

Houdeshel and Watson [8] have addressed the need for standard terminology in executive information systems. Use of standard terminology in the preceding step of the SBO method may allow for easier recognition of information linkages in this step. For example, forecasted annual sales may be required information for several business processes, but may be referenced by a different name in each process. The use of a GSS as a central repository for a model of the organization's SBOs allows executive group members and EIS developers to share a convention for naming data items.

Idea Organizer can be used to help identify information linkages across business processes. Each business process and its related information requirements would be loaded into Idea Organizer and also printed should the group find it easier to compare the information using a hard copy. Since this information was generated and stored within the GSS, this is simply a matter of displaying the information in a useful format for the group's review. Group members would then compare all pairs of business processes to identify possible linkages. The identified linkages would then be entered as categories within Idea Organizer. Supporting comments could be added to explain the identified linkage.

The documentation of the information linkages is a useful outcome of this approach. It helps EIS developers in the construction of the system, allowing them to tie together related data items. As with information generated during earlier steps of the SBO method, information linkages can be altered in the GSS as events change or additional linkages develop. Once all information linkages have been identified, EIS developers can then create an EIS that accurately addresses the executives' information requirements.

Step 6: Plan for EIS Development, Implementation, and Evolution. A development plan must be drawn up to determine which EIS modules will be developed first. This will be driven by the SBO ranking process that took place earlier. The GSS would be used here to ensure that the executive group has a common understanding of the plan.

Once a development plan is approved by executives, work on the EIS can begin. The successful development and implementation of the EIS relies on complete and accurate information being collected in the preceding steps of the SBO method. Effective communication is necessary to share understanding and evolve the GSS model of the organization's strategic business objectives. As this model is electronically stored by the GSS, it can be updated as needed.

One of the responsibilities of EIS developers is to insure that the system under development addresses the most current representation of executives' information requirements. The continued use of GSS during this step keeps the lines of communication open and allows executive group members and EIS developers to compare the delivered system to the modeled one. The executive group can use the GSS to comment on the various iterations of the EIS. This approach adds to the previous documentation for the desired system and allows easy access to specific requirements modeled in the GSS but not yet incorporated in the EIS. The GSS thus continues to be the central repository for all information relevant to the delivery of the EIS. The model for the system evolves within the GSS and EIS developers can continuously refer to the latest representation of this model to guide their development efforts.

## 6. Advantages and disadvantages of using a GSS to support the SBO approach

Several advantages accrue to EIS developers and the executive group from this approach. Some of these are:

(1) EIS developers will have a more thorough understanding of executives' information requirements;

(2) executives will better understand the interdependencies between their and other executives' information requirements;

(3) soft information is more readily identified by this approach; and

(4) executives utilize a tool more appropriate for identifying, maintaining, and evolving their information requirements.

Better communication and increased information sharing characterize these benefits. All individuals associated with EIS development are able to access the body of information which is used to develop the system. A thorough process of identifying and recording the information requirements leads to better understanding between the executive group and EIS developers. The flexibility of electronic information storage allows for easier modification when conditions change. If a model of the organization's SBOs is properly created and maintained as suggested, this approach should yield a data bank which identify the strategic information requirements of the organization.

Many approaches could be used to yield similar benefits. However, specific advantages of GSS merit its consideration for this application. Its flexibility allows pre-existing information to be imported and new information to be generated. Both types of information are likely to be important to EIS development. GSS text processing capabilities also allow information to be organized in meaningful ways. While many text processors also provide these capabilities, GSS couple the ability to allow several individuals to work on the resulting document in an organized manner. It is this capability as a group authoring tool that will allow executives and EIS developers to accumulate, share, and use information needed for the successful determination of EIS information requirements.

This approach, however, is not without shortcomings. For example, GSS are often bulky and expensive. Many require dedicated rooms with networked workstations, a public video screen that can project images from a facilitator workstation, and sufficient expertise to run a GSS session. GSS popularity has risen recently due to the recognition that many organizational meetings are not productive [9]. However, GSS do not automatically allow groups which lack good meeting skills to immediately become productive. In addition to learning how to use a GSS, groups may need to learn how best to generate ideas or make decisions as a group. A GSS provides tools to help increase a group's productivity, but it is no substitute for good group skills and practices. Trained facilitators are thus a valuable asset for most groups utilizing GSS technology.

An organization would thus have to “buy in” to GSS support before applying it in this context. Due to its flexibility and support for a variety of group activities, GSS are increasing in popularity $[11]$ . However, the presence of GSS in organizations is far from universal. Recognition by executives of its potential in important activities such as EIS information requirements determination may hasten its arrival in some organizations.

## 7. Conclusion

The system development process for EIS is iterative in nature. One or a few parts of the system are designed, developed, and delivered rather than the entire system at once. The information requirements determination process is also iterative. EIS developers must build and maintain a model of executives' information requirements. The SBO approach, augmented by GSS technology, is suggested here as an effective way to achieve this goal.

Executive information requirements are dynamic in many, perhaps most, organizations. Changes in business, in industry, or in the organization often require a change in the information executives need to make critical decisions for the organization. Use of GSS technology to capture and store a model of these information requirements allows for its easy evolution. Executives and EIS developers can revisit any step in the SBO method to reflect changes in the business, industrial, or organizational environment and evolve this model.

Successful EIS information requirements determination requires a thorough examination of the organization and input from executives that will use the system. The daunting task of coordinating the efforts of the executive group and EIS developers can benefit from well chosen tools and procedures. A GSS provides tools to support the generation, organization, and evaluation of information. Each step of the SBO method involves one or more of these activities. Groups performing these activities must not only communicate but also share understanding. GSS provide a channel which promotes communication between executives and EIS developers. While alternative channels might also be used, using GSS allows a data repository to be built to store all information related to the executives' EIS information requirements. This also increases the likelihood of shared understanding between group members. GSS provides a flexible communication mechanism for supporting the identification of EIS information requirements thereby enhancing the SBO method.
