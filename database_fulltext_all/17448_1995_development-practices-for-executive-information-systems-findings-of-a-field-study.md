# Development practices for executive information systems: findings of a field study

Hugh J. Watson $^{a,*}$ , Richard T. Watson $^{a}$ , Sanjay Singh $^{b}$ , David Holmes $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602, USA

$^{b}$ College of Business, University of Alabama, Birmingham, Alabama 35294, USA

$^{c}$ Knowledge Ware, Inc., 3340 Peachtree Road, Suite 1100, Atlanta, Georgia 30326, USA

## Abstract

A field survey of 43 organizations was conducted to explore how the initial version of executive information systems (EISs) are developed. The data provide interesting insights into EIS development practices. The motivations for developing EISs tend to be internal in nature with providing easier, faster access to information being the most important. EISs are expensive to develop with an average cost of \$325,000 for hardware, software, development personnel, and training. Developing an EIS often requires enhancements to the computing infrastructure with improving existing databases requiring the most attention. While a variety of developmental methodologies are used, 80 percent of the firms employed an evolving approach rather than the traditional linear systems development life cycle. The sequencing of the phases in developing an EIS varies considerably among firms; however, the more successful development efforts include an initiation, definition of systems objectives, and feasibility analysis. Depending on the phase, the key decision maker may be the executive sponsor, executive users, or IS. The IS group is the primary provider of expertise and the driving force for all of the phases. Most EISs contain a variety of features, with drill-down and colour screens included in virtually all systems. The major problems in developing an EIS are getting accurate data and identifying executives' information requirements.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Systems development; Systems analysis and design; Systems development life cycle; Prototyping; Evolutionary design

## 1. Introduction

Many practitioners have written about their EIS experiences (Barrow, 1990; Guiden and Ewers, 1989; Runge, 1989). As a result, there are many anecdote-based recommendations about how to develop an EIS. However, the literature contains only a few academic studies of EIS development practices and these are broad in scope (Watson, Rainer, and Koh, 1991; Watson, Rainer, and Frolick, 1992). As a consequence, there are many aspects of developing an EIS that have not been researched in a rigorous, systematic manner.

In order to better understand how EISs are developed, the authors investigated the subject in depth, both in terms of the issues explored and the number of organizations studied. The study was a cooperative effort between researchers at the University of Georgia and consultants at Deloitte and Touche who funded the project and wanted the findings to support their EIS consulting practice. $^{1}$ The findings provide a comprehensive understanding of current EIS development practices and also suggest opportunities for further research.

## 2. Systems development

How to most effectively and efficiently develop applications has been a concern as long as computers have existed. For many years, the dominant approach has been the systems development life cycle (SDLC), which breaks development into identifiable stages; each stage represents a distinct activity characterized by a starting point, ending point, and specific deliverables in concert with an express purpose (McKeen, 1983). While the efficacy of the SDLC is widely accepted, there is no standard way to divide systems projects into phases (Necco, Gordon, and Tsai, 1987). Different authors, organizations, consultants, and vendors have advocated specific breakdowns that they believe to be most appropriate.

Over the years, alternatives to the SDLC such as prototyping (Naumann and Jenkins, 1982), iterative design (Sprague, 1980), and heuristic development (Berresford and Wetherbe, 1979) were advocated and practised. Their similarity lies in recognizing the difficulty that users often have in specifying their information requirements and that systems analysts have in understanding the user's environment. In response, the stages in the SDLC are collapsed, typically made possible through the use of fourth generation software, in order to deliver an intentionally incomplete version of the application quickly. Subsequent versions are developed based on users' reactions to previous versions.

In addition to using an appropriate development methodology, other factors have been found to be important to systems development. For example, there must be an intrinsic need for the system (Ginzberg, 1978). A champion is necessary to sponsor it, allocate necessary resources, monitor its development, and overcome resistance (Beath and Ives, 1988). There must be a high level of user involvement (Ives and Olson, 1984).

These and other issues must be addressed with EIS development, as with any other kind of application. What makes EIS development particularly interesting and challenging, however, is the unique combination of considerations that affect the effort. Three factors are particularly relevant. A firm's senior executives are seldom hands-on computer users, probably are of an age to have missed the computer revolution, and have been successful without the use of computers and may question the need for them now. Executives perform highly unstructured work that is difficult for them to describe with sufficient precision to identify information requirements. An EIS is typically a new type of application for systems analysts and often requires learning and using new technology and understanding the nature of managerial work.

The development of an EIS usually begins with an executive sponsor who champions the system's development (Houdeshel and Watson, 1987; Rockart and DeLong, 1988). The executive sponsor's interest in the EIS typically is the result of external (e.g., increased competition) and internal (e.g., need for more timely information) pressures. An operating sponsor is assigned the task of overseeing the day-to-day development of the system (Rockart and DeLong, 1988). Typically, this person is an information systems manager (Watson, Rainer and Koh, 1991). The contents of the EIS should be driven by specific business objectives (Guiden and Ewers, 1989; Rockart and DeLong, 1988; Volonino and Watson, 1990). To build the system, the EIS development team works with executives, executive staff personnel, functional area personnel, information systems personnel, vendors, and – when appropriate – with consultants. The recent availability of commercial EIS software has facilitated EIS development in many firms (Main, 1989; Watson, Rainer, and Koh, 1991). Using a prototyping methodology, the initial version of the EIS should be developed quickly in order to get executives' reactions and to maintain their interest (Barrow, 1990; Runge, 1989). This version provides specific information and features to a targeted group of executives.

As the EIS spreads and evolves over time, additional users are given access to the system, more modules of information are added, and additional capabilities are put in place. In doing so, the system moves beyond the initial version and enters on-going support.

## 3. The study

The study explored the following research question: How is the initial version of an EIS developed? Specific issues studied include:

\- What are the motivations for developing an EIS?

\- What hardware, software, developmental personnel, and training costs were incurred in developing the EIS?

\- What features are included in an EIS?

\- What changes to the computing infrastructure are required by the development of an EIS?

\- What developmental methodologies are used and how successful are they?

\- What phases (e.g., steps) are followed?

\- Who participates in the development, at what points, and in what roles?

\- What problems need to be resolved when building an EIS?

Based on our EIS experiences, we have found these to be interesting and important questions for which answers based on many organizations experiences were not available.

A field survey questionnaire was developed to gather data about the respondent's organization, the organization's EIS development experiences, and the respondent's personal background. An EIS was characterized as a system that (1) typically is used directly by top managers without the assistance of intermediaries; (2) provides easy on-line access to current information about the plans of the organization; (3) is designed with management's critical success factors in mind; and (4) uses state-of-the-art graphics, communications, and data storage and retrieval methods. The questionnaire was pre-tested on EIS consultants at Deloitte and Touche and three EIS practitioners from the EIS survey population. The pre-test resulted in several minor wording changes but the instrument's content was judged to be valid.

Questionnaires were sent to 250 firms in the University of Georgia's EIS database, which consists of firms known or likely to have an EIS. Created from a variety of sources, including magazines (e.g., Computerworld, MIS Week), journals (e.g., Harvard Business Review), newsletters (e.g., EIS Conference Report), conference registration lists (e.g., The EIS Institute, DSS-XX), vendor, and personal contacts, the database is believed to provide a representative sample of firms with an EIS.

The first mailing resulted in the return of 47 questionnaires – 28 completed, nine from firms without an EIS, and 10 that were undeliverable. In order to obtain a higher response rate and to check on non-respondents, a second mailing was made a month later. This prompted the return of 15 additional completed questionnaires and eight more from firms without an EIS. In total, responses were received from 60 organizations, 43 of whom have an EIS. This response rate (25 percent of those delivered responded, 17 percent had an EIS) was judged to be acceptable in light of the detailed questionnaire which required 30-45 minutes to complete. The data from the second mailing were compared to those from the first and were not found to differ significantly. Using the second mailing returns as surrogates for non-respondents, it is believed that the study's findings are applicable to the population surveyed (Babbie, 1973).

## 4. Findings and discussion

## 4.1. Demographics

The surveyed firms are geographically dispersed and are located in 23 states and Canada.

New York (10 percent), Illinois (8.3 percent), and Canada (8.3 percent) provided the largest number of respondents. They come from a variety of industries with manufacturing (25 percent), insurance (15 percent), banking/financial services (8.3 percent), and government (8.3 percent) leading the way. Most of the firms are large, averaging \$895 million in assets and \$655 million in gross revenue. Seventy-eight percent of the firms have assets over \$1 billion and 64.4 percent have gross revenues over \$1 billion. Only 5 percent of the respondents have less than \$1 million in assets and gross revenues. These numbers confirm findings from previous studies that EISs are developed primarily by larger organizations (Watson, Rainer, and Koh, 1991; Watson, Rainer, and Frolick, 1992).

The respondents included information systems (IS) managers (41.9 percent), EIS support staff personnel (27.9 percent), IS staff members (11.6 percent), and executives (7.0 percent). Thus, the results reported are primarily the perception of IS personnel, the dominant category of respondents. Respondents average 18.0 years of work experience, 11.9 years of IS experience, and 2.6 years of EIS experience. Ninety-three percent of them participated in the initial development of their EIS and 97.6 percent currently are involved in its operation.

The EISs included in the survey range in age from 1.5 months to 12 years. Their average age of 2.2 years supports the notion that many firms have developed their EIS recently (Watson, Rainer, and Frolick, 1992).

## 4.2. Motivations for developing the EISs

EISs are developed for a variety of reasons. Respondents were given a list of nine motivating factors often mentioned in the EIS literature and asked to indicate on an anchored five-point scale (1 = unimportant, 5 = very important) the importance of each factor upon their decision to develop an EIS. Table 1 presents the factors rank ordered, with their means and standard deviations.

By far, the leading factor was to “provide easier, faster access to information.” It is interesting to note that all of the top five motivations are internal in nature. Factors that relate to the external environment – “competitive information” and “monitor the external environment” – are ranked sixth and eighth in importance, respectively. This finding is somewhat surprising in light of the frequent references to EISs being used to provide external information and to monitor the turbulent state of the external environment. Several possible explanations come to mind. Based on previous experience, executives may not think of formal information systems as a source of external information and thus may not expect it of them. Also, research suggests that senior executives choose to do much of their own environmental scanning because they feel that subtleties exist that only they will see (El Sawy, 1985). And finally, it is usually easier to provide internal than external data because it is more readily available. Many firms include electronic versions of popular paper-based reports in the initial version of their EISs.

## 4.3. Resource requirements

An EIS requires the commitment of hardware, software, and human resources. It is normally developed by a team of people with technical and business skills who often come from IS and the functional areas. Typical tasks include selecting hardware and software, identifying information requirements, locating and accessing needed information, designing screens, and providing training. The average size of the development team was found to be 4 persons.

EISs tend to be expensive to build. In addition to personnel costs, it is likely that additional hardware and software will be needed. Sixty-three percent of the firms had to acquire additional hardware and 81 percent additional software. The average cost of developing an EIS was \$325,000. This figure represents the incremental personnel, hardware, software, and training costs that the respondents associated with creating an EIS.

The availability and use of commercial EIS software certainly has facilitated the development of EISs. It often supports screen design and maintenance, access to multiple data sources, electronic communications, and access to external news sources. Despite these capabilities, 19 percent of the firms developed all of their EIS software in-house. This choice was due to perceived cost savings and in a few cases to the firms' EISs pre-dating the availability of commercial software. Sixty-three percent of the firms relied exclusively on vendor supplied software while 19 percent of the organizations used a mixture of custom-built and vendor software. The two most popular vendor products were Comshare's Commander EIS (37 percent of all EISs) and Pilot's Command Centre (10 percent).

## 4.4. EIS features

Survey respondents were given a list of 20 possible features and asked to indicate which ones were included in their EIS. Table 2 presents a rank ordered listing of the features based on their popularity. Heading the list, and common to virtually all systems, are drill-down and colour screens. Slightly over 50 percent of the EISs include integrated decision support capabilities and electronic mail. This percentage is somewhat low, given how often they are mentioned in conjunction with EISs and their potential usefulness. It is also interesting that few systems include office support tools such as word processing and electronic rolodexes. Though more is being heard about the potential for voice input and output, it appears that very few EISs currently have this capability.

## 4.5. Enhancements to the computing infrastructure

Developing an EIS may require an organization to enhance its overall computing environment. For example, a firm may have to upgrade its system software in order to use the EIS software it acquires. Or a local area network may have to be installed in order to accommodate the executives who will use the system.

Given seven statements about where changes may be needed before an EIS can be developed successfully, respondents were asked to indicate on a five-point anchored scale (1 = no effort, 5 = significant effort) how much effort was required in each area. More effort is required to improve existing databases than anything else. Many EIS developers find more data problems than they anticipated. It has been noted that an EIS can have a therapeutic effect on data management, because building an EIS highlights many data problems (e.g., missing data, lack of data integrity, inconsistent data definitions) and can lead to their resolution (Rockart and DeLong, 1988).

An EIS also is likely to require enhancements to organizational personnel, lead to the upgrading of software, and require the acquisition of new hardware. To a lesser extent, there may be a need to upgrade communication capabilities and change organizational structure and reporting relationships.

## 4.6. Developmental methodology

Respondents were asked to position the developmental methodology employed on an anchored five-point scale where 1 was a linear approach (i.e., a sequential phased approach similar to the traditional SDLC) and 5 was an evolving approach (i.e., a continuing cycle of developing and delivering a growing EIS). The number of responses to each point on the scale is shown in Table 4. While a number of methodologies were used, over 80 percent of the respondents marked a 4 or 5, which indicated an evolving approach.

## 4.7. Sequencing of EIS development

Nearly every systems analysis and design textbook prescribes a sequence of phases that should be executed for successful systems development. While texts generally acknowledge that different breakdowns of the phases are possible, that phases can overlap, and that there may be an iterative recycling through some phases of the systems development life cycle, there is general agreement on the sequence of systems development activities (Davis and Olson, 1985). In this case, however, there is no generally accepted EIS development life cycle. Therefore, one of the concerns of this study was to identify the sequence of activities practised by EIS developers. The key questions were: What sequence of activities do EIS developers follow? Is there a generally accepted sequence? Who are the key players in each phase?

An inspection of Table 7 shows, however, that most firms did not follow the same sequence of activities. In only one case, the transition between prototyping and project review, does the maximum cell value (shown with an asterisk) for a particular row exceed 50 percent of the row total. Following the definition of systems objective, practitioners show the least agreement about what to do next. Some 29 percent proceed to the feasibility analysis, 25 percent go on to development planning, and the remainder is divided about equally between four other phases. Clearly, organizations are following a variety of paths to their creation of an EIS.

This variety of paths could be due to two reasons. First, there may be confusion in the EIS development community about what is the proper life cycle model for EIS development. As a result, developers are experimenting with a variety of approaches. Second, there may not be a single correct path and a number of approaches may lead to a successful EIS. One way to check this second proposition is to compare the approaches taken by the more successful and less successful EIS developers.

## 4.9. Development problems

Developing and maintaining an EIS is not a trouble-free endeavour. Respondents were asked to indicate on an anchored five-point scale (1 = no problem, 5 = significant problem) how much difficulty they had with a variety of problems and whether each is a continuing problem, even though it involves on-going support rather than the initial version. The list of problems was based on the literature (e.g., Stecklow, 1989), conversations with EIS developers, and the authors' EIS development experiences. Table 13 presents the ranked ordered problems with the mean and standard deviation for each problem, and the percentage of firms that indicated that it is an on-going problem.

The top ranked problem is getting accurate data. Another data-related problem, combining data from multiple sources, is ranked fourth. Both point to the difficulties of accessing the data needed for an EIS.

The second most common problem is keeping abreast of executives' changing information requirements. It is also the most important continuing problem.

Determining the information requirements for the initial version of the system is ranked tenth.

It is interesting that surviving executive sponsor turnover is the lowest ranked problem. Two explanations are possible: because most firms have yet to experience executive turnover, it is not currently perceived to be a problem; or, more optimistically, the EIS may enjoy such a broad base of support that executive turnover does not pose a problem because there actually are many sponsors. It should be pointed out that executive turnover is a major problem with EISs that fail (Watson and Glover, 1989).

## 5. Conclusion

EISs are primarily developed to provide easy and rapid access to internal information. This conclusion is supported by the features included in most EISs. For instance, the prominence of drill-down suggests that executives see an EIS as a means of monitoring organizational performance and getting to the heart of identified problems. Other prominent EIS features such as colour, multiple output formats (e.g., graphs, tables, and text), and multiple user interfaces support improved executive efficiency by accelerating executive processing of information on screens. EIS features that provide integration of data from existing databases and multiple sources support the major motivations for EIS development. Not surprisingly, there is a strong link between the major motivations for EIS development and the features provided.

Respondents report that the development of an EIS, above all else, forces organizations to improve existing databases. This finding indicates that, despite a decade of relational database technology, data management is still a major issue for IS departments. The data needs of executives may force IS to pay more attention to this issue. The need to enhance personnel and acquire hardware and software are predictable responses to EIS development because they are typical of any new IS project.

Most EISs are created using a continuing cycle of development and delivery of an expanding system. This approach is possibly due to three factors. First, the difficulty of determining executives' information needs implies that prototyping is the appropriate development strategy. Second, the emergence of EIS software and other technology that support rapid application development. Third, the turbulent nature of current environments that caused ever-changing information needs. If anything is surprising about these findings, it is that any developers followed a linear development strategy.

Nearly all EIS development is evolutionary and includes several activities that are completed prior to prototyping (e.g., definition of systems objectives). But there is no agreement on the sequencing of these precursor activities. More successful projects may follow an initial sequence of initiation → define systems objectives → feasibility analysis. $^{2}$ This sequence is part of the traditional project management cycle. The lack of any commonly accepted EIS development sequence may reflect the system's newness and a paucity of accumulated, available expertise to guide EIS development.

The IS group, as one would expect, plays a very prominent role in EIS development. It provides the expertise for all phases and is the decision maker and driving force for feasibility analysis, systems objectives definition, and development planning. Executives and executive sponsors are the major driving forces and decision makers for the other phases. Perhaps the most surprising finding is that definition of systems objectives is dominated by IS personnel; we surmised that this would be the province of executives. This may explain why defining systems objectives is ranked third as a EIS development problem – executives are simply leaving rather than taking a leading role in deciding what their system should do. Further research is needed to study the abdication of these responsibilities by executives.

Other major development problems mirror the previously identified issues of data management and determining executives' information needs. Interestingly, data management becomes less important after the initial EIS development, but determining executives' information needs remains a continuing problem. These findings suggest that the data management problem gets resolved but determining executives' information needs is a persistent problem.

This study was more comprehensive and detailed than any conducted previously. It is interesting to note, however, that the broad findings are consistent with other studies in both the U.S. and the U.K. (Watson, Rainer, and Koh, 1992; Fitzgerald, 1992). The similarities include the importance of the role of the executive sponsor in initiating and driving the project, the use of an evolutionary development methodology, the size of the EIS development team, and the cost of the system. An interesting difference in EIS development practices in the U.S. and the U.K. is the much greater role played by IS in the U.S.

This extensive study of EIS development practice highlights some key areas for future research. First, the current emphasis on internal data and monitoring organizational performance is different from the external, environmental focus that some people suggest for EIS. Monitoring performance could be an easy starting point for EIS development, or it could be a prime need of executives. This issue could be addressed by future research, which could track EIS development to ascertain whether the focus of EISs shifts over time. Second, the data management problem appears to inhibit EIS development, and this suggests an investigation of the extent to which data management shortcomings influence the realization of the EIS concept. The data management problem can be addressed from several directions: Why is it a problem? Is it a transient or persistent problem? How do organizations handle data management? Third, attention should be paid to the on-going problem of determining executives' information needs. More comprehension studies of managerial work may be required to provide the fundamental understanding to make substantial progress on this issue. As Rockart and DeLong (1988, p. 41) conclude: "What top managers actually do remains somewhat of a mystery." Fourth, further research is needed to document successful EIS development paths. Clearly, there is limited agreement on the sequence of activities for EIS development. The concept of equifinality suggests that there are often multiple paths to developing a successful system. Case studies of highly successful EIS projects may indicate whether there is one right approach or multiple paths to success are possible. Alternatively, there may be key factors that determine success, and a contingency model may be required to guide developers. Fifth, the development experiences of successful EISs can be compared to unsuccessful ones in order to identify what works well and what does not. And finally, after a lag of several years, many other countries are experiencing significant EIS development. Studies of differences in countries' EIS development practices can be used to help assess the impact of culture. For example, theory (Hall and Hall, 1990) suggests that EIS will be most successful in low context cultures such as the U.S. and Germany where managers are more report than relationship oriented. As a result, they are often remote from their subordinates and need formal reporting mechanisms. Managers in high context cultures, such as France and Japan, may not need an EIS because they have an on-going relationship with their subordinates and gain a steady stream of informal data. These premised differences between low and high context cultures could be tested by comparing EIS development in appropriate national cultures. Clearly, a number of important and interesting research projects are identified by this study.
