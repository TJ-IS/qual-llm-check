# DSS theory: A model of constructs and relationships

Michael A. Eierman $^{a,*}$ , Fred Niederman $^{b}$ , Carl Adams $^{c}$

$^{a}$ IS / OM Dept. University of Wisconsin-Oshkosh Oshkosh, WI 54901, USA $^{b}$ Dept. of IQS University of Baltimore 1420 N. Charles St. Baltimore, MD 21201, USA $^{c}$ IDS Dept. University of Minnesota 271 19th Ave. S. Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

## Abstract

A theoretical framework for DSS research is developed in terms of the key elements of theory: domain boundaries, constructs, and relationships among constructs. Eight broad DSS constructs (environment, task, implementation strategy, DSS capability, DSS configuration, user, user behaviour, and performance) are identified. Based on a review of the literature, 17 relationships among these constructs are examined. The most frequently studied relationships in the model are the influences of: (1) DSS capabilities on user behaviour; (2) user behaviour on performance; and (3) DSS capabilities on performance. Studies regarding these relationships use a wide variety of measures and present generally divergent results. Although studied less frequently, fairly consistent relationships were observed, suggesting: (1) the strategy used for DSS implementation influences both user behaviour and DSS performance; and (2) environmental factors such as top management support and user training influence DSS performance. Conclusions include: (1) there has been some progress toward understanding DSS based on the accumulation of research to date; (2) past research has examined less than half of the possible relationships among constructs, creating opportunities for new insights; and (3) divergent results on key pairs of constructs may result from interaction with other constructs, necessitating the simultaneous examination of fuller DSS research models to understand the contingencies influencing these relationships. $^{2}$

Keywords: Decision support systems; Group decision support systems; Executive information systems

## 1. Introduction

Decision making is an important aspect of management activity [28]. Some theorists, including Simon [34], suggest that decision making is a principle function of organizations. High quality decisions are expected to lead to more productive actions, quicker problem solving, and better organizational performance. However, decision making within an organization is not always an easy task, particularly where the underlying problem is complex and ill-structured. Decision makers are limited in their cognitive abilities to process complex information [37]; they may succumb to a variety of biases [22]; and they may have a difficult time agreeing on a single solution that satis-

fies differing interests [46]. Adding further to the difficulty of decision making is the lack of certainty that a given decision will lead to the desired outcome.

As a result of the importance and difficulty of performing the task of decision making, opportunities presented by computer technology to develop support for decision makers have generated a great deal of interest. Computers for supporting decision-making, labelled decision support systems (DSS), are developed to: (1) facilitate the structuring of a decision so that analytical tools, possibly several in combination, can be used in generating solutions; (2) facilitate the use of the analytical tools that have been brought together through a structuring process [13]; and (3) facilitate the manipulation, retrieval, and display of data [3]. Issues pertaining to developing computer support for decision makers have generated a growing body of research, especially since 1975.

This body of research has been criticized by some for being a collection of atheoretical studies $[6]$ driven by a technology push rather than by a managerial pull $[1,33,36]$ . The danger in an atheoretical approach to a field of study is that, while interesting facts may be collected, no unifying themes or predictable patterns emerge $[24]$ . Theory describes the phenomenon of interest by identifying the “entities of interest” in the phenomenon, the states these entities can take, and the relationships among the entities $[12,23,44]$ . The development of theory is valuable for researchers because it can serve as a basis for accumulating and refining knowledge in the domain of interest. It can serve as a mechanism for relating diverse sets of facts to produce greater understanding $[45]$ . Theory also can be used to propose and test links among key factors indicating the direction and amount of impact they have on performance outcomes. This knowledge can provide practitioners with (1) guidelines for selecting among alternative strategies and actions for developing DSS, and (2) policies for DSS use and development.

This study promotes the development of a descriptive theory of DSS by analysing existing literature to develop a framework of key constructs and relationships. This organization will provide a basis for future studies of DSS to target particular phenomena in the DSS arena, broaden their consideration of constructs and variables, and aid in interpreting the results of studies and the implications for DSS theory.

The next section describes major concepts of theory (boundaries, constructs, and relationships). The third section describes the method used in this study to develop the presented framework. The fourth section defines each proposed construct and relates it to variables found in the literature. The fifth section completes the framework by examining 17 relationships among the proposed constructs. The sixth section discusses the concept of boundary or domain for DSS research. The last section discusses the conclusions and limitations.

## 2. Elements of theory

The concept of theory presented in this paper follows the notions of theory proposed by Dubin [12], Kaplan [23], and Weick [44]. According to these authors, a theory should include: (1) a boundary that describes the domain of interest; (2) key constructs within that domain; (3) the values those constructs can take on; and (4) the relationships among key constructs. Constructs in a theory are the units that represent the items of interest in a domain. These constructs can take on different states (for example, an atomic element may be a gas, liquid, or solid). The variables associated with constructs are used to operationally define the potential states that the construct may attain. The relationships among the constructs determine the sophistication of a theory [12]. Sophistication may range from very low where the presence of one construct at a positive or negative value suggests that another construct will be at a positive or negative value, to very high where the laws of interaction explicitly state the exact rate of change in the values of one construct with the change in the values of an associated construct [12].

The key elements of the model presented here are the constructs identified by examining past research. These constructs are the entities or phenomena that are important in the study of DSS. Part of the conceptualization of constructs is the ability to describe the states that a construct may take on. The literature discusses a large number of variables (independent and dependent) that are related to constructs. These variables, either singly or in aggregation, may be seen as a way of indicating the alternative states of the construct.

In identifying a set of constructs, there is a significant tradeoff to be resolved. On one hand, it is possible that relationships among aggregated variables may be complex, that some variables are difficult to measure, and that the various measures are difficult to combine into a single construct value. On the other hand, without aggregation, it is possible that a construct is important as an explanatory variable, but that individual measures do not reflect its overall significance. While we see the necessity for considerable thinking to develop the definition of each construct, at this stage, we are only able to present the variables identified in the literature that relate to each construct.

## 3. Method

The theoretical framework presented here was developed in five steps. First, a set of 15 articles exemplary in their use of theory were identified from an investigation of over 200 articles in MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Management Science, Decision Science, Decision Support Systems, and DATABASE between 1975 and 1990. Of the 200 articles, 109 were independently judged by at least two of the authors to be relevant to DSS. Articles that suggested, through either keywords or abstracts, that they were relevant to DSS were included. These articles, while focusing on DSS, included studies of group decision support systems (GDSS), executive information systems (EIS), and expert systems. From this sample, 15 articles were identified as having a relatively sophisticated theoretical basis and a goal of extending or testing DSS Theory. The sophistication of the theory used to develop hypotheses and the sophistication of the theory that the study was developing were measured based on the precision of the laws of interaction Dubin [12] (Appendix A). If both the theory base and the theory goal were relatively sophisticated, the paper was identified as exemplary.

Second, variables developed and used in each article were identified and organized into constructs. The 15 articles were reviewed independently by each of the three authors. The review process consisted of identifying referent disciplines, constructs developed or used in the paper, relationships among constructs, and, in the case of empirical research, the strength of the reported relationship. After independent review, the three authors met and discussed each article until they reached a consensus regarding the constructs and relationships as presented by the author(s) of each article. The 15 articles are listed in Appendix B.

Next, these constructs, and the relationships among them, were mapped into a single comprehensive model (see Fig. 1). Fourth, empirical results from the 15 articles were used as a basis for evaluating relationships. Finally, the authors scanned the original 200 articles for those that presented empirical data. More than 50 additional DSS-research articles were identified. The content of these additional articles was used (1) to note any constructs missing from the research framework (none were added), and (2) to extend the search for evidence of relationships among the identified constructs beyond the 15 high theory content articles.

These steps led to: (1) the identification of a set of constructs used in describing DSS theory and the dimension of these constructs; (2) an accumulation of evidence indicating the strength of relationships among tested constructs; (3) potentially important, but currently untested relationships; and (4) conclusions regarding key relationships.

## 4. Constructs

An analysis of the existing literature suggests the following eight constructs: environment, task, implementation strategy, DSS capabilities, DSS configuration, user, user behaviour, and performance. $^{3}$ In the following text, each construct is defined, its importance is explained, and the dimensions along which each may vary are expanded. In most cases, the dimensions for a particular construct are drawn from the variables studied in the research examined.

Environment refers to the organizational context or to major forces external to the DSS project. It is the setting in which the DSS is developed and used. The environment in which a DSS is to be developed and implemented has the potential to influence the attitudes and behaviours of both the users and the developers of the system. A theory of DSS would not be complete without considering the setting in which a DSS is developed, implemented, and used.

To identify the states that the environment construct may take, important dimensions of the environment must be identified. Variables that have been used in past research identify relevant dimensions of the environment construct. Variables identified in DSS literature include: (1) organizational culture or values [25,35]; (2) organizational direction or goals [25]; (3) stability/volatility [25,30], which can refer to either the market in which the firm operates or the organizational environment in which the DSS is developed; (4) resources/constraints [3,9], which can be quite diverse, including, for example, top management support [17] – a resource if it is available, a constraint if it is not; (5) organizational technology forming the information system infrastructure of the organization [3]; (6) organizational structure [30,43]; (7) top management support [35]; and (8) characteristics of the system sponsor [35].

Task is the set of functions that a working person, unit, or organization is expected to fulfil or accomplish. It is the job that is to be done using the decision support system. Some researchers consider task to be one of several dimensions of the decision-making environment. However, we feel the environment consists of attributes of the particular organization at a particular point in time, whereas task consists of characteristics of a particular problem or decision which may reappear, at least abstractly, in different organizations or in the same organization at different times. For example, the scheduling or routing problem of a particular airline at a particular time in all its detailed complexity can be viewed as an element of the environment. Alternatively, it can be viewed as an example of the class of scheduling or routing problems that share common attributes, whether being addressed by an airline, a railroad, or a trucking firm. Although it is possible that some problems can occur only in a particular environment (this might be a function of how much detail is included in the statement of the problem), by separating task from environment, investigation of classes of decision or problems can be viewed across environments.

Dimensions of the task construct are difficult to identify because across environments or among different decision makers, tasks may be defined or approached quite differently. In fact, firms may gain advantage by taking a new approach to what was viewed previously as a common problem. DSS researchers have identified the following dimensions along which the task may vary: (1) complexity [9,25]; (2) structuredness [9,25]; (3) level of decision based on Anthony's model [3,4]; (4) decision phase following Simon's model [3,25]; (5) the functional area in which the decision occurs [3]; and (6) the importance of the decision [25].

Implementation strategy is the method by which the decision support system comes into being and is integrated into the work environment. Alavi and Henderson [2], Sprague and Olson [35], and Houdeshel and Watson [20] emphasize the importance of the process of developing a system in determining both the acceptance (and resulting use) of the system, and its content and performance. Generally, this construct can take on two or three categorical values depending on the overall method selected. Although most often a formal system development life cycle is compared to a prototyping methodology, the construct could contrast ways of approaching any of the stages in the equivalent to a traditional life cycle, from feasibility studies and information requirements determination to training and maintenance. On one hand, the methods available for developing the new system may be constrained by environmental factors such as resource constraints, or by user constraints such as time limits or willingness to use training or documentation. On the other hand, actions taken toward developing and implementing the new system could logically affect the environment (e.g., generating or dissipating top management support) and/or end-user attitudes or abilities (e.g., through training or documentation).

DSS capabilities refer to the capacities of the system to support individual or group task performance. Capabilities are the functions of the DSS that support the information processing requirements of one or more tasks. Capabilities differ widely depending on the intended function of the DSS. For example, DSS capabilities range from the ability to electronically search a database for values that are greater than desired to the ability to apply specific decision models to a particular situation.

Variables that indicate the type of dimensions within this category include: (1) information characteristics such as accuracy [25] and (2) timeliness [17]; (3) representational format such as tables versus graphs [38]; (4) presence or absence of event-triggered processing [19]; (5) mode of interface [17]; (6) ability to interpret user values [8]; (7) level of support or number of functions available [3,4,10]; (8) decision range or number of different tasks for which the DSS is intended [3]; and (9) particular processes supported [3].

DSS configuration refers to the specific hardware and software that provide the capabilities of the DSS. DSS configuration refers to one of the possible ways that hardware and software can provide a specific capability. For example, a capability to calculate loan programs may be provided by two different configurations. One configuration may deliver the capability with a spreadsheet tool, while another configuration may deliver the same capability with an application written in a programming language. The focus of configuration is on alternative ways of making a function (capability) available to the user.

Dimensions in this construct are somewhat more difficult to specify. There are perhaps only two dimensions: hardware and software. The states that this construct may assume are specific arrangements and types of components of hardware and software. Examples of specific components include: (1) demons [19]; (2) belief modelling [8]; (3) knowledge base [4]; (4) information base [7]; (5) problem processor [7]; and (6) understanding processor [8]. The state of this construct may also differ, depending on the arrangement of these components [3] and the specific language in which the DSS is constructed [39].

User refers to the characteristics of any individual or group that uses the DSS or its results to perform some task. This construct does not include the behaviour of the individual or group while they are engaged in a task. Characteristics of the individual or group are considered aspects of the individual or group that may influence their behaviour.

This construct is very rich and has several aspects. It has dimensions that refer to both individuals and groups, only to individuals, and only to groups. First, variations among individuals and groups are examined by considering the following dimensions: (1) ability to develop formal processes [7]; (2) level of expertise with task [25,30]; (3) expertise with technology [9,25]; (4) motivation [25]; (5) attitude [25]; and (6) expectations [25].

Second, individual characteristics include perceptual style $[5,43]$ and logico-mathematical-structure (LMS) repertoire $[29]$ , also known as mental models and scripts. The latter characteristic resembles what Miller and Gordon $[27]$ term dispositional training, which is developed over long periods of time to allow problem solvers to apply more complex strategies toward solving specific problems.

Finally, attributes of groups relevant to their decision making activities include: formality, size, strength or clout within the organization, and proximity [30].

Although we grouped these together as attributes of the user, an argument can be made for viewing attitudes and abilities as different constructs. The study of the effect of differences in abilities and attitudes on behaviour in problem solving spawned rich literature that impacts DSS design, but that may extend beyond the bounds of DSS research. As early as 1975, Miller and Gordon [27] referenced several studies in this area. Attitudes may also be used as outcome measures. Typically, outcome measures of attitude focus on satisfaction or on confidence with solution rather than on attitudes such as feelings about technology.

User behaviours are actions taken by the individual or group to complete a task, either with or without a decision support system. User behaviours are important because often there is more than one way to complete any particular task, and the way individuals approach a task may vary independently of their individual characteristics. Separating user behaviour into a distinct construct aids in developing an understanding of how different methods or manners of task completion may facilitate or inhibit performance. User behaviour also is important as a distinct construct because it may act as an intervening variable and a critical link between DSS capabilities and performance. Everything else being equal, a well-designed, efficient system should outperform a poorly-designed and inefficient one; however, if the former is never used, the latter, even if not well conceived, may still generate more value and higher performance. Thus we would expect user behaviour to moderate relationships between capability and performance.

Attributes of user behaviour pertaining to both the individual and the group used in past research include: (1) conceptual level, referring to the number of dimensions taken into account and the nature of the rules used to combine these dimensions to arrive at a final decision $[27]$ ; (2) information interpretation $[9]$ ; (3) task comprehension $[9]$ ; (4) cognitive aspects $[9]$ , including the choice, development, and application of LMS $[29]$ ; (5) information used $[32]$ , including its amount, frequency, and type (age, format); (6) information processing $[19]$ , including both amount and type of processing or strategy used; and (7) DSS use $[25]$ , including both amount and type. Group characteristics include distributing and using power or influence, levels of participation, levels of group cohesion, and information exchange patterns $[10]$ .

DSS use is considered a user behaviour, rather than a separate construct. Using the DSS is viewed as an observable behaviour that may be interrelated with other kinds of problem solving behaviour. This variable is complicated by the fact that some studies do not clearly differentiate between the availability of one or more models or modelling techniques (DSS capability) and the usage of those techniques (user behaviour). This distinction may be moot where there is only one way of using the technology, but often there is variability in the way an individual or group adapts to and uses the technology.

Performance refers to evaluating the outcomes generated by the individual or group in accomplishing their task. The performance construct is obviously important to DSS theory because it provides a way to establish the effectiveness of the DSS project.

Performance measures fall into four general categories: (1) quality or effectiveness [9,25,32], focusing on tangible results of the decision or problem solution; (2) satisfaction or confidence [9,25,32], focusing on the attitude of group members regarding these outcomes; (3) economic value, referring to net profit, increased revenue, or decreased expenses to the organization resulting from introducing and using the DSS; and (4) efficiency, concerned primarily with time to make the decision or complete the task. Performance measures are likely to be idiosyncratic to the organization and the task in question.

The constructs identified in the preceding analysis suggest eight major factors to consider in designing a decision support system. The dimensions along which these constructs may vary were reviewed in the preceding text. This does not mean that these are the only dimensions or the important dimensions. They represent what has been studied in past research. Further analysis is required to determine if all relevant dimensions have been considered or if some dimensions need to be eliminated.

## 5. The DSS construct model

This section of the paper uses the constructs developed in the preceding section to develop a model of DSS research. The model identifies relationships among these constructs that have been empirically investigated. Based on this past research, it provides a qualitative assessment of the current understanding of those relationships.

Relationships are viewed as patterns of influence which describe how changes in the level or state of one construct correlate with changes in the level or state of another construct. Since the constructs we described are abstracted from, rather than observed in, the literature, we cannot describe the status of relationships among these constructs by drawing directly from individual studies. Further, describing relationships with precision is difficult due to a lack of standardized definitions and measurement techniques for variables. In addition, research studies report on relationships with differing degrees of specificity. For example, case studies tend to report on general conclusions reached by researchers or study participants, while laboratory and survey method studies may utilize a widely varying array of statistical tests (and may vary in the level of detail with which these are reported). Consequently, this paper summarizes the tested relationships among variables presented in the literature, but groups them according to the constructs presented in the previous section. This grouping allows us to develop a qualitative assessment of the relationships among elements of our model and may aid in developing an understanding of which constructs and relationships are more or less important in studying DSS.

If two one-way relationships can exist between each pair of constructs (for example, the influence of A on B and the influence of B on A), then there are 56 possible relationships in the model. This section examines the 17 pairs where we have found empirical evidence regarding relationships among the constructs and related variables (Fig. 1). The discussion includes an explanation of the relationships, the (potential) importance of the relationships, findings relevant to the relationships, deficiencies in understanding, and opportunities for study.

DSS capability to user (3). (Table 3.) This relationship examines the effect of the functions provided by the DSS on the attitudes and abilities of the user. For example, research may focus on the impact of a certain tool on the user's satisfaction with the task. The effect of the system on user attributes and abilities is important because user attitudes and abilities may contribute to the eventual success of the DSS. DSS capabilities examined in this relationship include format of presentation, accuracy, and response time. Again, results are mixed. Varying the format of the information available to decision makers does seem to affect user attitudes; however, the specific influence will vary with the situation.

Past studies of this relationship focused primarily on attitudes rather than on abilities. Understanding user attitudes may help predict use or non-use of the system as well as which tools users consider understandable or too complex. Research on the effect of DSS capabilities on user abilities is not as abundant although this relationship may be important regarding long term DSS use. More detailed questions concern which capabilities (or what order of presentation or use) enhance or impede learning. In the long run, questions such as these are important because desirable outcomes to DSS use may include enhanced employee abilities, as well as more effective task completion, making the employee a more valuable resource for the organization.

Implementation strategy to performance (4). (Table 4.) This relationship examines the effect of the method used to develop and implement the DSS on the ultimate success of the system. This relationship was a major focus of attention in the examined studies.

The relationship between implementation strategy and performance is fairly strongly supported. An iterative development strategy that involves users is generally more successful than a SDLC type approach. This suggests that how a DSS is developed and implemented should be considered carefully when approaching a DSS project.

However, understanding this implementation strategy could be refined. To the extent that user participation and involvement positively influences performance, is it due to: (1) special knowledge of users brought to bear on the project; (2) knowledge of the project gained from observing and participating in the project; and/or (3) greater involvement and more accountability of developers forced by user participation?

Among the four identified studies, there was some support for a relationship between the implementation strategy and the user's decision to use the DSS.

User behaviour to performance (7). (Table 7.) This relationship is concerned with the effect of the way users employ the DSS on the ultimate success of the system. It is viewed as an important relationship, based on the large number of studies. While some DSS systems may demand only one very specific method of utilization, other systems may allow great flexibility to the user in terms of what data to input into the system, what path to take through various options, and how to integrate the results of DSS utilization into specific decisions or organizational activities. To the extent that user behaviour can vary during contact with the DSS, that variation can potentially influence decision outcomes.

Empirical results regarding this relationship are mixed. Much of the user behaviour considered in the studies that examined this relationship was on use of the DSS, rather than on how it was used. Future studies will benefit from identifying more explicit variations in user behaviour. Thus, although this is one of the more heavily tested relationships in the DSS literature, much work remains to understand more thoroughly the connection between user behaviours and performance results.

User behaviour to user (8). (Table 8.) This relationship examines how a user's behaviour when using a DSS affects the user's attitudes about the DSS. Although research on this relationship in the DSS is extremely limited, cognitive psychology studies have shown many influences on attitudes, including influences from one's own behaviour [16]. For example, experiments by Fazio and Zanna [15] suggest that attitudes formed from direct experience are better defined and more confidently held than attitudes formed from indirect experience. In the context of DSS, the experience people have using DSS tools ought to influence their longer term view of the DSS. This relationship may be important for assessing the perceived value of such tools.

Environment to user behaviour (10). (Table 10.) This relationship examines the effect of the organizational context or situation on the way users make decisions and use decision support systems. Why would one be interested in the environment's effect on the user's behaviour? Two possibilities are suggested by the three studies investigated. First, this relationship suggests characteristics of the environment that influence user adoption of technology. Second, as suggested by Schroeder and Benbasat [32], there may be characteristics of the environment, external to the task, that influence how users will behave in their effort to make decisions. Thus, understanding the environment may aid in designing DSS for such environments.

Task to performance (13). (Table 13.) This relationship examines the effect of different task characteristics on the ultimate success of the system. Performance is expected to vary with different tasks because the given features of the DSS will vary in their ability to match the demands of the task.

Presumably, one may learn much about the capacities of a given DSS by observing performance across a range of tasks. Investigating the relationship between task and performance with a given DSS provides insight into what tasks or characteristics of tasks are effectively addressed by different DSS technology. Currently, the relationship is not examined thoroughly and the results are mixed. Only two task characteristics (newness and difficulty) were examined in the literature reviewed.

Task to user behaviour (14). (Table 14.) This relationship is concerned with the effect of the requirements and characteristics of the task to be performed on how the individual using the DSS behaves while completing the task. This relationship has not been thoroughly explored in the DSS literature. However, in the group dynamics literature, researchers found that different tasks imposed varied behaviour requirements on group members [26]. This led to intensive efforts to formulate valid classification schemes for distinguishing different types of tasks and for better understanding their impact on behaviours and performance variables.

Although relatively unexplored, this relationship could be very important to DSS in understanding what a DSS could do for the individual involved in a specific task, in understanding the commonality among tasks and what types of tasks are best addressed with a DSS, and for comparing task completion with and without DSS assistance.

Task to user (15). (Table 15.) This relationship examines the effect of different tasks or task characteristics on the attitudes and abilities of the individual performing the task (primarily with DSS use). Understanding the impact of a task on the user may aid in the development of a more effective DSS for a particular user. However, only over a relatively long period time period, or where one subset of users performs one task and a different subset performs a different task, would we expect user traits to vary as a consequence of different tasks.

DSS Configuration to Performance (16). (Table 16.) This relationship examines the effect of the underlying technology of a DSS on the outcomes of the task performed with the DSS (for example, does having an application generator improve decision performance?). Although this pair of constructs was studied in only two cases, the consistently positive relationship suggests a potentially strong link. It would appear that the DSS configuration presents an important technical constraint enabling or inhibiting the ability to deliver the kind of system that will be effective. However, care must be taken because presenting users with two versions of a technology, even if intended to compare configurations, often will provide subtly different capabilities. For example, when comparing two spreadsheet or word processing packages (even where clear behavioral or performance differences are observed), the researcher must be very careful in distinguishing impacts resulting from differing capabilities versus those resulting from differing configurations.

DSS Configuration to Implementation Strategy (17). (Table 17.) This relationship is concerned with the impact of the technologies chosen as the technical basis for a DSS on the way in which the DSS is developed. This relationship was examined by Tillman and Watson [39] in the specific context of DSS corporate planning models. "Non-procedural languages" such as IFPS were developed in less time and at less cost than "procedural languages" such as APL and Fortran. This relationship has received very little attention but may be consequential in determining constraints which support or inhibit efficient implementation activity. Additionally, while no studies were found, the reverse relationship (Implementation Strategy to DSS Configuration) may be of equal or more importance because different development strategies require specific tools. The use of these tools may constrain the DSS configuration.

The model of DSS developed in this study includes eight constructs: environment, task, DSS capability, DSS configuration, user, user behaviour, implementation strategy, and performance. In effect, the model says that the effective study of DSS should encompass or account for these constructs. Any DSS is developed and used in the context of an organizational environment. The DSS may be examined separately from the environment, but is influenced by it. Understanding the effect of environment on the other constructs may aid developers in implementing a successful system. The task that the DSS is to support is also important to consider in examining DSS. Tasks vary in what information processing is required to complete them. Therefore tasks with different requirements will have very different impacts on the DSS used and the effectiveness of the DSS to facilitate their completion.

The DSS itself is considered from two different views in this model. One view of DSS is its capabilities. The functions provided by a DSS are important to the effective execution of tasks. The other view of DSS is internal. This focus is on the hardware and software used to provide the capabilities of the DSS. Different hardware and software configurations may facilitate the offering of different capabilities as well as the effectiveness of the capabilities' implementation.

The actual user of the DSS is also considered through two constructs: user and user behaviour. The user construct represents the individual's attitudes and abilities, while the user behaviour construct represents the physical and cognitive actions taken by the user. User attitudes and abilities are important to understand because they are associated with the success of the DSS implementation. A DSS that does not fit user abilities is less likely to be used, while a DSS that positively impacts user attitudes is more likely to be used. User behaviour is a particularly important construct because it is probably an important intervening variable between DSS capabilities and performance. Presumably, the goal of introducing a DSS for a task is to change the behaviour of the individual performing the task so that the individual is more effective.

The performance construct of the model is important because it provides the basis for measuring the outcome of a DSS intervention. DSS may have an impact on the quality or effectiveness of the task outcomes, the satisfaction or confidence of the user, the bottom-line in terms of increased profit or decreased expenses, or the efficiency of task completion. Any of these is an important impact of the DSS on the organization. The performance construct is the ultimate dependent variable in the study of DSS.

The final construct in the model is implementation strategy. The implementation strategy is the method or tactics used by the designers to develop the DSS and place it in use in the organization. The implementation strategy is potentially very important for the eventual success of the DSS.

It is important to note that while Fig. 1 shows all the constructs of the model, only the relationships found to have been investigated in the literature are depicted. These 17 (of the 56 possible) may not be enough to provide strong guidelines for the design of DSS. However, there are some implications. For example, understanding how certain DSS capabilities affect user behaviour should provide a strong basis for DSS design, especially if the behavioral requirements for different tasks are known. Several studies examining this relationship [e.g., 17, 25] measure only quantity of use. Although examining quantity of use provides some initial understanding, a more fine-grained investigation of user behaviour is required to provide DSS designers with an understanding of how their products can elicit or support desirable behaviours with respect to the task. Also, understanding the impact of DSS capabilities on the user could aid DSS designers, particularly in their attention to the user interface. From the DSS designer's perspective, understanding how tasks and user behaviours vary, and the interaction between task and user behaviour, may provide rich guidance in choosing appropriate design options. Further development of DSS guidelines is best left for another paper. One caution is that in such endeavours, a pairwise approach to the constructs may be less than satisfactory. For effective design guidelines, one may have to consider many constructs at once.

## 6. Boundaries

The preceding sections of this paper identified and examined the constructs and relationships that are investigated in DSS research. To a large extent, these items set the boundaries of DSS. Boundaries distinguish between the phenomena of interest for a given domain and the phenomena excluded from the domain. For theory development, boundaries typically identify the constructs (or entities of interest) in the domain, the relationships of interest among these constructs, and when these relationships are valid (i.e., for which ranges of construct values the relationships hold).

Given the research examined, it would not yet be appropriate to produce a rigid definition of the boundaries for DSS theory. For example, the relationship between user and user behaviour would extend beyond the research boundary unless constrained within the decision making task. However, we would infer from this study that research that moves toward better understanding the identified constructs (e.g., development of instruments) as they pertain to organizational decision making, or toward testing the impact of the identified constructs on one another, would be included within the boundary of DSS research. Within this definition, however, some fuzziness remains. For example, studies oriented toward defining or specifying new technology, without testing the technology, were not considered in this literature review but might arguably be within the boundaries of DSS theory research. In a sense these studies define and delimit the nature of the “capability” and “configuration” constructs.

This study proposed a framework consisting of eight constructs. The empirical literature shows at least some testing of all eight constructs. Moreover, we observed findings pertaining to 17 of 56 possible relationships among the identified constructs. One interpretation could be that all eight constructs are within the DSS theory boundary, but those that were highly tested are more central within that boundary. Following this reasoning, the most examined constructs (DSS capability, implementation strategy, user behaviour, and performance) form an area of central concern to DSS theory. This is not to say that other constructs are not important, but rather that they are more prone to overlap with related fields. DSS configuration overlaps with engineering and computer science studies; user overlaps with psychology and small group studies; and the environment overlaps with organizational theory studies. Task can be viewed as something of a constant, to the extent that by definition DSS deals with decision-making tasks; but much work remains toward better definition and understanding of the kinds of variance that exist among different decisions.

## 7. Conclusion

This study promotes theory development by organizing the scattered literature into a theoretical framework. This framework accounts for all variables observed, at least in empirical DSS literature. Although it is less parsimonious than alternative frameworks (e.g., Dickson et al. [11]), it defines constructs more clearly by specifying distinctions between user and user behaviours, DSS capabilities and configurations, task and environment. It also adds the concept of implementation strategy. Mapping empirical findings from the DSS literature to this framework has shown evidence of relationships among some constructs, the potential importance of interactions among variables, and some gaps in the literature, the filling of which may provide important clues for better understanding the DSS domain.

As McGrath [26] points out, no single study can fulfill all desirable goals for scientific research. Certainly, this study is limited in the scope and selection of articles that served as basic input. While no effort was made to exclude any particular type of DSS research, the selection of articles from the targeted journals may have missed relevant studies. Where judgement was required, the authors used the approach of discussion to consensus. In its method, this research may have weighed studies more heavily where constructs and measures are well defined (favouring laboratory experiments over case or field studies) and where concepts are tested rather than simply proposed (favouring empirical over conceptual articles).

As with other complex domains of study, there are a sufficient number of variables such that individual studies of each pair of variables at each possible level or state would result in combinatorial explosion. This study aims to identify key relationships and to clearly display both the amount of study and the findings to date for each relationship. Researchers can use these observations (1) to expand on why observed relationships exist (e.g., between implementation strategy and performance); (2) to target relationships that have not yet been explored (e.g., the effect of performance on the environment); or (3) to further investigate contingencies explaining different findings across many studies on key relationships (e.g., DSS capability on user behaviour).

This study highlights relationships that appear to have some empirical support: (1) that implementation strategy influences both user behaviour and performance; (2) that environmental factors influence performance; and (3) that DSS configuration influences performance. A theory of DSS should guide development efforts. Each DSS is tied to the task(s) it supports, was developed and introduced into the environment in a particular way, manifests certain capabilities, and is constrained by technological limits. Although the amount and strength of support for these relationships is limited, these relationships suggest that 20 years of DSS research have generated findings that can be valuable to DSS designers. It is interesting that the array of factors which appear to influence success supports a generally socio-technical view that both behavioral and technical factors must be attended to for successful introduction of a new application into the work environment. Further studies can refine the manner and degree to which each of these factors contributes to successful DSS performance.

It is interesting that no findings were presented for 39 of the possible 56 relationships. Researchers used some constructs solely as independent or dependent variables. Neither environment nor task was viewed as a dependent variable. Performance was never viewed as an independent variable. This implies that while researchers may describe the DSS design and implementation as an iterative process in principle, in practice it is generally studied from a linear input-process-output model. Some case studies, however, suggest the existence of a feedback loop where the organizational environment impacts the quality of the DSS, and the immediate outcomes of the DSS, in turn, influence the environment. Gulley and Mei [18] detailed the impact of using decision models on federal coal leasing policy. Their study found: (1) using decision models became evidence of effective process, and (2) much of the decision models' value was in documenting rather than in formulating specific decisions. El Sherif and El Sawy [14] arrived at similar conclusions regarding the impact of DSS development and use by the Egyptian Cabinet. Ulvila, Brown, and Packard [41] found that using DSS decision analysis was perceived to be successful by managers, and in turn created the intention to use decision analysis to a greater degree in the future.

Another possible explanation for the lack of testing these relationships pertains to research method. Testing certain relationships requires adequate measurement of the constructs involved. Areas where measurement is sound or less demanding will be addressed first. In other words, the constructs that are more likely to be operationally defined and accurately measured will be focused on before the constructs that are more difficult to define and measure. It seems, for example, that empirical studies focus on the immediate impacts of the DSS (on user behaviour, user attitudes, and performance) rather than on the impacts of changed performance on the organizational environment or on the nature of the tasks being performed.

Observing several relationships that have generated divergent findings (notably DSS capabilities on user behaviour, user behaviour on performance, and DSS capability on performance) suggests the importance of possible interactions among variables. The implication of interactions is that key relationships will hold for some, but not all, values or states of the environment, task, implementation strategy, DSS configuration, and user.

This would suggest the need for studies that control some or all of these factors at different levels, as well as studies that simultaneously measure several of these variables. Laboratory studies aimed at exploring the effect of different information formats (a DSS capability) on user behaviour and performance might do well to utilize and compare findings across a number of different tasks. In this way we may learn which user behaviours are universal and which are task specific. Field studies or cases can plan for measurement of a wide variety, perhaps all, of the noted constructs. With enough of these studies, we may eliminate study of some aspects of some constructs (e.g., by learning which states of the environment are unlikely to occur) or we may begin to see states of constructs that vary together (e.g., fourth generation languages, a DSS configuration, may always be used with prototyping, a particular implementation strategy).

In summary, this paper has attempted to move the domain of DSS research toward a greater connection to a theoretical base by (1) identifying and defining constructs; (2) organizing these constructs into a usable framework and proposing potential relationships among constructs; (3) mapping existing empirical findings into the framework; (4) identifying relationships supported by findings to date, those with equivocal results, and those that have not yet been studied; and (5) offering possible explanations for these findings and suggesting directions for future research.

## Appendix B. Rating of theory sophistication

Both the theory base and theory goal of all 109 articles were rated for their level of sophistication. Each reviewer rated the theory used in the article on the following scale:

(1) No laws of interaction among constructs are described.

(2) Presence-Absence-if one variable is present at a positive or negative value, the related variable will have a corresponding positive or negative value.

(3) Directionality-the theory proposes the direction or the relationship between the values of one variable and the values of another variable.

(4) Covariation-the law of interaction proposes a convariation relationship (s-curve, u-curve, etc.) between the variables of interest.

(5) Rate of change—the law of interaction explicitly states the exact rate of change between the values of one variable and the values of an associated variable.

If the theory used in the article received an average rating of over 2.5 the theory was considered relatively sophisticated. The raters agreed that an article's theory base and theory goal were either relatively sophisticated or relatively unsophisticated 90 out of 109 times (82.6%), yielding a Cohen's Kappa of.77.

He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Minnesota. Eierman also holds a B.B.A. and M.S. from the University of Wisconsin-Madison. His research interests include problem formulation, decision support systems, creativity enhancement through information technology, and the global use of information technology.

Carl R. Adams is Professor and Chairman of the Information and Decision Sciences Department of the Curtis L. Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. His current research interests are focussed on managerial problem solving and decision support. Both his M.S. (Industrial Administration) and his Ph.D. (Management Science) degrees are from the Krannert School of Purdue University.

Fred Niederman is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Information and Quantitative Sciences at the University of Baltimore. He has a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. His current research interests include: decision support systems, facilitation of group decision support systems, problem formulation, international MIS, and MIS personnel.
