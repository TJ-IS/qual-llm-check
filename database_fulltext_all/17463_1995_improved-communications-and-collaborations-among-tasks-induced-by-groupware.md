# Improved communications and collaborations among tasks induced by Groupware $^{*}$

Piero Migliarese \*, Emilio Paolucci

Politecnico di Milano, Dipartimento di Economia e Produzione, Piazza Leonardo da Vinci 32, 20133 Milano, Italy

## Abstract

The recent developments of Information Technologies are characterized by some new classes of applications that are classified as Groupware. These developments are connected to the need of attaining better communications and new forms of collaborations among tasks or functional departments in modern organizational structures. The new strategic scenarios are fostering organizational structures in attaining increasing varieties of products, capacities of faster responses to market changes, flexibility in modifying strategies, organizational structures, technologies. Together with actual managerial policies looking for Just-in-Time and slack resources reduction, these points compel organization structures to manage tasks that are becoming more and more interdependent. Starting from this point of view, the paper analyzes from an organizational theory perspective the role that Groupware is assuming inside the emergent organizational models. Information Technologies appear as important opportunities in facing the problems of tighter interdependencies: they provide new communications channels and allow the introduction of new organizational forms which meet strategic requirements. Also the cooperation needs, critical in some organizational sub-systems like design departments, project management, cross-functional processes at managerial level as well at operational, can be sustained and improved through Groupware. These perspectives are presented in detail in the paper, together with a proposal of an architecture for GDSS design based on the classification of the organizational needs and of the opportunities offered by IT tools.

Keywords: Coordination processes; Group decision support systems; Groupware; Innovative organizational models

## 1. Introduction

The aim of this paper is to analyze and to define the role and the opportunities offered by

Groupware within organizations, focusing attention on the support to decision-making connected to coordination management. This analysis is made from an organizational theory point of view, although contributions given by technology and economic sciences will be briefly considered.

In a context of diffusion of new organizational models, (network models, flexible organization models, etc.) which are expected to be less bureaucratic and more communicative than in the past, the importance of organizational tools and particularly of IT based tools to the support of communications and collaborations in coordination processes is rapidly increasing.

The paper is divided into the following sections:

(1) Section one points out the relations among innovation in organizational models and the strategic scenario that is compelling firms to become more competitive in terms of (i) response to market and environmental stimulation, (ii) flexibility in changing and innovating processes/products, (iii) improving the quality of their outputs.

(2) Section two examines the role of teams and the support of IT within the emerging organizational models. In a scenario where strategic forces are augmenting the complex interdependencies among organizational units, team-work, communications, collaboration among tasks and the enhancement of lateral coordination mechanisms are solicited. The emerging new organizational paradigms for dealing with these problems are examined and related to new forms of support given by IT.

(3) Section three applies the previous results to Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) as tools designed to support the new structures in managing the coordination of tasks, the integration of organizational units, communications and cooperation in some subsystems in which teamwork constitutes a competitive edge. The relations between their design and organizational analysis are discussed, showing how these systems require a real appreciation of the organizational role that they have to sustain.

## 2. New competitive strategies and the innovative organizational models

Nowadays we are observing new characteristics in competitive environments, that are causing a more tight connection among strategic, technological and organizational variables. At present business organizations are facing new constraints and new requirements in terms of overall performances.

The evolution of the markets, moving away from mass production to products customization, together with the development and the diffusion of new advanced technologies, as well as the internationalization of the economic competition, are inducing an evolution in some internal organizational mechanisms of companies.

\- Competitive requirements are claiming for the improvement of the effectiveness (making right things) and of the overall efficiency (making things right), to be obtained through the reduction of every kind of resource buffers inside many organizational areas (production as well as design, planning and financial activities).

\- The execution of most of business activities is getting more complex, because of the new requirements imposed by the competitive and technological aspects.

\- The strategic relevance of time has increased dramatically [16]. Companies must obtain efficiency and effectiveness while making things fast.

The evolution of the competitive scenario is inducing important changes in the design of organizations and in their management. The investigation of the connections among strategic, technological and organizational variables will be the starting point of our analysis.

## 2.1. New requirements for organizational models

Flexibility, time-based competition, innovation in technologies and in products, are but only some of the performances required at present to organizations. They are compelling firms to abandon traditional organizational forms to adopt new models that can appropriately deal with new problems.

A brief investigation of the main features of innovative organizational models and of their coherence to the new strategic variables can be made first of all by adopting the classical Strategy-Structure approach (Fig. 1) proposed by Chandler [2]. In fact, a change in terms of strategy has to be followed by an adequate and coherent change of the organizational structure.

Fig. 1. The Strategy - structure model.

In this sense the presence within organizations of non-value adding activities, of time and other resources wasting, etc., is no more acceptable. These inefficiencies often concern bad integration between activities as well as difficulties in coordinating different technical competencies for obtaining an unified goal with a significant strategic relevance (for instance a new product, an innovative project, a common goal for a team, etc.).

The modern managerial policies and techniques (i.e., Just in Time, Pull Production Systems, Computer Integrated Manufacturing, etc.) look for the improvement of efficiency through the reduction or the elimination of many types of resource buffers.

On the contrary, organizational sciences have traditionally considered buffers as a sort of barrier that was necessary to protect the internal core of organizations from external turbulence [18].

The main effect of removing buffers is that organizations are less and less made of decoupled or loosely coupled parts and are assuming the characteristics of systems whose elements (the organizational units) are connected among them and to the external environment.

In this new context organizations are more exposed to the effects of external events (for example to changes in market demand) and therefore they have to look for new approaches to manage the integration of their actions with the relevant environment. At the same time the effect on the internal part of organizations concerns mainly a redesign of the integration mechanisms among interrelated tasks (we consider a task as a set of activities strictly connected, with defined inputs, outputs and responsibilities, whose execution is assigned to one or more organizational units or individual roles). Thus many tasks become more coupled than in the past, due to the technological complexity and the temporal requirements, too. It often happens that organizational units that seem to be independent when there are slack resources, become tightly coupled after these resources are removed.

The problem of integration of tasks is not new, but it is assuming different characteristics with respect to the past years, regarding also the integration among specific competencies and knowledge required for the tasks execution. The effect of the buffers removal, and of the derived integration needs, is that the structure of tasks inside the organization and the technical core of organizations are not as clear and rigidly defined as they were twenty-thirty years ago $[1,19]$ . At the same time the diffusion and the importance of the knowledge-based tasks and of the core competencies $[14]$ inside companies is increasing.

Organizational sciences presented two main streams for dealing with the problem of integration (Fig. 2):

(1) The first technique to obtain integration is the use of new resources in order to decouple tasks. Examples of these resources are inventories of raw materials and finished products, multi-skilled personnel, large time span for activities, etc. All these “slack resources” [3,4] are expensive but potentially reduce integration and coordination costs. The utilization of slack resources requires the evaluation of the trade-off among their costs and the savings that can be obtained in coordination expenses. The attempt to reduce or to remove slack resources even though it potentially increases overall efficiency, may also cause coordination problems, whose complexity is associated to the degree of turbulence of the external environment and to the variability of internal tasks.

![](/api/attachments/K954Q4DK/fulltext/images/304d983ca418780db1c55ac76fe224373b33365ab844874ae95f080850ca9bca.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The two alternatives for obtaining organizational integration.

(2) The second way to obtain integration is to improve coordination mechanisms through the adoption of organizational tools and techniques (i.e., the creation of new organizational roles and departments) or through the use of IT based facilities. Both these alternatives are related not only to the hierarchical/vertical dimension of the organizational structure, but also to the lateral/horizontal one. This choice, although guarantees a reduction of the areas of inefficiency, is associated with the introduction of new costs in the management of an organization.

The new strategic requirements are reducing the importance of alternative (1) because too expensive. Under the effects of their competitive environments, organizations are shifting their attention toward alternative (2), looking for new organizational models that can reduce overall costs for coordination. The development of Information Technologies is encouraging this migration, offering the opportunity to increase lateral relations within organizations [8,9], offering new communication and computing facilities at continuously decreasing costs.

There is a further aspect that has to be considered as problematic: in this scenario the elimination of slack resources together with the introduction of new technologies are increasing the degree of interdependence among tasks, moving from simple levels of interdependence to more complex ones. However this fact is not only a problem: the capability to manage interdependencies, instead than hiding them using slack resources, may become the source of new competitive advantages [13].

The migration toward alternative (2) imposes the reconsideration of principles that constitute the basis of organizational design. Traditionally, organizations have been designed following a functional perspective [17]. According to this paradigm, the criteria for differentiating organizational units was essentially based on the functional specialization and on the aggregation of technical knowledge, while the integration of tasks was essentially obtained through different forms of standardization.

In recent years some researches stressed the concept of tasks integration. During the Eighties, for example, Porter [20] suggested the concept of value chain: the traditional functional differentiation is assumed, but a strong emphasis on integrating activities is introduced. Nevertheless Porter's model considers activities as sequentially connected in a chain on the basis of the physical and economic processes of value adding. Organizational links among activities and more complex patterns of relations, like feedback ones, are not considered.

We can therefore affirm that at present the main focus of the emerging organizational paradigms is on the coordination and the integration of tasks within organizations. In these new models the goal of tasks coordination becomes the contemporaneous improvement of the speed and of the quality of decision-making processes, as well as of the management of coordination under unstable conditions.

## 2.2. The innovative organizational models and the management of interdependence

The choice of alternative (2) creates the problem of identifying tools that can offer a high degree of organizational integration at reasonable costs. It is in this frame that we are going to explain the new importance that IT based tools are assuming inside organizations:

(1) The lateral integration strategy becomes feasible from an economical point of view due to the technological developments of IT.

(2) IT as well as “ad hoc” organizational tools can create important opportunities in integrating tasks by establishing new communication channels for lateral relations; they are able to face the problem of managing different forms of interdependencies among tasks [15].

Interdependencies are a fact of organizational life and they depend on the relations among organizational units and/or on the execution of tasks. Their correct management is a crucial activity in many organizations. The necessity to augment the coupling among tasks increases the complexity of interdependencies, influencing the aspects of their management (costs, techniques, tools). The challenge for new forms of organization is therefore to find tools that can adequately manage interdependence in terms of efficiency, effectiveness and time requirements. Only an accurate analysis of organizational requirements can help the identification of these tools.

![](/api/attachments/K954Q4DK/fulltext/images/cb15d5bab41137f2944be07556eda2869afa51a217ef01080d2709ea7021af05.jpg)  
Fig. 3. The tasks interdependencies.

There exist three types of interdependencies among tasks: generic, sequential and reciprocal (Fig. 3) [18]. The generic interdependencies appear when tasks share only common equipment or resources (for example among strategic business units). The sequential interdependencies appear when tasks are linked in a chain with linear and well-defined relations (for example between the production units and the sales office). The reciprocal interdependencies are bi-directional relations among not totally pre-defined tasks and are presenting a mutual or feedback based influence among tasks (for example between design and production departments in concurrent engineering).

The coordination mechanisms are the organizational tools designed to manage interdependencies and to obtain integration. Thompson differentiates three main classes of coordination mechanisms: standardization, planning and mutual adjustment. Each of them is suitable and effective for different types of interdependencies. In a similar line, Mintzberg [11] proposed five basic mechanisms in coordinating activities: mutual adjustment, direct supervision, standardization (of work input, work process, work output).

Although mechanisms based on standardization and planning remain fundamental in many situations, their effectiveness seems to decrease quickly with the growth of tasks complexity. At the same time the importance of mutual adjustment is growing, together with the need to adequately support it, especially when dealing with activities at a high organizational level or at a high-abstraction one.

IT can support different coordination mechanisms, depending on the type of interdependence considered and on the characteristics of organizational context.

The removal of slack resources transforms many generic and sequential interdependencies into reciprocal ones.

Reciprocal interdependencies are critical in areas where there are many ill-structured and coupled tasks (for instance products design and engineering, or project management) which claim for coordination techniques based essentially on rapid communications, feed-back loops and mutual adjustment. In terms of organizational variables, the attention in these areas seems to shift from a rigid functional approach to the adoption of dynamic, task focused teams. The importance gained by team work is due to its potential capability to deal with the unavoidable complexity of reciprocal interdependencies and to effectively integrate competencies in not standard settings.

Although team work seems to be the most suitable tool for getting such results, there are some traditional obstacles in its applicability: the spatial and temporal constraints among team members, its cost, the conflicts among team members' sub-goals, increasing complexity and importance assumed by interdependencies management, etc.

IT tools seem therefore to be addressed towards the support of teamwork in coordinating complex interdependencies and to the removal of these obstacles.

The comprehension of the link between organizational environment and the support required from IT is fundamental for the design of effective applications, especially for what concerns reciprocal interdependencies. In authors' opinion, this frame constitutes the organizational domain for team-work, where new forms of technological support find their main opportunities to be used from an organizational point of view, both in the coordination of tasks and in the reinforcement of cooperation in team-work.

## 3. Teamwork, group support and innovative organizational models

The role that IT tools are supposed to play inside this framework of organizational change is linked to the problems that are characterizing the everyday life of many organizations. IT tools are particularly useful in the areas where the changes in organizational requirements or in plans are frequent because of the high level of environmental turbulence; in these areas, arrangements must be rapid and effective, according to the temporal requirements induced by time-based competition.

Although coordination can be cheaply obtained by standardization and planning, these mechanisms cannot deal with the growth of interdependencies complexity. On the other side, we have already outlined how it is impossible to simplify these interdependencies in organizational areas where they appear. Under these circumstances, the introduction of new tools for dealing with the integration of complex interdependencies really becomes a key success point.

Teamwork becomes an important organizational tool in these situations and Groupware becomes the technical set of IT based tools for supporting teamwork.

Groupware needs to be first of all a connecting infrastructure among coupled tasks and teams involved in their execution. Domain-specific applications have to be designed to cope with particular problems of teams, also in order to improve cooperation among individuals. In this perspective, Groupware means the feasibility of a connecting technology that support the different forms of lateral coordination (i.e., face-to-face or remote, synchronous or asynchronous, etc.) managed through team-work.

Instead of looking for the removal of complex interdependencies, Groupware makes it possible to build the basis for the creation of new tools and thus of new forms of organization, where an important nucleus of not or partially structured activities is linked within a complex network of relationships.

## 3.1. Six parameters of interdependence affected by groupware applications

The recalled schema about the organizational concept of interdependence shows first of all the existence of various types of relations among tasks that can be classified into three main classes of interdependence.

According to this classification, the aim of this section is to propose six parameters of interdependence that have the property to be significantly affected by Groupware applications. Although the proposed parameters are the ones influenced by IT applications, they do not pretend to describe completely the interdependence concept and relation.

It follows that these parameters have to be carefully analyzed in the design phase of Groupware applications.

The proposed six parameters are the following:

Time. There are various types of tasks with respect to time: simultaneous, independent, concurrent or parallel, connected, sequential. When the structuring of tasks is done, we can consider that time is relevant with respect to two aspects.

\- The first one concerns the starting time of a task. In this case, temporal constraints, inside a given structure of tasks, are propagated among interrelated tasks.

\- The second one concerns the pace (unit per time).

The effect of time and the way it influences work flow depends on the part of the organization that we are considering.

For instance, in production areas (under JIT and Pull Systems approaches) the last operation of a process is the one that sets the pace and the values of temporal constraints.

In the design areas, during the product development cycle, the relations among tasks are complex and reciprocal: the starting time of a task imposes many requirements on the tasks related to it.

The increased strategic role of time-based competition reduces the admitted lag time (that is a slack resource) among tasks; it also increases the pace of every task.

This fact causes a growth of the importance of the sequential and reciprocal interdependencies, which are the most time sensitive ones, and of the role of IT based tools for supporting them. For example, an integrated procedure for orders fulfilling takes into account many connections and interdependencies among multiple aspects and organizational units: technical and economic variables for the order evaluation, production planning constraints and variables to establish order due-date and thus many organizational units like the Orders Acceptance Office, the Technical Department, the Production Department, the Administrative Department.

Space. Some tasks are spatially linked and they cannot be accomplished separately or in a remote way.

The reasons of the linkage may be various and different: physical constraints, the nature itself of tasks requiring frequent adjustments among differentiated tasks operating on the same object (for example, various specialized tasks in construction industry operating in the same building, or many physician specialties operating on the same subject), constraints and opportunities in a given technical context.

IT applications, and thus the availability of another technology, can transform previously spatially linked tasks in disconnected ones, to be performed in a remote way. The connection can be obtained through a communication network (see for example many administrative and bureaucratic tasks and services that can be spatially distributed through a common data base and an interconnecting infrastructure).

Quality. The quality of the output of a task does not depend only on the execution of the task itself, but also on many others factors deriving from other tasks (for example the quality of its input). The quality requirements impose new forms of interdependence among connected tasks. The goals of Total Quality imply tight procedures for quality control in every step of production process.

The existence of a quality information system available for various tasks in a process, influences the way in which tasks are performed.

Information sharing. The execution of interdependent tasks requires the extensive availability of information. The existence of a shared database among differentiated organizational units strongly affects the global efficiency of work.

In CAD-CAM applications, for instance, a common data-base regarding product drawings can reduce the time for product modification and innovation, as well as the time to market of a new product.

Competence Sharing. This parameter deals with the phenomenon of cross-fertilization among different technical expertise and methods that can be important in some areas. The availability of a connecting infrastructure for the communication potentially influences this aspect, speeding up the transfer of expertise.

Relevant dimensions. The complexity of the management of the interdependence among offices is heavily influenced by the number of employees or generally speaking by other relevant dimensions (number of organizational units, number of specializations involved, etc. [4]). The availability of some IT tools can reduce the complexity of managing interdependence at a given value of the relevant dimensions.

Thus, IT can reduce the constraints imposed by some parameters like time, space, information sharing needs, relevant dimensions, and can make achievable some goals that are outlined by some of the proposed parameters like quality and competencies sharing.

## 3.2. Task interdependence, group decision processes and IT supports

In order to understand the relation existing between technology and organization we will investigate the characteristics of activity areas where coordination is relevant.

An effective and efficient system of coordination is strictly related to the approach followed while designing the differentiation of roles and the integration mechanisms inside an organization [7]. The criteria that drives the organizational differentiation and the integration depends on many aspects, like the company strategy, the characteristics of the environment, the critical success factors, etc.

We argue that the traditional approach to coordination, based on hierarchy and procedures is no more sufficient, but it cannot be completely removed. Groupware can support the management of interdependencies and of lateral coordination within some organizational areas where hierarchy and procedures do not work adequately. This support is effective and valuable from an economic point of view, not with respect to all the types of tasks but specifically to the ones that are semi-structured and tightly coupled. For deepening this argument we propose to consider two variables:

(1) The degree of Interdependence among interrelated tasks.

(2) The degree of Decentralization of decision processes connected to the management of coordination among organizational units.

With reference to the first discussed variables, tasks can range from loosely coupled to tightly coupled. With reference to the second variable, we distinguish two different situations in decision processes:

(1) Individual decision process: a person/organizational unit takes the decision.

(2) Coordinated decision process: in this case the decision process needs a global view of the problem, either through a hierarchic decision making or through a group decision process involving different organizational units.

The two selected variables define the matrix depicted in Fig. 4, representing the domain of coordination situations.

Each combination of their values needs different coordination techniques and tools because of its distinctive properties. There are two areas of unfeasibility in Fig. 4.

\- The lower left-hand corner of the figure represents an area where there exists no coordination problems, due to the low degree of interdependence and to the high level of collaboration. Nevertheless this is an area of inefficiency, because the low degree of interde pendence is faced with coordination techniques that are too complex and expensive (group coordination tools).

![](/api/attachments/K954Q4DK/fulltext/images/fe8fe7b5ef50e8d338da28fff8def252b8ee21a2fedaacf0fb5af5c6bc1ee62e.jpg)  
Fig. 4. The domain of coordination processes.

\- The upper right-hand corner (individual decision process and high level of interdependence) represents a situation where coordination cannot be managed. This is an area of ineffectiveness: the high degree of interdependencies is ignored, coordination is not attained and conflictual behaviours are likely to appear.

The central area represents the feasible configurations for coordination and here it is necessary to deepen how IT can support organizations.

The new competitive strategies based on slack resources reduction are increasing, as we pointed out before, the degree of interdependence among tasks and the requirements of coordinated decision process. The visualization of these effects in the matrix is the following:

\- The number and the importance of tasks that are loosely coupled are decreasing; when slack resources are reduced, tasks become more coupled, shifting their position toward the right side of the matrix.

\- Also the necessity to involve many actors in decision making shifts the upper border of the matrix toward the lower one.

The area of feasible coordination can be divided into two parts, namely H (Hierarchy area) and G (Group area). These two areas are significantly different with reference to how coordination can be obtained. The difference can be described through the problems in coordinating tasks, the types of interdependencies and the requirement in terms of IT systems. Moreover, the effects that the six parameters of interdependencies have on the two areas are different.

The H area contains mainly generic and sequential interdependencies with tasks that are coupled, but whose integration can be obtained through a centralization of decision-making. Interdependencies are known and stable and therefore the centralization of decision making works effectively. Under these circumstances, hierarchy and standardization (of input, output and work processes) are the main mechanisms for getting coordination. These coordination tools are useful to manage interdependencies whose more relevant parameters are constituted by space, information sharing and partially time. Here the “traditional” IT applications are successful, while Groupware is an inefficient tool.

The G area deals with the coordination of tightly coupled tasks in situations of reciprocal interdependencies, where the centralization of the decision and hierarchy fail. This failure takes place when uncertainty is high due to strategic and technological environment changes and when specific, not transferable, knowledge becomes important for effective decision-making. For example it may happen that, due to centralization, the flow of decisions concerning coordination has to reach a too high level in the hierarchy, wasting time and other resources, or failing in finding adequate competencies to solve the local problems. The G area is strongly affected by the effects of competence sharing, quality and time (regarding the accomplishment of group activities).

Centralization works right in the H area, where tasks are coupled but their borders and the techniques for execution are well defined. It means that tasks can be sequentially ordered and accomplished following standard routines.

The G area is substantially different: the execution of tasks, the nature of relationships among them and the mechanisms for their management are often not completely defined. Personal and specific knowledge and the cooperation among team members becomes fundamental for the execution of tasks.

## 3.3. The shifting role of it support

The earlier IT applications have been directed to the H area. They have been designed to automate the work and therefore they put the emphasis on the reduction of direct costs, by means of substituting human work with computer processing; no organizational transformation is induced on how the job gets done and on the coordination of tasks. IT is used to automate activities previously accomplished manually in order to increase work efficiency. The adoption of IT is directed essentially toward the improvement of information storage and processing.

The studies in management sciences led to a second phase of development, where IT widened its role within organizations as a support for managerial work. In this new perspective, IT provide information for task execution, generate new information as by-product of the execution of basic tasks and impose the development of new skills and competencies inside organizations.

The development of communication networks supports the integration and coordination of activities. By using the new communication facilities, a firm can for instance coordinate the sale departments and the production units. Communication facilities are used to collect information and to make them available for managers with planning and control competencies. This new capability to collect information facilitates a centralization of the control and of the decision processes for coordination.

We can look at the role of IT support with reference to two different perspectives, a vertical and a lateral one.

The Vertical Perspective. The vertical perspective constitutes the basis for the design of systems dedicated to managers (MIS, EIS, etc.) and maintains the separation between roles that generate information and roles that use these information. The architecture and the flow of data within the information systems reflect the vertical dimension of organizational structures.

In this perspective IT has two main functions: automate and informate (following Zuboff's taxonomy [20]), fitting the coordination requirement of the H Area. They reflect substantially the introduction of IT in organizations where the focus of attention is on point-to-point communicating and computing.

With reference to the discussed six parameters of interdependence, in the vertical perspective IT can act as follows:

Time. Time can be shrunk toward zero, or at least consistently reduced. The temporal dimension concerns time required both for task execution and for communication.

Space. Distance can be shrunk toward zero, making it simpler to manage tasks that are spatially linked but are not performed in the same location (remote tasks). Moreover new forms of tasks design become feasible: tasks can be spatially decoupled without introducing relevant coordination problems, under the hypothesis that the required amount of uncertainty on task execution and competencies remains low (for example telework).

Information sharing. The availability of common databases and communication tools reduces the need of synchronous interactions among organizational units, simplifying the management, especially of sequential interdependencies.

Relevant Dimensions. The capability to automate most of the standard activities reduces in many cases the effects of complexity induced by this aspect.

There are some limits in this approach to IT within organizations. Decision making concerning non-standard problems is often made too high in the hierarchy where the available knowledge for interpreting collected data is not always adequate. IT can support managers in gathering only a fraction of information that they need for decision making. The attempt to gather more information is a time-consuming activity that can be useless or impossible to be accomplished, also because tasks coupling and their coordination problems change continuously.

The Lateral Perspective. The hierarchy and the centralization of decision making fail in managing the reciprocal interdependencies when there are strong implications in terms of the six proposed parameters, namely (i) time, (ii) sharing of competencies, (iii) quality and (iv) relevant dimensions. The vertical IT tools are not able to deal with all these interdependence parameters.

The G area reflects the new perspective of organizational models. Until the end of the Seventies the number and the importance of organizational situations that could be classified within the G area were really low. On the contrary, the management of interdependencies in the Nineties takes place in an a dynamic environment where, together with the centralized perspective of coordination supported by vertical information systems, there is the lateral coordination, in which the collaborative approach based on team work is included.

At present IT is evolving its role inside organizations to extend its support in this area, adding new features to the traditional ones. The innovation of organizational models imposes the integration of tasks on the basis of interdependence management. Teams are created to establish lateral links where there are complex interdependencies and Groupware applications have to be designed to support teams.

The goal of this shift of the attention, from a vertical perspective to a horizontal one, is to facilitate the integration of competencies needed for tasks execution owned by different organizational units. Groupware is addressed towards the support of group communication and computing, removing the barriers that make it time consuming and expensive, and supporting adequate patterns of members interactions. The Groupware applications become IT tools that transformate (following Zuboff's taxonomy [20]) the organizational models within G area, with the addition of new forms of coordination close to the traditional ones.

## 4. The application of the organizational analysis in GDSS design

In this section, the organizational requirements pointed out from previous investigations will be linked to the matter of designing and realizing effective GDSS. This section will present also concepts and results derived from a research project $[10]$ regarding the realization of a GDSS whose functioning reflects issues derived from the previous organizational analysis.

Our analysis indicated GDSS as tools for managing coordination within a group whose members accomplish tightly coupled tasks that involve complex reciprocal interdependencies. In this organizational context, GDSS are expected to deal with competence sharing, quality and time as parameters of interdependence.

\- The support to the sharing of competencies is required to permit group members to access requisite data and expertise from each other when accomplishing interrelated tasks, pointing out where specific competencies are kept, how communications can be established, etc.

\- The goal of the quality aspects analysis is to reveal which tasks are really interdependent, even if they are accomplished sequentially. A GDSS must indicate to group members the nature of these hidden links among tasks.

\- The time aspect concerns the capability of speeding up the pooling of competencies within the group, following (if necessary) parallel patterns of decision making within the same group.

Our analysis addresses the support offered by the technological tools toward the quick sharing of competencies within a group for managing coordination, solving problems and eliciting new knowledge. The sharing of competencies is obtained at an organizational level, and the technological tools just facilitate and speed up this process.

Besides pointing out the patterns of competencies sharing, a GDSS has to support the group members in outlining and exploiting all the relevant aspects that concern the specific task they are accomplishing. This means that a GDSS must be focused on a particular organizational context and that for its functioning it requires the embedding of organizational knowledge regarding that specific environment.

This knowledge is necessary to dynamically address the discussion, pointing out relevant issues, deciding which is the subset of group members to be involved in a meeting, etc.

In this perspective a technology-based system should offer the users a support in using its features (which one, when, etc.) in order to facilitate the decision making beyond the simple structuring and improvement of communications. The users do not exactly know which is the problem to be discussed (often it is ill-defined and continuously changing), the competencies to be used for obtaining a solution and the ordering for using them; the technological system facilitates the problem setting by pointing out the objects to be considered and the relations connecting them, accesses the stored experience concerning that topic, suggests (it does not impose) alternative patterns of decision.

The result of this increased capability to access and to pool competencies concerning a topic (obtained by analysing all the relevant issues and relationships regarding it) is given by a significant improvement of meeting outcomes.

These concepts concerning the definition of the role of GDSS have been embedded in an comprehensive architecture, that we tried to keep as much independent as possible from technological aspects and from the field of utilization. The architecture will be briefly presented, focusing the attention especially on the relations that have been established among requirements derived from the previous organizational analysis and the technological aspects. These requirements are the following.

\- The integration of system features has to be accomplished hiding as much as possible the technical characteristics of the system, in order to keep the users' attention on the problems and not on how to use the support tools.

\- The mechanisms for applying organizational knowledge must be separated from the knowledge itself.

\- The GDSS functioning has to be case-based. When the discussion concerning a problem starts, the system displays to the group members the stored organizational knowledge that is the “nearest” one to the considered topic, or indicates group members that are supposed to own the expertise in that discussion field.

In Fig. 5 an architecture founded on these requirements is roughly depicted. It is built on “traditional” GDSS features and contains a module (the Facilitator) that supports users in accessing applications.

The functioning of the architecture can be described by explaining how the Facilitator works as a simple mechanism of integration.

\- The Facilitator gets assertions concerning some events made by the users in a loosely structured language.

\- The Facilitator works as the server for communications within the group. The goal of the centralization of communication is to keep transparent to each user the location of system modules and of group members.

\- The functioning of the Facilitator is based on the organizational knowledge concerning the discussed topics. The knowledge about the patterns of decision making along a cluster of interdependence is represented by a set of rules and models that can be displayed to the users according to different views. The models contain all the relevant entities and relationships among them (representing interdependencies linkages) to be considered in managing coordination. The rules contain knowledge about actions to be accomplished and people to be involved (a skill knowledge base creates links between people and kinds of expertise). Each rule contains methods for activating people or software applications and for processing their answers. Rules and models accessed by the Facilitator do not explain what should be exactly done, but focus the group attention on which are the main topics to be investigated and, if possible, on their ordering. Rules are not normative, but they simply point out opportunities to be investigated or patterns of discussion derived from past experiences in dealing with some specific situations.

![](/api/attachments/K954Q4DK/fulltext/images/256b1e8dd53d19537cc27fa5a5036d8f669453ef128d133178417329fd1a36df.jpg)  
Fig. 5. The proposed GDSS architecture.

\- The available knowledge is accessed through a case-search module. This module checks which is the stored model whose characteristics are closer to the ones of the case discussed within the group.

\- Flexibility is not guaranteed if the set of existing rules and models is fixed. When the structure of the problem to be faced is not comprised among the existing rules and models, it is necessary for the users to update the models or to create new ones on the basis of their experience, of the occurred events, etc. This capability can be obtained by using modules for knowledge elicitation [6,12]. In this case the Facilitator accesses a Truth Maintenance Systems (TMS) to control if all the new assertions are consistent with the existing knowledge.

\- Group reasoning is accomplished according to a blackboard schema [5]. The group members can read and write on a common area (the blackboard) during their meetings. At the beginning of the discussion the blackboard contains suggestions indicated by case-search modules. In this common area during the discussion the users can sketch, on the basis of their own competence, the objects to be considered, their properties, the relations existing among them, etc.

The TMS checks the consistency of the new knowledge that is added on the blackboard. The blackboard reasoning schema makes it possible to support overlapped sub-groups working on different sub-problems contemporaneously. Each group has its blackboard and each group member can access more than a blackboard at the same time. This structure enables a parallel organizational computing within the same group.

From a theoretical perspective, this architecture has some important properties.

\- It avoids the centralization of decision processes and does not constraint group members in using the technological features. Its functioning is as much as possible independent from a specific group.

\- It keeps the separation among the mechanism of knowledge application, the knowledge bases and the modules containing support facilities.

\- The utilization of the system features can be adapted to the characteristics of the considered problem and organizational model. The same technological features can be applied in different ways by changing only the knowledge on how to use them, without redesigning them. A result of this separation is an increased reusability of the same features and a shortening of the development cycle of GDSS. But it is important to note that this is not an issue for software engineers only, but it involves also organizational designers.

The experience gained during the research suggests that from a technological perspective, this architecture can be implemented using available commercial software tools. Some proposals in this direction are the following:

\- Different technologies and software applications have to be layered within the architecture.

\- The required software environments, which must embed basic communication functions, are widely available.

\- The software applications concerning reasoning mechanisms, knowledge acquisition and retrieval, etc., are offered by many commercial packages, too.

\- The existing multi-tasking and network operating systems enable the effective functioning of the entire architecture, guaranteeing the required computational performances.

\- Graphic tools are useful to display to the users the organizational models and rules, and to facilitate the elicitation of their own knowledge.

## 5. Concluding remarks

In this paper we traced a simultaneous development of organizational models and of IT applications. Our work has been directed towards the definition of the opportunities that Groupware can create inside organizations and the areas for its application.

Following the perspective of organizational change, Groupware applications are described as tools that can support coordination processes when the complexity of interdependencies is so high that traditional approaches do not work.

In this sense, Groupware plays an active role inside organizations, being its contribution directed towards the support of the transformation and the innovation of organization models. We can underline a bi-directional relationship between organizational and technological development. Organizational models are changing and therefore they are inducing new requirements in terms of IT support; on the other side Groupware can offer to organizational development the required degree of support.

This analysis constitutes a theoretical basis for the proposal of an architecture for the design of effective GDSS and for the understanding of their role inside organizations.

Within this architecture the role of GDSS is focused on the capability (obtained by embedding organizational knowledge within the system) to support users in pointing out relevant variables for problem setting and to indicate how and when to use alternative system features.
