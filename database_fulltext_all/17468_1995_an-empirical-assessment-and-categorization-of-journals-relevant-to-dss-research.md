# An empirical assessment and categorization of journals relevant to DSS research

Clyde W. Holsapple ${}^{a,*}$ , Linda Ellis Johnson ${}^{b}$ , Herman Manakyan ${}^{c}$ , John Tanner ${}^{d}$

$^{a}$ University of Kentucky, Department of Decision Science and Information Systems, Lexington, KY 40506, USA

## Abstract

This paper reports on an objective empirical study of the decision support system literature for the purpose of assessing journals relevant to DSS research efforts. Here, we provide DSS researchers and those who evaluate them an up-to-date picture of the forums available for scholarly discourse and research. We conducted a citation analysis of the Decision Support Systems journal for the years 1985-1993 to obtain a citation base containing over 7,500 citations. The citation base is then used to (1) examine citation trends for books, proceedings, and journals; (2) determine a relative ranking of journals based on total citations, and (3) organize journals into categories. Guidelines are provided for utilizing the categorization.

Keywords: DSS research; Citation analysis; Journal rankings; DSS publications

## 1. Introduction

Within the broad and growing realm of business computing a concerted effort to characterize the field's intellectual structure has been unfolding. This characterization includes studies that identify influential contributors, prominent institutions, journal rankings and categories, significant articles and books, and widely used research methodologies and themes. A summary of such efforts appears in Holsapple et al. (1994a). A number of these topics remain to be examined in the major subfield of business computing concerned with decision support systems. In particular, there are no large scale up-to-date, objective studies of citation patterns in the decision support system (DSS) literature, even though it dates back almost a quarter of a century to the work of Gorry and Scott Morton (1971).

Previous studies of the DSS literature have identified major contributors and institutions with which they are affiliated (Eom and Lee, 1990), dichotomized sources of knowledge into research and practice (Elam et al., 1986), and identified journals which publish DSS research most frequently (Omar and Goodwin, 1991). Other studies have provided annotated bibliographies and overviews of particular types of DSS applications (Eom and Lee, 1990a,b). These articles provide various views into the unfolding nature of the DSS field. We contribute an added perspective with a substantial, objective, systematic study of DSS citation patterns. Such a citation analysis provides insights into the communication network DSS scholars have constructed for research and discourse.

DSS citation patterns include information about the relative citation frequency of journal articles, books, and conference proceedings, as well as citation patterns for individual journals and categories of journals. Results of this analysis are beneficial in a number of ways. First, they apprise DSS researchers of the most widely utilized journals in the field, ultimately helping a researcher choose appropriate forums for reading, for disseminating research, for inclusion in library collections, and so forth. Second, such a study is useful for documenting promotion, tenure, and merit review cases. By introducing relative rankings and impact categories of DSS related publications, it helps administrators, deans, and colleagues outside the DSS field gauge the quality of a DSS researcher's work as reflected in the relative importance of particular journals to the field. Third, it provides doctoral students in the business computing field an overview of what journals are important to monitor for DSS research (e.g., as a background for developing a DSS dissertation).

We begin with a description of the research methodology employed in ascertaining DSS citation patterns. Results are then presented. These include citation variations across years, a ranking of journals' importance to the DSS field in terms of how frequently they are cited by DSS researchers, and a categorization of journals in terms of their relative impacts on the field. We conclude with an assessment of the value and implications of these results.

## 2. Research methodology

Citation analysis methodology typically involves examining and tabulating references in a set of articles. This is considered an objective methodology if the researchers do not exercise judgement in selecting the articles. It is less objective if articles are ‘hand picked’ by the researchers themselves. On the other end of the spectrum, wholly subjective do not involve citation analyses at all, but rather surveys of academics' perceptions of journal quality. Our approach here was to use the reference list of every article published in Decision Support Systems from its origin in 1985 through the most recent publication year of 1993.

In selecting Decision Support Systems to define our base set of articles for citation analysis, we have chosen a journal that is (1) well-established-now in its tenth year of publication, (2) ranked among the top five journals in the broad business computing field according to a recent large scale citation analysis (Holsapple et al., 1994b), (3) ranked by a large margin as the leading journal publishing DSS research (Omar and Goodwin, 1991), and (4) has an editorial policy that focuses on decision support system research. Thus, we have a large base of representative, quality DSS articles for which no subjective judgements had to be made as to whether an article should or should not be included based on subject matter.

Whatever base is selected for a citation analysis, it is likely to contain many citations to other articles published in the same journal(s). This is especially the case if a base journal is well-established and its contents are highly regarded for their quality. For instance, for the years 1987–1991, Communications of the ACM was the most frequently cited journal by its articles, MIS Quarterly was the most frequently cited journal within its own pages, Journal of MIS was the third most frequently cited by its articles, and Information and Management garnered the fifth highest citation count within its own set of articles (Holsapple et al., 1993). In the present case, attempting to dilute such an effect by broadening the base of articles would introduce undesired subjectivity into this study, as we are aware of no other journal with such a pure focus on DSS research.

Following Holsapple et al. (1993), we identify the number of books, conference proceedings, and journals cited in each base article. In addition, the number of citations to each specific journal was tabulated. Dissertations were counted as books. Not included in the citation count were items such as technical reports that did not have a publisher, working papers, and personal correspondence. The data set was used to trace the relative frequency of book, journal, and conference proceeding citations across the years. Concentrating on the cited journals, a ranking based on total citations to each was produced. In addition, the citation counts can be used to organize the journals into categories reflecting the extent of their impacts on DSS research.

## 3. Results

For the time period 1985–1993, a total of 7,599 citations were collected. Of these 3,445 were journal articles from 500 distinct journals, 3,166 were book citations, and 988 were citations to conference proceedings. It is interesting to note that the 80-20 rule commonly noted in the library science literature holds true in this case.

This heuristic holds that 80% of the citations come from 20% of the journals. In the DSS case, 80% of 3,445 is 2,756 which is exactly the number of citations tabulated for the 100 most frequently cited journals! Table 1 summarizes our findings for each of the years studied.

## 3.2. Journal ranks based on total citations

Citations to articles from specific journals were identified to assess the relative importance of various journals contributing to the total body of knowledge in the DSS field. Table 2 shows the total number of citations to various journals cited by articles in Decision Support Systems during the nine-year time frame. Rather than list all 500 journals, we report only those that have been cited at least 25 times. The result is a ranking of what have been the 26 most important journals to the DSS field through 1993.

In 1991, Omar and Goodwin ranked 22 journals based on the frequency with which they have published DSS research. There are some interesting similarities between their study and the ranking reported here. First, 14 of the 22 journals identified by Omar and Goodwin, as frequently publishing DSS research, are ranked among the first 50 journals most frequently cited in Decision Support Systems. Additionally, there are several similarities in ranks (e.g., Decision Support Systems and MIS Quarterly are ranked first and fourth respectively in both studies, while Decision Sciences ranks 7th in our study and 6th in Omar and Goodwin's).

A simple ranking methodology such as the one in Table 2 does understate the relative importance of new journals in a field. For example, newer journals such as ORSA Journal on Computing (ranked 31st/19 citations) and Information Systems Research (ranked 38th/14 citations) are not among the top 26 journals. However, if we look only at data for 1990–1993 and calculate ranks, ORSA Journal on Computing moves up to 19th and Information Systems Research to 25th.

Another limitation of the ranking in Table 2 is that it tends to ignore journals for which only some niche area of the DSS field is of interest. For instance, the study of multiparticipant decision support systems has become a major niche area within the DSS field. Articles on group decision support, organization decision support, and negotiation support appear in such journals as The Information Society, Journal of Organizational Computing, Group Decision and Negotiation, and Computer Supported Cooperative Work. However, they do not appear high in the rankings because they generally do not have an interest in DSS articles outside of the niche.

## 3.3. Journal impact categories

It is instructive to explore the possibility of organizing journals into categories. Categories provide an alternative means of making relative comparisons and may be more meaningful than a simple ranking. Categories of journals are useful for other research purposes (e.g., see Manakyan and Tanner (1991)) and for more clearly assessing a journal's impact on the field. As example of the latter, it may happen that a dean or department head is unfamiliar with a particular journal. Providing evidence that it is in the same category as another with which there is familiarity can be useful in anchoring the administrator's impression and fairly assessing a researcher's contribution. Categorization can establish whether one journal is roughly on a par with another.

It is quite common in business disciplines to have three journal categories (see e.g., Manakyan and Tanner (1991); Tanner et al. (1992)). We identify ten categories initially, but then collapse these into three categories. The latter are characterized as comprising journals that have had high impact on research in the DSS field, versus a moderate impact, versus a low impact. One approach to deriving categories is to look for natural breaks in the overall citation data. Yet another is to develop and consistently apply a rule to the data. This second possibility allows replicability in subsequent years by follow-on studies.

Although we have chosen to use the second approach here, the rule we apply results in categories that appear to conform to breaks in the data for the journals ranked in Table 2. The rule is as follows. Every journal in a category must have at least three-fourths as many citations as the category's most frequently cited journal. ## 4. Conclusion

Every DSS researcher and educator is faced with the problem of how to allocate a limited amount of time to the task of monitoring current journal publications. As our study shows there is a multitude of journals containing articles relevant to the DSS field. The ranking in Table 2 or the categorization in Tables 3–5 may be regarded a guides in prioritizing monitoring efforts. A caveat is that use of these guides should be supplemented with the inclusion of relatively new journals as well as specialized niche journals reflecting a researcher's area of focus within the DSS field.

A researcher in the DSS field naturally seeks to place his or her article manuscripts so as to maximize their potential impact on the field. Here again, the ranking and categorization we have derived can be beneficial. They provide an objective basis for targeting manuscript submissions with respect to an impact criterion. Of course, other criteria come into play such as fit with editorial objectives, submission processing time, and perceived prominence of the journal outside the DSS (or business computing) realm.

Deans, department heads, and others involved in the assessment of DSS researchers are frequently unfamiliar with or only partially informed about leading journals for publishing DSS research. Such assessments lie at the core of important merit review, promotion, and tenure decisions. A common ‘remedy’ is to rely on subjective opinions of other faculty and of external reviewers for judging the calibre of a DSS researcher’s journal placements. The journal ranking and categorization introduced here help round out the remedy by way of a complementary objective perspective on journal caliber from a DSS point of view.
