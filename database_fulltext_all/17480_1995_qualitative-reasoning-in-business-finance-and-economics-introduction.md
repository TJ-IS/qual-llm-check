# Qualitative reasoning in business, finance, and economics: Introduction

Walter Hamscher $^{a,*}$ , Melody Y. Kiang $^{b}$ , Reiner Lang $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Price Waterhouse UP, 160 Federal Street, Boston, MA 02110, USA $^{b}$ Department of Decision and Information Systems, College of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287-4206 USA $^{c}$ Institute of Business Informatics in Operations Research, Free University of Berlin, Berlin, 14195 Germany

## Abstract

This special volume of Decision Support Systems includes six papers on Qualitative Reasoning in Business, Finance, and Economics. These papers represent a growing interest in using the techniques of Qualitative Reasoning – an approach to formulating and solving physics and engineering problems which has undergone much recent development within the field of Artificial Intelligence – in intelligent decision support systems. Readers of this journal need no introduction to the issues in decision support systems in general, but a short introduction to the field of Qualitative Reasoning (QR) from the perspective of Artificial Intelligence is a useful prologue to the papers themselves. This introduction is necessarily the personal view of the editors, an addition to the many views of QR that have been published and debated elsewhere $[2,4,5,7,15,16]$

Keywords: Qualitative reasoning; Decision support systems; Qualitative simulation; Qualitative process theory

There is obviously a vast wealth of mathematical techniques for solving problems once they have been modeled as equations. The difficulty is in formulating those models and interpreting the results of analysis: mapping real-world problems having ambiguity and imprecision into equations, and translating results expressed in the language of the formulation back into common sense terms. To use an example (perhaps two) familiar to the QR research community, suppose we want a program to predict what will happen if a pot of water is heated on a stove. Most people can understand and answer this problem easily: the water would heat up to the boiling point, boil away into steam, and then the pot itself would get hotter and hotter until it was as hot as the burner. Among the goals of the QR community was to have computer programs that could do this too. Now, traditional mathematics would have us specify this problem as differential equations relating the temperatures of the water and burner to the volume of water, its specific heat, and so forth. Solving the equations is trivial – easier, in fact, for a program than for a human – given initial conditions, heat transfer coefficients and the like. There are some problems, however. How did we know what variables to include in the model and which effects to neglect? Why do the ambient temperature of the air, the height of the pot above the burner, or the height above sea level not appear in our equations? What values should we use for those initial conditions such as the volume of water? Humans can make this prediction with confidence even if given only qualitative information about these numbers, such as that the burner temperature is greater than the boiling point, which in turn is greater than the initial temperature of the water. Finally, how does one derive from those differential equations the fact that there are three distinct regions of behaviour, in which the water is heating up, then boiling away, and then in which there is no water at all? Without the transitions between regions of behaviour it is all too easy to construct valid equations which yield absurd results, but it is of little use to specify the regions and the ordering of transitions in advance, since that is part of what we wanted the program to produce for us.

The essence of QR is the recognition that programs which solve such problems must be able to accept and derive useful inferences from problem statements having much less information than is usually known in a traditional mathematical formulation. In the stove example, the program should be able to infer that the pot and water will pass through those three behaviour regions while knowing only the relative magnitudes of, and approximate functional relationships between, the physical quantities involved. In the current volume of papers, the problems of interest are those which arise not in the physical world but in finance and economics, where precise quantitative information is not always available, so that qualitative techniques appear to be an appropriate or even a necessary approach.

When choosing the appropriate level of detail and precision in model specification, the issue of tradeoffs arises. In our case, the fundamental trade-off is in the use of quantitative detail versus the effort required to specify the qualitative description of a domain. The answer depends on many factors, including the nature of the decision itself. As scholars in many disciplines are interested in knowing how to best present information to decision makers, the cost-benefit issues of human decision making process have been extensively examined. Information representation studies attempt to determine which form of information presentation is most ‘effective’ and ‘efficient’ for the human decision-making process. For example, data with only ordinal properties may be easier to process under certain circumstances than numerical tables or figures. The qualitative abstraction mechanism adopted by qualitative reasoning approaches matches well with the simplifying decision rules and information format proposed by many researchers. The qualitative abstraction mechanism, which filters and reformats the information presented to the decision makers, works like a screening tool that will substantially reduce the effort in complicated decision making processes. When precise numerical information is available, quantitative analysis is still considered the most appropriate and efficient reasoning method to apply. However, most of the time, complete numerical information about the problem under study is not available at the time of analysis. The possible reasons for not having accurate numerical information are: (1) there is not enough numerical information available to formulate a quantitative model (i.e., incomplete knowledge), (2) the exact form of that function is not known with certainty (the underlying theory is not precise enough to support accurate quantitative models (i.e., imprecise knowledge)), and (3) the information about the exact form of that function is not needed for the type of analysis to be carried out. Business and economics problems, because of their intrinsic complexity, generally lack complete quantitative models and thus offer particularly suitable application areas for qualitative modelling.

One of the techniques for qualitative analysis is qualitative simulation, a term usually associated with its widely used implementation in the QSIM program [10]. In traditional numerical simulation, a set of differential equations over continuous variables is translated into a set of difference equations; each variable is assigned a real number and the behaviour of the system evolves from one time-step to the next time-step according to numerical computations. In qualitative simulation, the differential equations are translated into a set of constraints over interval-valued variables;

each variable is assigned an interval, and because of the ambiguity thus introduced, the behaviour of the system branches to encompass all of the possible evolutions of the system. The first paper in this volume, 'Qualitative and Quantitative Simulation of Interacting Markets' by Wyatt, Leitch and Steele, presents an example of qualitative simulation of interacting markets in housing and mortgages, comparing and contrasting it to numerical simulation and other techniques. This paper provides a good illustration of the technique and is a good introduction to a fundamental – but frequently misunderstood – issue in QR, namely, that once precise numerical information is lost then the results of analysis are ambiguous. The key point is that qualitative simulation can be useful even in contexts where the numerical information was not even there to be 'lost' in the first place.

One context in which numerical information is typically not available is in automated design. For example, consider an elaboration of the stove example: suppose the program is supposed to design mechanisms involving burners, pots, water, steam, and so forth. Although the physical principles are the same as in the simulation example above, the quantitative equations are of little use until one has a parameterized design, at which point those equations can be used to evaluate whether the design achieves its goal. By contrast, qualitative simulation can be used to evaluate a whole class of designs having different parameter values; the ability to deal with underspecified situations is crucial for this task. The second paper in this volume, 'Controlling the Complexity of Investment Decisions using Qualitative Reasoning Techniques' by Benaroch and Dhar, is an example of how qualitative reasoning can be exploited in the task of designing financial instruments. The power of qualitative reasoning is further illustrated via a technique the authors call qualitative synthesis in which functional forms are manipulated and reasoned about in order to achieve particular design goals.

Diagnosis is another task in which numerical information may not be available. Findings of empirical research in understanding human diagnostic reasoning processes [3] suggest that experts make decisions using qualitative and causal reasoning. A system built on the qualitative modelling approach can not only be able to render a final answer to the user but also provide an explanatory path, a cause-and-effect picture of the organization's problems. The third paper, 'Market Share Analysis and Prognosis using Qualitative Reasoning' by Alpar and Dilger presents a formulation of qualitative interactions within and among markets derived from qualitative process theory [8]. In Forbus' approach, models are composed of processes (for example, 'heat transfer' and 'boiling' are processes), each of which has an associated set of qualitative variables, and the analysis proceeds by reasoning about the interactions among the processes via their shared variables. Qualitative process theory is distinguished from the QSIM approach in that it explicitly treats the initiation and termination of processes. Alpar and Dilger show how their process-centred formulation of market dynamics can be used both for diagnosis and prognosis.

As noted earlier, reasoning with incomplete quantitative information typically leads to ambiguous results. One approach to solve the problem is to combine qualitative and quantitative methods to allow stronger predictions than using qualitative reasoning alone while avoiding the assumptions required for a numerical simulation. Several quantitative methods, including probabilistic [11], interval [1,9], discrete time simulation [13], and fuzzy approaches [12], have been suggested by researchers to be coupled with qualitative reasoning methods to alleviate the problems of pure qualitative reasoning methods. Each of the suggested quantitative methods has its own strengths and weaknesses that are inherent in their underlying algorithms. A recent theme in QR literature has been the use of probabilistic information to discriminate between likely and unlikely outcomes [6], for example through the use of fuzzy sets [17] to represent the distribution of likely values for each variable. The fourth paper in this volume, ‘Liquidity Management with Fuzzy Qualitative Constraints’ presents a novel approach to optimization using a representation incorporating probabilistic information about qualitatively distinct values.

Finally, explanation is a task closely related to diagnosis for which it is often useful to suppress quantitative information even when it is available. For example, suppose we want the program to produce a narrative explaining why the water in the pot boils away. The differential equations and their numeric values by themselves provide little insight. The technique of causal ordering, pioneered by [14], offers a way of analysing sets of equations in a way that groups the variables into blocks and then specifies how changes in the values of one block of variables may be understood to cause changes in the values of variables in other blocks. Causal ordering is a technique which can exploit qualitative relationships to explain perturbations. In ‘Causal Ordering in Economic Models,’ Berndsen presents some alternative approaches to causal ordering and demonstrates how conclusions can be drawn even using only the signs of causal influences among variables. In the last paper, ‘Causal Reasoning in Econometric Models’ by Lin and Farley, a detailed example showing the power of the causal ordering approach with Keynesian macro-econometric models.

Qualitative reasoning is an exciting and active field of study in the field of artificial intelligence because of its potential to enable computers to reason about physical situations and to communicate effectively with humans about those situations. The preliminary results of applying qualitative simulation techniques to business, finance, and economics have been encouraging. However, current qualitative reasoning approaches work well only in simplified problem domains. When the size and/or the complexity of the problem increases, the system usually encounters the intractable branching problem or renders uninteresting results (i.e., the results are too general for the decision maker to draw any useful conclusion). In business settings, the information available at the time we need to make a decision is usually incomplete, and therefore the qualitative reasoning method may be more suitable than traditional quantitative methods to build a decision-making model. More research needs to be done in order to develop decision-making tools that can be used for analysing large-scale (realistic) problems. We hope that the current collection of papers, representing among them applications of the most advanced QR techniques to financial and economic domains, will excite your interest as well.
