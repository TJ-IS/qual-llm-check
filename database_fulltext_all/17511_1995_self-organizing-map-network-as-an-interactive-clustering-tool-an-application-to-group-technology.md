# Self-organizing map network as an interactive clustering tool – An application to group technology

Melody Y. Kiang $^{a,1,*}$ , Uday R. Kulkarni $^{a}$ , Kar Yan Tam $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Decision and Information Systems, College of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287, USA $^{b}$ Department of Business Information Systems, School of Business and Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## Abstract

The Self-Organizing Map (SOM) network, a variation of neural computing networks, is a categorization network developed by Kohonen. The theory of the SOM network is motivated by the observation of the operation of the brain. This paper presents the technique of SOM and shows how it may be applied as a clustering tool to group technology. A computer program for implementing the SOM neural networks is developed and the results are compared with other clustering approaches used in group technology. The study demonstrates the potential of using the Self-Organizing Map as the clustering tool for part family formation in group technology.

Keywords: Kohonen; Self-organizing map; Competitive learning; Neural networks; Clustering analysis; Part family formation; Group technology

## 1. Introduction

The self-organizing map network is one of the most important neural network architectures developed during the time between the Adaline network of the early 1960's and the Hopfield network of 1982 [30]. Unlike other neural network approaches, the SOM network performs unsupervised training, that is, during the learning (training) process the processing units in the network adjust their weights primarily based on the lateral feedback connections. The more common approach to neural networks requires supervised training of the network, i.e., the network is fed with a set of training cases and the generated output is compared with the known correct output. Deviations from the correct output result in adjustment of the weights attached to the processing units. Such networks are said to be trained (attain equilibrium) when they work satisfactorily for the test cases. The SOM network, on the other hand, does not require the knowledge of the corresponding outputs. The nodes in the network converge to form clusters to represent groups of entities with similar properties. The number and composition of clusters can be visually determined based on the output distribution generated by the training process.

Clustering for group technology (GT) is an important issue in manufacturing, however it is also a very complex problem. This problem involves grouping of similar parts into part families to be manufactured on similar machine tools. GT research has tended to focus on commonality of machine resource requirements as the sole criterion for forming of part families. This is based on the assumption that there is a one-to-one mapping between machines and operations. However, today's more advanced and versatile production machinery can perform more than one related operation. Hence, although grouping based on part-machine data can reduce routing time between manufacturing cells, if parts within a group have very different operation sequences, the benefits of grouping may not be fully realized. For example, consider two parts, $P_{1}$ and $P_{2}$ , requiring three identical operations in opposite sequences (e.g., $P_{1} = "ABC"$ , $P_{2} = "CBA"$ ). Assuming that a single machine can perform all three operations, it would take at least five set-ups to manufacture the two parts compared with only three set-ups, if the operation sequences of the two parts were the same. This important issue has been discussed in Tam [34].

In this paper, we introduce the self-organizing map network and illustrate its application to group technology. This method serves as a clustering tool that can group parts into appropriate categories based on the similarities in the characteristics of a given set of parts. The similarity measure that we use for grouping parts into families can be comprised of multiple criteria, e.g., commonality in machine resource requirements, similarity in the patterns of operation sequences. Each part has a similarity associated with every other part. While grouping n parts into families, one has to consider the similarity of every part with respect to the other n - 1 parts simultaneously, which makes this a multi-dimensional (n - 1 dimensions) problem. SOM networks are especially good at accepting such multi-dimensional input and transforming it into a map of fewer dimensions, such as a 2-dimensional plot. This map can then be used by a decision-maker to visually cluster parts into families.

The rules for clustering parts can be difficult to define, and the problem is highly subjective. SOM offers the flexibility to decide the composition and the number of clusters based on other objective and subjective criteria, such as availability of machinery, capital, volume demand, etc. It can be used as a decision support tool to interactively generate clustering designs for designing cellular manufacturing systems. Another advantage of the SOM networks (or, neural networks in general) is that, once trained, they can be easily used for classification of new entities into existing clusters; the whole clustering process does not have to be repeated. Thus, if there are new parts to be manufactured, or if there are minor changes in the design or processing requirements of existing parts, the SOM can quickly assign such parts to appropriate groups. Part groupings can thus be continually adjusted in response to the short-term changes in the business environment.

The balance of this paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we briefly discuss the variety of approaches used to address the problem of group formation in GT. Section 3 presents the basic concepts of self-organizing map networks. Section 4 illustrates the use of SOM as a clustering tool for forming part families. Section 5 presents the experimental procedures and the test results. Section 6 describes how SOM can be used as an interactive decision-support tool. Section 7 concludes the paper.

## 2. Group technology and clustering analysis

The well known need to reduce routing time, set-up time, and increase machine utilization led to the development of group technology. The introduction of group technology for manufacturing is attributed to Mitrofanov [25]. "He found that considerable reductions in setting time and therefore increases in capacity, could be achieved... if similar parts were loaded on the machines one after the other" [2]. The concept of group technology is to identify groups of machines that could produce parts with similar manufacturing processing requirements. This is also the first step in the design of cellular manufacturing systems (CMS). According to Gallagher and Knight [10], "The crux of the problem of introducing group technology is the identification, from the large variety and total number of components, of the families requiring similar manufacturing operations on similar machine tools...”

Early efforts to group parts and machines date back to the seminal work by Burbidge [1], in which a multistage procedure – called production flow analysis (PFA) – was proposed to provide a total solution for group technology. In production flow analysis, machines are classified into different types according to the operations they can perform. Groups are identified through an iterative process involving the swapping of rows and columns of a part-machine matrix. An advanced approach to production flow analysis, called rank order clustering (ROC), developed by King [15], treats the rows and columns of the part-machine matrix as binary words. Alternating between the rows and the columns, the binary words are reordered until both the rows and the columns appear in descending numerical order. This approach provides a heuristic which is guaranteed to arrive at a solution. Unlike Burbidge's method, rank order clustering does not require an exhaustive search of the problem space. However, the ROC algorithm is inefficient for some large problems, since the size of the binary value of a row or column can easily exceed the magnitude which can be represented in a 16-bit computer word. King and Nakornchai [16] revised ROC; the new algorithm, ROC2, while capable of solving problems of any size, does so at the expense of computational efficiency. Rank order clustering has been shown to be capable of identifying exceptional elements and bottleneck machines. A robust PFA technique, called Cluster Identification Algorithm by Kusiak and Chow [20], is arguably the most efficient technique for identifying groups.

In 1972, John McAuley challenged Burbidge's claims that PFA offered a solution to the problems associated with the introduction of group technology [24]. McAuley cites two problems with PFA: (1) The lack of a metric for judging the goodness of one grouping against other possible solutions, and (2) The lack of an efficient algorithm for finding groups of parts. As an alternative to PFA, McAuley applied the techniques of numerical taxonomy popularized by Sokal and

Sneath [33]. McAuley's approach consists of a three-stage procedure: (1) Computation of similarity coefficients for all pairs of machines, (2) Performing cluster analysis, and (3) Assigning parts to groups. The single linkage clustering algorithm (known as SLCA or SLINK) was used to perform the cluster analysis.

Several approaches to the group technology problem are derived from the principles of numerical taxonomy. Carrie's [3] research expands on McAuley's original efforts. Mosier [28] proposes a weighted part-machine matrix instead of the binary matrix presented by McAuley. This approach opens the analysis to considerations of the relative importance of products and therefore offers a mechanism for incorporating an enterprise's market objectives. Gupta [11] provide a succinct examination of clustering algorithms. The result of their analysis shows that McAuley's use of the single linkage clustering algorithm is prone to a problem known as chaining, in which a few very large groups are formed and several very small groups remain, leading to a generally poor solution. DeWitte [9] proposes a set-theoretic approach to clustering. Machine types are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary, allowing the problem to be broken down into smaller problems.

The techniques applied to the group analysis problem have been largely derived either from Burbidge's work in production flow analysis or McAuley's application of numerical taxonomy. In short, the benefit of production flow analysis is that it provides a quick and straight-forward technique for identifying groups; its weakness is that it does not provide a computational model capable of factoring in the many variables associated with group technology. The strength of the application of numerical taxonomy to the group technology problem is that it is a quantitative model. Furthermore, it is extensible, and therefore it is capable of expressing the effects of the many variables associated with group technology. The weakness is that as more variables are introduced to the model, the multiple dimensions of the problem space are increasingly unmanageable, and computationally expensive.

In summary, there are a number of issues associated with group technology; a few are listed as follows: (1) Selection of clustering algorithm and criteria, (2) Efficiency of the grouping process, (3) Measures of performance, (4) Consideration of exceptional elements, (5) Consideration of bottleneck machines, (6) Consideration of process sequence and tooling requirements, (7) Consideration of production volume, and (8) The need for subjective analysis. To date, most approaches focus on only a subset of these variables.

Recently, a variety of new approaches have been applied to the group technology problem. Ranging from fuzzy clustering [6] and pattern recognition [41] to kth Nearest Neighbour (kNN) analysis [34] and even a complete knowledge-based architecture [21], the application of artificial intelligence and expert systems to the group technology problem is quickly gaining momentum. A few researchers have also investigated the application of neural networks to GT [5,14]. Neural networks have the ability to learn by recognizing patterns and memorizing special features associated with input data. This learning ability makes them flexible and adaptive. In the case of GT, patterns found in data, such as part-features, part-machine associations, and operation-sequences can be recognized by neural networks.

Moon [26] introduced a simplified form of interactive activation and competitive network, a neural network-like approach, to GT. It uses a constraint satisfaction algorithm to form part families and machine cells starting with the part-machine matrix. The weights attached to the processing units are initially calculated according to the relationship, and are unchanged throughout the clustering process. Moon and Chi [27] extended the method of calculation of similarity coefficients proposed in Moon [26] to potentially include other manufacturing information, but used the same network. Chu [5], Kaparthi and Suresh [12], Kaparthi et. al., [13], and Malave and Ramachandran [23] used different variations of competitive learning networks which are all based on unsupervised learning approach. These authors also use the part-machine matrix as input. These are fairly simple networks with one-dimensional input and output layers. This type of network learns by adjusting the weights of connections between the nodes of the input and output layers to correctly classify the parts into the appropriate cluster. The number of clusters has to be pre-specified by the decision-maker; the network has to be re-trained if a different number of clusters is desired. The authors have demonstrated that this is a simple yet effective approach for well-structured data, i.e., data sets with no bottleneck machines or exceptional elements.

In the next section, we present the self-organizing map (SOM) network for forming part families. The architecture and the learning algorithm of this network is quite different compared with the neural network approaches mentioned above. This comparison is presented at the end of the section. Moreover, the SOM network offers other qualitative advantages, such as flexibility in clustering leading to its use in a decision support environment. This aspect of SOM is discussed in section 6.

## 3. Self-organizing mapping

Neural networks are especially suited for simulating intelligence in pattern-recognition, prediction, and classification activities $[19,31]$ . They have been successfully employed in applications requiring diagnosis, detection, and forecasting $[35,37,38]$ . A neural network consists of a set of interconnected nodes, also called neurons or processing elements. The network is divided into layers – the input layer, the hidden layer(s), and the output layer. In a feed-forward multi-layered network, a common network architecture, the input layer accepts the input signal which is propagated through the hidden layer(s) to the output layer. The signal is carried along the connections from node to node, such that the output of a node becomes the input to other nodes to which it is connected. One of the important elements in a neural network is the strength of connection between two nodes. These connection strengths determine the relationship between the input and the output of the network and thus, in a way, represent the knowledge stored in the network. Neural networks acquire this knowledge through a process of training during which the connection strengths between the nodes are modified. Once trained, the neural network retains this knowledge and it can be used for the particular task it was designed to do.

There are two primary categories of neural networks: those which perform supervised learning and those which perform unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, the network acquires knowledge by comparing the generated output with the known correct output. A set of training cases consisting of input data and corresponding output has to be available. The connection strengths are adjusted to tune the network over a number of training cycles. An example of supervised learning is a network being trained to recognize handwritten characters $[36]$ . On the other hand, in unsupervised learning, the network does not require the knowledge of corresponding output for comparison and learning. Input data presented to the network consists of the complex characteristics of the entities of interest. Through repeated training cycles the learning algorithm adjusts the connection strengths and causes the network to represent a simplified feature map of the characteristics from which meaningful information can be identified.

In order to economically represent data in the brain, research in biological information systems has speculated that human information processing utilizes methods to compress information by forming reduced representations of the most relevant facts, without the loss of the topological relations [17]. This process is mimicked by the SOM network [18] by creating a two-dimensional map of the input data categorizing similar objects in such a way that the orders of the interrelationships among objects are preserved. Teuvo Kohonen developed the SOM network between 1979 and 1982 based on the earlier work of Willshaw and von der Malsburg [39]. The SOM network performs unsupervised learning; its primary use is for tasks such as clustering, pattern recognition, and various optimization problems [4,29]. It is designed to capture topologies and hierarchical structures of higher dimensional input spaces.

The SOM network typically has two layers of nodes, the input layer and the Kohonen layer. The input layer is fully connected to a two-dimensional Kohonen layer. The Kohonen layer, the core of the SOM network, functions similar to biological systems in that it can compress the representation of sparse data and spread out dense data using a two-dimensional map. This is done by assigning different sub-areas of the Kohonen layer to the different categories of information, therefore the location of the processing element in a network becomes specific to a certain characteristic feature in the set of input data. The resulting network resembles the tree structures that can be derived by conventional clustering methods [18].

During the training process, input data are fed to the network through the processing elements (nodes) in the input layer. All the nodes in the input layer are fully connected to the nodes in the Kohonen layer. Depending on the nature of the problem under analysis, the input pattern can consist of one or more values, each fed to one of the input nodes. The M-valued input pattern can be denoted by a vector of order M as:

$$
\mathbf {x} = \left(x _ {1}, x _ {2}, \dots , x _ {m}\right)
$$

There is one input pattern for each entity of interest.

Each node in the Kohonen layer has a weight vector with M weight values associated with it. The M weight values correspond to the M values of an input pattern. Assuming that there are N nodes in the Kohonen layer, the weight vector of a node i can be denoted by:

$$
w _ {i} = \left(w _ {i 1}, w _ {i 2},..., w _ {i m}\right)
$$

where $w_{ij}$ is the weight associated with node i corresponding to the input node j. During the initialization process of the training cycle, the weight values are randomly assigned to the nodes in the Kohonen layer. As the training process proceeds, the nodes adjust their weight values according to the topological relations in the input data. The Kohonen layer determines the winning node by measuring the distance of its weight vectors from the input vectors, for each input pattern. The node with the minimum distance is the winner and adjusts its weights to be closer to the value of the input pattern. The most common way of measuring the distance between a node in the Kohonen layer and an input pattern is the Euclidean distance measure ( $D_{i}$ ) given by:

$$
D _ {i} = \sqrt {\left(x _ {1} - w _ {i 1}\right) ^ {2} + \left(x _ {2} - w _ {i 2}\right) ^ {2} + \dots + \left(x _ {m} - w _ {i m}\right) ^ {2}} \dots \dots\tag{1}
$$

To avoid one node ending up representing too much of the input data due to the effect of the initial random values assigned to it, the “conscience” mechanism introduced by DeSieno [8] is added to bias the distance measurement. The bias $(B_{i})$ is formulated as:

$$
\mathbf {B} _ {\mathrm{i}} = \gamma \left(\frac {1}{\mathbf {N}} - \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{i}}\right)
$$

Where $\gamma$ starts at a large value and then decrease over time. The recommended values for $\gamma$ are any number between 2.0 and 10.0 [30]. N is the number of nodes in the Kohonen layer and $F_{i}$ is assigned the value $\frac{1}{N}$ at initialization, thus initially $B_{i}=0$ . $F_{i}$ represents the winning frequency of node i and it is updated constantly during the training cycle. The adjustment of the distance is proportional to the difference between the average and the node winning frequency. In this research we use the formulas suggested in the NeuralWare [30] to update the value of $F_{i}$ .

For the winning node i at time $t + 1$ : $F_{i,t+1} = F_{i,t} + \beta (1.0 - F_{i,t})$

For all other Kohonen nodes: $F_{i,t+1} = F_{i,t} + \beta$ $(0.0 - F_{i,t})$

Therefore the modified distance can be represented as:

$$
\mathbf {D} _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {\prime} = \mathbf {D} _ {\mathrm{i}} - \mathbf {B} _ {\mathrm{i}}
$$

And $D_{i}^{\prime}$ is the value that will be used to compute the winning node. Based on the above frequency function, if a node has higher winning frequency than average its distance will be adjusted to a higher value to prevent it from winning again. On the other hand, the distance of a node with low winning frequency will be adjusted to a lower value to make it more likely to win. The purpose of the conscience mechanism is to help each node in the Kohonen layer to represent approximately equal information about the input data.

During training, the weights of the winning node are adjusted based on the following formula:

$$
\mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{ij}, \mathrm{t} + 1} = \mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{ij}, \mathrm{t}} + \alpha (\mathbf {X} _ {\mathrm{j}} - \mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{ij}, \mathrm{t}})
$$

Where the learning coefficient $\alpha$ is a slowly decreasing function of time to guarantee convergence. In order to preserve the order of the input space, the neighbours of the winning node also need to adjust their weights to be closer to the same input data. Previous research has shown that it is preferable to start with a wider neighbourhood and slowly shrink the size of the neighbourhood over time. Similar to the problem of deciding the appropriate number of hidden units for a multi-layered network, a proper choice for $\alpha$ and the size of the neighbourhood is also problem specific and can only be determined by experience [18]. At the end of the training process, the resulting Kohonen layer will contain the topological relations of the input data while performing a dimensionality reduction of the representation space. In other words, the network is able to project a multi-dimensional pattern onto a two-dimensional space. Therefore, if there are clusters in the higher dimensional representation, they will be projected as clusters in the two-dimensional Kohonen layer as well. The location of the nodes in the network only shows the ordinal relation of the input data; the actual distance between inputs is calculated by a weighted sum of primary signals [18].

Compared to the earlier neural networks used in GT, the architecture of SOM is more complex in that the output layer is two-dimensional and therefore has a much larger number of nodes than that of the competitive learning network. The learning algorithm is also more complex as the weights of nodes in the neighbourhood of the winning node are also adjusted with every iteration. By doing so, the physical locations of the nodes in the SOM output layer become meaningful. After a network is fully trained, the nodes that are close to each other represent entities with similar characteristics. Therefore, different areas in the output layer represent entities with different characteristics. In contrast, in the competitive learning network, the output layer nodes are independent of each other and each node represents one cluster. Thus, the number of nodes in the output layer is determined by the desired number of clusters which needs to be pre-specified. The output of SOM is a two-dimensional map that depicts the similarities between entities to be clustered, from which visual groupings can be interactively formed by a decision-maker. Whereas, the output of the competitive learning network is a list of entities in each cluster.

For this research, a system written in $C^{++}$ was developed to implement the self-organizing map network [14]. Each processing unit in the network is implemented as an object in the program and the calculation is performed through the message passing between objects. The algorithm of the computer program is mainly based on the previous work of Kohonen [18]. Some detailed implementation issues are designed in accordance with the suggestions provided in NeuralWare [30] and will be discussed in the next section.

## 4. Self-organizing map network for clustering of parts

Successful grouping of parts into families requires a similarity measure that represents essential features of a part and its relation to other parts in terms of production operations. One such measure is the commonality of machine requirements given by a part-machine incidence matrix [15,16]. This information can be used to group parts that require similar machinery resources so that they can be manufactured in self-sufficient cells. It is evident that this technique can reduce routing time between cells. All the neural network approaches mentioned in section 2 use the binary (0, 1) part-machine instance matrix as input to form the part families. However, part similarity has another dimension; it is the similarity between the patterns of sequences of operations performed to manufacture those parts. With advanced production machinery which performs more than one operation, interleaving between identical operations of different parts can increase production efficiency. Interleaving is feasible only when the patterns of operation sequences of parts within a cell are similar. This calls for an ability on the part of the designer to recognize such patterns [7].

## 4.1. The similarity measure

We use the operation sequence based similarity coefficient developed by Tam [34] for part clustering. This similarity measure is derived from Levenshtein's distance measure $(d_{n})$ between two patterns [22] and also takes into account the commonality $(d_{c})$ of operations between parts. Both $d_{n}$ and $d_{c}$ have a range of [0, 1]. $d_{n}$ is the normalized transformation coefficient between any two parts. It is defined as the smallest number of transformations required to derive the operation sequence of one part from that of the other part, divided by the maximum number of transformations among all pairs of parts. A transformation can be a substitution, a deletion, or an insertion of an operation. For example, derivation a sequence “ABFDHG” from a sequence “ADHI” requires a minimum of three transformations, e.g., insertion of B and F after A and substitution of G for I. The algorithm for determining the minimum number of transformations required to derive one operation sequence from another is given in [34]. The lower the values of $d_{n}$ , the more similar are the operation sequences of the parts. $d_{c}$ is a coefficient representing the commonality of operations between two parts. It is defined as the number of common operations between the parts divided by their total number of distinct operations. The higher the values of $d_{c}$ , the more similar are the machinery requirements of the two parts. The overall similarity coefficient is a function f of $d_{n}$ and $d_{c}$ . An example of this function is the weighted average of $d_{n}$ and $(1 - d_{c})$ :

$$
\mathrm{f} (d _ {n}, d _ {c}) = \mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{n}} d _ {n} + \mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{c}} (1 - d _ {c})
$$

where

$$
\mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{n}} + \mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{c}} = 1 \text { and } \mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{n}}, \mathbf {W} _ {\mathrm{c}} \geq 0
$$

In the above function, the complement $(1 - d_{n})$ is used, instead of $d_{c}$ , to be consistent with $d_{n}$ . The similarity coefficient has the range [0,1] where the value 0 represents identical parts and the value 1 represents completely dissimilar parts.

$W_{n}$ and $W_{c}$ are the weights attached to the similarity of operation sequences and commonality of operations, respectively. By making an appropriate choice of $W_{n}$ and $W_{c}$ values, the decision-maker reaches a compromise between a possible increase in the throughput of the system to be designed and the cost of the machinery. If the machinery resources are inexpensive and the availability of machines does not seriously constrain the formation of manufacturing cells, then the goal would be to group together parts that have similar operation sequences. This can increase the throughput by allowing a machine to interleave between identical operations of different parts. On the other hand, if the machinery resources are scarce and/or expensive, the goal would be to shift the grouping criteria toward commonality of operations by assigning a larger value to $W_{c}$ . This would reduce the cost and effort of forming manufacturing cells, although the order of the operations of the parts to be manufactured in a cell may be very different, resulting in increased set-up times.

It is possible to generalize the transformation coefficient, $d_{n}$ , by assigning different weights to different transformations, such as $W_{s}$ , $W_{i}$ , and $W_{d}$ , for substitution, insertion, and deletion, respectively. This can allow a decision-maker to incorporate situation-specific criteria in the grouping of parts. For example, a lower weight for substitution operation will encourage parts with similar production complexity (measured in terms of the number of operations) to be grouped together.

Experiments were performed to determine the usefulness of the Self-Organizing Map as a clustering tool. We were mainly interested in evaluating its usefulness as a clustering tool for group technology. Our literature review did not reveal any evidence that SOM has been used for raw clustering, however several examples are found in which SOM has been used as a preprocessor in a classification process. We used three data sets as test cases for the clustering process and compared different clustering techniques using operation sequences of parts as input. One of the data sets has been used in Tam [34] (see Table 1). This data set consists of operation sequences of 19 parts. The other two data sets are much larger (containing 50 parts and 200 parts).

## 4.2. The distance adjustment

Each value in the similarity coefficient matrix of Fig. 1 can be thought of as the distance between two points in a 18-dimensional space. A low value means that the points are near to each other and therefore the corresponding parts are similar to manufacture. Hence, such parts should be grouped together. A high value, means the opposite, i.e., the parts are dissimilar and should not be grouped together. A low value of the similarity coefficient, therefore, contains much more information than a high value. This is because, in a multi-dimensional space, the fact that two points, $P_i$ and $P_j$ , are equally far from a third common point, $P_k$ , does not necessarily mean that they ( $P_i$ and $P_j$ ) are near to each other. On the contrary, if $P_i$ and $P_j$ are both near $P_k$ then it can be concluded that they are near to each other. For example, consider an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ of part $P_j$ and a weight vector $\mathbf{w}_i$ of a Kohonen node i. A corresponding pair of elements $x_m$ , $w_{im}$ in $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{w}_i$ represents the similarity of $P_j$ and node i with respect to $P_m$ . Even if the values of $x_m$ , $w_{im}$ are close, they are less informative if they are high, say (.7,.8), than if they are low, say (.1,.2), with the same amount of difference. Hence, while computing the Euclidean distance ( $D_i$ ) between an input vector and the weight vector of a node in the Kohonen layer, we make the following adjustment to equation (1) of section 3:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{D} _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {*} & = \left\{\left(\mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{d}} (x _ {1} - w _ {i 1}) + \mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{h}} (\max \{x _ {i}, w _ {i 1} \})\right) ^ {2} + \dots \right. \\ & \quad + \left(\mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{d}} (x _ {m} - w _ {i m}) \right. \\ & \quad \left. + \mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{h}} (\max \{x _ {m}, w _ {i m} \})\right) ^ {2} \Bigg \} ^ {1 / 2}. \end{array} \tag {2}
$$

where both $M_{d}$ and $M_{h}$ have the range [0,1], and $M_{d} + M_{h} = 1$ . $M_{d}$ is the effect of the raw difference between the two vectors (the input vector representing an actual part and the weight vector of a Kohonen node representing a hypothetical part). $M_{h}$ is the effect of the magnitude of the similarity coefficients in the two vectors. Because high values of similarity coefficients, in general, do not contain as much information as low values do, we penalize the high values. This adjustment, called the magnitude adjustment, made to the Euclidean distance $D_{i}$ by $M_{d}$ and $M_{h}$ is designed to have this effect: Equation (1) is a special case of equation (2) with $M_{d} = 1$ and $M_{h} = 0$ . A nonzero value for $M_{h}$ will result in higher values of $D_{i}^{*}$ when the similarity coefficients in the input vector and/or the weight vector of the Kohonen node are higher. This would reduce the chance of the particular Kohonen node to be chosen as the winning node. However, the adjustment made will not affect the distance measure when either $x_{j}$ or $w_{ij}$ has a value of 0, which means that the difference between $x_{j}$ and $w_{ij}$ is the true distance between those two points.

## 5.2. Performance evaluation

In this section, we compare the performance of the SOM technique with that of two other clustering techniques, the kth-Nearest-Neighbour (kNN) and the single linkage (SLINK) clustering techniques. The kNN method, developed by Wong [40], is a density linkage clustering technique. This technique was used by Tam [34] to group parts into families and provided reasonably good clusters. SLINK, on the other hand, is a classical approach to clustering in group technology and has been used since McAuley [24]. Both these techniques can make use of the same part-similarity measure (as we have used in SOM) to group parts. kNN and SLINK are somewhat rigid in terms of the composition and the number of clusters that are suggested for a given input data set. By contrast, SOM allows the decision-maker to generate clustering designs with desired number of clusters. Moreover, the decision-maker can control the composition of the clusters, taking into account other factors as described later.

Any metric developed to measure the goodness of a clustering design will, in general, find that a clustering design with a large number of clusters to be a better design than that with a fewer number of clusters. This is because, for a given data set, a design with a larger number of clusters has fewer parts in each cluster and therefore the parts within a cluster are more likely to be closer to each other. A bias can be introduced in the metric to counter this effect in measurement, but this adjustment is always subjective. The desired number of clusters is problem-dependent. It is therefore fair to compare the performance of different clustering techniques across clustering designs with only equal number of clusters. For one technique to be judged better than another, it should perform consistently better than the other across different clustering designs.

We employ the usual measures of central tendency to compare the performance. We compute the overall quality of a clustering design by first computing the cohesion of parts within each cluster in the clustering design. We define the cohesion measure $C_{i}$ of a cluster i as the mean similarity between a pair of parts in the cluster. The mean is the most commonly accepted measure of central tendency. In our case, the mean cohesion measure tells us how close together are the parts within a cluster. The lower the value of $C_{i}$ , the better is the cohesion of parts within the cluster. The cohesion measure is calculated as:

$$
C _ {i} = \left(\sum_ {x \neq y} S _ {x y}\right) / \binom {n _ {i}} {2} (x, y \in \text { cluster } i),

If there are $n_{i}$ parts in cluster i, then there will be $\binom{n_{i}}{2}$ such pairs in the cluster. $\binom{n_{i}}{2}$ is the number of ways in which the $n_{i}$ parts in cluster i can be grouped into pairs of 2 without regard to order.

The overall quality of a clustering design is the weighted average of the cohesion measures, $C_{i}s$ , of all clusters within the design. The overall cohesion is thus calculated as:

$$
\mathbf {C} = (\mathbf {n} _ {\mathrm{i}} - 1) \mathbf {C} _ {\mathrm{i}} / (\mathbf {n} - \mathbf {k})
$$

where k is the number of clusters, and n is the total number of parts. For a cluster i with $n_{i}$ parts, there are $(n_{i}-1)$ distance measures from any part to all the other parts in the same cluster. Therefore, $(n_{i}-1)$ is used to weight the cohesion measure of cluster i and the sum of the weights is $(n-k)$ . The lower values of C indicate a better cohesion of parts within the clusters of that clustering design.

The variance is the most commonly used measure of dispersion. In our case, the variance $S_{i}^{2}$ indicates how uniformly the similarities between parts within a cluster are dispersed around the mean $C_{i}$ . The variance of $C_{i}$ is given by:

$$
S _ {i} ^ {2} = \sum_ {x \neq y} \left[ \left(C _ {i} - S _ {x y}\right) ^ {2} / \left(\binom {n _ {i}} {2} - 1\right) \right]
$$

The variance, V, of a clustering design is calculated as the weighted average of the variances of all clusters within the design as:

$$
\mathbf {V} = \sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{k}} \left(\frac {\mathrm{n} _ {\mathrm{i}} - 1}{\mathrm{n} - \mathrm{k}}\right) ^ {2} \mathbf {S} _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {2}
$$

The lower values of V indicate that the parts are more uniformly distributed around the group means within the clusters of that clustering design.

## 6. SOM as an interactive clustering tool

This section describes the utility of SOM as an interactive tool to support decision making at strategic level as well as at operating level. At the strategic level, SOM can be used for facilities planning. At the operating level, SOM can be used to accommodate daily changing conditions by continuously adjusting part groupings when new parts are introduced or when existing parts are discontinued from a production plan, or when parts are re-designed to meet new specifications.

## 6.1. Flexibility in clustering for strategic planning

Unlike any of the other techniques for grouping parts into families, SOM can give managers the flexibility to decide upon the composition and the number of clusters based on other objective and subjective criteria. In this sense, SOM is truly a decision-support tool; it does not make the grouping decision for the manger. SOM offers a graphical map of the parts to be grouped, such that the multi-dimensional similarities between them are translated into physical distances in 2-dimensional space from which visual clustering is possible. If a decision-maker does not want to completely rely on visual clustering, sophisticated algorithms for automatic clustering can be used in conjunction with the SOM output.

These criteria or constraints can be incorporated into the assessment of the feasibility of the clustering designs. For example, if machinery resource is a major constraint, the decision-maker can use the SOM map to generate alternative clustering designs until a design that satisfies this constraint is found.

## 6.2. Dynamic clustering to support daily operations

One of the most important requirements in a dynamic production system is the ability to classify new parts into families and the ability to regroup parts according to changing specifications. Sometimes a response time of as low as a few minutes is all that can be afforded to incorporate the changes into a master production plan.

The procedure for classifying a new part or reclassifying a part with modified specification is given below:

(1) Compute the similarity coefficients of the new part with every other part.

(2) Input this as the input vector of the new part to the SOM network.

(3) Plot the winning node from the SOM network on the two-dimensional map of the existing clustering design.

(4) Visually group the new part with one of the existing clusters taking into account other subjective factors as mentioned above.

The important advantage of SOM is that the individual part can be classified without changing the composition of the existing groups. It is not necessary to perform the total regrouping of all parts by retraining the SOM network from scratch for each new part added. Hence the classification task of a new or re-engineered part can be completed almost in real time. This speed of response can also enable the decision maker in interactively evaluating different plans if the choice is to be made between different parts to be added and/or different modifications to be made in a production cycle.

## 7. Summary and conclusion

In this research, we have investigated the problem of grouping parts into families independently of the machine assignment problem. The problem formulation takes into account both the commonality of operations and similarity in operation sequences. Our part groupings are based on operations and not on machines. Part groupings that are based entirely on part-machine relationships are not realistic in the context of advanced machine tools that can perform multiple operations and can be flexible in terms of switching between operations.

We use the Self-Organizing Map (SOM) network, a variation of neural networks, as a clustering tool for group technology. The clustering is based on multi-dimensional data consisting of similarity coefficients. “There are no satisfactory methods for determining the number of population clusters for any type of cluster analysis,” and “It is always a good idea to look at your data graphically” [32]. However, it is impossible to graphically represent a data set with more than three dimensions. In this research, a SOM network is designed to take the multi-dimensional input and transform it into a 2-dimensional map. The physical location of points on the map shows the relative similarity between the points in the multi-dimensional space. Thus, the graphic representation of the clustering distribution, a unique feature provided by SOM, can help decision makers visualize the relationship of parts within and between clusters. Since the SOM network does not try to group a part into a particular cluster, exceptional elements, a problem that usually requires special treatment (such as exclusion from the training process) in the competitive learning networks, is not an issue in the case of SOM.

We compared the performance of the clustering designs obtained using the SOM with that of the kNN and the SLINK clustering techniques. We conducted experiments to generate several clustering designs using three different data sets containing operation sequences of 19, 50, and 200 parts. We developed a cohesion measure to judge the goodness of clustering designs. We found that SOM performed significantly better than the other two techniques, especially for larger data sets and for clustering designs with larger number of clusters. Moreover, the clusters formed by SOM were more uniformly distributed in terms of number of parts and had lower variances in their cohesion measure.

The major problem with the SOM network, like most neural networks, is the time required to train the network. We used the IBM RS/6000 cluster of workstations for running our program. For our 19-parts data set, we trained a $12 \times 12$ network which required 1500 training rounds and took about 15 minutes of CPU time to converge. The larger data sets (50 parts and 200 parts) used a $15 \times 15$ network and took about 40 and 150 minutes of CPU time, respectively, to be trained. Compared to these times, the CPU times taken by the kNN and the SLINK techniques are relatively lower. In strategic decision-making, such as facilities planning, the long training times are not a problem. Once trained, the same network can be used for re-classification of parts with changed specifications and for classification of new parts into existing clusters. We found that the CPU time required for doing such classification was less than two seconds.

SOM can give decision makers the flexibility to decide the number of clusters (manufacturing cells) needed based on other criteria (e.g., the availability of machinery and capital, and the expected production volume) contingent to the individual problem situation. The flexibility offered by SOM also allows the decision-maker to dynamically group parts into different clusters to comply with the changing conditions such as volumes, part-specifications, machine availability, etc.

The preliminary results obtained in this research are encouraging. We are currently working on investigating the possibility of incorporating other design criteria such as production volumes, capital cost, and other part/machine parameters into the part-grouping process. We are also exploring the applicability of this technique to problem domains in the manufacturing area where real-time assistance is necessary for decision-making. The capability of the SOM technique to provide assistance in dynamic settings makes it a good candidate for building intelligent manufacturing systems.
