# Anchor-and-adjustment behaviour in a dynamic decision environment $^{*}$

William Remus $^{a,*}$ , Jeffrey Kottemann $^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of Hawaii, 2404 Maile Way, Honolulu, HI 96822, USA $^{b}$ University of Maryland, Adelphi, MD 20783, USA

## Abstract

Research on the anchor-and-adjustment paradigm has concentrated on discrete, static judgment tasks rather than on decision making in a continuous, dynamic environment. In the experiment reported, we found anchoring-and-adjustment occurring in a continuous, dynamic environment. We also established that the anchoring-and-adjustment model differed from the classical models in both fit and economic performance.

Keywords: Decision making; Biases; Anchor-and-adjustment; Production scheduling

## 1. Introduction

Anchor-and-adjustment models are one of the more widely researched models of human decision making. These models describe decision makers as “anchoring” on a decision and then adjusting the decision based on additional or updated information (Anderson, 1991, p. 118; Hogarth, 1987, p. 54; Northcraft and Neale, 1987; Plous, 1993, pp. 147-148). Although simple in structure, these models also appeal to our common sense about how decisions are often made (Anderson, 1991, p. 118).

The anchor-and-adjustment models proliferated following the seminal articles by Lichtenstein and Slovic (1971) and by Tversky and Kahneman (1974). The models seem to be descriptive of a wide variety of judgment tasks: intuitive estimation of numerical expressions (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974), forecasting (Bromiley, 1987), stock management (Sterman, 1989), risk assessment (Lichtenstein et al., 1978), preference for gambles (Lichtenstein and Slovic, 1971), predictions of spouse's preferences (Davis et al., 1986), causal attribution (Einhorn and Hogarth, 1986), judgment under ambiguity (Einhorn and Hogarth, 1985), and judgments of experienced real estate agents (Northcraft and Neale, 1987) and auditors (Butler, 1986). Given the volume and variety of studies, anchor-and-adjustment appears to be pervasive.

The above studies share two basic limiting characteristics. First, the experimental tasks used in these studies generally have a discrete, static nature. Hogarth (1981) argues that many empirical findings in such discrete, static environments may be limited to such environments. He points out that continuous, dynamic task contexts common in real-world environments give decision makers repeated feedback that allows incremental corrections to be made $^{1}$ . Second, while the earlier cited studies show that anchor-and-adjustment models characterize decision making, these studies do not test whether the anchor-and-adjustment models differ from competing models.

The purpose of the present study is to overcome the above two limitations and thereby establish whether anchoring-and-adjustment occurs in a continuous, dynamic environment. We will organize our analysis of the anchor-and-adjustment model to reflect the approach suggested by Campbell and Fiske (1959). We will try to establish that anchoring-and-adjustment is occurring by using multiple measures; Campbell and Fiske termed this convergent validity. This part of the analysis will basically replicate our earlier work (Remus and Kottemann, 1987).

Next, we will estimate the anchor-and-adjustment models. The question will then arise whether we have completely specified the appropriate anchor-and-adjustment model. To establish that the anchor-and-adjustment models are correctly and fully specified, we will use the Durbin h test $^{2}$ (Durbin, 1970). Finally to establish what Campbell and Fiske termed discriminant validity, we will try to establish that the anchor-and-adjustment model is different from the widely used linear decision rule. The linear decision rule was selected for comparison because it is the most prominent model used for representing judgmental processes (Kleinmuntz, 1990).

One important difference between these two models is that the anchor-and-adjustment model is argued to be a literal description of the process of judgment while the linear decision rule is viewed only as paramorphically descriptive (Hoffman, 1960). That is, linear decision rules do not infer that an explicit weighing process is undertaken by the decision maker. However, given the strong correspondence between the output of linear decision rules and the decisions of human judges, it is often claimed that judges behave as if they were performing the above operations.

## 2. The experimental design

The experimental design is based on the Holt, Modigliani, Muth, and Simon model of the production scheduling problem (Holt et al., 1960). The task is to decide how many units to produce and workers to employ given uncertain future demand and knowledge of current work force size, productivity, and inventory level. The managerial intent is to minimize the average cost of the decisions as computed by a quadratic function. The cost is a function of changes in work force level, overtime or idle time costs, and departures from the ideal level of inventory. The set of equations representing this model is shown in Appendix A.

The production scheduling problem was selected because it is a relevant managerial problem and it has been calibrated with real-world data (Holt et al., 1956, p. 163). Most importantly, the task is representative of decision making in continuous, dynamic environments rather than the static judgment tasks used in past research. The thirty-one subjects in this study were graduate students from an evening course in management science; over 80% of the subjects were employed. The subjects were paid a ten dollar fee. The subjects had no prior experience in the scheduling exercise and they had no knowledge of the optimization models for this decision. Remus (1986) found no evidence of performance differences in this task between managers and graduate students.

A computer presented the task information and collected the subject's decisions; Fig. 1 shows the CRT display used. The display contained the sales forecasts for the next three periods. The three period forecast horizon was selected based on Moskowitz and Miller's (1975) research demonstrating the superiority of the three period forecast horizon in this experimental task.

On the basis of the three periods of forecasts, their inventory position, current work force size, and worker productivity, the subjects made their decisions. After they input their decisions, the computer gave them an opportunity to check their decisions.

The subjects then received the actual sales and costs, the new inventory level, and the average cost so far as shown in Fig. 1. The subjects were given no bench mark average cost level for the task. All the cost and inventory calculations were done by the computer. This decision cycle was repeated for each of 24 periods.

The experiment used a carefully controlled display (Fig. 1) to limit the information the subjects could use in the anchoring-and-adjustment process; this is important since subjects may use irrelevant information in the anchor-and-adjustment process (Switzer and Sniezek, 1991). Thus, the set of potential variables for use included only this period's forecasts, work force, production, and inventory as displayed in Fig. 1. Since this is a recurrent decision task, some of the variables in the period t decision could (and should) be used for the next decision; this is reflected in Eq. (1). Note that the use of lagged variables is a characteristic of common dynamic, continuous tasks (but usually not of static, discrete tasks).

The quadratic cost function used in the experiment was found by Holt et al. (1956) p. 163 to characterize a paint plant; however, following Moskowitz and Miller (1975), only the quadratic cost terms were used.

The initial demand was set at 2500 units; the demand grew at 20 units per period. To the preceding model an eight period sinusoidal pattern with an amplitude of 20% of the unadjusted demand was added. The demand pattern was then given variability by adjusting it with a uniformly distributed variation of $\pm400$ units of demand. This level is comparable to Moskowitz and Miller's (1975) intermediate variability treatment. Forecasts were the demand plus uniformly distributed forecast error; the further into the future the forecast, the higher the error. Each subject received a unique pattern of adjusted demand by having the uniformly distributed variation controlled by a random number generator. These demand and forecast equations are consistent with realistic organizational environments (Wholey and Brittain, 1989) and with high levels of task complexity (Campbell, 1988).

## 3. The analysis plan

## 3.1. The anchor-and-adjustment model operationalization

The anchor-and-adjustment model is often specified in the most general of terms. This lack of operationalization makes it difficult to rigorously test the model. Also, the anchor-and-adjustment model is operationalized in some experimental settings as the classic linear decision rule (this is discussed later in this section). For these two reasons, we will use a model that is (1) a clear operationalization of the anchor-and-adjustment model, (2) clearly differentiable from the classic linear decision rule, and (3) appropriate to recurrent decisions.

A particularly clear formulation of the anchor-and-adjustment model by Anderson (1991) fulfils the above requirements. For a recurrent decision in period $t + 1$ , Anderson (1991), p. 118 provides the following model of the decision maker's response, $R_{t+1}$ , using the anchoring-and-adjustment process:

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{t+1}} = \mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{t}} + \mathrm{w} _ {\mathrm{t+1}} (\mathrm{S} _ {\mathrm{t+1}} - \mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{t}}).\tag{1}
$$

The decision maker's response in period $t + 1$ is the response in the prior period $t$ adjusted by the weighted difference between the new stimulus, $S_{t+1}$ , and the prior response. Anderson (1991) p. 78 notes that "the prior response, $R_t$ , may be called an anchor, and [the second term] an adjustment in the direction of the present stimulus."

If $S_{t+1}$ is a stimulus prompting response $R_{t+1}$ , then the recurrent decisions $R_{t+1}$ will lag $S_{t+1}$ as long as $w_{t+1}$ is less than the optimal value. For example, suppose a manufacturer wishes to meet next period's demand $S_{t+1}$ by deciding how much to produce, $R_{t+1}$ ; assume no forecast error for demand $S_{t+1}$ and no inventory on hand in period t. If $w_{t+1}$ is less than one, the decision $R_{t+1}$ will lag $S_{t+1}$ . Underadjustment is likely to occur since Tversky and Kahneman (1974) and others have noted conservatism in the adjustment process, in general, and we had earlier noted underadjustment in the current experimental task.

If the stimulus in Eq. (1) has a sinusoidal pattern, responses will depart from the stimulus pattern in systematic ways. The decision maker will both lag the stimulus and not fully adjust to the peaks and troughs of the stimulus. In the tracking literature, these errors are called “phase lag” and “range effects” respectively; see Poulton (1974) for an extensive discussion of tracking models, their artifacts, and anchor-and-adjustment-like models. The experimental task described in the prior section has been shown to have both phase lag and range effects (Remus and Kottemann, 1987); this suggests that the Anderson model in Eq. (1) might model well the decision process.

As a caveat, it should be noted that not all studies on anchor-and-adjustment models use models as defined by Anderson. Some of the early studies (e.g., Tversky and Kahneman, 1974) focused on only irrelevant anchors whereas more recent work focuses on relevant anchors (e.g., Anderson, 1991, p. 118; Hogarth, 1987, p. 54; Northcraft and Neale, 1987; Plous, 1993, pp. 147-148). Also, many studies of discrete, static decision tasks use models where the decision maker does not anchor on a lagged variable like $R_t$ nor adjust as in Eq. (1). This class of “anchor-and-adjustment” models is not in the form of Eq. (1) and perhaps should be thought of as a weighted combination of decision variables (i.e., a linear decision rule). Given that linear decision models perform so well (Kleinmuntz, 1990), this second class of “anchor-and-adjustment” models also performs well. In this paper, we will adhere strictly to the definition of the recurrent anchor-and-adjustment model using a relevant anchor by Anderson (1991), p. 117–119.

In their standard form, anchor-and-adjustment models are similar to time series models used in forecasting. For a discussion of the equivalency of these two models, see Appendix B. Also in this appendix is a review of the relevant side-by-side testing of forecasting models that are similar to the anchor-and-adjustment models.

## 3.2. Identifying model artifacts

To establish that anchoring-and-adjustment was occurring, three measures derived from Anderson's model were used to analyze the production decision data: the correlation of the decision and anchor, the phase lag, and the range effect. Estimation of the correlation of the decision and its anchor (last period's decision) was straight forward. To estimate the phase lag and range effect, we used non-linear least squares to fit the decisions to a sinusoidal functional form; this form was appropriate given the sinusoidal demand pattern provided to the subjects.

## 3.3. Estimating the anchor-and-adjustment models

To estimate the anchor-and-adjustment model, we created anchor-and-adjustment models for the production decision following Anderson (1991), p. 118; the model used is shown in Eq. (1) above. To fully evaluate the anchor-and-adjustment model, we chose two variables to use as $S_{t}$ . In one case, the decision maker was viewed as naive; the stimulus was the forecast for the needed production. Since forecasts are the true value in noise, the second model assumed that the decision maker had perfect knowledge of the needed production and could use it in the adjustment process. The following paragraphs provide more details on these anchor-and-adjustment models.

In the experimental task we will use, the first anchor-and-adjustment model views the managers as anchoring on their prior production decision and adjusting it to obtain their new production decision. In this model the adjustment is a function of the difference between the forecasted production and the prior production decision $^{3}$ .

This model requires the estimation of a coefficient of adjustment, $w_{t+1}$ , in Eq. (1), which is multiplied by the above difference to represent the adjustment process. We shall refer to this anchor-and-adjustment model as the “simple” anchor-and-adjustment model.

A second anchor-and-adjustment model was created by viewing the decision makers as anchoring on their prior production decision but adjusting by the difference between the actual production required (the perfect noise-free production level needed) and the prior production decision. Since the adjustment is based upon the actual production required, this model must fully take into account all relevant information (as in Svenson, 1984,1985). By doing this we are modelling decision makers as having perfect understanding of the demand and production processes, thus, being able to see through the noise. We, therefore, term this the “ideal” model.

The weights in the anchor-and-adjustment models were estimated using least squares regression. The measure of fit for these models was the mean squared difference between the model and the actual decisions. To establish that these models fully incorporated any anchoring-and-adjustment that might be occurring, we calculated the Durbin h when each anchor-and-adjustment model was estimated.

## 3.4. Estimating the comparative models

To estimate the linear decision rules, each decision maker's production decisions were modeled with regression-based linear decision rules used by Moskowitz and Miller (1975) and others; the betas (for work force, forecasts, and inventory) for each subject were estimated using least squares regression. The fit of this model to actual decisions was measured with mean square error.

## 3.5. Additional considerations in the analysis

Three additional notes on our analysis need to be made. The first note is that our analysis used two measures of model quality. The mean squared error earlier discussed was used to establish which model fit best. In real businesses, however, the relevant criterion would be the economic performance of the models rather than model fit (Lee et al., 1993; Remus, 1991). Thus, we also determined the cost resulting from decisions based on the various models; this was done by evaluating the decisions using the cost function contained in Appendix A.

## 4. Results

The central research question focuses on existence of anchoring-and-adjustment when making recurrent managerial decisions in dynamic, continuous decision environments.

First, we tested whether learning was completed by the twelfth period as found by Remus, Carter, and Jenicke (1979). Using a repeated measures analysis of variance with the period number as the independent variable, we found no statistically significant evidence for learning beyond period 12 (p < .05).

Next we examined convergent validity by examining if our three measures show anchor-and-adjustment to be occurring. The first measure was the correlation between a decision and its anchor (the prior decision). The correlation was .47 (p < .05). As in the earlier study (Remus and Kottemann, 1987), the subjects' decisions lagged the sinusoidal demand function and failed to peak production as highly as required by the demand function. In the data gathered in the current experiment and using the analysis technique described in Remus and Kottemann (1987), both the phase lag (.58 radians when learning and .14 radians thereafter) and range effect (56% when learning and 17% thereafter) were again found to be occurring at significant levels (p < .05).

Given that the three measures consistently demonstrated that anchoring-and-adjustment was occurring, we next estimated the simple and ideal anchoring-and-adjustment models and calculated the Durbin h test statistic for each. For the simple model, the average value of the Durbin h statistic across the 31 subjects was .7979. For the ideal model, the average value of the Durbin h statistic across the 31 subjects was .2903. In neither case was there significant evidence of positive autocorrelation occurring (p < .05). Thus, we have some assurance that the anchor-and-adjustment model was not inadequately specified.

## 5. Discussion

Although past studies found anchoring-and-adjustment behaviour to be ubiquitous, the task environments in the past studies have been static and discrete. Hogarth has both (1) emphasized the need to test alternative models side-by-side in critical experiments (Hogarth, 1986) and (2) argued that some research results may apply only to discrete, static tasks (Hogarth, 1981). This led Hogarth (1981) to question the correspondence of many experimental tasks to the continuous, dynamic environments common in real-world tasks.

The experiment just reported found anchoring-and-adjustment to be occurring in a continuous, dynamic task. This was demonstrated with the significant correlation of an anchor and the decision, the range effect, and phase lag. We next built two anchor-and-adjustment models. The models appeared complete since the Durbin h test failed to provide evidence that variables were missing from the anchor-and-adjustment models.

We also examined if the anchor-and-adjustment model was different from other models, in particular, the widely used linear decision rule.

This difference was supported by the data; the linear decision rules consistently fit the actual decisions better than the anchor-and-adjustment models. This was a surprising result since the anchor-and-adjustment model is commonly viewed as literally descriptive whereas linear decision rules are viewed as only paramorphically descriptive; thus, we would have expected the anchor-and-adjustment models to fit better than the linear decision rules.

It is noteworthy that the ideal anchor-and-adjustment model fits the decisions better than the simple anchor-and-adjustment model. This finding suggests that simplistic anchor-and-adjustment models are inadequate to model complex real world tasks; more complex versions of the anchor-and-adjustment model (like our ideal model) are required.

Discriminant validity was also supported by comparing the economic effectiveness of the anchor-and-adjustment models and the linear decision rules. The models differed in their cost performance. The linear decision rules had significantly lower costs than the anchor-and-adjustment models.

It is noteworthy that bootstrapping with linear decision rules has often been shown to improve decision maker's economic performance. The strong superiority of the linear decision rules over the anchor-and-adjustment models suggests that there is little to be gained from bootstrapping using anchor-and-adjustment models.

The experimental task is a realistic decision task that requires complex decision making involving multiple variables and multiple time periods. A priori, this would suggest that the linear decision rule be more appropriate than the anchor-and-adjustment model since the former explicitly takes multiple variables into account whereas the latter does not. However, we believe it is still an appropriate task for establishing discriminant validity.

First, this is an appropriate task because the anchor-and-adjustment model has been advocated for just such tasks (e.g., Hogarth, 1987, p.54; Plous, 1993, pp. 147-148). Consider the earlier cited literature on the model. It has been used for real estate judgments (Northcraft and Neale,

1987), auditors' judgments (Butler, 1986), and other multivariate tasks. Also, there is evidence that the adjustment process can include all relevant multivariate information (Svenson, 1984 and 1985). Therefore, the existing literature suggests that this is an appropriate task.

Second, in this experiment, we found three pieces of evidence that anchoring-and-adjustment was occurring. The evidence includes significant phase lag, range effects, and a lagged correlation coefficient.

Third, this is a fair test since the statistical procedures help make it so even though there are more variables in the decision rule than in the anchor-and-adjustment model. Including more variables in a linear model does not automatically improve its performance. Consider the measure of fit used (mean squared error). This measure of fit for models is calculated by dividing the squared error by the degrees of freedom. If variables that did not help explain managerial decision making were included, the mean squared error would actually increase since the numerator would stay the same while the denominator decreased. Therefore, the division by degrees of freedom help ensures an equitable comparison of the models.

Fourth, the Armstrong (1985), pp. 404–412 review described in detail in Appendix B demonstrated that merely increasing the number of variables (as with causal models) did not give the causal model a significant advantage over the naive model with fewer variables. Paquette and Kida (1988) corroborated Armstrong's conclusion for several decision models.

As a caveat, it is important to point out that this experiment does not establish that anchoring-and-adjustment occurs in all dynamic, continuous decision environments. Thus, the findings of this experiment need replication in other continuous, dynamic environments. Also, the experiment compared the linear decision rule and anchor-and-adjustment models to attempt to establish discriminant validity. This, too, needs replication in other environments since we cannot be sure this generalizes to other continuous, dynamic environments.

In spite of the experimental decision having artifacts suggesting anchoring-and-adjustment behaviour and in spite of all the above caveats, the results of this study do not suggest using anchor-and-adjustment models when attempting to support improved decision making. The model of choice for improving managerial decision making as shown in this and other studies (Kleinmuntz, 1990) is the linear decision rule.

## Appendix B

Anchor-and-adjustment and time series models

The testing of the discriminant validity of anchoring-and-adjustment models has not been done in judgment research but similar tests have been performed in the forecasting literature. This comparison will be found in the literature comparing causal and naive forecasting models; the only measure generally used is model fit.

In this appendix, we will first argue that exponential smoothing models are closely related to the anchor-and-adjustment models. Then we will argue that causal models and linear decision rules are also closely related. This will lead us to review the literature comparing the two classes of models.

The simple exponential smoothing and anchor-and-adjustment models are similar. Consider the Wheelwright and Makridakis (1985), Eqs. 4-5 formulation of the simple exponential smoothing model to forecast next period, $F_{t+1}$ :

$$
\mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{t+1}} = \mathrm{aX} _ {\mathrm{t}} + (1 - \mathrm{a}) \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{t}}.\tag{4}
$$

That is, the forecast for period $t+1$ is just the weighted combination of the actual value of the forecasted variable for period t, $X_{t}$ , and the forecast for period t, $F_{t}$ . This equation can be restated as:

$$
\mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{t} + 1} = \mathrm{X} _ {\mathrm{t}} + (1 - a) (\mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{t}} - \mathrm{X} _ {\mathrm{t}}).\tag{5}
$$

Here the forecast for period $t + 1$ is the actual value of the forecasted variable for period t and a weighted adjustment based on the error between the forecast for period t and actual value of the forecasted variable in period t. If Eqs. (1) and (5) are compared, it is apparent that exponential smoothing is mathematically similar to the anchor-and-adjustment model. If in the anchor-and-adjustment model in (1) the forecasted future response is $F_{t+1}$ , the prior response is $X_{t}$ , and the stimulus is the forecast in t, then the models are identical.

The exponential smoothing model anchors on the most recent actual value and adjusts proportionately to the most recent forecast error. This is a special case of the general model in Eq. (1) since the general model doesn't fully specify the stimulus variable, $S_{t}$ .

In the forecasting literature the exponential smoothing is referred to as a naive model since exponential smoothing is univariate and generally uses past and current values of a variable to predict the future value of a variable.

There is a close relationship between causal forecasting models and linear decision rules as used in the current experiment. Linear decision rules contain only independent variables (cues) which affect the decision. Therefore, these decision rules are causal models if the decision makers use these cues in the decision process. In a sense, a linear decision rule is a forecast of the decision to be made.

Given these parallels between anchor-and-adjustment and the naive simple exponential smoothing model and the parallels between causal models and linear decision rules, it is appropriate to examine the relevant forecasting literature.

The forecasting literature fully supports comparative testing of these kinds of models. Armstrong (1985), pp. 404–412 reviewed the comparative literature and found twenty-one sound empirical studies comparing causal and naive short range forecasting models. In one study naive models significantly outperformed causal models (p < .05); he also identified five other studies in which he believed naive models outperformed causal models. In one study the causal model outperformed the naive model (p < .05); Armstrong identified eight other studies in which causal models outperformed the naive model. In the remaining six studies neither model seemed to have an advantage. Thus, Armstrong's review of this series of studies provides no evidence to suggest the superiority of either causal or naive models.
