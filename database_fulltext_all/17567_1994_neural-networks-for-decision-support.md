# Neural networks for decision support

Kar Yan Tam

The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong

Keywords: Neural networks; Decision support systems; Classification; Nonlinear modeling.

Neural computing refers to the class of massively parallel computation architectures inspired by the modelling of neurophysiological phenomena. The subject has received considerable interests from both academics and practitioners in recent years. There is a growing perception that neural networks can be a viable tool to support decision modelling across a wide spectrum of tasks that are currently based on human judgements and quantitative models.

The power of a neural network comes from its ability to model nonlinear relationships and its robustness to deal with noisy and incomplete data commonly found in real life situations. While many management science models are theoretically rigorous, their validity is grounded on the assumptions on the real world phenomena they intend to model. In cases where these assumptions are unrealistic or exist simply for ease of implementation and estimation, conclusions generated by these models are questionable.

For a number of years, neural computing has been viewed by many as a subject of empirical nature and apparently criticized for being so. With the advent of hardware technology in the last two decades, neural networks have been successfully applied to solve problems in forecasting, control, optimization, speech understanding and pattern recognition. This has created tremendous interest in both the empirical and theoretical aspects of neural networks. Theoretical foundation has been established recently to explain the capability of a neural network and address issues which could only be dealt with in an ad-hoc manner before. For example, it was shown that a 3-layer network with a single hidden layer can approximate any real functions $[2,3,4]$ . Baum and Haussler show the relationship between the generalizability of a network and the size of the training sample $[1]$ . The subject of neural computing is interdisciplinary and research is currently being pursued in all fronts by researchers in physics, mathematics, computer science, engineering and MIS among many others. Many national and international conferences on neural networks have been organized and new journals published to provide a medium to exchange ideas and document research results.

The purpose of this special issue is to present recent progress in the applications of neural networks to various decision problems. The first paper gives an overview of the opportunities and limitations of neural networks in building decision support systems. The remaining papers can be grouped under four categories: Decision analysis, forecasting, integration with expert systems and applications.

## Neural networks in DSS development

“Neural Networks for Decision Support: Problems and Opportunities” by Shimon Schocken and Gad Ariav. This article discusses how neural networks can facilitate the decision modelling process in a general DSS framework developed in one of the authors’ previous work. The authors suggest that many decision problems share a generic structure which lend themselves nicely to neural computing solutions. Because of its nonlinearity and learning capability, a neural network does not require the level of structurability commonly required by formal mathematical models. Neural networks also support different levels of managerial tasks and different phases in the decision making process. The authors outline the implementation of neural networks and their integration with other resources of a DSS. Problems relating to neural network based systems such as face validity and interplay with expert systems are also discussed.

## Decision analysis

“Artificial Neural Networks Versus Natural Neural Networks: A Connectionist Paradigm for Preference Assessment” by Jun Wang. The author develops a neural network model to perform preference assessment in a general multicriteria decision setting. It is contended that connectionist preference models relax the independence assumptions on the functional form of existing preference models. Two preference models, one for holistic ratings and another for pairwise comparisons, are formulated. Different functional forms of utility functions including additive, multilinear, Quadratic and Polynex are specified and instantiated using an interactive supervised learning procedure developed by the author. Empirical findings indicate that preference models represented by the synthesized neural networks correctly determine the best alternative with relatively low training and testing error rates.

"A Neural Network Approach to Decision Alternative Prioritization" by Rick Wilson. The author starts with a review of problems encountered in existing alternative prioritization techniques and proposes a neural network approach to the general prioritization problem. Based on attribution and ranking theories, five characteristics of the transfer function as they relate to the general ranking problem are specified. A transfer function with these characteristics is introduced and applied to three decision settings. The neural network approach alleviates some of the shortcomings of existing techniques by allowing a sparse pairwise comparison matrix and incorporating degree or amplitude of dominance in comparisons. It is also not paralyzed by inconsistency of the decision maker. Using selected data samples, the author finds that a neural network ranks objects correctly if a prior ranking results are known and provides rational prioritizations otherwise.

“Neural Network Models for Intelligent Support of Managerial Decision Making” by Tim Hill and William Remus. Neural networks and linear decision models for managerial decision making are compared in the paper. Limitations of linear models are presented and the potential of neural networks to alleviate these limitations are discussed. Using production scheduling as the experimental task, neural models are compared with human subjects and classical composite decision models. Empirical findings show that neural networks perform as effectively as regression rules in minimizing production cost. Both of them outperform human subjects. There are evidences that the performance of a neural network is related to the level of environmental variance.

## Forecasting

"A Two-Stage Neural Network Approach for ARMA Model Identification with ESCAF" by Jae Kyu Lee and Won Chul Jhee. In this paper, the authors apply multilayer feedforward neural networks to identify ARMA models for forecasting. Instead of manually interpreting ESACF tables to identify autoregression and moving average orders, a neural network is specified to perform this task automatically. This approach is evaluated using randomly generated data as well as real world data including U.S. Quarterly GNP, U.S. monthly consumer price index, and caffeine levels in instant coffee taken every weekday. Experimental results show that neural networks can identify ARMA models with about 90% of accuracy using ESACF inputs in conjunction with a noisy filtering network.

"A Decision Support System for In-Sample Simultaneous Equation Systems Forecasting using Artificial Neural Networks" by Louis E. Caporaletti, Robert E. Dorsey, John D. Johnson, and William A. Powell. In simultaneous equation forecasting, one has to decide on the inclusion/exclusion of exogenous and endogenous variables in order to derive an exact simultaneous equation model (SEM). The task is laborious and depends to a large extent on the experience of the decision maker. A neural computing approach is introduced to reduce a prior non-sample information such as the functional form of equations in order to alleviate restrictions imposed by existing estimation techniques. A genetic algorithm is adopted to overcome the paralysis problem by allowing the search to escape from local optimal solutions in training a network. Monte Carlo experiments with two SEMs are conducted to compare the performance of neural networks with 2SLS, 3SLS, and ULS estimators on a Cyber 205 supercomputer. Neural networks have outperformed conventional estimators in minimizing in-sample errors measured by SSE and RMFE in both models. While evidences of neural networks as a tool to support SEM specification are observed, the authors have been careful not to generalize the findings to out-of-sample forecasting.

## Integration with expert systems

“Integrating Artificial Neural Networks with Rule-Based Expert Systems,” by Youngohc Yoon, Tor Guimaraes, and George Swales. A major limitation of neural networks as perceived by many is the lack of explanatory power. While impressive results of neural networks have been documented in many application areas, there is a general concern about their “black-box” processing mechanism. Little is revealed about the structure of the underlying problem. This makes integration with symbolic rule-based systems very difficult. The authors propose a method to extract if-then rules from a trained feedforward neural network. A statistics called relative strength is adopted to measure the relationship between input and output variables. The statistics allows the effect of any input variable upon the output variable to be computed for each training sample. Symbolic rules are then generated to explain this effect. The concept has been applied to build the Integrated Finance Expert System (IFES) for stock performance prediction with encouraging results.

## Applications

"A Classification Approach Using Multilayered Neural-networks" by Selwyn Piramuthu, Michael Shaw, and James Gentry. The authors present a modified back-propagation algorithm based on the Newton-Raphson method for training neural networks. Networks so generated are shown to outperform probit and similarity-based learning techniques in two financial classification tasks: loan-payment default predictions and credit-worthiness. Experimental results also indicate the modified back-propagation algorithm outperforms regular back-propagation algorithm in terms of prediction accuracy and speed of convergence.

“Decision Support in Non-Conservative Domains: Generalization with Neural Networks” by Soumitra Dutta, Shashi Shekhar, and W.Y. Wong. The authors start with an overview of two categories of domain problems, namely recognition and generalization. The latter can be subclassified into conservative and partially conservative domains. It is suggested that neural networks can be used to learn the underlying model of a non-conservative domain. Three data sets, one using artificial data and two with real world data, are used to evaluate the performance of neural networks. Although results of the controlled experiment favour neural networks, results of the other two are mixed.

“Bankruptcy Prediction Using Neural Networks” by Rick L. Wilson and Ramesh Sharda. There is a growing need to properly monitor the performance of a firm by observing its financial ratios published in periodical statements and reports. The authors propose a neural network approach to meet this need by training a network using samples of bankruptcy cases. Using bankruptcy cases reported in Moody's Industrial Manuals from 1975 to 1982, neural networks are compared with multivariate linear models to predict failures one year ahead. A full two-way factor experiment design with 3 different set compositions for both training and testing samples is used. Empirical findings show that neural networks outperform discriminant analysis under different training and testing conditions. The experimental design enables the authors to address issues pertaining to training sample composition and its impact on prediction accuracy.
