# The 1990s research program: Implications for management and the emerging organization

Michael S. Scott Morton

Sloan School of Management, MIT, Cambridge, MA 02139, USA

The external business environment has become more turbulent over the last several years and this is likely to continue. At the same time the functionality and cost of information technology has been improving steadily. The combination of these two forces can give rise to a quite different new organization – the flexible organization. Research has shown the nature of the inhibiting factor that has caused this transformation to only occur in some organizations.

A corporation's structure, shape, and practices reflect its strategy. This point was made eloquently by Chandler [2] in the 1950's. In turn, a firm's strategy is a function of the opportunities and threats in the external environment, and its own internal strengths and weaknesses. It follows that as the external environment changes there will have to be compensating changes in the organization.

The external environment, as is well known, is changing: the changes in Europe from 3 years ago and now, for example. Looking at the next ten years, there is no reason to believe that this turbulence will stop, or even slow down. There are many different causes of turbulence (Figure 1). For example, productivity, quality, and global competition have produced new players who have re-defined for the West the expected standards of quality and productivity. The manufacturing sector is responding well, but the services sector has scarcely moved (Figure 2). Given the percentage of the workforce in services (Figure 3), this has a big overall impact on a nation's competitiveness; and similarly for a corporation. Its productivity must be close to that of its competitors or it will eventually be dealt with by the market place – a harsh taskmaster.

The demographic picture provides another example of a major external force that will inevitably have an impact on strategy. Demographic reality is often masked by the immediate reaction of current unemployment, or the trauma of a major downsizing (e.g. Philips, Siemens, etc.). However, stepping back and looking forward 10 years, it is a fact that the USA and the major western economies are facing a major reduction in the number of available employees. Not only are the numbers smaller than they have been for 20 or 30 years, there is a change in character; for example, there are now many more females in the workforce (in the US, 2/3 of the entering workforce will be minority or female by the late 1990's). There is also a change in attitude towards being a “corporate” person, there are shifting values, and changing attitudes toward work.

Complicating this number shortage and attitude change is the growing gap between those that have the skills that will be in demand in the 90's and those that have been left out, either by accident of birth or by an inappropriate emphasis of the nation's educational system. No country is immune from these gaps.

The conclusion is obvious: the external changes are sufficiently large that “business as usual” simply will not be enough to allow organizations to come through the 1990’s and prosper. All organizations will have to make major adjustments. In many cases, nothing less than a transformation will be sufficient.

One of the major external forces is changing technology. New materials, such as composites, the biotechnology revolution, and a host of others all provide a set of threats and opportunities. Of all these, the most significant are the changes in Information Technology (I.T.). It is most significant, as it affects every single organization without exception. Information, after all, is the lifeblood of any collection of individuals organized for a common purpose. I.T. is not computer hardware. It is not even computer hardware and software.

I.T. can be thought of as consisting of at least the following 6 elements (Figure 4). This goes way beyond Data Processing (D.P.). This seemingly obvious fact flies in the face of thirty years (1955–1985) of extensive exposure to computers. This, historically, was almost always mainframes and was variously called EDP, DP, Informatics, and MIS. However, I.T. as it is being used here is not just computers. Devices such as the Apple Macintosh and the subsequent development of 'windows' have changed the way humans can work with technology. Global communication networks, and local area networks when coupled with O.S.I., can put data, information, and processing power at virtually any location.

These realities are well known. Actual practice in organizations suggests that their implications are only just beginning to be realized. But these realities, which we are only just beginning to react to, are the technical realities of yesterday. The technical realities of today and tomorrow are more extensive. Of these, four seem to be most important:

(1) Shared data and information. The ability for multiple users to contribute to, and draw from, a logically central (but perhaps physically quite distributed) store of data and information.

(2) The availability of a wideband (satellite and fibre optics) communications infrastructure on a global basis. This enables, for example, low

They talk about “flexible specialization”; others have talked about the “networked organization”. I want to suggest that the recently completed MIT Sloan School’s program, “Management in the 1990’s” (Figure 7) provided the data that allows me to suggest a complementary view – the emergence of the “Flexible Corporation”.

The 1990's research program at MIT attempted to take a broader view of the impacts of I.T. on organizations. Three of the findings are relevant here:

(1) Many organizations are merely automating existing procedures, and that does not provide significant economic benefit. Benefits come from re-thinking the way work gets done.

(2) Electronic integration between tasks within an organization and between organizations is one of the areas of high payoff.

(3) I.T. does not provide sustainable competitive advantage. Rather, it is increasingly a competitive necessity and/or a platform on which to base continuing innovations. The program did find isolated areas where there were impacts from particular applications of I.T.

However, despite all the very real applications of I.T., the overall economic evidence of their impact is, at best, mixed (see [8]). While there may be clear evidence of economic payoff for one portion of a company, or for the whole company but at one moment in time, evidence of significant payoff for a firm is, at best, conflicting. There is a variety of reasons for this disturbing fact, beyond poor choice of application area and poor execution:

(1) Benefits of I.T. investments often are not capturable by the organization making the investment; they end up in the consumers' hands. Competition does not allow a firm to keep the benefits; it becomes a necessity for doing business.

(2) I.T. is used to responding to new demands that do not show up in current improved economic returns: responding to new government regulations, adding significant new customer services, etc. Again, these are required for long-run viability.

Both of these types of very real impacts have major implications for the level of active understanding of I.T. that is required of senior management. The classical ways of making decisions about I.T. investments are simply inadequate in the face of the basic choices about fundamental changes in the way business will be done. Beyond these two reasons, a third cause of the lack of visible impact on corporate economic returns emerged from the 1990's research program (see [1,9]). The program developed a way of looking at an organization, a view with strong theoretical roots (Figure 8). With this in mind, it can be seen that I.T. will not impact a firm's strategy and performance unless there is a corresponding investment in:

When these significant investments are made, then it becomes possible to get the systemic, broad, impacts that come from an I.T. enabled new form of organization, one that we have called the “flexible corporation”. It is not an organization caused by I.T. Rather, it is an organization responsive to the new demands from the business environment and alert to the opportunities of new ways of working and organizing that are enabled by I.T.

The business environment, customers, competition, and the new global economics are requiring that a corporation at least:

(1) Be responsive to customers, meeting real needs.

(2) Make effective use of assets.

\- Cycle times (such as time to market)

\- Physical and financial (such as inventory, cash, etc.)

\- Location of work (to leverage scarce resources)

To achieve these twin goals requires an organization to be willing to think about how it does business – to “re-engineer” the organization. Given the enormity of the task and the turbulence of the environment, this cannot be done by the CEO or the Board of Directors alone; it requires the active involvement of everyone. In particular, this implies:

(1) Empowered employees who understand the corporate goals and feel able to do something about them.

(2) That the organization is building-in systems for organizational and individual learning, so that the organization constantly improves, a task made necessary as global competition never stands still.

As this new “flexible corporation” begins to emerge – as one invests in the “gray elipse” (Figure 9) – a new type of enterprise takes shape. Such organizations will be smaller (for the same level of output). Note, however, that they are not smaller because one has “cut staff”. They are smaller because one has re-designed, or re-engineered, how one is going to do business.

The flexible organization will be flatter, more horizontal. This maximizes the contact points with the outside world and reduces the time delay for vertical communication. Such a flatter organization assumes effective use of I.T. to permit the shrinkage of time and distance, and installation of powerful new co-ordination mechanisms.

Because organizational boundaries are permeable to I.T., it becomes possible to link any segment of one's value added chain to any segment of any other organization. This disaggregation and re-combination permits the creation of whole new entities put together to achieve new value for the customer (Figure 10). In essence, to borrow a computer term, one is creating a “virtual corporation”. People are working together to create and deliver a product or service, and yet they are not legally part of the same corporation.

The extension of these ideas is that the flexible corporation is making greater use of the increasing number of “electronic markets” (see [6]). Some electronic markets, such as many financial markets, are well known, but such markets are now moving into the airline industry and a wide variety of other domains.

Flexible corporations are only now coming into existence. Lithonia (see [3]; Figure 11) is one such early example. However, there are divisions, or functions, within many corporations that are moving toward this model. To get there, however, involves a central task – that of the management of change. Serious systemic change is a non-trivial task that requires a consistent serious purpose beyond that experienced by many firms. The enabling conditions for such change seem to be:

(1) An organization-wide, shared vision of the future. This seems to be most effective when vigorously propagated by the CEO (see [4]).

(2) An I.T. infrastructure which involves an open systems architecture and understood, shared, data standards.

(3) A massive investment in education. Members of the organization are being asked to think about changes in the way they work and with whom they work – a major conceptual task, often requiring new skills.

(4) Creative human resource practices that encourage innovation and yet provide a safety net for those who take thoughtful risks in moving the organization forward.

In summary, the environment and global competition are producing an inexorable set of pressures which in turn are demanding new skills. At the same time, new technology is changing how work should most effectively be done. With fewer new entrants, the retention of employees (who are part of an ongoing training process) becomes key.

In other words, the decade of the 1990's is going to give new meaning to the concept of investing in human resources (Figure 12). At the very least, this will mean organizations are having to engage in a change process that is deeper and more fundamental than anything that Western corporations have yet done. It will become necessary to move toward an organization that continually adapts – a “learning corporation”.
