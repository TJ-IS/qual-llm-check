# Decision support systems: Scope and potential

F.J. Radermacher

FAW, Ulm, Germany

The paper addresses the scope of decision support systems starting from the three words, decision, support, and system, that make up the term. Based on a decision-theory point of view, the notion of decision and related cognitive concepts are discussed, particularly in connection with the idea of supporting human decision makers in making decisions. The aspect of systems brings in automatic devices that assist the decision maker on various levels of cognitive sophistication. This aspect includes a connection to artificial intelligence whenever a high degree of sophistication is required. With a view to these connections with AI, the paper takes a model-oriented approach, aiming at an integration of the modelling necessities from decision theory, from human-machine interfaces, from application domains, and from system Eigen models as the core of every advanced architecture in this field.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence; Biases; Decision support; Decision theory; Modelling; Optimization

## 1. Introduction

This paper was invited as a contribution to a volume on the topic: “15 years of decision support: a buzzword or a challenge?”. The paper is a very personal answer to this question, reflecting a number of experiences with new topics and terms.

The topic of decision support systems [3,4,18,19,20,32,33,34,35,36], now about 15 years old, has stimulated great interest in its research and application. It is probably correct to say that it has been the right topic at the right time. Moreover, there is evidence that this area has a promising future and that both the needs of and the means for good decision support will increase over time. The present paper takes up this topic from the perspective of a particular field, namely artificial intelligence. The background of this perspective is the need of support for application-oriented work in quite a number of topics ranging from computer-integrated manufacturing to office automation, environmental information systems, assistance systems, and the handling of distributed resources.

This paper first discusses, in section 2, the cognitive aspects of decision making. The aim here is to emphasize the point that decision making is one of the essential tasks that mankind must deal with on every level and at all times, and that human abilities in this task are among our most impressive cognitive strengths, and in fact are comparable in importance to every other aspect of human intelligence. In the subsequent section 3, we will ask what type of support can be given at all. Up to now, support has mainly concentrated on a low cognitive level. For example, support often takes the simple form of manipulation of data, with storage and retrieval, consistency checking, small calculations, updating processes, and so forth. Much less support has been given to the tough issues in decision making, namely, the use of the right kind of framing,

Similarly, the problem of finding hidden information in huge data sets has not yet been solved. However, such problems form the medium or high level of cognitively involved questions that decision makers must address all the time and would like to see supported by good systems. A very difficult question for future research is thus: What is the methodological basis for these higher cognitive levels of support? Asking this question leads naturally to the general topic of intelligent and cognitively competent systems, which in turn leads to the field of artificial intelligence and its strong connection with decision support.

This paper concentrates on this problem, and identifies modelling in a very general sense $[29,30]$ as the core of advanced system development in artificial intelligence. Modelling has a broad scope, and it is not necessarily limited to symbolic modelling, or, even more specifically, to the primarily logic-oriented models that dominate artificial intelligence today. Instead, all the classical tools from, for example, statistics, data analysis, operations research, decision analysis, and probability theory can be integrated into the more recent logic-oriented framework to build powerful symbolic working environments. Also envisioned in the future, moreover, is a coupling with neural networks and, at an even deeper level, with more fundamental (analog) intelligent operators. With respect to the future, also the use of cooperating autonomous agents has to be taken into account. With these discussions as context, section 5 comments on the actual state of decision support systems, which is seen as a topic of great potential, and not at all a buzz word or a “band wagon” topic. Instead, this area is found to be a challenging and fertile ground for researchers in our field, whose promising future is already visible but yet to come.

## 2. Cognitive aspects of decision making

Decision support deals with decision making. Decision making pervades almost all human activities, and it is perhaps the most constant challenge for individuals and societies. It might be argued that almost all key positions in society are oriented towards the need – and the right – to make decisions, hopefully good ones. This central role of decision making in society has perhaps not yet been fully articulated. The same is true for the need for good support for all this decision making. Despite the profusion of reference to decision-support needs and the many contributions in the field, the true needs are actually always surpassing our estimates. This is true for many day-to-day problems, but is even more true for the major issues concerning the survival of our species. We also tend to underestimate the power and importance of the human cognitive abilities at work in decision making, which can differ from person to person. The understanding in this paper is based on the position that making adequate decisions over long time periods in a changing environment and subject to incomplete information, misinformation, uncertainty, and changing preferences is one of the central and most sophisticated human cognitive abilities $[16,29]$ . Understanding the determinants of good decision making resembles the understanding of intelligent behavior. The extreme cognitive complexity of human decision making is to some extent still overlooked in artificial intelligence. In fact, as has been argued in a number of recent papers, the contributions of modern AI to the most difficult problems in decision making, such as those confronted in major industrial or political decisions, is comparatively small $[9,28]$ . There are a number of reasons for that, which can be summarized essentially as follows:

Hard decisions usually deal with processes over time. Major determinants of what will eventually happen are not controlled by the decision maker. Essential components come from general processes at work in the world, which include an enormous degree of chaotic behavior and certainly allow only limited options of guaranteeing or controlling particular outcomes. The situation becomes even more complicated by the involvement of other decision makers, who may act partly independently, but also partly in reaction or in the form of feedback. In general, it is not clear what other people will do, how they will react to one's own moves, how the general public will see certain things in the future, how politics in general will evolve. This uncertainty about the state of the environment and the limited cause-effect relationships within that part of the system where the decision maker has at least some control create an extremely difficult situation for decision support. But the situation is complicated even further by the difficult problems of varying preferences. Usually, whatever strategy is followed, there is an opportunity for different outcomes, and these different outcomes have a number of interesting aspects. Generally, there will be no strategy that gets dominating solutions in all respects in comparison to other strategies. So, generally, there will be a kind of tradeoff or balancing of different aspects against each other, meaning that preference modelling aspects will be involved, often for quite nasty tradeoff-situations. Usually, it is hard to formulate the preferences of even a single individual, and the problems are multiplied if different humans have to agree on one preference model. In this case, the indication of a personal preference might already have influenced the preference of others in one way or another. Thus the problems are extremely involved, and the situation would be nearly hopeless, if the decision makers did not have at least some influence on defining a posteriori what the outcome really is and how it is to be interpreted [9,28].

This very complicated situation imposes hard limitations on what can be done. In fact, we are often confronted with very weak forms of insight into particular situations, that is, with insights of limited predictive power in the sense that the statistical coincidence between our view of the outcome and the real outcome is loose at best. Furthermore, as a society we often need individuals to be responsible for an outcome, particularly if the outcome is bad. Thus, for several practical and psychological reasons (limited insight, personal responsibility, human influence on defining a situation), we need to have people involved in hard decision making. Involving individuals with their personal risk also seems to be society's best insurance against deliberate wrong-doing by the people in charge. With this in mind, we must look carefully for the kind of methodological frameworks that may be used in decision making and in decision support systems.

## 3. What support is possible?

If one looks at the kind of support that decision support systems give today, one finds a surprising emphasis (and importance) given to lowlevel support of all kinds. With this low-level support, which also is not unusual for many AI systems, the following tasks, among others, are addressed. A system may allow data storage and manipulation in many ways. Relations and connections might be dealt with, for example, in the form of hypertext, and local or even transitive consistency checks might be carried out. Lists may be produced, depending on the value of certain attributes, data may be stored and retrieved, put in different formats, and so on. Characteristic of this level is the organization of an enormous amount of computations of an easy but tedious nature. Typical examples are spread-sheet programs, which have made a number of interesting contributions to the field of decision support. Similarly, on that level, different kinds of statistical analysis may be applied in the form of simple manipulations of data and, equally important, of visualization of that data. An interesting observation is that visualization and organization often can be equally or even more important than optimization because there is not the right model framework to allow a reasonable optimization. Of course, the level of support described here is quite low and restricted, and much more is needed. Additions might include suggestions or proposals of what to do in particular situations, even when no optimization is involved. There might, for example, be a mechanism to prove the consistency of certain proposals, or to give consistent proposals, or to infer that there are no consistent proposals, hopefully along with some relevant information about how to eventually produce such proposals. Such facilities might help users and might shape their ideas of the problem. Of course, one should be aware that if a user too easily accepts suggestions made by the system, it might be because he or she attributes more power to the system (e.g., optimization) than it really has. Whereas the user may have expected only good or reasonable proposals, the system may actually have produced just one proposal and had no idea whatsoever of how good this proposal is. In fact, a similar situation is also possible if the system does optimization, for example in graph-theory problems such as a shortest-path computation. Here, the system might find a best solution, but the optimization may be true only with respect to an inappropriate framing or modelling. One should add that artificial intelligence introduced another aspect of support, namely, the chaining of small inferences of a logical syntactical nature (easy-going arguments), and that explanations based on such argumentation-chains can also be requested and delivered. Thus, a user is supplied with a whole set of arguments as to why certain things should be done in a particular way, and the user may be cognitively able to see whether the arguments are solid in themselves and decide to stay with the solution. Again, however, this by no means proves that the solution produced by the system is necessarily the best, or even satisfactory, in terms of arguability or fit within a given context.

To summarize, support today concentrates on lower levels of application problems. A huge number of tools and generators are available that include adequate interfaces, graphics, and visualization for such basic support. Basic data or description levels can also be combined with simple models of different types, for example, linear optimization and regression.

The open question is what more might be done in the future. A few comments address that question next, focusing on some of the very difficult issues; one is finding (hidden) dependencies in data $[26]$ , another is helping users find their preferences in a multiattributive decision situation, and a third concerns the handling of evidence in support systems. All these partial aspects of modelling of a particular application should eventually lead to framing a problem in a sophisticated model whose (perhaps only approximated) solutions might yield strong proposals for the solutions requested. Such sophisticated model-based background reasoning is what in the long run a user might and should expect from systems that call themselves “decision support systems”. Hopefully, such systems will improve decision making in many pressing practical applications where such a support is badly needed.

Returning to huge data sets, all kinds of statistical methods and tools from data analysis, such as correlation, regression analysis, or factorial analysis, the identification of statistical dependencies or of hidden variables in a statistical sense are about the best one may hope for in general settings. In cause-effect relationships, statistical evidence is important, as long as no deeper models have already been identified and (again statistically) validated.

Of essential importance from the point of view of decision theory is, as already mentioned, preference modelling, that is, trying to very carefully model tradeoffs between certain aims of a user (at least on the level of a single human). For this task, the available theory helps to exploit the potential of decision making in the best way possible. Still, one will generally be confronted with the risk of a “bad” decision (seen ex post), whatever was decided ex ante. Good decisions can in general at best increase the probability for a good outcome, but cannot guarantee it. As mentioned above, the evaluation of an outcome is a question of preferences, where preferences may change over time; even if a decision yields the solution originally preferred, it may no longer be wanted when realized. Luckily, it may also be the other way around.

The very involved nature of the intermingled aspects of preferences and uncertainty in decision making have somewhat limited the degree to which humans have a systematic and uniform ability to cope satisfactorily with difficult decision situations. It is known from cognitive psychology that a quite stable human reaction pattern is found only in relatively simple decision situations. In more complicated situations reactions may become instable, may be revised after a thorough examination, and may often also contradict certain logical principles embraced by the decision maker. Behavioral science research on such so-called Bias phenomena $[6,13]$ constitutes an important aid in the design of adequate user interfaces for advanced systems. The basic underlying concepts in this area, first formulated in the work and the central representation theorem of von Neumann and Morgenstern $[37]$ , identifies good decision making as maximizing the expected utility over an action space with regard to (subjective) probabilities for the world-states involved and with regard to a (multiattribute) utility function for the evaluation of (vectors of) results $[7,21]$ .

This utility-theory framework has now become very rich and has been successfully used in many applications. At present, a number of software tools, have also become available, for example, for finding single or multiattribute utility functions or for decomposition of such functions. Also available are quite general decision-theory framing systems, such as MAUD, PREFCALC and

TREEVAL. Similar tools are available in multi-person decision situations, especially of a game-theory nature, either in a cooperative or nonco-operative framework. However, given the much greater complexity with many people involved, the normative basis for solid inferences is naturally much more limited.

In this context, a sophisticated tool developed at the FAW in Ulm should be mentioned, namely the FAW preference elicitation tool $[17]$ . This tool addresses the frequently encountered situation in which a user gives a number of direct or indirect references to his or her preference function (perhaps the user is risk-averse, certain attributes may be more important than others). An ordinal choice among a number of alternatives, real or hypothetical, may also be given by the user. In addition users might constantly give indications of their preference functions by accepting or refusing proposals made by a decision support system. Such a system might then give not just one solution (as it does today) but evaluate thousands and thousands of solutions on the basis of a preference function of the particular user, as specified at that time so that the proposals made are the best proposals with respect to a present preference function and thousands of considered solution proposals. If, at some point, a user rejects a new proposal on the basis of a comparison to an older one, the user in effect has given an indirect indication that the preference function presently being used is not completely correct. It is important to be able to integrate this kind of feedback in addition to all the other indirect information in a PREFCALC-kind of approach, including means to check and communicate eventual inconsistencies. In the system $[17]$ developed at the FAW, this is done in a general way so that it may be used as an add-on in many developments of decision support systems. The scenario presumed is one in which groups of experts usually have formulated in advance attributes and scales for a problem area, while the personal preferences of an individual user are captured and reflected in that individual's particular preference function. Note that this approach can, when properly used, also be employed by groups, as long as the members can agree on basic choices. Also, this approach has a powerful extrapolation property; if ordinal choices from a number of alternatives lead to an approximate preference function, with which hundreds of alternatives might be ranked afterwards. Note that ranking so many alternatives explicitly is often impossible for humans or groups of humans, either for reasons of time or of cognitive stress. The FAW has successfully employed these extrapolation capabilities in applications concerning the design of a groundwater control network $[17a]$ in cooperation with the State of Baden-Württemberg Department of Environmental Protection.

In addition to preference modelling, another basic normative ingredient to general model building in decision support is probability theory. General principles such as ergodicity, limit theorems, best statistical tests, or, more recently, stochastic bounds make important contributions to the solution of practical problems. With general results and principles, probability theory partly overcomes data acquisition problems that it seems to have caused in the first place, and it does so in a normatively correct fashion [16].

Note that such general theories and theorems, as present, for example, in decision theory, probability theory, and statistics appear to offer the strongest inference mechanisms available for use with vague data. For reasons of efficiency and consistency, their use is recommended over other forms of knowledge-processing, whenever possible $[16]$ . It is worth mentioning here that in a number of interesting applications the stochastic aspects are the dominant elements of modelling. Thus, in certain situations, good systems should do automatic stochastication and a subsequent sensitivity analysis $[2,16]$ , if needed, and communicate identified risks to the user (who may be entirely ignorant of the theory behind such system performance).

In this context, it should be pointed out that classical theories for the treatment of preferences and probabilities, when properly employed [26], allow the handling of most problems recently addressed under the notion of fuzziness. Such treatment is based on solid scientific insight and corresponding theorems. Naturally, certain extensions of classical models, such as the Dempster-Shafer theory of evidence [31], must be integrated with a solid theoretical foundation.

To summarize, decision making is a particularly difficult subject from a cognitive point of view. Additional support is particularly reasonable for decisions of a sophisticated nature but a limited social impact. The more responsibility a certain decision carries the more support will be limited to information collection and recommendations. But in all cases, the employment of the typical models for this area that deal with data-identification and with notions of uncertainty and preference modelling are in order. Classical tools for this purpose are available, and new tools are being developed at places such as the Research Institute for Applied Knowledge Processing (FAW) in Ulm [18,24]. Such basic research efforts should make the respective models easier to handle and integrate, and thus bring them into more effective use in decision support, thereby extending decision support far beyond the scope of topics adequately addressed by such systems at present.

## 4. Modelling and artificial intelligence: the core of advanced systems

When talking about decision support via automated systems, the major question is always the cognitive power and adequacy of the system providing the support. Clearly, there can be no end to the arguing and questioning, as long as there is a significant gap between the user's and the system's insight into the nature of a problem. The higher the level of the problem and the greater the expertise of the user, the harder it is to give comparable support. Because the cognitive power and the intelligence of the support system are so essential, model management and artificial intelligence must form basic cornerstones for all system developments in this area.

In discussions about intelligent systems or artificial intelligence in general, notions of intelligence, learning, adaptation, insight, and, at a very high level, pre-consciousness or consciousness all have immediate connotations. Of course, the question of the nature of intelligence and, similarly, of the potential of artificial intelligence is an old one, for which many answers have been suggested, but the questions are hardly fully settled $[5,14,15,30,38]$ . The traditional paradigm of artificial intelligence is that symbol manipulation is at the core of intelligent behavior. With the strong emphasis on neural networks in recent years, however, this view is changing. The present paper, therefore, takes a different perspective, already discussed in a number of publications [1,2,3,16,22,23,29], and sees modelling and the manipulation of models as the core of intelligent behavior and thus of competent decision support. Herein, modelling is to be understood in a sense that surpasses symbol manipulation [30], and encompasses, for example, aspects inherent in pure geometric form or chemical structure (cf. the working of the human immune system) or in the dynamic aspects of neural networks, seen as a kind of blackbox modelling of the surrounding world. Even within the symbolic processing paradigm, the types of models that might be used go far beyond models of a logical nature, or inheritance, or decomposition and constrained propagation mechanisms that presently dominate the approaches used in AI applications. Other interesting models that should come into view are, for example, models from optimization theory, statistics, decision theory, stochastics, game theory, differential equations, theory of automata, data bases, and others [16], as already argued above. In fact, there is a particular potential in employing the more recent frameworks from artificial intelligence as a meta representation scheme with which to integrate more classical modelling frameworks towards specific goals, in particular, decision support. Meta-level knowledge to be represented will especially concern the ways in which human professionals use and employ the whole range of classical tools for problem solving. Symbolic manipulation as seen in this broad context can be particularly helpful with regard to decision making and decision support, as discussed above. Here we simply note that viewing intelligence as the ability to master a variety of models of the environment allows the easy integration of other concepts as well. The ability to modify and manipulate such models in response to experience can be seen as processes of learning and adaptation; finding invariants, dependencies, and connections within such models are forms of insight and the basis for future model variations and adaptations. Proceeding this way, hierarchies of Eigen models of systems may eventually be seen as forms of a preconsciousness of systems and even as the very first step towards a consciousness of systems [30]. Certainly, these are difficult issues of a basic and philosophical nature that reach into the future. Nevertheless, the framework of model manipulation, when seen from a broad enough perspective, seems to hold

enormous potential to cope with phenomena encountered in all areas of real life. Consequently, the test bed for the modelling and model management approach to (artificial) intelligence should be real-life applications, where the essence is the ability to model relevant parts of the world in a meaningful way and to draw relevant conclusions (inferences) from such models as a basis for further actions. Herein, notions like “meaningful” must ultimately be traced back to the evolutionary principle of “relative fitness” or “survival” as the final criteria of “successful behavior.” Of course, “reality” may shrink to sub-worlds, when attention and performance requirements are restricted to very specific domains. Extreme specializations of this type are “toy worlds,” such as the block worlds in special robotics applications or the humanmade “artificial worlds” of simple games or specific mathematical theories. However, the more we deal with real-life applications, and decision problems are usually of this type, the more we must deal with phenomena that humans themselves understand only to a certain extent. That is, we deal with a reality for which we possess no more than (perhaps quite elaborate) models that are open to falsification in the course of scientific progress. As we know such modifications, sometimes major, have occurred several times in the history of science [25,27]. Given this emphasis on evolutionary principles, building such mechanisms into intelligent systems seems to be a very important point, and to some extent learning (in essence) seems to be partially of that nature. Considering the data situation, trying to find out what dependencies, connections, or hidden variables might be there, making reasonable decisions on what to assume, staying to some extent with such decisions over time (a form of truth maintenance), monitoring one’s own performance, and then, on a higher level, trying to intermix different but partly successful approaches into always new generations of systems is the kind of genetic feedback cycle one should have in mind. This includes genetic algorithms and special variants of this approach such as simulated annealing or the so-called tabu method [10,11]. From this position, evolution is seen as the meta-principle that naturally accompanies every evolved process of adaptation, learning, and reproduction. It allows us to understand how higher levels of life eventually carry models of their environment. Models are very coarse in the beginning, reducing the whole world, for example, to two states, but they become more involved over time in the evolutionary process driven by the ultimate criteria of survival. Often to survive is to attain a higher degree of granularity in modelling; for example, splitting given states into sub-cases can be an important achievement. The principles involved can be characterized as processes of recombination and randomization. Recombination is a safe way to produce new solutions in an implicitly defined solution space. Randomization is a smoothing device and a cautious way of extending the borderline of an implicitly defined solution space. The driving force is the survival criterion that statistically gives a higher degree of influence on shaping the next generation to individuals that “behave” better. With sexual reproduction and group coherence, more weight is placed on carrying latent information, spreading information among group members, or even carrying mutually beneficial abilities over various cooperating group members.

Imitating the evolutionary process is the best hope for really “smart” systems which should eventually show very surprising and impressive behavior. There is a promising approach in this line of thought mentioned already above, namely the so-called “evolution strategy” or “genetic algorithms” [8,12]. Weak versions of evolutionary strategies are statistical adaptation, simulated annealing, and other related approaches which, in their simplest form mean that one favors actions successful in earlier stages relative to other available actions by decreasing accordingly the probability of activating unsuccessful actions. This is a simple way of accumulating knowledge and system experience over time. This approach can be combined with possible modifications of the notion of “situation” (i.e., the basic entities) through simple set-theory or logic operators. Often, local optimization alone leads only to locally good solutions or notions. The annealing principle, therefore, to a certain extent prefers worse solutions to better ones in order to avoid local optimality traps. For reasons of stability, the probability of accepting bad steps decreases over time, following natural analogies to thermodynamic processes. Of course, all such “blind” adaptive processes are only recommended if deeper (human) insight is missing.

Also, the more vague and unintentional these “learning” or “adaptive” processes become, the slower they work. There is often no way of using old collected data for training and acceleration of such an adaptive process, as the interaction of the constantly modified system with this old data would be missing. Thus, adaptation is slow; it is comparable to the adaptation of living beings, and it limits exaggerated expectations of applications in typical sophisticated problem areas involving constant human–machine interaction.

## 5. Decision support systems: a topic, potential, not a buzz word

Many of the remarks and hints given here have already emphasized a particular point of view on the subject. Decision support is an important umbrella framework for many research activities that are badly needed now and in the future. Eight years ago Henk Sol asked the question [32]: "Is decision support a buzz word or a new challenge?" The answer is that it is a new challenge and not a buzz word. But his question raises the general issue of band-wagon labels and buzz words, on which I would like to comment. In the development of science, situations always arise in which new words, new descriptions, new phrases are "in". They are around and they almost have to be invited. They can organize the scientific process and they are often badly needed to circumvent our conservative, and often quite unflexible, structures in the sciences – and may be even more useful in application fields. On the other hand, personal chance is involved in constantly creating new words, even for old topics and even without any real need other than personal interest or promotion. The more people look for those personal chances, the more eager they may also be not to have other people standing under the new umbrella. (Clearly, there may also be good scientific reasons for not wanting this.) Certainly then, a sociological problem has emerged. With respect to decision support, for example, many people worked in decision support before this phrase came up, and hundreds of people work in decision support today without ever using the term. In fact, once a new term arises, it makes the situation particularly difficult for those who have already been in that field a long time. They must choose whether to adopt the new phrase, or to try to prevent the term's usage, or to simply ignore it. All three alternatives have their particular pros and cons. In any case, it is very much a personal question of flexibility and of judgement as to how much potential a particular phrase at a particular time actually has. It is certain that at particular times words can change a situation totally and that even without any change in scientific content, people adapting the term may have advantages. In fact, some people may hide behind such a word, hoping to gain exclusive advantages from this policy. Talking about buzz words or band wagons may then also be instrumental in stabilizing such a situation. That is, in some situations, the creation of a new word is a particularly unfair way to free oneself from the stiff competition found in established fields. The more this is true, the more those people who are already working in competitive, established fields may legitimately use this word in their own sense and for their own purposes whenever it seems right and appropriate to them. In this very dynamic interaction of old and new groups, old and new words, society discovers which topics are right and where one should invest. On a higher level of interpretation, therefore, the following proposal seems reasonable: whenever someone invents a new topic, that originator should see it as a success if good established groups join in behind it. This is certainly what happened with decision support. It is a topic that addresses a basic need and has a lot of connotations. It deals with decisions, an important human task, and it talks about support, which is very much in favor of people who want to maintain their competence. Naturally, such a topic must have a future, and in many of senses, over the long run it may gain and keep a comparable importance with the extremely successful notion of artificial intelligence. More easily than AI, it allows the integration of topics from classical fields of all kinds from optimization to stochastics, to decision theory, and so forth. With its bridges to AI as well as to decision making, decision support offers all the potential and all the challenge for the future that a field can hope for.
