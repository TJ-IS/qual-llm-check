# Decision support systems: Learning from visual interactive modelling

Valerie Belton \* and Mark D. Elder

Department of Management Science, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, Scotland G1 1XH, UK

The two research fields of, in the first instance, Decision Support Systems (DSS) and then secondly, Visual Interactive Modelling (VIM) have developed by mostly independent research. Yet these two fields seek many common goals. The VIM field has been seen as a largely technical development aiding certain Operational Research methods. On the other hand DSS have developed in the wider context of a recognition of a need to provide directed and helpful information to support decision making. In this paper we develop, firstly, an understanding of VIM in the context of current thinking on DSS. Ostensibly, it would seem that VIM is simply a DSS with a sophisticated user interface. However, we argue that VIM is an approach which has at its core an ability to allow decision makers to learn about their own subjective values while modelling technical issues. We suggest that embracing this learning objective at the design stage will enhance the power of Visual Interactive Models in particular and Decision Support Systems in general.

Keywords: Decision support systems; Visual interactive modelling; Learning

## 1. Introduction

In 1987, in a paper reflecting on the next decade of Decision Support Systems (DSS), Peter Keen suggested a shift in the definition for understanding DSS from

"The application of available and suitable computer-based technology to help improve the effectiveness of managerial decision making in semi-structured tasks

Applying intellectual and computer-related technologies to help improve creativity and learning for decisions that really matter." (our emphasis)

He proposed an agenda for research which focused on the following questions:

“(1) What decisions really do matter in an organisation and how should we build better environments to help decision makers handle them?

(2) How should we formulate and help solve ‘wicked’ problems that require representing values, ethics and aesthetics?

(3) How do creative people learn? How should we design our DSS tools to facilitate a real dialogue that stimulates learning?

The principle objective of this paper is to illustrate that the Visual Interactive Modelling (VIM) approach can make a significant contribution to the development to DSS in this direction.

Although the two fields have much in common VIM and DSS have developed largely independently of each other. In the next section of the paper we comment on the reasons for this. We each then describe our own involvement in VIM – experiences which differ in the depth and nature of involvement, and in the type of application, but which both arrived at the same conclusion – that a principle benefit of VIM is to enhance learning. In the following section, reflecting on these experiences, we propose a categorisation of learning for VIM and relate this to some of the literature on learning. Finally, we comment on the significance for VIM and DSS and on the way forward.

## 2. Historical context

The field of Decision Support System is undoubtedly well established. There is an extensive literature, a regular conference series and a journal on the subject. Objectives for DSS in terms of academic development and practical implementation were well established early on, and have been extensively documented and debated in the literature.

Conversely there is little written on Visual Interactive Modelling. The field has developed with little scholarly comment, debate or direction. VIM has been developed in an ad hoc manner principally by practitioners [3] to meet their needs, as they emerged. It was originally seen as a technical development applicable to a few Operational Research techniques.

We briefly review and contrast developments of VIM and DSS. The driving forces behind each were substantially different but nevertheless they share a number of common goals and there is potential for mutually beneficial exchange of ideas.

## 2.1. Decision Support Systems

The development of the Decision Support System field is well described in the literature. In the mid seventies the Information Systems field was criticised for failing to provide useful management tools. For example, Mintzberg [34] wrote that “.. it has become increasingly evident that these giant MIS systems are not working – that managers are simply not using them..”. Keen and Scott Morton [28] formalised the concept of Decision Support Systems in their seminal text. This came at a time when a response was required to the challenge to MIS if Information Systems were to continue to provide effective managerial support. Much was written on the research and development work which preceded the formalisation of the concept of DSS and which continued to accompany the growth of the field – providing a substantial grounding to aid developers of DSS. This is indicated by a recent review [14] which cites no less than 9 authors (page 66) as contributing to the definition of DSS in the period from 1978 to 1982.

Emergent from these early writings on DSS are a number of aims which are summarized by Sol [37] as follows:

“.. the development of approaches for applying information systems technology to increase the effectiveness of decision makers in situations where the computer can support and enhance human judgement in the performance of tasks that have elements which cannot be specified in advance…”

We think this is a useful definition of DSS because of its breadth and formulation in terms of an objective for DSS, rather than a technical specification.

In our view, an essential distinguishing feature of the DSS field is that it recognises a need to link the judgemental powers of the decision maker and the computational analysis powers of the computer.

## 2.2. Visual Interactive Modelling

In common with DSS the development of VIM was in response to a practical challenge, but in contrast, it was not accompanied by academic debate.

Visual Interactive Modelling was developed in response to Operational Research practitioners' need for a communication tool [3], [5], [23]. Bell [3] describes how VIM development was a “.. practitioner to practitioner phenomenon.” By this he means that it was developed by practitioners and sold (literally) to other practitioners.

VIM progressed from being a concept specifically associated with simulation $[23]$ to one with a much wider range of applicability $[15]$ . Practical needs of the time lead to ad hoc developments. Organisations such as Alcan $[40]$ desired to extend their success in the simulation field to scheduling problems and this stimulated commercial organisations to extend the facilities of VIM tools beyond simulation. Products such as OPTIK $[25]$ and PROVISA $[2]$ were marketed. The approach is currently used by many Operational Research units $[29]$ .

VIM has, perhaps, suffered as a result of less direction and thought by the academic world than that enjoyed by DSS. The term VIM is often interpreted loosely to mean an interactive computer system with a graphical interface; however the pioneers of the VI approach and those who now use it regularly, understand it to be much more than that. Despite its undoubted practical success it has received little attention from the DSS community. (Exceptions are: Turban and Carlson [39], Angehrn and Luthi [1])

Thus, in comparison to DSS, VIM has developed in an ad hoc manner and without clear guiding principles. However, it is now a well established field and those who use it are in no doubt that it increases the effectiveness of decision making, particularly in some tasks which have elements which cannot be specified in advance.

## 3. Definition of VIM

As with DSS it is difficult to give a simple definition which captures the richness of the field.

We feel that the following largely captures the essence of VIM and its aims:

A Visual Interactive Model combines meaningful pictures and easy interactions to stimulate creativity and insight; promoting a process of 'generate and test', it facilitates a rapid cycle of learning.

It is our aim in the remainder of this paper to describe how the two authors, working in separate fields, have reached a similar conclusion about the benefits VIM provides to decision makers and how this matches with Peter Keen's suggested shift of emphasis in the DSS field, quoted in the introduction to this paper.

## 4. Experiences of VIM

We saw in the previous section that VIM is a much smaller field than DSS. Bell [4], [6] and Bell and O'Keefe [5] give a comprehensive history of the development of VIM and we do not wish to repeat this here, but rather, to present the experiences of the two authors with VIM. The purpose of this is to illustrate, in a more comprehensive way than can be achieved in a definition, the nature of VIM and how the two authors reached the same conclusions about its potential.

## 4.1. Experience one

In order to bring out the character of VIM this first story will take the reader through the emergence of ten benefits of using VIM. These are described as they are seen with hindsight.

As we indicated earlier, there is no historical evidence of a wish to achieve these benefits. VIM developed incrementally, initially with only simple aims such as communicating more easily with clients.

This author was involved in the development of the first commercial VI simulation package, SEE-WHY. This was developed by the OR unit in the UK automobile manufacturer British Leyland [16]. The reasons for its development and that of other early VI modelling systems (see Hollocks, [19]) are summarised in benefits 1 to 3 listed below.

(1) Convince the client that the solution is “right” It was seen that the results of simulation studies were often not implemented and it was presumed that the reason for this was lack of confidence on the part of the client that the solution identified by the analysts was indeed the correct one. If clients could be “shown” the solution in operation, using the model, they might then be more willing to accept it.

## (2) Understanding of Technique

Simulation was viewed as a black art which made use of a black box. If the clients could see the model in operation, in a manner similar to an animated board game, they might understand simulation better and appreciate its power as a decision making tool.

(3) Communication of Sensitivity Analysis
The ramifications of changes in assumptions could also be communicated.

As experience was gained of the use of VIM in practice it was observed that there were benefits in addition to those initially sought.

## (4) Avoiding Mistakes

On the face of it this is a trivial point but when every detail of a model's operation can be seen on a screen it is very easy to see when it is behaving in a way different from that intended by the programmer.

## (5) Better Solutions more Quickly

This is where VIM started to take on a new order of importance. It was quickly realised that it was now much easier for analysts to find good solutions. Watching a model on a colour computer screen made it much easier to quickly move towards the correct ‘ball park’ for the solution. It was easy to see causes of problems and this helped generate good ideas.

## (6) Control Rules

Seeing a model on a screen you can watch each individual instance of control rules in action. This makes it possible to see when circumstances occur which would benefit from modifications in control rules. VIM made it possible to use simulation models to analyze and modify control rules more accurately.

## (7) Experimental Journey

Solutions are rarely found in one simple step. This means that when a solution is presented to a client (even using a VIM) the client has to undertake a conceptual leap which even the analyst did not have to make. Because of the improved communication as a result of VIM it became possible to involve the client in the complete solution finding process. VIM made it feasible to position the analyst in the client's office. Each model run and analysis session could involve the client. Thus the client could go on the same experimental journey as the analyst.

All this was realised in a matter of a 3 or 4 years and the VI concepts had only been applied to simulation!

Due to client demand the VI approach was extended to other techniques. As experience was gained in using the technique for many applications it emerged that there were benefits of a more significant nature than those reported above.

The following benefits are to do with helping the client acknowledge and incorporate subjective and judgemental views in the process of analysis.

## (8) Handle Judgemental Issues

Once clients had been thoroughly involved in the modelling and problem solving process, it became clear that we were able to tackle a much larger slice of the “problem”. There are many aspects of problems which cannot be easily measured in numerical terms and are a matter of “judgement”. The clients are the only people qualified to make choices in such a situation.

Because a VIM allows clients to see the numerically handleable facets of a decision situation easily, they can take these quantified aspects into account while using their own abilities as a decision maker to deal with the judgemental aspects. Furthermore, when a client considers making a particular decision the interactive aspects of a VIM allow the client to quickly try out this potential decision to see the consequences on the modelled aspects (via visual feedback).

Before moving on to the next of these benefits it is necessary to explore what is meant by the phrase “subjective views”. “Subjective” means “of the individual”; in the case of views, those which have developed in a way which is specific to the individual. They are based on the individual’s previous knowledge and experience. Some views are the same as the views of other individuals. They are based on experiences which are very common. (They are regularly occurring and experienced in the same way by other individuals). However, some views are not held in quite the same way by other individuals. Managers in different parts of the same organisation may hold different views on the relative importance of, say, smoothing labour requirements and minimising WIP because of differing previous experiences. Continuing along this dimension, some people will not hold opinions (or will have very weak opinions) on some issues because of little experience in a field or because they have little desire to think about and reflect on experiences they might have had in a field.

Having experiences, and reflecting on these experiences, has the effect of creating structures in the mind onto which one can pin further thoughts. Neisser [35] calls these structures “schema”. The structures can be reinforced by experiences which match the structure and can be weakened or modified by experiences which are in some way incompatible with the structure. The firmer the structures, the more use they are to us in understanding further experiences or making statements about our own views.

In running a business we often have to make judgements when we do not have as much information to hand as we would desire. The missing information is either factual (like the price of materials next month) or judgemental (like the importance of finishing a particular order on time). Considering the judgemental information, a decision maker will use intuition in making a judgement. The decision maker is perhaps not able to state a clear set of logic which tells you why a particular decision is made because it was made on the basis of some incomplete and unclear structure.

The final two benefits relate to the decision maker's subjective views.

(9) Understand and clarify one's subjective views.

Visual Interactive Models can be a source of experiences to help clients learn about their own subjective views. VIMs do this by providing fast, understandable feedback about the consequences of any decision the clients might try. Clients are able to explore their own subjective decision space. They are able to get many examples of the affect of applying constraints or priorities which they perhaps have in mind and, as a result, learn how important each aspect of a subjective issue is to them. They are able to start to make explicit in their own minds the nature of underlying sub-conscious views.

(10) Changing one's subjective views.

Just as clients can learn the nature of their sub-conscious views they can come to change these views. Through the experience of making them explicit, they perhaps find inconsistencies and incompatibilities either between the subjective views themselves or between their subjective views and external (modelled) issues.

Again, it is the ability of a VIM to encourage fast and stimulating cycling around a “learning loop” that causes this possibility to arise.

These final two benefits mentioned in this first story of the development of VIM are both related to “learning” of some kind. In the first case the clients are learning (in the sense of understanding already existing things which exist deep in their minds). In the second case the clients are learning in the sense that thoughts in their minds are being changed.

## 4.2. Experience two

Meanwhile, working independently, the second author was seeking an effective vehicle for the communication of sensitivity analysis in multiple criteria decision models. Existing software allowed the easy investigation of the effect of changes to a single parameter of the model – usually a weight reflecting the relative importance of one criterion – but to investigate the effect of simultaneous changes in two or more parameters required time consuming and clumsy operations of the available software. Inspired by the work of Korhonen and Laasko [31] in developing VIG (Visual Interactive Goal programming) Belton [8] proposed and with Vickers subsequently developed, V.I.S.A [10],[11] – a program for visual interactive sensitivity analysis within the framework of a multi-attribute value (MAV) function. The program does not help with problem structuring, it is simply a tool for analysis. Once a preliminary structure has been developed and entered, V.I.S.A allows the interactive input and change of scores (the performance of options with respect to individual criteria) and weights (scaling factors reflecting the relative importance of criteria – and thus the users' priorities), using a simple visual representation of the problem. As values are input and changes made, the effect of these is seen immediately.

As suggested above, the motivation of developing V.I.S.A was to overcome some limitations of existing software. Earlier work with decision making groups had revealed that they sought more rapid feedback (see, for example, Belton [7]) and experience suggested that analyses tended to be confined to those that were easily facilitated by the available software. Thus it was anticipated that the extended facilities provided by V.I.S.A would encourage more extensive analysis both by the analyst and the problem owners.

Naturally, after some initial experience with the new software we began to reflect on how it was influencing the process of decision making. It was felt that the impact was more significant than had been anticipated initially. In trying to assess the benefits of using V.I.S.A we wished to distinguish between benefits derived from using the model – which are already well documented [7], [9], [12], [41] and the additional benefits derived from the VI implementation. The following claims are, at this point in time, based on observation and experience. They are not proven benefits, nor do we know to what extent they are generalisable across situations, problem types, decision making or cognitive styles.

It seemed that in providing the facility for decision makers to easily investigate the implications of changes in the parameters of the model what we had actually provided was a more effective vehicle for learning. The process of building a model of an abstract system of values and beliefs can be regarded as a process of disaggregation and explication of judgements which are then re-aggregated to reflect the whole, seeking a convergence between intuition and the model. Only when such convergence is achieved can the model be said to be requisite $[36]$ . The VI addition to the MAV model enables the user to identify more easily where mismatches between the model and intuition occur, to learn from these and to revise the model, by altering the structure or updating current values, or their intuition as appropriate.

Thus, in separate fields which were both making use of VIM, there emerged some recognition that “learning” might be one of VIM’s benefits.

## 5. Learning

The word “learning” is in common use in everyday language. We use it in the way it is used by psychologists; to mean “change” in the mind (see, for example, Hill [18]). It can be a change in the sense of an increase in information. But it can also be a change in the structure of the way one thinks about some subject, or in how one values something.

The notions of learning, understanding, educating ... underlie many approaches to decision support, as the following quotes illustrate:

“.. SSM [Soft Systems Methodology] is a learning system. The learning is about a complex human situation ....” Checkland [13]

“.. the process of modelling stimulates the user's learning about the unstructured situation.” Angehrn and Luthi [1]

"I believe that decision analysis is a ... tool that helps decision makers explore and come to understand their beliefs and preferences ..." French [17]

“Metagame analysis is ‘Socratic’: it asks the client questions, by answering which it (the client) educates itself. When answers are analysed, this is … to summarize and state clearly what has already been sensed intuitively.” Howard [20]

It is clear from the above statements and the agenda for DSS set out in the introduction to this paper that the notion of learning is of central importance to DSS. The experiences recounted earlier arrived at the same conclusion for VIM. However, it is a matter of concern to us that there appears to have been little consideration of what learning means within the context of DSS, nor any attempt to relate it to established learning research in other disciplines. The concept of learning is a complex, multi-faceted one. We feel that in order to design DSS which effectively promote learning we should be more aware of what it is we are trying to promote. Towards this end we propose a categorisation of learning which we feel is in accordance with our experience of using VIM's.

Firstly we feel it is important to make the simple distinction between “how” (“learning” as a verb) and “what” (“learning” as a noun). To our surprise we have not encountered this distinction in the literature; some writings seem to be primarily concerned with “what”, others with “how”, and yet others integrate the two.

We believe that the aim of a decision support system should be to bring the decision maker to a level of understanding of the problem situation and their own subjective values which generates commitment to a specified course of action. The “what” of learning is the insights, clarification and explication that lead to this understanding. The “how” is the process by which these are achieved.

Our experiences with VIM have led us to be aware of what is learned. Clearly, if such learning is occurring then VIM must also be facilitating the “how”, the process of learning. In the remainder of this section we suggest five categories of “what” is learned in accordance with our experiences, and reflect on the literature on “how” learning occurs and the way in which this can be related to VIM.

## 5.1. "What" is learned

We propose five categories for what is learned:

Discovery - understanding logical relationships

Explication - formulation of un-articulated views

Clarification - understanding the implications of articulated views

Change - adopting new views

Creation – generation of new ideas

In order to expand on these categories we will make use of the tripartite representation of problem solving illustrated in Fig.1.

The process of decision support may concern itself with the identification and exploration of any of these spaces and with the understanding and exploration of links between the spaces. We will expand briefly on each of the five categories listed above.

Discovery The process of identifying and understanding links between decision space and solution space. For example, a simulation model of an automated warehouse may enable the user to see clearly the relationship between the number of AGVs and time to withdraw items from the warehouse.

Explication The process of formulating links between solution space and value space – namely, identifying which criteria are important in the evaluation of solutions, the relationships and interactions between those criteria. These links need not, necessarily, be formalised in the model.

Exploring and understanding the links established between solution space and value space. If the links were formalised as a multiple criteria model this would be the equivalent of sensitivity analysis.

As a result of new information coming to light, a changing environment, persuasive argument, or exploration of the model, the decision maker's values may change, causing the link between solution space and value space to be redrawn.

Creation The generation of new ideas, or a broadening of the problem, resulting in an enlarged decision space.

The three spaces are not necessarily clearly distinguished in all decision problems. For example, in a multiple criteria analysis it may not be necessary to distinguish between decision space and solution space – the analysis is focused on the link between solution space and value space. Most VIM's have not explicitly identified value space or the link to solution space, that part of the problem has remained an unarticulated mental model of the decision maker. However, “explification” and “clarification” of the decision maker's mental model of the link between solution space and value space can still occur as the VIM is used to gain experience of the solution space. It is exactly to this that benefits 9 and 10 outlined earlier refer.

The categories of learning we have identified here correspond to an extent with the levels of analysis identified by Humphreys et al [21], [22].

## 5.2. "How" it is learned

There is much work in the fields of Cognitive Psychology and Organisational Behaviour on the process of learning. Much of it is concerned with the learning of skills or facts $[32]$ , but some is relevant to the type of learning with which we are concerned. The literature we have found most relevant to date is the collection of papers edited by Sternberg and Smith $[38]$ on “The Psychology of Human Thought”, in particular, the paper by Johnson-Laird $[26]$ which proposes a taxonomy of thinking, corresponding to our “how” of learning, and work on learning cycles, for example that by Kolb et al (1984).

Johnson-Laird categorises thinking into thinking without a goal (daydreaming) and goal directed thinking. Within the latter category he identifies calculation, deterministic progress through a set of operations, and nondeterministic thought, of the kind which takes place in most problem solving activity. Nondeterministic thought is further categorised into creativity, deduction and induction. Apart from this he identifies self-reflection, or metacognition, the process of thinking about how we think.

The notion of a learning cycle which incorporates reflection on experience or focused thought is identified in the psychological literature $[33]$ and the organisational behaviour literature $[30]$ .

We feel that the principle contribution of VIM to aiding the “how” of learning is the provision of a facility to generate and test ideas and to go rapidly around a learning loop. This is made possible by the effective, carefully designed, visual displays combined with easy to use interactive facilities. A well designed display, or meaningful picture, is one which the user can easily and immediately relate to the problem, which captures all the important elements of the problem (which may be the operation of a physical process, a planning board, the user's subjective judgement, .... or a combination of such elements), and which stimulates the creation of new ideas.

## 6. Summary

We began this paper by stating an agenda for DSS which placed significant emphasis on the understanding and promotion of creativity and learning. We described two experiences of VIM which led us to the inference that a major benefit of VIM is the facilitation of learning. This prompted us to explore the concept of learning, and the way in which it is supported by VIM, in greater depth, leading to the categorisation of learning proposed above. These suggestions were based on hindsight, derived from our experiences with VIM. However we feel that they are potentially significant for the design of VIM in particular and DSS in general.

Returning to Peter Keen's agenda for DSS and the learning related issues it raises, we feel there are two levels at which DSS designers can benefit by consideration of the experiences with VIM reported here. Firstly, we feel that VIM is an approach which has proved to be effective in stimulating learning and as a whole offers significant potential for the enhancement of the field of DSS. Secondly, we believe that a survey of DSS and VIM would reveal examples which together have supported the whole spectrum of “what” and “how” of learning we have identified. However, we do not know to what extent consideration of these issues guided the design of these systems. We speculate that a methodology for designing DSS, including VIM, based on explicit consideration of learning issues could result in more effective systems.

The suggestions put forward in this paper are based on hindsight and one of the aims of writing the paper is to seek feedback from others in the field. Our intention for the future is to seek to verify our claims for VIM through experimentation and action research and to increase our understanding of the “what” and “how” of learning with VIM to enable us to provide more effective support.
