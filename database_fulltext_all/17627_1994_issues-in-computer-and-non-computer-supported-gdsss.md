# Issues in computer and non-computer supported GDSSs

Fran Ackermann \* and Colin Eden

Department of Management Science, University of Strathclyde, 26 Richmond Street, Glasgow G1 1XH, Scotland, UK

Considerable attention in recent years, especially in the US, has been paid to computer based Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS). These systems expect to capitalise on the benefits provided by computers and networks. These computer based GDSSs are heralded as being relatively new, however systems for supporting group decision making developed in the UK which use only manual methods and techniques have been available for much longer. This paper aims to examine these two types of group decision support system, and suggest a third type – that of the partially computer based system. The paper considers aspects of GDSS design such as location, flexibility of design, levels of participation in data capture, presentational difficulties, managing complexity of data, client control, and management of group dynamics. By so doing the paper aims to demonstrate that all three types of system can benefit from consideration of the other types, that each type has both positive and negative features, and that some combination of all of the types could provide groups with the best form of support.

Keywords: Group Decision Support Systems; Computers; Group decision making; Interactive modelling

## 1. Introduction

What is a Group Decision Support System (GDSS) and why are they being developed? GDSSs have emerged in order to enable managers to deal with the increasing complexity of their jobs generated through the escalating amounts of information available in a group setting. Although Decision Support Systems (DSS) have been available for some time, these systems only supported individuals and provided little aid for groups of decision makers. This lack of support was pointed out by Keen [32] at the Decision Support Systems conference when he commented that most decisions are made by groups rather than individuals. GDSSs have been around for some time, particularly in the US and UK, although they have rarely been defined as such. Strategic Choice [24] and Soft Systems Methodology [10] are both examples of methodologies developed in the UK for supporting the decision making processes of groups, and Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing – SAST [38] is an example from the US. They are however, predominantly non computer supported and rely instead on tools such as large sheets of paper, ‘blutac $^{tm}$ ’ and coloured pens. They are also reliant on facilitation. Nevertheless they are quite explicitly systems of support to help groups make decisions [23] through the use of designed methods of problem construction [48].

In contrast computer supported GDSS have recently become an area of great interest in the United States and considerable amounts of money are being invested in their development. The definition of these systems is more specific than their non-computer supported counterparts. One of the earliest definitions by Huber [29] presents a GDSS as “a set of software, hardware and language components and procedures that support a group of people engaged in a decision related meeting”. The other most commonly quoted definition originates from DeSanctis and Gallupe [15] who suggest that “a GDSS aims to improve the process of group decision making by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for structuring decision analysis and systematically directing the pattern, timing or content of discussion”. Notably the first definition focuses primarily on the technological characteristics of the system, whereas the second is more concerned with identifying the functions of a GDSS. Whilst it appears from the literature that US researchers are doing the majority of work in this specific area of GDSS it is also a growing field of interest within Europe [47], [33].

The emergent US designed GDSSs through their focusing on computers are “hands on”, allowing each group member to enter ideas or numerical estimates relating to a problem directly into the system rather than rely on the facilitator or another to capture them. These systems then allow members of the group, rather than just the group leader, to manipulate the data according to their wishes and the system's facilities and undertake other activities in relation to analysis which may be available within the system. This requires the group members to know how to use the system and to have a small amount of computer literacy.

A combination of visiting and observing some of these computer supported systems whilst discussing them with users and developers alongside an examination of the literature has lead the authors to speculate that researchers in this field are ignoring some important issues that have been addressed by the proponents of non-computer supported GDSSs. In particular the authors shall argue that there may be an opportunity for significant progress if the ideas and ideals of the two groups can be brought together to form a second generation system. Thus the paper aims to focus on some of the issues of concern, namely:

\- the constraints of fixed locations for hi-tech rooms,

\- the lack of flexibility of specially designed physical environments,

\- levels of participation in data capture,

\- presentational difficulties,

\- managing the inherent complexity of large volumes of data,

\- the degree of control to team leader/client manager, and in relation to the control by chauffeur/facilitator,

\- behavioural impacts: the management of group dynamics, the nature of conflict, and the inability to cater for informal as well as formal languages.

These issues will be addressed by comparing the characteristics of computer supported and non computer supported GDSSs and stem not only from observation and exploration of the US systems but also from working with a large number of client groups of decision makers using a particular GDSS methodology SODA. Thus although the material has no hard empirical basis it is founded upon reoccurring issues and supported in many cases by previous research in both this field and others. Measures of validity, often a problem when working with qualitative research, that were used to produce and support the above issues and their elaboration were those of convergent validity and contextual validity [46]. A further notion of validity used was that of Pettigrew [41] who suggests that there is a need for triangulation of data to support its validity. Thus the material presented in this paper is founded upon re-occurring issues which through their frequent appearance provide valuable insights into the characteristics associated with these types of system.

## 2. The GDSS environment

## 2.1. Location

One consequence of developing a computer based GDSS as proposed by Huber with the function of enabling group members to enter into the system their own ideas is that workstations, a large public screen and a network is needed in order for it to function. Other requirements may include video cameras for recording the discussions for further research, specialised lighting, white boards, multiple screens and sub rooms called “break out” rooms. As such a set of basic elements is, by its very nature, expensive it reduces the likelihood of companies investing in such a facility of their own and being able to run meetings on site. This is especially true for small companies who need support in group decisions just as much as large groups $[1]$ . This forces groups wishing to use the system to use specific sites whether or not they are close or convenient.

When problem solving teams are addressing the sort of issues that need support from a GDSS facility they often understand the rationale behind getting out of the office to escape the incessant phone calls $[9]$ . Nevertheless there is a limit to how far they will willingly travel without other psychic pay-offs such as attractive surroundings. For example, the authors have used Scottish castles on the side of a loch as a site for group decision support meetings. They are chosen because of the direct pay-off from disengaging participants from their everyday “firefighting” roles and providing the opportunity to focus their attention on the more strategic issues. There is a great danger that the technology needs drive the choice of location.

A non computer supported GDSS clearly avoids this situation. Nevertheless, for successful meetings, facilitators do have to be fairly careful in their choice of venues, taking note of whether they can use “blutac” and how appropriate the wall surface is for writing against $[27]$ , $[17]$ . Appropriate seating is also an issue to several GDSS facilitators $[17]$ , $[24]$ . However, it is possible to take advantage of relaxing surrounding where group members are able to enjoy getting out to walk about. This is not typically the case for hi-tech rooms which are often set within the head office building of large organisations (for example the ICL POD $[5]$ in central London and Reading). Use of such facilities has resulted in the observation of group members complaining about the lack of day light, the chance to ‘stretch their legs’ and the need to use the breaks effectively.

Although a greater flexibility of location is open to a non-computer supported GDSS as seen from a review of these systems there appears to be on closer examination another type of GDSS. These GDSSs – SODA [16], [20], [2], Decision Conferencing [42] and Decision Techtronics [44] – use computers as support tools but do not rely on networks and terminals for individual users. Instead, one or two portable PC's and a large monitor or three coloured projector is used alongside the traditional requirements of non-computer supported GDSSs. The computers are operated by the facilitators, leaving group members able to concentrate fully on the issues at hand. Thus these portable and facilitated systems can also be relatively flexible considering where to hold a decision support workshop.

Therefore there are three not two different categories of GDSS; a totally non-computer supported system, a system which uses computers but does not require group members to directly operate them, and a totally computer driven system.

## 2.2. Internal flexibility

The internal environment of the totally computerised systems is also inflexible. In order to be more flexible by designing the room to be able to accommodate relatively large groups, hi-tech decision rooms tend to be large – sometimes holding up to 25 computers, one for each individual. One result of this is that they may become in some cases “classroom like” (the decision support room at Arizona is an example of this design). Consequently from observation of such groups members tend to form cliques, where an individual predominantly talks with those group members who are immediately on their left and right and doesn't interact with those a few machines away. One step further in this lack of group interaction appears to be when the group members may not even speak at all limiting their verbal communications to simple technical questions [4] thus becoming a ‘nominal’ group in practice.

Furthermore, the rooms through their large size may cause group members to view them as cavern-like especially if the group using it is between 6–8 people. It has been found that such small groups benefit considerably less than larger groups from such systems [40] however, many decision groups may only contain 5 or 6 members [39] and so rooms supporting this size of groups becomes important. “Break out” rooms may be one way some designers have tried to avoid this ‘lost’ feeling as well as possibly interacting more. However, in the case of large groups this usually requires breaking the group into subgroups and therefore either limiting the amount each individual is able to contribute to or possibly producing different views which may require merging. The “break out” rooms do provide the group with a different environment often a refreshing feature and still enable them to maintain a network of communications rather than being isolated subsets. This rigidity of room design is not so greatly felt by either of the other two types of GDSS [27] (see Hickling for a demonstration of the flexible use of group decision support rooms).

## 3. Capture and presentation of information for problem structuring

## 3.1. Data capture

For non-computer supported systems this capturing is mainly carried out using large sheets of paper around the walls of the decision room. In some cases it is also captured in advance of group work through one-to-one interviews (for example SODA [16]). Depending on the particular methodology either the facilitator writes down ideas or members of the group do. Common techniques are Nominal Group Technique and Delphi-type processes [14], Brainstorming [45],

Dominoes [21] and Metaplanning [51]. If the facilitator is recording the meeting then there is the danger of losing information while she/he tries to write it down [37]. Conversely where the individual group members are encourage to write their ideas down they may feel self-conscious about their ability to express ideas clearly or feel embarrassed about their handwriting ability. There is also a lack of any anonymity which may inhibit those less socially confident.

Computer supported groups however are able to enter their ideas anonymously into the computer which then projects them onto the large public screen. This enables group members to judge ideas on their content rather than who proposed them therefore supposedly enabling group members to be more open. This as has been observed is not always the case $[25]$ . Anonymity has also been seen to have a direct affect on the levels of conflict within the group and as such is dealt with in more detail in the section concerning conflict. Nevertheless a similar characteristic to the lack of handwriting skills may be experienced by those members who are unfamiliar with keyboards and thereby feel frustrated and inhibited. US research seems to contradict this hypothesis as it is reported that there is less of a problem than originally thought $[4]$ , $[40]$ , $[37]$ . This apparent discrepancy may stem from a cultural difference between managers in the US and UK.

With the majority of these computer dependent GDSSs the techniques available are replicas of existing pen and paper systems; for example an 'outliner' software package [used by the Capture Lab at CMI and others] providing additional speed and a large database to hold onto the massive quantities of data. Other available techniques include Electronic Brainstorming, Topic commentators, Issue Analyzers, Policy Formulations, Stakeholder Analysis, Option Preferences, and Voting [40], [15]. As a result they are specific packages with a limited amount of flexibility as to the nature of their application. In some cases, for example Group Systems [40], these packages are bundled together allowing for some flexibility in choice of support toolkit. However, once the decision has been made concerning which tools are to be used it may be difficult to change them or their order as the group may need to negotiate this process which may take up a considerable amount of the meeting time. It appears from the literature that the decision about which tool to use is regarded as straightforward however some understanding about the general principles and theories behind the processes is also advisable [9]. Finally they are predominantly of a text nature taking little advantage of the graphical representations or techniques that have grown out of exploiting the particular benefits of computer technology.

## 3.2. Presentation

Presentation in non computer supported systems can be as varied as required. It may incorporate text alongside graphics, use all the colours available as well as the different styles, and allow models to be built up from those previously generated. By enabling the group members to be able to see all the stages of the decision making process around the room and take ideas from one model or picture to help build another not only allows the group to be more creative but also provides a sense of history, a sense of achievement [27], [17]. This ability to see where the group has come from, and all the intermediate stages, is important to the welfare of the group – it provides them with a set of ‘milestones’ against which to measure their progress [3]. In addition where the group is working on a long term project with several meetings, it useful to exploit the nature of the human memory – that it is easier to remember pictures and so group members will be able to recall the basic outlines if not the actual words of images [6].

Both the partly and fully computer based GDSSs are more limited in their presentation. Screen sizes limit the amount seen preventing the group from gaining a holistic sense of the model with its composite elements - often a consequence of being able to capture more data. Displays are changed when new information is entered into the model causing confusion and time loss as the original picture is overwritten and the revised one has to be read and understood. Graphics are rarely mixed with text to stimulate and refresh participants and in some cases colour might not be used at all. Rather than using the computer's power in helping manage the complexity of problems some systems merely reflect pen and paper techniques without harnessing the power and facilities of such. Nevertheless non-computer based systems are highly restricted in their ability to re-draw and edit and may restrain members from altering their composition and moving forward [43]. The exceptions to this lack of a multi-dimensional approach are COPE [13], Gibis [12] and Cognoter [50]. The advantages of these will be discussed later.

Providing members of a group with output representing the intermediate and end results is usually taken to be important in providing commitment to action $[1]$ . Feedback to groups after the session is therefore an important consideration. In the case of totally or partially computer based GDSSs the feedback is immediately available during and after the meeting providing each group member with ‘instant’ minutes of the meeting with a concomitant increase in ownership $[3]$ . This however, mainly is provided in black and white and in many cases is in a text format which if too long or complicated may not be read. Non computer based systems use a variety of techniques among them the use of copies of photographs taken of the flip charts to provide minutes $[27]$ . Decision Conferencing $[42]$ uses photocopying white boards to provide group members with a hard copy.

## 4. Managing the complexity

## 4.1. Identifying key issues

When working with complex problems, with or without computers, some means of managing the large quantities of data that emerge is required $[11]$ . One particular method used by non-computer supported GDSS is grouping or clustering ideas of a similar nature together which is easily done if the group is using dominoes or post-its or using “cut and paste” techniques. This grouping provides the participants with an overall impression of the structure of the content helping in the identification of the key emerging issues or themes which may present the problem in a more manageable form. These key issues can then be given a title or heading which describes their contents. From this point it is possible to explore the relationships/linkages between the different groups and create an abbreviated/skeletal network of group titles and links to be used as an overview of the information. The overview can then be used as a navigation aid. However, as decision support models grow in size it becomes harder to cross reference the ideas as often as may be necessary to the group.

This grouping or clustering may also be carried out in many of the computer supported GDSS. In the case of Capture Lab which uses an outlier package, the headings for lists of ideas may be considered as group titles. Group Systems begins first with a brainstorming session then encourages the group to decide on categories or groups for the generated data and places each of the ideas in the most appropriate categories $[40]$ . COPE, Gibis and Cognoter all use a combination of ideas and linkages which can be manipulated through analysis or manual means to provide groups with a pictorial view of the issue. Therefore groups of data may be comprised of many different sorts of “slices” of the material ranging from structural to contextual. For example a contextual group may include all the key issues within a problem whereas a structural group may contain all those ideas which support a root set of ideas $[18]$ .

## 4.2. Linked models

In both COPE and Gibis these links allow a multi-dimensional representation of the information to be developed. Through this multi-dimensional ability ideas are able to be linked whenever appropriate and constraints on how interlinked the model becomes are not imposed. As a consequence various structural analyses, such as domain density – how busy are the ideas-, are possible. These analyses along with colour and style which denote particular conceptual 'slices' of the model can be used to manage the large amounts of data captured [22]. However, this variety of different slices or pictures of the information can often be confusing as the group member loses track of where he/she is in the model. Various navigation aids have been developed to facilitate this difficulty such as providing a overview map similar to the non computer supported systems [12]. The method of obtaining an overview of the key issues mentioned in non computer supported GDSS by providing group members with a picture on a 'flip chart' of how the key strategic issues seem to be related to one another is also used with computer-supported facilitated meetings. Another device is to use multiple screens. Whilst one screen holds on to the original path of interest the second is able to be used for further exploration and editing [26]. This is especially useful when used in conjunction with the overview picture as it provides three levels of information within the model to be displayed.

## 5. Control

## 5.1. Team leader / client control

Regardless of the level of involvement of computers within the GDSS, one important consideration for the team leader/client is his/her control over the outcome of the meeting. In the case of non-computer supported systems the client or senior decision makers are able to use the skills that they have developed over time, ie use of rhetoric, clear concise well structured arguments for and against particular options, for problem solution. They are also able to have some influence over the agenda being set and the key areas they want addressed. In addition the process addresses the political elements of organisations [7].

With fully computer supported GDSSs however, the client has considerably less control [52]. These GDSSs emphasise equality of participation of group members [28] thus removing or reducing the client's immediate control over the process. Consequently they are unable to use their meeting skills as effectively – if at all – as communication is reduced to an absolute minimum [4]. In some cases it is possible to set a specified list of tools or an agenda prior to the meeting [40] but during the meeting it is in many cases either extremely difficult or impossible to change direction. Furthermore, as total equality is stressed each member can enter, rank and order ideas into the computer anonymously. This may not only be a disadvantage to the client in not being able to control the meeting but also to the proponent as the ideas are not associated with any individual thus reducing the possibility to 'shine'.

As a result, this usage of computers may cause those decision makers responsible for the implementation of the solutions to feel nervous especially if they have little or no keyboarding skills themselves. An example of this was noted by the team at the Capture Lab [37] or Group Systems (see BBC Video on ‘Strategy on the Screen’) as senior managers at first tried to bring in secretaries to type or tried to limit the information being entered in. One direct outcome of this was that the participants either took machines home to practice on or bought typing tutor programmes in order to perform well [4]. Corporate culture may play a very strong emphasis here depending on the level of ‘ownership’ the client has for the system [25]. Voting is also an issue especially with large groups as all votes are regarded by the computer as equal and thus, the outcome of the vote may reflect the view not agreed with by management. This may well lead to the team leader who through not ‘owning’ the solution does little or nothing about it. This outcome has serious consequences regarding the extent to which members of the group have been supported in not only their decision making process but also in their demand for action.

## 5.2. Facilitator / chauffeur control

In the case of facilitator driven GDSS groups the client and facilitator are able to meet before the group meeting and discuss areas that are sensitive and need careful handling. Although directing the meeting to ignore the options or problems emerging is a dangerous alternative as the group may become discouraged and cynical towards the process, it is possible to move the group around these problematic issues for example avoiding them from emerging at inopportune moments or providing the client with an opportunity to explain the rationale behind an issue. In addition the facilitator is able to channel the group's energies into what he or she takes to be productive areas, and so is able to prevent too much time being spent on side issues – the role of a guide [3]. Although the computer based GDSSs do allow the client to participate in building up an agenda the direction the meeting will take is may still be believed to be potentially out of their immediate control. It may however, be argued that user driven computer supported GDSSs encourage greater participation and equality which a facilitator may not be able to achieve.

## 6. Behavioural aspects

## 6.1. Management of group dynamics

Facilitated computer supported GDSSs such as SODA and Decision Conferencing take account of organisational theories and group decision making theories. However, in the fully computer supported GDSSs, little explicit acknowledgement of these theories have been made. With the exception of Jelassi and Beauclair [30], Lewis and Keleman [34] there is little mention of the importance of group dynamics and the resulting consequence on group behaviour of computer intervention. This impact is visible in the reduction of non verbal and verbal interaction between members as they focus on either their personal screen or the public screen thus reducing the amount of interaction possible. The interactions that are made predominantly appear to deal with the technological aspects rather than the problem at hand [4]. The impact of this lack of interaction on the outcome of the meetings has not yet been assessed in any real manner but it is the authors' belief that it is an important area to be considered and therefore warrants attention. As a result the main drive of the US computer supported GDSS research appears to have been technology oriented.

## 6.2. Conflict

It has been noticed that groups working on messy and complex problems usually experience difficulties because individuals have particular solutions they feel are appropriate but which are different from their colleagues. In the struggle to find a portfolio of options that are mutually agreeable interpersonal and intellectual conflict amongst group members may emerge. In the case of computer supported GDSSs a considerable amount of this conflict can be reduced through two different mediums, anonymity and the central screen. The central screen acts as a buffer between the two points of view and it is interesting to note that often when these points of view are being debated group members direct their argument towards the front screen rather than each other thus reducing the personal'ness of the debate. Anonymity helps this as group members are forced to judge an idea by its content rather than its proposer and therefore are less influenced by personality or social status. However anonymity may not be necessarily good as it may cause some conflict as group members become more objective and feel able to pick an idea to pieces and without any care for the emotional consequences [49], [30], [35].

## 6.3. Language

A closely related body of work, Computer Supported Cooperative Work – CSCW [47], emphasises a need for two languages to be present in any group decision support system in order to make it successful. A formal language representing argument, and a ‘cultural’ language providing the protocol for the process of arguing and interacting. It is the presence of both languages together that provides the group with a means of structuring information and interacting with each other. In the case of GROVE [47], a CSCW tool, decision makers have both a formal language in the form of an ‘outliner’ and a simple cultural language in the form of a telephone link. It is suggested by Robinson that in the case of many of the fully computer supported GDSSs the cultural language is either missing or ignored. An example of this, is Gibis which provides only one of the two languages at a time. Robinson suggests that in most cases only the formal language is available. Strategic Choice, SODA, and Decision Conferencing all take account of both levels through managing process and control [19] which may help to understand why they appear to be particularly successful with groups of real managers working on complex strategic issues.

## 7. Future directions

An advantage of using computer supported user driven GDSS is in its ability to be able to access outside information. This facility enables the decision room to offer participants not only word processing or spreadsheet facilities but also the ability to access material from other areas both inside the company and outside. This easy access of information from one tool or location to another has obvious benefits. Information needed to settle a discussion can be speedily obtained, easy access to the outcomes of previous meetings both from within the group and other groups can be incorporated into the existing data base and then visibly displayed on the public screen. However, although this capability may well prove useful there is the danger that group members may spend time searching for information instead of participating in the meeting or that the increased information availability will encourage the group to wander off on unprofitable side tracks. It is in this area that the facilitator as a guide may become a powerful medium [3] and as such the role of the facilitator in all forms of computer assisted meetings is becoming more widespread taking into account process as well as task [8].

In addition to this is the work currently being investigated allowing groups in different locations to meet (Teleconferencing, [31]) and groups at different times to meet (Information Lens, [36], MERMAID [53]). The existing belief that meetings are face to face and at the same time is being challenged. Increasingly, enabling decision makers in different countries to meet and discuss situations and problems is a valuable contribution. This is especially the case as managers have less and less time available and arranging a meeting that they can all attend is a formidable task.

## 8. Conclusion

It soon becomes apparent from reviewing two or three types of systems alongside each other that all three have positive and negative features. An optimal way forward would suggest that some method of combining the best features of these approaches could provide groups with the maximum support. This is facilitated by the continual developments made in technology used to support both fully computerised and partially computerised GDSSs. It may be possible for small laptop computers to be linked to each other and a portable public screen and to be connected via an infra-red or radio network, thus rendering the fully computer supported systems relatively flexible. This would not only solve the flexibility of location constraint but also would be easy enough to use when appropriate and be put aside when not. Other useful tools may include scanners and other means of digitalizing information written down on flip charts. There are few limitations.

However, it is likely that a tension remains between facilitators such as Phillips, Friend and Hickling, and the computer based enthusiasts as to the appropriateness of aiming for fully computer supported systems for anything other than well structured problems.
