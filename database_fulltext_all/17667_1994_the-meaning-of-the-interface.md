# The meaning of the interface A functional and holistic evaluation of a meeting software system $^{1}$

Gerardine DeSanctis, James R. Snyder and Marshall Scott Poole

University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

A method for conducting a preliminary evaluation of a meeting software system is described and illustrated. The method assesses the degree of match between designer and user perspectives of a system by first defining and then evaluating the system in terms of its (1) interface, (2) functionality, and (3) holistic attributes. Applying the method, we were able to identify some preliminary strengths and weaknesses of a specific meeting software system. The application of this method illustrates how general principles developed by Card, Moran, and Newell [4], Silver [41], and others can be applied in a specific situation to assess the match between designer and user views of new meeting software technologies.

Keywords: Meeting software; Software evaluation; Software development; Interface; Decision support systems; Group support systems

## 1. Introduction

Evaluation of a newly completed software product is one of the more challenging activities in the systems development process. Ultimately, evaluation involves assessment of the impacts of the product on some aspect of the organization's functioning, determining whether there is “added value” brought to organizational work. But evaluation starts ahead of such a comprehensive assessment, before the product is even put into practical use. When a system or prototype is completed, or when an existing system is modified in some way, the first evaluative issue is whether the product should be put into use at all or whether changes in the system's functionality or interface are needed. Although few would disagree with the importance of a preliminary evaluation, it is less clear how this preliminary evaluation process should be conducted. Although some basic principles and approaches may apply across systems, the evaluation must also cater to the particular software at hand. The criteria for assessing the effectiveness of a graphics package, for example, are somewhat different from those we might apply to an electronic mail system (see [2]). Consequently we must tailor general approaches for conducting system evaluations to new forms of systems as they emerge. In this paper we expound a preliminary evaluation approach for a Group Decision Support System (GDSS). GDSS refers to an agglomeration of meeting software systems that combine computer, communication, and decision support technologies to support the decision and related activities of organizational groups [6,7,17]. Numerous GDSS prototypes and products have been developed, taking various design approaches (for product descriptions see [10,18]). Although researchers and other developers surely are evaluating new GDSSs prior to their use in group settings, this first step in evaluation has been neglected in the formal sense. There have been no documented cases of preliminary evaluations or recorded debate about how preliminary evaluations of GDSS should be conducted. Since difficulties in interface design have been blamed for early failures of these systems [3,15] and there are many unresolved problems as to what meeting software products represent to users [10], the development of systematic methods for preliminary evaluations of GDSS is needed.

This paper outlines a rationale and method for conducting a preliminary evaluation of a GDSS based on principles developed by Card et al. [4], Silver [41], and DeSanctis and Gallupe [7], and techniques prescribed by Roberts (35, 36, Card et al. [4], and Rubinstein and Hersh [38]). We do not advocate the method as the only approach to preliminary evaluation of GDSS, nor do we advocate it as the ideal approach. Our goal is to contribute to greater formalization of preliminary evaluation procedures for GDSS software and to encourage development of more theory-based procedures for this important first step in the software evaluation process.

We begin by outlining the conceptual basis and proposed procedures for the preliminary evaluation. Next, we illustrate application of the procedures to two versions of a prototype GDSS that was designed for use in face-to-face meetings. We present the results of the preliminary evaluation and then conclude by discussing how the method might be improved and applied to other GDSS development efforts.

## 2. An evaluation approach

## 2.1. Purpose of the preliminary evaluation

In its classical sense, software testing is “proof of correctness” [43] and includes debugging and systematically looking for errors or problems. The goals of testing are to detect faults, prevent faults, and/or to assess the match between the program and its specifications; such testing is typically performed by the software designers or programmers, not users [11,44]. The preliminary evaluation represents an extension of the classical testing process for applications software – going beyond fault detection and proof of correctness. It is distinguished from other testing activities such as software analysis (e.g., timing analysis, data flow analysis, proof of correctness) and software reviews (e.g., walkthroughs and inspections) [11]. The preliminary evaluation proceeds once debugging and other error-correction procedures are complete; it involves operation of the software by users so that their operations and perceptions of the system can be compared to designer standards, or benchmarks (see [4,35,36]).

The purpose of the preliminary evaluation is to provide an initial assessment of the match between designer expectations and user behavior. Users sometimes adopt very different cognitive models of a system than those intended by the designers $[5, 23]$ . In the case of meeting software systems, researchers have noted distinctly different interpretations by users during early adoption of the technology $[27,28]$ . For example, some groups may perceive a GDSS to be a complex problem solving aid, whereas others view the same technology as merely an electronic notepad [33,47]. Because group experiences vary depending on training experiences, the task, and other contingencies, it is important to assure minimal understanding of and satisfaction with the software by individual users before training programs are initiated and the software is introduced to the group setting. Consequently, the preliminary evaluation begins by having individuals (rather than groups) interact with the system, to assure that there is a minimal level of operational quality.

Satisfactory findings through a preliminary evaluation cannot guarantee successful use of the system in group settings; rather, the objective is to systematically rule out the possibility that conceptual or operational difficulties in the software might prevent groups from adapting successfully to the GDSS. Since groups are collections of individuals, it is important that most individuals, or the “typical user,” can operate the system easily, recognize its capabilities, access its features, and apply an appropriate cognitive model to operational use. The preliminary evaluation exercise checks whether there is a match between designer and user views of the software’s operations. In cases where different versions of the software contain different levels of functionality, then the preliminary evaluation can be useful in determining whether these differences can indeed be detected by users.

## 2.2. Approach to the preliminary evaluation

Silver [41] points out the importance of applying taxonomies to decision support systems when conceptualizing their design or evaluating their impacts. Classification of systems is particularly important in the case of GDSSs because, although existing systems have some commonality in features and design philosophy, there are also many differences across implementations of meeting software. For example, some systems aim to support specific types of group tasks, such as idea generation or systems development work, whereas others aim to be more generic, supporting a wide variety of group tasks. A few systems support only discussion and communication needs, providing input, display and exchange of ideas, whereas others provide decision modelling that structures the group judgment process or provides a specific method for solving a problem (see [14,24]). Taxonomies for differentiating system types can aid in organizing results of evaluation efforts. Furthermore, taxonomies are useful when comparing across systems and when evaluating any one system as it is modified and enhanced over time.

Our method combines the software taxonomy proposed by Silver [41] - which characterizes decision support systems in terms of their functional capabilities and holistic attributes - with an evaluation method proposed by Card et al. [4] - which identifies three aspects of functionality and interface characteristics (see Figure 1). Our preliminary evaluation of GDSS software occurs at three levels: interface, functionality, and holistic attributes. The evaluation examines each level in terms of the software's relationship to the user, rather than technical aspects of design per se.

## 3. Preliminary evaluation levels

Interface characteristics. It is generally accepted that software intended for “end users” should be easy to learn, easy to use, and promote user satisfaction $[1,22,37]$ . Experience with using a system provides comfort in accessing and applying its features and tends to raise user satisfaction $[40]$ . A conservative assessment of ease of learning and use should include naive users, who are untrained and have no expressed interest in using the software in the future. Naive users can be instructed to perform the operations of the system according to a structured script; the length of time it takes the user to do this and the number of errors (incorrect keystrokes, mouse strokes, or menu choices) made as the script is implemented, serve as indicators of ease of learning $[35,38]$ . For a GDSS, the structured script requires the user to perform operations such as: enter, save, and display meeting agenda items; enter and weight criteria and alternatives for group problem solving; and so on. To control for amount of information entry, and to make results across users comparable, all users are instructed to enter the same number of agenda items, problems, criteria, alternatives, ratings, comments, etc. To control for social comparison and related factors, such as users watching how others approach the task, users perform the exercise one-at-a-time. Error rate is scored by taking the total number of errors $^{1}$ committed by the user in the structured exercise and dividing by the total time to complete the exercise. The average across all users is the overall error rate [4,35]. Variance in time and errors committed provides an indication of the range of responses people may have in learning the system. From the designer's perspective, these variances probably should be as small as possible. For example, Rubinstein and Hersh [38] suggest a guideline of 1.5 minutes to complete each operational (or feature) task in end-user software. Ideally, designers would expect error-free completion of these tasks, but an acceptable error rate, perhaps stated as number of errors per minute, can be established as a benchmark. Following completion of the structured task, users can be asked to complete a user satisfaction survey. For example, Doll and Torkzadeh [8] provide a validated measure that assesses ease of use, format of the interface, readability of reports, and other aspects of interface satisfaction for decision support systems.

In trial usage, if naive users exceed benchmark task completion times, exceed benchmark error rates, or report unacceptably low satisfaction ratings, changes in the software and/or training, manuals, or other instructional materials would be indicated.

Functionality. The functionality of a system refers to the range of operational tasks it supports; indeed, the functions of a system are what make it identifiable as a certain type of system. Functions can be described in terms of core features, optional features, and model or type [4]. There are a basic set of functions or core features that a given technology should support. Functions supported beyond these are considered optional and unnecessary to characterize the system as being of a certain type. To use an analogy, we know that an electric washing machine is an electric washing machine and not some other type of technology because of the core features it provides: automatic entry of water, automatic water turnoff and drainage, control of hot and cold water input, agitation, and high speed spinning. Optional features on a washing machine might include choice of washing cycle time and speed choice of water temperature combinations, adjustable legs, bleach holder, etc. Some, but not all, implementations of the technology include these capabilities. We can further differentiate systems into models or types; for example, we can categorize washing machines as fixed or portable, standard size or mini (apartment) size. Decision support systems can be evaluated in terms of the range of core and optional features they provide. These functions, in turn, can be assessed across different models or types of systems.

Through the “look and feel” of the system, as well as documentation materials and training, designers try to communicate the system’s functionality to prospective users. Nevertheless, the functionality of a system may not be immediately obvious to the user, particularly in the case of novel, nonstandard technologies such as GDSS. Part of the preliminary evaluation, therefore, consists of identifying the core features for the technology and its specific type or model, and then assessing the usability of these functions from the perspective of naive users (see $[35]$ ). To identify core features, we can consult the literature on GDSS (e.g., $[14,21,24]$ ) and catalog the most frequently suggested features, or functions. These constitute the core features; those less frequently mentioned can be considered optional features. Applying this approach, Table 1 lists core and optional features for GDSSs in face-to-face meeting settings.

To assess usability, naive users can be asked to interact with the system and to rate how efficiently each core and optional feature can be accomplished $[35,38]$ . People who use the system without being informed through training or other documentation about the features contained in the system, its cognitive model, or its intended uses or impacts, can provide an objective assessment of the system's functionality. Applying Roberts' method, task rating levels are: "can't be done," "can be done at manual speed," "can be done clumsily," "can be done efficiently" (see $[4]$ ). Scores are summed up to give the system an overall functionality rating. The scores can be partitioned into different task categories to show the strengths and weaknesses of the system as a whole, or its different models or types. The scores of the naive users can also be compared to scores given by designers as a measure of the degree of consistency in the two groups' perceptions of the software.

With regard to system model or type, we differentiate between two levels of GDSS sophistication [7]. Level 1 systems provide mechanisms for eliminating communication barriers in groups, such as input, recording, and display of group ideas, voting methods for evaluating ideas, and commenting and notepad features. Level 2 systems include the features of level 1 but also add decision modelling facilities, such as stakeholder analysis, risk analysis, or multiattribute utility models. Level 2 systems are more sophisticated in design and intrude more severely into the natural, unsupported meeting process of the group. They support users in following a sequence of steps, thereby structuring their meeting process [7,29]. We would expect level 2 systems to include a larger set of core features than level 1 systems and to include more decision modeling tools in this core set. Differences in core features for two levels of a GDSS are noted in Table 1. To determine if these differences in software types are apparent to users of a meeting software system, we can expose one group of naive users to a level 1 version and a second group to a level 2 version of the software. The usability ratings of the two groups can then be compared to each other, to determine if differences in detectable functionality emerge, and to the designers' ratings, to determine if these differences are in the expected direction.

Holistic attributes. Whereas functionality addresses the questions “what can the system do?” and “what does the system look like?”, holistic attributes refer to questions like “how is the GDSS likely to affect behavior in a group?” and “what is the group likely to do with the system?” We shift our attention from the features themselves to the application of the software in action, anticipating how users will integrate the technology into their decision processes [41]. Meeting software contains a set of resources for concerted action, and the functions have to be assessed in terms of the roles these resources can potentially play in groups as the software is used. Winograd and Flores [46] refer to these desired roles as “phenomenological” aspects of software; Poole and DeSanctis [33] call them the “spirit” of the GDSS. They are the general ends and attitudes designers aim to promote in human behavior through the technology. Possible attributes for a GDSS include:

\- the type of group decision process that is being promoted; for example, consensus, empirical, rational, political, or individualistic [34].

\- the likelihood of leadership emerging in the group when a GDSS is used; whether a leader is more likely or less likely to emerge, or whether there will be equal participation versus domination by some members [17].

Designers have preconceived notions about the system's holistic attributes and hope that these are apparent to users in the operational system [5,45]. One approach to identifying holistic attributes might be to carefully analyze user manuals or information provided to users during training sessions. A more direct approach would be to ask designers to characterize their intentions for the system according to attributes such as those listed above. During the preliminary evaluation, these characterizations can then be compared to those provided by naive users following their initial uses of the system.

Another indicator of a system's holistic qualities is the kind of metaphors that designers or users apply when describing it [27,3033]. For example, is the GDSS perceived to be an electronic chalkhoard or an electronic secretary? Do people treat it as taking on human qualities, such as advising or acting like a person, or is it understood more as an inanimate object, like a conference table or writing space? Designers or instructors in the use of the technology may suggest metaphors to aid in group adoption of these systems, but identifying the metaphors people apply without such instruction may reveal something about the early framing effects that emerge as users try to apply the system in their work. Again, we can capture and compare the metaphors that designers and users ascribe to a particular meeting software system. Further, we can assess whether different metaphors or attributes are applied to different versions or types of GDSS software.

In sum, the preliminary evaluation tests the following proposition:

P1: There will be a high degree of match between designer and user perspectives of the software's interface, functionality, and holistic attributes.

If there are multiple versions of the software and the relative value of one version over the other is of interest, then the following proposition can be examined:

P2: There will be differences in interface, functionality, and/or holistic attributes between different versions of the software. Both designers and users can detect these differences.

If these propositions are not confirmed, or if effects occur in unanticipated directions, then changes in the software's design or implementation are most likely needed prior to use of the GDSS in group settings.

## 4. Illustrating the evaluation approach

## 4.1. The SAMM meeting software system

As part of a larger research project concerned with GDSS design and impacts, a nine-person development team implemented several different versions of a multi-user software product for use in small group meetings. Over a period of years, the Software Aided Meeting Management System (SAMM) was modified and expanded in functionality, and the development team became interested in applying more systematic procedures for preliminary evaluations of new versions prior to their use in group meetings. The current study provided a feasibility test for a preliminary evaluation method.

Similar to other systems, SAMM is designed to promote structured, participative decision-making in small group meetings [6,14,20,27]. Designed to be operated by the group without technical or other special assistance, SAMM provides agenda-creation, public and private messaging, notetaking, and a number of decision tools, such as problem definition, brainstorming, idea or solution evaluation, and stakeholder analysis. Its interface is menu-based and simple in design. The group assembles at a horseshoe-shaped conference table with a workstation and keyboard for each group member. At the front of the room is a large screen for display of group information (such as comments, notes, vote tallies, or idea lists) (see Figure 2).

At each private workstation, the user is provided with a set of features for use in the conduct of team meetings. Team members typically enter some inputs at their respective workstations and view all the team's inputs on the large screen at the front of the room. Teams normally follow an agenda of using system features, however, the sequence in which they use the various system features varies across meetings.

For this study, two versions of the SAMM system, a level 1 type and a level 2 type, were compared. Neither version contained all of the core and optional features given in Table 1; but each version was believed (by designers) to have sufficient functionality to yield perceived holistic benefits. Further, designers believed that the level 2 version contained a sufficient number of additional features so as to yield a detectable increase in functionality and meaningful improvements in usability and holistic attributes over the level 1 version. The level 1 version supported 15 core tasks; the level 2 version support 18 core tasks

## 4.2.Procedures

In order to test the two propositions of the preliminary evaluation, the SAMM design team established benchmarks for interface, functionality, and holistic attributes, which then were contrasted with the behaviors and perceptions of naive users in an evaluation exercise.

Establishing benchmarks. Simple benchmarks were established by the authors for both the level 1 and level 2 versions of SAMM. In some cases the benchmarks were threshold measures (such as error rates not exceeding a maximum level); in other cases, user performance was compared to general norms (such as published norms for user satisfaction ratings of decision support software); in still other cases, members of the design team were asked to provide rating scale or other descriptive information about SAMM that then could be compared to information provided by the naive users (such as holistic attributes). The designers completed a user satisfaction scale, usability ratings for the core and optional features of SAMM, and ratings of holistic attributes. Table 2 summarizes the measurement approach and benchmarks for interface, functionality, and holistic attributes. Table 3 summarizes designers' anticipated differences between the level 1 and level 2 versions.

The evaluation exercise. Thirty naive users performed the exercise individually, sitting at position 6 at a conference table arranged to seat 8 people (see Figure 2). The researcher sat about 5 feet behind the user, using a stopwatch to record task completion times and noting errors made by the user. All sessions were videotaped so that time and error counts could be verified. The researcher welcomed the user to the session but after that provided no oral information or instructions on the SAMM system or how to use it. The user was provided with the following information in writing:

Software Aided Meeting Management, or SAMM, is a software program developed at the University of Minnesota. It is intended to be used in group meetings. As part of our evaluation of the software, we are asking people to use the system while we observe them. One way of evaluating a software system is to have individuals use the system without formal training or explanations of how the software operates. By observing the kinds of mistakes or difficulties they have in using the system, we can discover ways of improving the software.

The user was asked to complete a background questionnaire providing information on age, experience, typing ability, computer experience, time spent in business meetings, etc. Next, the user read the following:

This section consists of a series of tasks for you to complete using the SAMM system. Perform each of the tasks according to the steps indicated. Do not be concerned if you have difficulty using the system. Just try to complete each step as best you can.

The user then interacted with the SAMM system using a structured script; the script provided an interactive “walk” through the operation of the system, including entry and viewing of information on the private workstation as well as the large public screen. The script was similar to scripts used by Roberts and Moran [36], designed to illustrate the operations of a system. For example:

ENTER the following item into the AGENDA:

Use the SAMM system without training and then evaluate SAMM

RECORD the information in SAMM.

VIEW the AGENDA on the large screen.

ENTER the following PROBLEM/ISSUE: I am going to decide on a restaurant to take a special friend to dinner on Saturday.

etc.

Fifteen of the users were given a script appropriate for the level 1 version of SAMM, and fifteen interacted with the level 2 version. The user performed system operations following each of the steps in the structured script. The script told them what to accomplish with the system but stopped short of telling them how to access or use the menu items.

After completion of the structured script, the user was given the set of 33 core and optional tasks listed in Table 1 and asked to rate SAMM's ability to perform each task with a 5-point version of Roberts' scale (1 = can't be done at all, 2 = can be done at manual speed, 3 = can be done clumsily, 4 = can be done fairly easily, 5 = can be done extremely easily). Next the user was given a questionnaire to assess the holistic attributes of the system. To assess the "decision process" attribute, the user was asked to rank order the kind of decision-making process they believed SAMM would promote: consensus, empirical, rational, political, and individualistic. A description of each process was provided, based on Quinn and Rohrbaugh's [29,34] Competing Values Approach to organizational analysis, which in turn is derived from Parsons' [32] social theory of systems. All other attributes were measured using 5-point semantic differentials, such as "democratic-autocratic," "orderly-chaotic," and "fun-tedious." As an indication of metaphor descriptions, a list of possible metaphors was provided and the user was asked to check those that applied or to provide their own metaphorical description of the system. The user was also asked to provide a personification description of SAMM. We hoped that these would provide additional insights into SAMM's holistic attributes. Finally, a 12-item measure of user of satisfaction with a decision support system was administered [8]. The user was permitted to use SAMM when responding to any or all of these questionnaires.

## 4.3. User subjects

Thirty students from introductory classes in business administration served as naive users for our study. They participated voluntarily and received a small number of course points for their participation. On average the students were 27.4 years in age with 5.36 years of full-time work experience. Nineteen of the participants were male. Typing ability ranged from “hunt and peck” to those who said they “could be employed as a typist,” and frequency of weekly computer use ranged from “never” to “more than ten times per week.” The students’ experience working in groups was relatively high (mean = 2.5, where 1 = very high and 5 = very low), and their experience in making actual business decisions was moderate (mean = 3.4, where 1 = very high and 5 = very low). On average, they reported that they were spending 3.8 hours in work-related meetings each week.

The subjects were randomly assigned to use either the level 1 or level 2 version of SAMM. There were no meaningful differences in age, experience, typing ability, or familiarity with computers across the two treatment groups. Also, none of these background variables was found to be significantly related to the outcome variables under study – learning or ratings of satisfaction; ratings of functionality; or descriptions of holistic attributes.

## 5. Findings

Our interest was in descriptively comparing users' impressions of the SAMM software with those of designers. In addition, the perceptions and behaviors of the naive users of the level 1 and level 2 versions of the system were compared for statistical significance. Because our interest was in surfacing possible differences between the two implementations of the software, the liberal significance level of 0.10 was used for all statistical analyses.

## 5.1. Interface characteristics

The mean time required by level 1 users to complete the structured script exceeded the benchmark time of 22.5 minutes by 4.3 minutes, but the error rate was much lower than the 0.33 benchmark set by designers (mean error rate per minute = 0.23). The time to complete the script increased by 25% for the level 2 version, which was a modest gain considering the 58% increase in the number of screens available in that version. As expected, task completion time was significantly longer in the level 2 condition (t = 1.73, p = 0.05). Level 1 users made an average of 6 errors (standard deviation = 2.7) and level 2 users an average of 5 errors (standard deviation = 2.1); the mean error rate among level 2 users was lower, at 0.15, compared to an error rate of 0.22 among level 1 users (t = 2.23, p = 0.03). This may be explained as a “practice” effect, since level 2 users interacted with the system for a longer period of time and saw a larger number of similarly-designed screens. It also is possible that the core tasks supported by the level 2 version allowed the users to detect sequential decision procedures that made the system more easy to comprehend, and, therefore, led to more efficient learning (fewer errors per minute).

Contrary to the designers' expectations, user satisfaction ratings were not significantly higher for the level 2 version (Chi square = 0.35, p = 0.55). However, the variances in these ratings were significantly different (F = 5.18, p = 0.02), with an extremely high variance in satisfaction reported within the level 2 condition (see Table 4). The greater variance in user reaction to the level 2 version is puzzling and warrants further investigation by the design team. Furthermore, the data suggest that overall user satisfaction with SAMM may be less than desirable. Applying normative information provided by Doll and Torkzadeh [8] in their study of major applications from 44 firms, we see that the design team's level of satisfaction with the system was at the 55th percentile, while the level 1 and level 2 naive users reported average ratings at the 30th and 20th percentiles respectively. Again, this information is useful to the design team and warrants further consideration. Hopefully, proper training and experience in using the system in a group setting – which were not present in this highly restrictive evaluation setting but would be present in a more realistic use of the software – would improve users' satisfaction. On the other hand, the data trigger the need for the design team to consider how the interface might be modified to yield more positive first impressions of the software by users.

## 5.2. Functionality

Ratings for each task within the level 1 core, level 2 core, and optional feature categories were summed and then averaged to create usability scores (see Table 5). Reliabilities for these scales, using Cronbach's alpha, were 0.75, 0.77, and 0.80 respectively. The naive users generally gave lower usability ratings than the design team, but not alarmingly so. Naive users of the level 2 version rated this set of core features significantly higher than did the naive users of the level 1 version (t = -1.90, p = 0.07), providing evidence that users could detect important differences in functionality between the two implementations of the software. Lack of significant differences in usability ratings for the level 1 core features (column 2, Table 5) and the optional features (column 4, Table 5) is not surprising, and confirms that these are supported equally well in both the level 1 and level 2 versions of the software.

The usability scores were not expected to be “perfect” in the sense of achieving ratings of 5 (“can be done extremely easily”) for each task rated. From the designers’ perspective, SAMM did not provide extremely easy support of all possible core or optional tasks; the software had areas of strength and weakness, with some core tasks supported more fully than others. On the other hand, the marked difference between a “perfect score” and actual ratings of usability for the level 2 version of the system suggests that this version of the software could benefit from improvement in usability of core features. Both designers and level 2 naive users rated three of the level 2 core features particularly poorly – budget allocation model, problem formulation tools, and choice making – due to the fact that these were not adequately supported in the software.

## 5.3. Holistic attributes

Designers reported that their intention for SAMM was to promote a consensus-oriented decision process, with secondary emphasis on a rational-problem solving approach. The type of decision process was not expected to be different across the two versions of the system (see Tables 3 and 6). Naive users of the level 1 version agreed that the system would promote consensus decision-making, but they put less emphasis on the rational process than the designers. Naive users of the level 2 version ranked the consensus process second and indicated that the major decision process promoted by the system was an empirical one. Both user groups ranked the empirical decision process more highly than the designers, perhaps because as first-time users they were enamored with the data displays provided by SAMM. Overall, however, there were no significant differences in average ranks between level 1 and level 2 users, indicating that the same types of decision processes are promoted by the two versions of the system (at least in the preliminary evaluation). There were high average ranks by both designers and users on all items (i.e., all ranks averaged less than 4.0, where I = highest and 5 = lowest) and rather large standard deviations, suggesting that SAMM is viewed by all parties as promoting competing values in the group – a state which Quinn and Rohrbaugh [34] say is desirable. On the other hand, the data suggest that the design team may want to clarify among themselves what the exact goal of the system is, since users may be confused about the ultimate goal(s) of the system when it is used in practice.

Although designers intended for the software to take on some human-like qualities, this data suggests that this goal was not evident in the level 1 version but was evident in the level 2 version.

## 5.4. Summary

The preliminary evaluation explored two propositions: first, that there would be a high degree of match between designer and user perspectives of the software's interface, functionality, and holistic attributes; and second, that there would be detectable differences in interface, functionality, and/or holistic attributes between different versions of the software. The first proposition was tested using the benchmarks and comparative measures given in Table 2. With regard to interface, the time to complete the structured script exceeded established minimums and user satisfaction levels were less than desirable. On the other hand, the total number of errors and error rates were below the established benchmarks. In other words, users were able to complete the operational tasks with little difficulty, but the time required to do this was unacceptably long. The implication is that designers must find ways to make system use more efficient without increasing complexity; one approach might be to reduce the number of system menus (without reducing functionality). With regard to functionality, the GDSS received high (though not “perfect”) usability ratings. Usability ratings given by naive users were not as high as those given by designers, and some specific features were targeted for improvement, particularly within the level 2 version. There was some match, but also some differences, between designer and user views of holistic attributes. For example, both groups reported the GDSS as promoting consensus-oriented decision making, but users generally gave more positive ratings of attributes than did designers. Designers provided people-oriented metaphors and rational personifications, whereas users were less likely to attribute human-like qualities to the software. The results suggest the need for the design team to project more consistent impressions of the system, either through changes to the interface or through user manuals or training procedures.

The second proposition of the software evaluation was tested using the level 1-versus-level 2 comparisons given in Table 3. Users were able to detect the increase in core features in level 2; they noted differences in two (structure and atmosphere) of the five holistic attribute dimensions; and they attributed more human-like metaphors and personifications to the second version. Somewhat to the surprise of the designers, the error rate in using the level 2 version was actually lower than in the simpler level 1 system.

Some need for improvements in the level 2 version were noted; for example, there is a need to enrich the set of core tasks supported, and designers might explore ways to raise initial satisfaction levels.

## 6. Discussion

We believe the preliminary evaluation method presented here would be useful to others involved in GDSS development. GDSS design is based on the assumption that a group is composed of individuals, each of whom can benefit from interaction with technology in the course of conducting group work. So, a thorough evaluation of a GDSS must first evaluate how the individual apprehends and is influenced by the GDSS. If the GDSS is not first and foremost effective in capturing the individual's interest and ready use of the technology, then its eventual success in the group context may prove troublesome. As it is, designers surely expose new GDSS software to individuals before evaluating its use in groups. But what do designers look for in such evaluations, and how should the evaluation proceed? The method proposed here formalizes the preliminary evaluation process and gives it conceptual grounding.

As outlined here, the preliminary evaluation provides developers with a way to examine the individual user's relationship to the system on the dimensions of interface, functionality, and holistic attributes. The key implication for designers is to operationalize measures such as “usability” and set benchmarks so that progress on such measures can be meaningfully assessed. Further, our approach emphasizes the importance of going beyond the operational aspects of the interface to consider its meaning to the user, to consider whether naive users can even recognize the structural potential in the system and whether “what they is what we (as designers) see.” Systematic assessment of the match between designer and user views would appear to be a critical component in the evaluation of end user software. In our study, we were able to detect places where this match was strong and where it was lacking. A key goal in systematizing the evaluation is so that benchmarks can emerge from which later versions of the system, or indeed other implementations of GDSS, can be compared. The method assesses whether the GDSS meets the minimal threshold of performance intended by the designers; it does not determine whether the system is high in quality or low in quality, whether the interface is as usable or friendly as it could be, or whether the software will, in fact, bring positive benefits when applied in group meetings.

A major benefit of a preliminary evaluation procedure to researchers is that it can provide a basis for comparison of laboratory and commercial systems that are being studied for their broader impacts on groups and organizations. GDSS researchers currently are using a variety of systems to study the role of computing technology in group work, and these systems differ in terms of their interface design, functional support, and holistic attributes. George [12] and others [14,28] have attributed contradictory findings in GDSS impacts to, among other things, differences in the specific software systems used by different researchers in the early phases of development of the technology. The taxonomy applied in this paper allows us to evaluate GDSSs in their entirety rather than one feature at a time. Feature-at-a-time evaluation is not only tedious to conduct, it is conceptually inconsequential because “features” have the repeating decomposition problem; there are features within one feature (e.g., menus within menus, or icons within icons), making it difficult to isolate one system “feature” from another. More importantly, systems vary so much in the presentation of their features that information based on features alone makes it virtually impossible to compare systems or versions of systems. Consistent with Card et al. [4], Silver [41], and Roberts [35], we believe that comparisons based on dimensions such as interface, functionality, and holistic attributes are more useful than feature-by-feature analysis. The approach offered here allows researchers to compare GDSS systems against some common benchmarks, and so promote cumulative understanding of design differences.

Our preliminary evaluation procedure is in need of further refinement, and alternative methods for conducting the evaluation are certainly possible. Limitations of the current approach include the large amount of time required to gather data (observing each user individually) and lower-than-desirable reliabilities on some of the self-report measures. Further, our user subjects were undergraduate students, and we compared only two versions of the same GDSS (rather than multiple versions or more than one GDSS). Card et al. [4] point out that an important constraint on software evaluation methodologies is that they must be relatively simple and efficient to use. This is why only overt (stopwatch and self-report) measures are applied and why a minimal number of users are measured in the time and learning experiments. The sophistication of a preliminary evaluation method depends on many factors, including the evaluator's familiarity with the method, the effort required to prepare the materials for the specific system, and the difficulty of recruiting and observing users for the experiments.

The preliminary evaluation is just that – an initial assessment of a software system outside of its use in a live context. It is not a complete evaluation, but rather just one piece of information in our overall understanding of how and why systems have the impacts that they do. A thorough evaluation of a meeting software system would study the social processes, or group dynamics, involved in operating it. Observations based on two or more users would be desirable in subsequent evaluations; and ultimately, it is how these systems are actually used in organizational contexts that matters most in determining their ultimate impacts on users. Studying the process of group use of a GDSS is a complex process and requires a series of studies in itself. However, such research is not grounded unless the relationship between the individual and the technology is first established. To the extent that it is important for developers and researchers to understand how meeting software design figures into eventual impacts on group decision making, then interface, functional, and holistic evaluations by individual users are important first steps in this process.
