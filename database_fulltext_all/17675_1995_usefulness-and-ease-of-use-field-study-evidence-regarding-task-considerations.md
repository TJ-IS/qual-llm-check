# Usefulness and ease of use: field study evidence regarding task considerations \*

Mark Keil $^{a,*}$ , Peggy M. Beranek $^{a}$ and Benn R. Konsynski $^{b,1}$

$^{a}$ Computer Information Systems Department, College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta, GA 30302-4015, USA

$^{b}$ Harvard Business School, Soldiers Field Road, Boston, MA 02163, USA

Usefulness and ease of use (EOU) are both believed to be important factors in determining the acceptance and use of information systems. Yet, confusion exists regarding the relationship between these two constructs and the relative importance of each in relation to use. Usefulness is seen as a function of task/tool fit, while EOU is viewed as a task-independent construct reflecting intrinsic properties of the user interface. This paper presents the results of a field study illustrating the hazards of focusing on EOU and overlooking usefulness. Based on the study, the authors suggest that perceived EOU may be a function of task/tool fit.

Keywords: Usefulness; Relative advantage; Ease of use; EOU; Complexity task; Task-Tool fit; Usability

## 1. Introduction

Usefulness and ease of use (EOU) are both believed to be important factors in determining acceptance and use of information systems. Software developers now place considerable emphasis on building systems that are “easy to use.” In many cases, however, the intended users have been unimpressed with the usefulness of the resulting product. Two examples illustrate this point.

A computer company encountered difficulties implementing an internally developed expert support system for its sales representatives. Usage was low and user feedback indicated that the system was cumbersome to use, so the company's developers performed a major rewrite of the user interface. After redeploying the new and improved tool they surveyed the users (more than 100) and found that there was no significant increase in use. What is more, the users still perceived the software to be cumbersome to use.

The designers of a CSCW (computer supported collaborative work) tool based their software on a sophisticated view of language and how people work together. The software they produced, which combined electronic mail and project management tools, offered functionality that was not available through other products. Despite the developers' emphasis on designing a tool that would be easy to use, users' opinions of the product were generally negative. One user reportedly tossed the software out of his office when asked what he thought of it.

The above vignettes, based on case studies, suggest that ease of use (EOU) is an important, yet elusive, concept. Further, the relationship between EOU and perceived usefulness in determining users' acceptance of information systems is unclear. Why are there so many cases in which developers have emphasized EOU and still produced systems that users perceive as “cumbersome to use?” One reason is the confusion that currently exists among both researchers and practitioners regarding the nature of the relationship between usefulness and EOU and the relative importance of each in relation to use.

While many argue that design for ease of use is an issue separate from the user-driven issue of design for usefulness, empirical evidence suggests that there are important correlations that require attention. This study shows that certain elements of task consideration may offer partial explanation for the correlation. We further suggest that leverage of task attributes in design may accrue benefits in both dimensions of usefulness and ease of use.

Perceived usefulness can be defined as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” [1]. Perceived EOU can be defined as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort” [1]. This paper begins with a brief review of the literature on usefulness and EOU followed by a framework for discussing the relationship between these two constructs. Using the framework, we describe the results of an empirical field study on usefulness and EOU. The field study data are used to illustrate two important points: (1) There are hazards associated with focusing on EOU and overlooking usefulness, and (2) There may be a task component to perceived EOU.

While the first point has been raised by others $[1,2]$ , the field study provides a particularly compelling example of the problem. Since there appear to be many cases of systems that have failed to gain acceptance despite developers' emphasis on EOU, the hazards associated with focusing on EOU and overlooking usefulness appears to be a message worth repeating.

The second point deals with an issue that has not been adequately addressed in the IS literature: namely, how should we define and measure EOU and how can we design for it? In the past, there has been a tendency to view EOU as reflecting certain user interface characteristics that are intrinsic to the software. Based on empirical field data using accepted measures for perceived EOU, we suggest that an individual's perception of EOU encompasses issues that are broader than user interface design. In particular, we make the assertion that a broadened conceptualization of EOU requires consideration of key attributes of task context.

While additional work is needed to test this assertion, one purpose of this paper is to promote a re-examination of the concept of EOU, both in terms of how we measure it and how we design for it. The paper concludes with a usability framework that will allow managers and developers to produce systems that are more likely to be perceived as both useful and easy to use.

## 2. Theory and evidence for usefulness and EOU

This section provides an overview of existing theory and evidence pertaining to the usefulness and EOU constructs and the role that each may play in the adoption and use of software. The material presented here is drawn from the diffusion of innovation and IS implementation literatures and is not intended to represent an exhaustive review on the subject.

## 2.1. Prior literature on usefulness construct

In diffusion of innovation terms, usefulness can be mapped to the concept of “relative advantage,” or the degree to which the innovation is perceived as better than existing practice. Relative advantage has consistently been shown to be among the more important factors governing adoption and diffusion of innovations $[3,4]$ . In a meta-analysis of the innovation literature, Tornatzky and Klein concluded that relative advantage was “positively related to adoption” (p = 0.031) $[4]$ . Hence, the diffusion of innovation literature implies a positive correlation between usefulness and use. This correlation appears to be supported by various studies reported in the IS implementation literature.

Cale [5] studied the implementation of a standard software package across multiple sites within the U.S. Forest Service. He found that the receptivity of end users was largely a function of their perceptions of the usefulness of the application in relation to existing practices. Other studies of IS implementation also lend support for the relationship between usefulness and system use.

Maher and Rubenstein examined the factors affecting the adoption of a Monte Carlo simulation program for project selection [6]. They found a strong positive correlation between the perceived value of the data generated by the model and an individual's willingness to adopt the system. The notion of perceived value is similar to the concept of usefulness.

Souder et. al. developed a model of users' willingness to adopt systems where certain system characteristics such as relevance of the output (a notion similar to perceived usefulness) were viewed as "the originating variables in a causal chain" [7]. They constructed a survey to measure other factors which were seen as mediating variables in the relationship between system characteristics and willingness to adopt. Souder et. al. conducted three field studies in which they used the data that was collected to construct a system that would maximize users' willingness to adopt. Essentially, they used their model to forecast the acceptability of different system forms. While the authors found utility in this approach, it is difficult to assess the predictive value of their model due to the small number of cases they examined.

Other researchers have investigated the relationship between users' attitudes toward a system and use. Since perceptions of usefulness have been shown to affect attitudes toward a system [8], these studies are also relevant. Lucas conducted six different studies in which he investigated the relationship between users' attitudes toward a system and various measures of implementation success (generally usage or satisfaction) [9]. In all six, he found that favourable attitudes were associated with implementation success. This finding is consistent with a study conducted by Schultz and Slevin in which user attitudes were strongly associated with implementation success [10].

## 2.2. Prior literature on EOU construct

In diffusion of innovation terms, EOU can be viewed as inversely related to the concept of “complexity” [8]. Rogers and Shoemaker [11] define the complexity of an innovation as “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use.” Complexity has consistently been shown to be among the more important factors governing adoption and diffusion of innovations [3,4].

In their meta-analysis of the innovation literature, Tornatzky and Klein concluded that complexity was negatively related to adoption (p = 0.062) [4]. Although it would be imprecise to equate ease of use with the inverse of complexity, it would be surprising if one of these factors was found to be significant and the other was not. Hence, the diffusion of innovation literature implies a positive correlation between EOU and use. The broad body of research on diffusion of innovation lends support for this belief [3]. In addition to the diffusion of innovation literature, Davis [1] suggests a number of other theoretical bases such as self-efficacy theory that support the concept of EOU.

## 2.3. Relationships between usefulness, EOU, and use

In recent years, several IS researchers have focused on developing aggregate measures of usefulness and EOU [1,12]. While factor analysis has shown that EOU and usefulness are separable constructs, they appear to be highly correlated and the exact nature of the relationship between the two and how they affect use remains unclear [13]. During the past few years, Davis has conducted several studies that provide empirical evidence on the relationships that exist between usefulness, ease of use and system use [1,8,14].

In a field study, Davis administered a survey to 120 users within IBM Canada's Toronto Development Laboratory. Survey recipients were asked to rate the usefulness and EOU of two software systems: the PROFS electronic mail system and the XEDIT file editor. Participants in the study were asked to self-report their frequency of use for each system. In this study, usage was found to be significantly correlated with perceived usefulness and ease of use for both PROFS mail and XEDIT.

PROFS mail usage correlated 0.56 with perceived usefulness and XEDIT usage correlated 0.68 with perceived usefulness. PROFS mail usage correlated 0.32 with perceived ease of use and XEDIT usage correlated 0.45 with ease of use. All correlations were found to be significant at the 0.001 level. Davis also found that the two constructs, usefulness and EOU, were themselves highly correlated (0.56 for PROFS and 0.69 for XEDIT). Similar results were also obtained in a laboratory study in which Davis administered a survey to 40 MBA students asking them to evaluate two PC-based graphics packages (Chart-Master and Pendraw). Table 1 shows the correlations reported by Davis.

Table 1 reveals that perceived usefulness had a higher correlation with use than did EOU. This finding is consistent with the results of an earlier study conducted by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [8]. It is also consistent with results reported in the diffusion of innovation literature [4].

The exact nature of the relationship between usefulness, EOU, and use is unclear. While it is tempting to believe that usefulness and EOU are two separate and distinct constructs that independently affect use, Davis suggests that the nature of the relationship between EOU and usefulness may not be quite that simple. Through regression analysis, he has found that the effect of EOU on use virtually disappears when controlling for the effect of usefulness $[1]$ . He has also found that the direct affect of EOU on use appears to drop off over time, as users gain familiarity with a piece of software $[8]$ . From these data, Davis has suggested that the primary impact of EOU on use is expressed indirectly through its effect on usefulness. Therefore, while factor analysis has shown that the two constructs are separable, Davis' work suggests that EOU and usefulness are not only highly correlated but that there may be other subtleties in the relationship that require further thought and investigation. In a replication of Davis' work, Adams et. al. $[13]$ conclude that “the relationship of these two constructs [usefulness and EOU] to usage is perhaps more complex than is typically postulated.” Task context, as we shall discuss later in the paper, may explain some of these subtleties.

## 3. Usefulness and EOU: A framework for discussion $^{2}$

Regardless of the exact relationship between usefulness and EOU, it is helpful to have a framework for discussing the impact of these two factors on software acceptance. Figure 1 illustrates a simple framework for thinking about usefulness and EOU that we call the “Usefulness/EOU Grid.”

As Figure 1 indicates, usefulness and EOU can be arrayed on a $2 \times 2$ grid where each quadrant represents a different combination of the two attributes. In the context of software development and implementation, the usefulness/EOU grid provides a mechanism for discussing the current mix of usefulness and EOU and for plotting a future course if a different mix is desired.

![](/api/attachments/J2KRWUF6/fulltext/images/6c1936a4e0004cf407c1d33d64715b042c3e36540210e5d683edefb6a08d4f2f.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Usefulness/EOU grid.

Quadrant I represents systems that exhibit low usefulness and low EOU. From a technology acceptance perspective, this combination of attributes holds little or no appeal. Since these systems are neither useful nor easy to use, we use the term “Rejects” to describe systems that fall in this quadrant of the grid. Many of the CASE tools that are presently on the market, for example, may be characterized as rejects based on their lack of acceptance. Prospective users often complain that the tools are not useful because they embody an unfamiliar design methodology and are difficult to use.

Quadrant II represents systems that exhibit low usefulness but high ease of use. Systems in this quadrant of the grid are generally easy to use but lack the power needed to be considered truly useful. Systems that have nice user-friendly interfaces but for which there is a poor degree of task/tool fit would also belong in this quadrant of the grid. We use the term “Toys” to describe systems that fall in this quadrant of the grid. Users may embrace toys initially if they provide some limited functionality or are sufficiently novel to merit investigation, but such systems stand very little chance of lasting acceptance. Many low-end word processors and graphics packages, for example, are toys because even though they may be easy to use they offer only limited functionality. Most users outgrow such systems fairly quickly.

Quadrant III represents systems that exhibit high usefulness but low ease of use. Systems in this quadrant of the grid are generally useful but are lacking in user-friendliness. Systems that are extremely powerful but difficult to learn or complex to use would fall in this quadrant of the grid. We use the term “Power User Tools” to describe systems that are in this quadrant. Some fraction of users will often adopt power user tools because the functionality of the tool outweighs its low ease of use. Most users, however, will avoid these tools because the learning curve or complexity is judged to outweigh the potential benefits. The command language interface of the UNIX operating system is an example of a power user tool. Commands such as “cat” (to type a file listing to the screen) and “ls” (to type a directory listing to the screen) are neither intuitive to learn nor easy to remember. More sophisticated commands such as “awk” and “grep” are exceedingly powerful, but difficult to learn and use.

Quadrant IV represents systems that exhibit high usefulness and high ease of use. Systems in this quadrant of the grid are both powerful and user-friendly. We use the term “Super Tools” to describe systems that fall in this quadrant. Super tools stand the greatest chance of gaining acceptance among users. Many of today’s GUI-based statistical analysis packages designed for workstations and PCs can be considered super tools because they provide the full functionality of traditional mainframe-based tools such as SPSS and SAS, but are considerably easier to use. Similarly, many of today’s spreadsheet packages can also be classified as super tools.

For both researchers and practitioners, the usefulness/EOU grid provides a simple way of representing the different combinations of usefulness and EOU that are possible. The value of the framework is that it encourages analysis and discussion regarding the position of a particular system. From a managerial perspective, the grid provides a means of mapping out the changes that are needed to move a system to a more favourable position. In the next section, we use the grid in describing the results of a field study conducted on usefulness and EOU.

## 4. CONFIG: A field study on usefulness and EOU

This section describes the results of a field study on usefulness and EOU [15]. The study focused on a computer company's efforts to modify an internally developed expert support system in order to generate greater acceptance among end users. The system, known as CONFIG, $^{3}$ was designed to aid the company's sales representatives with the increasingly complex task of configuring the computer hardware and software they sold.

## 4.1. Background on CONFIG and the redeployment

In the 1980s, the managers of the company had estimated that roughly 25% of the orders being logged by its sales organization had some type of configuration error. This situation posed several problems for the company. First, it led to costly allowances – situations where the company had to give away free hardware because the quote or the configuration given to the customer was inaccurate. Second, it contributed to delays and inefficiencies in the order fulfilment cycle. Third, it caused a considerable amount of frustration for the customer. Finally, it was an embarrassment to the company.

In order to address these problems, the company developed an expert support system called CONFIG that would help its sales reps with the increasingly difficult task of creating and validating system configurations. CONFIG was first deployed in the mid-1980s. A 1986 user survey showed that approximately 75 percent of the company's U.S. sales representatives had tried CONFIG, but only 25 percent were actually using it. By 1989, the company estimated that only 9% of its U.S. sales reps were using CONFIG.

Based on prior feedback from the users, the CONFIG developers were convinced that one of the major barriers to using CONFIG was in the area of ease of use. In the 1986 user survey conducted by Leonard-Barton [16], users who had stopped using CONFIG were asked: "What is the major reason that you stopped using CONFIG?" One of the most frequently cited reasons was that the software was not user-friendly enough. The other barriers most frequently cited included lack of availability, slow response time, and lack of training – factors relating to the delivery platform and implementation infrastructure rather than the tool itself.

Fig. 2. Plotting the redeployment strategy: The developers' view of CONFIG.

Based on this and other sources of feedback from the users, the developers embarked upon a project to redeploy a more user-friendly version of CONFIG. A pilot redeployment of CONFIG across five of the company's U.S. district sales offices was scheduled for 1990. The developers operated under the assumption that CONFIG was in quadrant III of the usefulness/EOU grid (high usefulness, low EOU). Since CONFIG's ability to configure systems had been demonstrated, and since the users' complaints seemed to focus on ease of use, the developers reasoned that the tool had a high degree of usefulness. In their eyes, CONFIG was a power user tool that needed to be modified so as to be more acceptable to the general user population. As shown in Figure 2, the plan was to move CONFIG from quadrant III into quadrant IV of the usefulness/EOU grid.

In the redeployment project, the developers spent a considerable amount of time and effort revamping the user interface without changing the basic functionality of the tool. Historically, CONFIG had sported a character-based interface that incorporated both commands and menus. One reason for this was that the original specifications for the program called for the ability to run on line-printer type terminals because this was the type of hardware commonly found in the sales districts. Figure 3 shows the type of interaction that occurred between the user and the system under the “old” interface. Users disliked the old interface because it was difficult for them to remember commands and to navigate through the many layers of menus. Many users felt lost within the program; they would find themselves forced into making a choice at a particular menu without knowing where it might take them or if they would be able to return to their previous position.

In response to these problems, the developers prototyped a new user interface that was based on the look and feel of an office automation package that the users were already accustomed to using on a regular basis. The rationale for this approach was to provide an interface that would be familiar and comfortable to the users; one that would minimize the amount of amount of learning necessary to use CONFIG.

In 1989, the developers built a prototype of the new user interface and presented it at a user design group meeting. The users responded enthusiastically to the new interface and urged that it be implemented. In 1990, the company embarked on a project to redesign the user interface. Since much of the user interface code was intertwined with the expert system code, the project proved to be a fairly large and complex undertaking.

The new interface was still character-based (due to limitations imposed by the equipment available in the field sales offices), but it employed a combination of simple forms and pop-up menus to help guide the user through the configuration process. Figure 4 shows some of the screens from the new user interface. In a series of semi-structured interviews with users, we found that most users who had experience with both the old and the new version of CONFIG agreed that the new user interface was a considerable improvement over the old interface.

As part of the redeployment plan, the company also committed substantial resources toward improving the implementation infrastructure. They provided local hardware and software for each sales district to ensure adequate response time and system accessibility. They also provided extensive on-site training coupled with local user support to ensure that the users had the network of support they needed in order to use the tool.

Using usefulness and EOU measures developed and validated by Davis [1] and then further refined by Moore and Benbasat [12] (see Appendix), we studied users' reactions to the new user interface as part of a longitudinal field study of CONFIG. Users' perceptions of usefulness and EOU were measured before the redeployment to assess perceptions of the “old” version of CONFIG. Then, approximately four months after the redeployment, another survey was conducted to assess perceptions of the “new and improved” version of CONFIG. Pre- and post-deployment survey data were separately analyzed to calculate the reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha) of the measures so that they could be compared to the reliabilities reported by Moore and Benbasat [12]. The first thing we observe from the table is that users' pre-deployment perceptions of CONFIG reflect relatively low usefulness and low ease of use. Using the usefulness/EOU grid, the survey results would tend to place CONFIG somewhere in or near quadrant I as shown in Figure 5. This suggests that the company's strategy of enhancing the ease of use without making any substantive changes to the functionality of the tool may have been flawed. At best, they would have moved CONFIG from quadrant I to quadrant II of the grid. Thus, the CONFIG data illustrate the importance of having an accurate indication of which quadrant a system is in before any attempt is made to reposition it. As Table 4 indicates, there was no substantial change in either perceived usefulness or perceived EOU. In other words, the positioning of CONFIG within the usefulness/EOU grid did not change appreciably. There was also no significant change in use. These data suggest that the effort expended to improve the user interface was misplaced, thus illustrating the hazards of focusing on EOU and overlooking deeper issues relating to the usefulness of the tool.

## 4.3. Explaining the results obtained for perceived usefulness

As Table 4 indicates, there was actually a slight decrease in perceived usefulness. On a 7-point scale (with 7 representing strong agreement that CONFIG was useful), the mean response on the pre-deployment survey was 3.96, indicating that prior to the redeployment respondents were neutral regarding the usefulness of CONFIG. The mean response on the post-deployment survey was 3.54, suggesting that after the redeployment there was slightly more scepticism concerning the usefulness associated with using CONFIG. Since there were no changes in the basic functionality of the software, one would have expected no changes at all on the usefulness dimension. It seems surprising, therefore, that this variable actually decreased slightly. This result can be explained in at least two ways.

First, the business conditions faced by the company deteriorated during the interval of time between the two surveys. During this period, sales reps were under increased pressure to meet their sales quotas. Therefore, they may have felt they had less time available to sit in the sales office configuring systems. In effect, management was telling the sales force to “get their feet on the street” – a directive that may have lowered the priority of the configuration task in the mind of the sales rep, and hence the perceived usefulness of CONFIG as well.

Second, it is possible that the users were expecting more than the new version of CONFIG delivered. If this were the case, respondents may have downgraded CONFIG simply because it did not meet their expectations. Extensive interviews with sales reps did suggest that expectations concerning the functionality of CONFIG were unrealistically high. The following remark was fairly typical:

With all the ballyhoo around the project, I was expecting to see an integrated sales tool that would really address our needs... For months they had said: “We’ve been listening to the field and here it comes – we’ve got the package for you, at least that’s what I got.” So, I didn’t really see that much difference between the old and the new version of CONFIG. I’m sure there were differences, but nothing really jumped up and hit me.

Regardless of which explanation seems more plausible, the change in perceived usefulness was not meaningful in a practical sense; the mean perception after the redeployment was very close to where it was on the pre-deployment survey. In other words, the problems with perceived usefulness that existed prior to the redeployment were not corrected and may have actually been exacerbated by certain environmental factors. But why was the perceived usefulness not higher in the first place? In order to answer this question, we turn to some of the qualitative data that was collected regarding task/tool fit.

Based on data gathered through interviews and observations, it appears that the developers harboured misconceptions about the sales process and therefore developed a tool that was not well-matched to the task (as envisioned by the users). Figure 6 shows an overview of the sales process as viewed by many members of the development organization. As shown in the figure, the primary focus of the redeployment was on configuring systems. The developers' model of the sales process represents a series of distinct steps. Indeed, Figure 6 implies that each step is independent and that the flow is sequential. In reality, claim many sales reps, the selling process is more complex; there are interdependencies across the steps and the flow can involve iterative sequences of activities. There are at least two areas where the developers' model falls short of capturing the complexity of the sales process:

(1) Configuring and quoting are tightly coupled.

## 4.3.1. Configuring and quoting are tightly coupled

The developers' model presents configuring and quoting as separate and distinct tasks. Consistent with this view, the CONFIG software was originally conceived as a stand-alone application. As of 1990, CONFIG provided no pricing information and was not well integrated with the quoting system used by the sales reps. The developers had given the reps the capability of passing a file from CONFIG to the quoting system but this was a one-way linkage and was not interactive.

Interviews with sales reps indicated that they perceived quoting and configuring systems to be tightly coupled activities and would have preferred to have an integrated application that addressed both tasks. Due to the minimal level of integration that existed between the configuration and quoting systems, many reps avoided using CONFIG because they saw it as an extra step that impeded the process of generating a quote. One rep explained that the task of configuring systems is not an end in itself. As he put it:

The goal is not to generate a configuration. The goal is to generate a complete, bottom-line number. You don't do a configuration for its own sake. You don't have time to play a lot of what-ifs around different configurations. Generally, you're trying to configure around a price point and there are usually 2-3 scenarios you need to look at. The what-if doesn't do you any good without pricing information in CONFIG.

Another rep explained the interaction between quoting and configuring systems this way:

When you're configuring a system, you need to play with the price in order to get the quote right. You need to balance the technical objectives of configuring a system with the customer's constraints... There has to be pricing information in CONFIG. The developers of CONFIG don't understand the business aspect of selling systems.

Comments like these suggest that configuring is not easily separable from quoting; price is an important characteristic of a system and is very often the starting point in the configuration process.

## 4.3.2. Detailed configuration does not always precede quoting

The model depicted in Figure 6 also implies that a detailed (and accurate) configuration should precede every quote. While many reps agree that in the ideal world this would be the most sensible approach, they report that life is more complex than that. Reps often go through a series of quotes with a customer during the sales cycle. Early quotes are used to help identify an appropriate solution that the customer is willing to buy. As one rep explained:

The first quote is often part of identifying the solution. The quote is a major selling tool - it's like waving a flag in front of the bull.

The quote is therefore more than a price attached to an accurate configuration - it is a negotiating tool that often drives subsequent changes to the initial configuration.

Depending on the nature of the selling situation and their relationship with the customer, many reps prefer to run “quick and dirty” quotes during the early part of the sales cycle. Later in the sales cycle they become more concerned with producing a quote that represents a detailed and accurate configuration. These findings are based upon interviews with sales reps and are confirmed by the survey data.

On the post-deployment survey, respondents were asked to agree or disagree with the following statements:

The accuracy of my initial configuration is important to my customers.

The accuracy of my final configuration is important to my customers.

Responses were recorded on a 7-point scale with 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree. Mean response to the first question was 5.9 while the mean response to the second question was 6.8. This difference was statistically significant at the p < 0.001 level. These results suggest that while reps were concerned about accuracy throughout the sales cycle, they were significantly more concerned about the accuracy of their final configuration than they were about the accuracy of their initial configuration.

Both the survey and the interview data suggest that the sales process is more complex than the developers' model depicted in Figure 6. From the sales reps' perspective, the front-end of the sales process involves a potentially long and tedious process of "qualifying" the customer. Qualifying involves determining what the customer wants or needs, what the customer's timetable is, how much the customer is willing to spend, who the decision-maker will be, and so forth. During this process, initial quotes are used as tools to tease out information from the customer and to work toward a more refined solution that will be acceptable. As the qualifying process proceeds and the solution becomes more refined, the rep's need for an exact configuration and an accurate quote increases. Hence, from the reps' perspective, Figure 7 portrays a more realistic overview of the sales process than the one shown in Figure 6.

The above discussion indicates an apparent lack of task/tool fit – an issue that was not addressed in the redeployment effort. This may explain why perceived usefulness was not higher. We now turn to explaining the results obtained for perceived EOU.

## 4.4. Explaining the results obtained for perceived EOU

For the purposes of the redeployment, the major change to CONFIG was not in the area of increased functionality, but rather, the user interface. Based on the substantial changes and improvements made to CONFIG's user interface, we expected to see some improvement in perceived ease of use. Therefore, given that CONFIG appeared to be in quadrant I of the usefulness/EOU grid prior to the redeployment, we expected some movement toward quadrant II based on the changes that were made. Instead, as noted earlier, the positioning of CONFIG within the usefulness/EOU grid did not change appreciably. As Table 4 indicates, there was no significant change in perceived ease of use. Based on these data, the improvements made to the user interface did not seem to be reflected in users' perceptions of ease of use. This surprising result prompted us to look for explanations as to why there was no significant change in perceived EOU.

There are several possible explanations for the lack of movement in perceived EOU. Some of these explanations are stated below as alternative hypotheses:

H1: Users did not perceive any improvement in the interface

H2: Learning effects swamped out interface improvements

H3: Instrument was not sensitive enough to pick up an improvement in EOU

H4: Perceived EOU is influenced by task/tool fit.

Each of these hypotheses is discussed below.

## 4.4.1. H1: Users did not perceive any improvement in the interface

The simplest explanation for the result that was obtained is that the users did not perceive any improvement in the interface despite the changes that were made. Data from semi-structured interviews with more than 50 sales representatives cause us to reject this hypothesis. Sales reps who had experience with both the old and the new version of CONFIG almost invariably expressed a preference for the new user interface. The following remarks were typical:

I went through a whole year not using CONFIG because it was cumbersome. I really like the new interface a lot better than the old one.

I liked the new version of CONFIG much better. It was much easier to use.

The difference between the old and the new version of CONFIG is like night and day. I was exposed to the old version of CONFIG in sales training and I would never have used it, whereas I can see myself using the new version of CONFIG.

Even reps who had not used the old version of CONFIG and thus had no basis for comparison felt that the new user interface was relatively friendly and straightforward.

## 4.4.2. H2: Learning effects swamped out interface improvements

Another explanation is that the learning curve associated with using the new version of CONFIG may have overshadowed any improvements in EOU. In other words, the changes made to the tool and the new learning curve associated with those changes may have swamped out the improvements made to the user interface. If the post-deployment survey had been administered relatively soon after the users had first been exposed to the new version of CONFIG, we might find this learning curve effect to be a plausible explanation for the result that was obtained. Given the heavy emphasis on training, however, and the fact that the post-deployment survey was administered more than three months after the new version of CONFIG was deployed, we think there is sufficient reason to reject this hypothesis. It would seem highly unlikely that users were still overcoming the learning curve by the time they were asked to respond to the survey.

Another explanation is that the instrument was not sensitive enough to pick up an improvement in EOU. In order to explore this hypothesis, additional data analysis was conducted. Respondents who filled out both the pre- and the post-survey were sorted into four categories based on their self-reported use of CONFIG. 4.4.4. H4: Perceived EOU is influenced by task / tool fit

Rejecting the previous hypotheses leaves open the question of why most users did not perceive an increase in EOU. While there may be other alternative hypotheses, one possible explanation is that perceived EOU is influenced by task/tool fit. We suggest that the qualitative data collected in our study offer some “weak signals” for the hypothesis that perceived EOU is influenced by task/tool fit. In particular, the apparent lack of task/tool fit discussed earlier may well explain why most respondents perceived no appreciable change in EOU, despite an obvious improvement to the user interface. Thus, in addition to the correlation between EOU and usefulness (see Table 4), there are other indications that this hypothesis is worthy of further investigation.

## 5. Discussion

The CONFIG data illustrate the hazards of focusing on EOU and overlooking usefulness. In this respect, our data confirm what others have suggested: that usefulness is a more important factor than EOU in determining system use $[1,13]$ . Our data also suggest that perceived EOU extends beyond user interface issues and may depend on more macro-level considerations of task/tool fit. Therefore, we contend that designing for EOU must begin with some type of task analysis that goes beyond the typical considerations of ergonomics and user interface. This broadened conceptualization of EOU is consistent with the concept of usability discussed in the human-computer interaction (HCI) literature as well as recent IS research on the subject of task/tool fit. A framework is presented that illustrates the various dimensions or levels of usability and the interactions that can occur between levels.

## 5.1. Designing for usability

The HCI literature suggests that usefulness and EOU are both important aspects of “usability engineering” [17,18]. Traditionally, HCI research has focused primarily on the more objective aspects of usability (such as execution time and error rates) and has, at times, exhibited a technology-centred view of usability. In recent years, however, there has been a growing emphasis on the more subjective aspects of usability (such as user satisfaction) and the field has been moving toward a more user-centred view [2,19]. This evolution of the field has brought about an increased appreciation for the importance of task analysis in designing for usability.

Designing for usability requires integrating a user centred design philosophy with traditional software engineering methodologies so that task-related considerations become part of the design process. Techniques for accomplishing this have been improving and the literature shows that there is increased interest in applying them earlier in the design process [20]. The developing area of task analysis [17,18,21-26] therefore provides a set of tools for designing systems that are both useful and easy to use.

## 5.2. The usability triangle framework

Although varying definitions of usability exist within the HCI literature, most are consistent with a tripartite view of usability that includes ergonomics, interface, and task considerations. The usability triangle framework shown in Figure 8 suggests that these dimensions exist within a hierarchy in which ergonomic issues are enveloped by interface issues that are in turn enveloped by task considerations.

The ergonomics dimension of usability is primarily concerned with the physical attributes of delivering software to the user. Size of the monitor, colour and resolution of the screen, keyboard layout, keyboard feel, and so forth are typical ergonomic concerns. Moving up the triangle, the interface dimension of usability is concerned with software issues relating to human-computer interaction. The structuring of menus, forms, and dialogue boxes are typical of the kinds of issues that would fall under interface. The interface dimension also encompasses issues of how information should be presented to the user so that it can be efficiently absorbed and acted upon. Moving still further up the triangle, we reach the task dimension of usability. This dimension concerns the more macro-level issues of task/tool fit. At this level of the usability triangle we are concerned not about whether the user can manipulate the tool, but whether the tool will enhance the user's ability to execute tasks and perform his/her job.

![](/api/attachments/J2KRWUF6/fulltext/images/769cf1fdb6f89c1d3b0dc58d33c5f4e0ff81f9844930f457339b74025b0c0364.jpg)  
Fig. 8. The usability triangle.

The traditional approach toward designing for ease of use has been to focus on the lower levels of the usability triangle. Often times, attention is not placed on EOU until after the basic system has already been developed. At this point, interface designers are put on the project with the assumption that the more macro-level considerations of task/tool fit have already been addressed. The problem with this approach is that designing for usability must begin at the task level. If the tool does not fit the task, expending a lot of design effort at the lower levels of the usability triangle will not necessarily lead to an increase in perceived EOU.

While developers have acknowledged that task is important, they often regard EOU as if it were a characteristic of the system that is independent of any particular task context, leading to the types of problems illustrated by the CONFIG field study. Evidence from the IS literature, however, suggests that there are important interactions that can occur as we move down the usability triangle $[27-29]$ . That is, interactions can exist between task and interface and between interface and ergonomics.

Studies on the use of graphs vs. tables to support decision-making tasks, for example, have found that the relative effectiveness of the two approaches is a function of the cognitive fit between the task and the representation (i.e. graphs vs. tables) [27,28]. The significance of this finding goes beyond clarification of the graphs vs. tables debate within the IS literature [27,28,30-35] and suggests that task considerations can, and should, influence interface design, and that interface design cannot be evaluated outside of the task context.

Similar research has shown that the impact of colour cannot be easily separated from task or interface considerations. Hoadley [29], for example, investigated the impact of colour (monocolor vs. multicolor) and the form of presentation (pie charts, bar charts, line graphs, and tables) on individuals' ability to extract information. She found that "colour improves time performance for pie charts and bar graphs... while the data for line graphs indicate a deterioration in time performance with colour..." This result suggests that ergonomic issues (such as colour) can be influenced by interface considerations – in this case, the form of the presentation. These results provide further evidence of the interactions that can occur between different levels of the usability triangle.

A major implication of the findings described above is that one cannot assume that any dimension of usability exists independently from the other dimensions. Therefore, in designing for usability, it is important to remember that there can be complex interactions between task, interface, and ergonomic issues. Conclusions and directions for future research are discussed in the next section.

## 6. Conclusions

Usefulness and ease of use (EOU) are both believed to be important factors in determining the acceptance and use of information systems. Yet, confusion exists among both researchers and practitioners regarding the nature of the relationship between these two constructs and the relative importance of each in relation to use. While prior research suggests that usefulness is more important than EOU, many developers continue to put a disproportionate amount of emphasis on EOU. The CONFIG field study illustrates two important points: (1) There are hazards associated with focusing on EOU and overlooking usefulness, and (2) There may be a task component to perceived EOU.

While the first point has been raised by others, it bears repeating because no amount of EOU will compensate for low usefulness. With the growing attention that has been placed on designing “good” user interfaces, it has become tempting to believe that interface improvements alone can cure the problems of low acceptance and use. The CONFIG field study described in this paper illustrates the hazards associated with this approach. Before embracing a strategy for increasing use that hinges on user interface improvements, it is important to consider existing perceptions of both usefulness and EOU. The CONFIG field study clearly illustrates that without a careful assessment to determine if a system is in quadrant III of the usefulness/EOU grid (i.e. high usefulness, low EOU), it is unlikely that user interface improvements will have much of an impact on acceptance and use.

The second point - that there may be a task component to perceived EOU - suggests that task/tool fit plays a role in shaping perceptions of whether or not a system is easy to use. While the notion of task/tool fit has been around for some time, this point requires further discussion because of the implications it holds for the way we think about and design for EOU. While many would argue that EOU is something that exists independently of usefulness and can therefore be optimized separately, this appears not to be the case in actual practice.

Previous studies as well as the research reported here suggest that the degree of task/tool fit may help to explain the subtleties in the relationship between usefulness and EOU. If task/tool fit is as critical as the literature and our own study suggest, then developers need to understand the users' tasks as well as the context in which these tasks are performed. The CONFIG field study also illustrates the risks of developing a narrow stand-alone system to support one task without a careful analysis of how that task (in this case, configuring systems) relates to other tasks (in this case, quoting) and to the overall job context.

Further research is of course needed in order to explore the relationship between task/tool fit and perceptions of usefulness and EOU. Additional field studies using the same instruments and different degrees of task/tool fit may help to further explain the relationship between usefulness and EOU. Controlled laboratory experiments should also be conducted to test the hypothesis that perceived EOU can be influenced by task/tool fit. Further studies such as these will help both researchers and practitioners better understand how to design and implement systems that are both useful and easy to use.
