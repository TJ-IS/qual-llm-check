# Synergetic expert systems

Yosef Beeri, Israel Spiegler \*

The Leon Recanati Graduate School of Business Administration, Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv, 69978 Israel

## Abstract

A model for integrating Expert Systems is presented. The model – Synergetic Expert System (SES) – contains several expert systems which can be arranged synergetically to suit the particular needs of a problem. An object-oriented approach is used to design the model and handle its various components. The building blocks of the model, arranged in series or parallel, are defined together with a formal delineation of efficient and economic expert systems. These lead to a definition of marginal cost and value of an expert to a system. The model may be applied when different experts or expert systems are needed to tackle a complex problem. Treating experts or expert systems in parallel may also be viewed as a form of Group Decision Support System (GDSS).

Keywords: Expert system; Decision support system (DSS); Artificial intelligence; Decision making; Object-oriented design; Group decision support system (GDSS); Value of information

## 1. Introduction

Organizations today use many experts to support decision making. Decision makers, at every level of a firm, are compelled at times to turn to one or several experts in order to make a single decision. This dependence on experts is likely to continue as the industrial and commercial world keep specializing while professionals and experts increase their level of expertise in smaller and smaller areas. Virtually any field has its experts, who may work as individuals or in groups, and Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) are gaining recognition as tools to aid decision makers $[3,5,7]$ .

The dynamic and changing environment of an organization also impacts decision making. This means that systems for decision support need to be open-ended, flexible, adaptive, and cover a wide range of applications and expertise. As such, decision makers must rely on several experts, sometimes concurrently, if they are to make the best possible decisions.

This aspect of decision making – the fact that decisions are based on several experts working together – has not received adequate attention, though the utilization of an Expert System (ES), as a component of the overall system, is gaining momentum. For instance, Han and Kim [8] included an ES in a municipal information system, and Turban [12] proposed integrating several ESs with a Decision Support System (DSS). Gallant [4] used a connectionist network to automate the generation of an expert system. “Blackboard” systems provide for some integration of the knowledge generated by several experts [9]. However, those systems barely consider cost and value factors such as the value of an expert or an ES, or the benefit to a particular problem of adding or replacing a specific ES. Little attention has also been paid to the placement of the ES in the decision process and to the mutual influence of answers given by one ES on the others.

Current computerized ESs are barely sufficient to solve an integrated problem. Such systems are self-contained and independent units which treat only a narrow aspect of the problem space. Indeed, an ES attempts to mimic a single human expert. Therefore, when decision makers need the advice of several experts, they must approach each expert separately. But then, output generated by one expert or ES may not match the input needed by the next expert or ES, and worse, answers obtained from two or more experts may contradict one another. In short, decision makers can hardly use a set of individual ESs when they need to use them as a whole.

The paper proposes a decision theoretic model for integrating the knowledge of experts to support decision makers in such situations. The model – a Synergetic Expert System (SES) – is a system design which integrates a number of ESs in a synergetic manner according to the needs of the particular problem. The SES is an “umbrella” system for the individual ESs; it provides a strategy for determining which particular expert systems to integrate into the decision process. As such, it can be a solution to complex decision making processes which operate in a dynamic environment, relying on a number of experts for its decision support.

The SES model treats the ESs as independent objects, and the subset, an arrangement of ESs used to solve a problem, as a meta-object. An object-oriented approach $[10]$ is used here which views the world as made up of many interrelated objects. The problem facing a decision maker is assumed to be solved in phases, either sequentially or concurrently. The model also consists of the computer, communication, and interface components as well as modules to support GDSS $[7]$ .

The objective of the SES model is to obtain from the user, in an interactive mode, data about a specific problem facing a firm and to come up with a proposed solution and explanation, obtained by a number of existing and independent ESs. Knowledge of those ESs is integrated by the SES. The characteristics of the problem facing the SES model are:

1. Vast knowledge exists for parts of the problem.

2. Several ESs accumulate a large amount of knowledge.

3. Decision makers lack adequate knowledge on how to solve the problem.

4. Additional problems, hence additional ESs, of which the decision maker is unaware when faced with the original problem, are continuously appearing.

5. Experts are not always available at critical times.

6. Linking and connecting pieces of knowledge obtained from the collection of experts is difficult.

7. Evaluating a proposed solution is hard to do.
Such problem characteristics are found in many real life situations, i.e., construction, manufacturing, and consulting when several experts with different points of view are asked to address a problem. Similar characteristics are found too in a GDSS environment when a group of decision makers attempt to come up with a consolidated decision $[3,5,7]$ .

The paper has thus far described the different components needed for SES. We now provide a formal definition of the group of experts or ESs needed to solve a problem, defining an efficient and economic group and dealing with the economic cost and value of an expert.

## 2. The approach

We take an object-oriented approach in developing the SES model. This approach views the world as made up of objects and messages [10]. An object is a complete entity which performs a definite task and contains all components needed to carry out the task. The link between an object and its environment, other objects, is made via messages, which invoke the input and output operations of the object. The effect of a message on the object depends both on the message and the receiving object, that is, the same message may cause different action from two different objects.

The envisioned SES operates in an open, dynamic, and changing environment. Such an environment constantly faces new problems whose level of complexity keeps increasing. Due to its flexibility in handling changes and new problems and its ability to adapt to the particular problem on hand, the object-oriented approach is thus suitable for modeling such an environment.

As already noted, the SES model is an umbrella system for a subset of experts, or ESs, which are configured to solve a given problem. To arrive at a pertinent fit between a subset and a problem environment, we use a prototyping approach $[10]$ . This involves defining the problem, and constructing an initial subset of experts to solve it. Such a subset will be iteratively updated until the best fit to the problem has been achieved.

As an “umbrella” model, the SES consists of several ESs and a user interface, as schematically shown in Fig. 1.

While ESs are basic to our model, we nonetheless emphasize a human-centered approach, viewing an ES as a tool rather than as a replacement of the human expert. Accordingly, in SES we introduce three levels: (a) a fast level which enables the user to solve a problem with a subset already in the system, (b) an in-depth level which constructs a subset for the problem on hand, and (c) a control level which tests the effectiveness of a subset in the two levels mentioned above. These three levels and an overall view of the SES model are shown in Fig. 2.

\- an ES that is executed when the SES gets to that frame;

\- the data needed by the SES to solve the problem;

\- the data needed by the specific ES participating in the solution of the problem.

The knowledge base of the SES is a collection of individual ESs resident in a computer system. We call it the ES knowledge base. The linkage of this knowledge base with the SES is made via a task tree [6] and the tree of frames [11] mentioned above.

## 2.2. Objects

The following are objects in the system:

Problem definition: This object helps the user define the problem. It contains supporting tools for problem specification, different ESs, programs, and a linkage to other objects in the system.

Subset generator: An object to configure a new subset when an existing subset has failed to come up with a solution. A subset may be generated in one of two ways:

\- fast - objects are selected by the user;

\- in-depth – an algorithm is executed which iterates until a subset for the problem is constructed.

This object also incorporates an expert selection object, the relationship between experts (series or parallel), the knowledge base of the SES about the different ESs, and other frames needed to generate a subset of experts or ESs for a problem.

Expert system base: This object is the collection of independent ESs from which the model configures a subset. The SES treats each ES as an independent object.

Subset executor: This object runs an existing subset of experts or ESs, which means applying a set of those ESs needed to give an integrated solution to the problem on hand.

Message handling: An object which collects data defined for a specific subset and transfers them to the appropriate ESs.

Subset control: This object tests the solution according to a set of parameters to determine its success.

Output: An object in the system that generates the solution, giving the following information:

• the proposed solution;

\- other possible solutions;

\- an explanation of the selected solution and how it was reached. The model generates the expert system tree involved in the process, including all its frames.

## 3. The model

In analyzing the use of several experts for obtaining a consolidated solution to a problem, we define basic relationships among experts, and between them and the decision maker. We then define an efficient as well as economic expert group. With these definitions, we can delineate and analyze the cost and benefits of an expert to a decision process and the value of an expert or an ES to the SES system.

## 3.1. Building blocks

Subset: A subset is a group of experts or ESs arranged to solve a particular problem.

Series: A series is a subset in which the decision maker approaches the first expert or ES, using the answer of that expert as the basis for turning to the second and so on.

Parallel: A parallel is a subset in which the decision maker concurrently approaches several experts. The same problem is presented to all experts at the same time. From the set of solutions, the decision maker and SES select the one preferred.

An example of a series is a production manager who consults a quality control expert and then talks to a machinery expert. A parallel is the consultation with financial and marketing experts concurrently. A combination takes place when seeking the advice of a financial, then quality control, and then job shop experts while talking in parallel with the financial expert also with a marketing professional (See scenario in Section 5.

Other states may include combining both relations defined above. That is, a situation may be characterized by a parallel of series or by a series of parallels. And finally, there may be situations in which parallels of several series are constructed together, or series that are made up of several parallels. But, the basic units, indeed the building blocks, for analyzing the use of several experts are the series and parallels defined above. The overall process covered by the SES, from accepting an initial problem through generating consolidated advice for solving it, is made up of such building blocks. Having these building blocks, it is possible to generate any desired combination of the series and parallel configurations.

## 3.2.1. Definition of a series

A series is a subset in which the decision maker approaches the first expert or ES, using the answer of that expert as the basis for approaching the second and so on. The series can consist of more than two experts, as long as the output of expert i-1 becomes the input of expert i.

A group of experts, or ESs, is defined as a series for giving a single solution to a compounded problem if

$$
\mathrm{O} ^ {\wedge} = \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{n}} (\mathrm{M} _ {\mathrm{n}}),
$$

$$
\mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{i}} = \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{i}} (\mathrm{M} _ {\mathrm{i}}) = \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{i}} (\mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{i} - 1}) = \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{i}} (\mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{i} - 1} (\mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{i} - 2})),
$$

$M_{i}=$ problem data given by the user to the first expert, then

$$
\mathbf {O} ^ {\wedge} = \mathbf {F _ {n}} (\mathbf {F _ {n - 1}} (\mathbf {F _ {n - 2}} (\dots (\mathbf {F _ {2}} (\mathbf {F _ {1}} (\mathbf {M _ {1}}))) \dots))).
$$

## 3.2.2. Definition of a parallel

A parallel is a subset in which the decision maker concurrently approaches several experts. The output of all experts is weighted and a solution is presented to the decision maker.

Let

$F^{\wedge}=$ a process performed by the SES on all output obtained from experts participating in the parallel.

Therefore, a group of experts, a subset, who share information given to each of them in order come up with a solution to a compounded problem is defined as a parallel if

$$
\mathrm{O} ^ {\wedge} = \mathrm{F} ^ {\prime} (\mathrm{O} _ {1}, \mathrm{O} _ {2},..., \mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{i}},..., \mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{n}}),
$$

$$
\mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{i}} = \mathrm{F} _ {\mathrm{i}} (\mathrm{M} _ {\mathrm{i}}),
$$

$M_{i}=$ problem specification given by the user to expert i.

## 3.3. An efficient subset

The SES model is flexible and basic building blocks may be added whenever necessary. However, it often happens that an expert or an ES make little direct contribution to a solution. Experts may overlap, contradict, or be improperly used. Thus, we need to define an efficient subset.

Subset $\alpha$ is defined as more efficient than subset $\beta$ if at least one of the two conditions occur:

1. The solutions of both subsets are identical, but the number of experts in $\alpha$ is less then the number in $\beta$ .

2. The number of experts, n, is equal but the utility obtained from the solution of the $\alpha$ subset is higher than the utility obtained from $\beta$ .

Formally, subset $\alpha$ is more efficient than $\beta$ if $\mathfrak{n}(\alpha) < \mathfrak{n}(\beta)$ AND $O^{\gamma}(\alpha) = O^{\gamma}(\beta)$ ,

or

$$
\mathrm{n} (\alpha) = \mathrm{n} (\beta) \text {   AND   } \mathrm{U} (\mathrm{O} ^ {\gamma} (\alpha) > \mathrm{U} (\mathrm{O} ^ {\gamma} (\beta)).
$$

That is, $\alpha$ is more efficient than $\beta$ if the outcomes (O) are equal but the number of experts in $\alpha$ is smaller than $\beta$ , or the number of experts is equal but the utility from the outcome of $\alpha$ is greater than that obtained from $\beta$ . The utility of any subset $\delta$ ( $\delta = \alpha$ , $\beta,\ldots$ ) is defined as

$$
\mathrm{U} (\mathrm{O} \gamma (\delta)) = \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ \mathrm{u} _ {\mathrm{k}} (\mathrm{O} \gamma (\delta)) \cdot \mathrm{w} _ {\mathrm{k}} \right],
$$

where the utility of a subset is the sum of utilities obtain by any criterion k times the weight of that criterion.

## 3.4. An economic subset

After different expert groups are compared for efficiency, the economic value of a subset must be considered. A subset may indeed give the best solution but be very costly. There may be other solutions, perhaps not as good, but much lower in cost.

Subset $\alpha$ is defined as more economic than subset $\beta$ if at least one of the following two conditions occurs:

1. The cost of obtaining the solution by subset $\alpha$ is equal to or less than the cost of getting the solution by $\beta$ , but the utility obtained from subset $\alpha$ is greater than that obtained from $\beta$ .

2. The cost of obtaining the solution by subset $\alpha$ is less than the cost of getting the solution by $\beta$ , but the utility obtained from subset $\alpha$ is equal to or greater than that obtained from $\beta$ . Formally, subset $\alpha$ is defined to be more economic than subset $\beta$ if

$$
\mathrm{U} (\alpha) > \mathrm{U} (\beta) \text { AND } \mathrm{C} (\alpha) \leq \mathrm{C} (\beta),\tag{or}
$$

$$
\mathrm{U} (\alpha) \geq \mathrm{U} (\beta) \text { AND } \mathrm{C} (\alpha) <   \mathrm{C} (\beta)
$$

where, for any subset $\delta$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{U} (\delta) & = \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{h}} (\delta) \cdot \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ u _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\delta) \cdot w _ {\mathrm{k}} \right] \right], \\ \mathrm{C} (\delta) & = \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{h}} (\delta) \cdot \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\delta) \right]. \end{array}
$$

## 3.5. Value of an expert

The above definitions allow comparison of any two subsets to determine their economic value. At times, we may want to assess the value of adding or removing an expert i (such as a materials expert). (For an analysis of the value of information see [1,2].) To do this, we assume that we have two identical subsets and that an expert i may be removed from one of them. We now check which of these subsets is more economic.

Definition: subset $\alpha$ is more economic than subset $\alpha - i$ (i.e., $\alpha$ without expert i) if the benefit resulting from subset $\alpha$ is greater than that of subset $\alpha - i$ , benefit being defined as the difference between the revenue obtained from the solution and the cost of applying the subset.

Formally, subset $\alpha$ is more economic than subset $\alpha - i$ if

$$
\mathrm{R} (\alpha) - \mathrm{C} (\alpha) > \mathrm{R} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) - \mathrm{C} (\alpha - \mathrm{i})
$$

where, for any subset $\delta$ there exist

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{R} (\delta) & = \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{h}} (\delta) \cdot \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\delta) \cdot w _ {\mathrm{k}} \right] \right], \\ \mathrm{C} (\delta) & = \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{h}} (\delta) \cdot \sum_ {1} c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\delta) \right]. \end{array}
$$

We can now define the marginal value of $ES_{i}$ to any subset $\alpha$ as the difference between the benefit obtained from $\alpha$ and that obtained from $(\alpha - ES_{i})$ . Thus, the economic value of any given expert or ES to the SES system is the sum of marginal values of any expert over all subsets in which that expert takes part.

Adding to the equation the marginal revenue of adding an expert i, which is denoted $\pi_{\alpha}(i)$ , we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{R} (\alpha) - \mathrm{C} (\alpha) = \mathrm{R} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) - \mathrm{C} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) + \pi_ {\alpha} (\mathrm{i}), \\ \text { or } \end{array}
$$

$$
\pi_ {\alpha} (\mathrm{i}) = \mathrm{R} (\alpha) - \mathrm{R} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) - \mathrm{C} (\alpha) + \mathrm{C} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}),
$$

and replacing and rearranging the terms in the equation, we get a definition for the marginal revenue of an expert, or ES, i:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {\alpha} (\mathrm{i}) = \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{h}} \cdot \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\alpha) - r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) \cdot w _ {\mathrm{k}} \right] \right. \\ \left. - \sum_ {l} \left[ c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\alpha) - c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) \right] \right]. \end{array}
$$

We assume here that $p_{h}(\delta) = p_{h}(\delta - i)$ , that is, the probability of failure h occurring remains unchanged as a result of adding or removing an expert i.

With the equations developed above, we can define the value to an organization of adding an expert or ES i to an SES. The value is defined as

$$
\pi (\mathrm{i}) = \sum_ {\alpha} \pi_ {\alpha} (\mathrm{i}),
$$

and the full equation, after bringing in the cost and revenue terms is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {\alpha} \pi_ {\alpha} (\mathbf {i}) = \sum_ {\alpha} \left[ \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} p _ {\mathrm{r}} \cdot \left[ \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\alpha) \cdot w _ {\mathrm{k}} \right] \right. \right. \\ \left. - \sum_ {\mathrm{l}} c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\alpha) - \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) \cdot w _ {\mathrm{k}} \right] \right. \\ \left. + \sum_ {\mathrm{l}} c _ {\mathrm{hl}} (\alpha - \mathrm{i}) \right] \Bigg ]. \end{array}

The value of ES(i) defined here assumes that any type of problem has only one subset for solving it. However, we must consider the case where a problem can be handled by two or more subsets. Therefore, we replace the variable $P_{h}$ with $P_{h}P^{h}$ , which is the product of the probability that any subset will be executed when failure h occurs. The equation is redefined as follows:

\sum_ {\alpha} \pi_ {\alpha} (\mathrm{i}) = \sum_ {\alpha} \sum_ {\mathrm{h}} p _ {\mathrm{h}} p ^ {\mathrm{h}} (\alpha) \cdot \left[ \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \left[ \left[ r _ {\mathrm{hk}} (a) \right. \right. \right.

## 4. Scenario

While not implemented as an empirical system, the SES model and its algorithms have been applied as a prototype and strategy in an industrial application. To illustrate the subject matter and the SES model, we narrate a short scenario of a problem which contains multiple experts in its decision making. A manufacturing firm follows the following phases in its production process:

1. Raw materials are prepared for producing parts of a product; quality control of materials is performed.

2. Parts are produced and checked again for quality.

3. Parts are assembled into the product.

4. Quality control tests are performed on final product.

5. Product is packaged and stored for shipment.

When a problem emerges in phase 4, the quality control person stops the production line and calls his or her superior to test and check the problem. He or she turn to a log, kept on all system statistics and figures, to compare the probability of failure, MTBF, and other data, and to record the new figures in the log. The senior quality expert tests the various parts produced at different stations and identifies the causes that created the problem. This information together with the log is now passed on to the production manager and to the maintenance expert to remedy the faults. The maintenance expert identifies the factors that caused the quality problem and deals with the machine or the process involved. When done, the expert directs restarting the production line, first for a short test, and if results are satisfactory, returns the production line to normal operation.

The system log is thus the basis for determining utilities, weights, costs, and other figures. Some of the figures are actual data, i.e., cost of service contract, cost of expert. Others have been determined by past performance, i.e., saving/hour of production halts, revenues (e.g., \$0.1 per unit) and more. These data, objective as well as subjective, help the use of the model in practice.

If, however, the expert fails to locate the problem, he or she attempt other approaches, and when these fail, may turn to a materials expert to seek a replacement of some of the raw materials used. Using the model, the manager may assess the value of such an outside expert in the way shown in Section 3.5.

The materials expert selects a different raw material according to the problem data described, defines it for inventory control, and transfers its characteristics to the maintenance expert for tooling the machines and line to the new material. This scenario depicts a typical problem of a production process which utilizes and relies upon several ESs. It is taken from a can manufacturing firm and is used to describe how the SES model can be applied to solve a problem involving several ESs.

Problem Quality control found a defective can which leaks and has low conductivity. Controller stops the production line and calls the supervisor, who generates the SES.

Object Problem Definition: Problem is defined by user and system module as "Production Processing Quality".

Object Subset Execution: Search for a subset which matches the problem. No subset is found.

Object Subset Creation: Using the system and algorithms to generate a series. This generates an ES tree which may match (even partially) the problem. SES asks user to select ES. User selects: Quality ES. Define Quality ES as the root for a new subset.

Object Frame Generation: Generate a frame and define the specific problem: "product not insulating and leaking" Message: temporary subset with full ES tree.

Object Execute ES: Running the frame with the ESs.

Message ES output: · Since leak is in bottom, and point of leak not fixed, cause may be lack of power in attaching bottom to body. · Low conductivity may be caused by not covering the body during the welding process.

Object Subset Creation: Message passed on to supervisor for approval. User: Answer not satisfactory, continue.

Object Subset Creation: generate an ES tree. Ask user to select another frame that may match the problem. User response: Select Maintenance ES. (Tree structure: Quality ES -> Maintenance ES)

Object Subset Creation: Problem defined with user - "lack of power in attaching bottom to body".

Message: Temporary subset with full ES tree.

Object Execute Subset: Response of Maintenance ES:

· To increase power, add oil to pressure point, release cylinder by 0.5 mm. · To get better cover, slow the conveyor and increase the furnace by 250 degrees. Output given to supervisor and technician. Corrections are made. Insulating problem is solved but conductivity remains low. User returns to SES and asked to continue.

Object Subset Creation: Problem redefined by user, system generates an ES tree and user selects: Materials ES. (Tree structure: Quality ES -> Maintenance ES -> Materials ES)

Execute Subset: Running the Materials ES with problem defined. The response of Materials ES is a set of specifications to correct and replace some of the materials used. Answer given to supervisor, corrections are made and results are satisfactory.

Object Subset Generation: Add the new subset to the database of the system.

As shown briefly, the model helps structure the problem solving process from definition, analysis, and creation, to the execution of a subset of ES to meet the particular user needs.

## 5. Knowledge integration

The integration of knowledge of several experts can be done in several ways or methods. Turban [12] outlines five such methods which are briefly reviewed and compared to the SES model. Methods for integrating the knowledge of ESs are the following:

\- Consensus methods

• Analytical approach

\- Select a specific line of reasoning

\- Automated process

\- Blackboard systems.

Consensus methods come from behavioral sciences (e.g., Delphi and others) and have been used also in GDSS. They are normally used in a human oriented decision making process. An analytic approach can be used when the experts decision involves numerical values such as in multiple criteria decision making. A specific line of reasoning may be selected after allowing each expert to follow his/her path without interaction and at a specific stage, based on predefined criteria, to select the course most appropriate for the problem domain. Such selection can be done manually or automatically by applying a set of rules and algorithms. Blackboard systems [9], a method used in several systems, maximize the independence among knowledge sources by appropriately dividing the problem domain. The integration among experts is kept to a minimum. As such the “blackboard” acts as a database of the available knowledge. Conclusion of the different knowledge sources are posted on the blackboard and are available to all sources of the system. Such data sharing is also found in GDSS by placing experts’ knowledge on the public screen.

Similar to blackboard systems, SES shares the knowledge across experts or ESs. However, the SES design provides for several other features which are unique and uncommon. They are:

Object oriented approach which allows for modular design of a system and compatibility to the existing information system without locking the user to a specific design. The Building Blocks of a decision system allow for tailoring the system to the particular needs of the user and problem domain.

Framework for evaluating expert systems in economic terms and determining the marginal contribution that a single system makes.

Synergism of information provides for a more flexible, open-ended and adaptive to change.

## 6. Summary and conclusion

The paper described a Synergetic Expert System (SES) model by which a decision maker can use several experts in the process of confronting a complex problem which needs a consolidated solution. The model facilitates configuring a subset – an arrangements of experts or ESs – that suits the specific problem. A formal definition of two building blocks, series and parallel, and efficient and economic subsets were given, as well as a delineation of the value of an expert to a decision making process.

The object-oriented approach taken in this model makes it flexible and adaptive to decision making situations which may require different configurations of expert systems. The linkage of several ESs allows coupling a variety of building blocks to suit the particular problem needs.

The parallel construct of ESs in the model, which is an arrangement of experts used concurrently, is suggested as a case of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS).

Synergetic use of experts, ESs, as well as the knowledge found in various frames in the model offer a unified view of information available at the macro SES level, together with the specific information and knowledge each ES possesses.

The work described here is conceptual. It outlines a direction in the design of future integrative systems that can be more general than current ESs while at the same time contain such ESs as part of a larger problem solving method for organizations and decision makers.

Appendix A. Algorithms for series and parallel

procedure series;
open a series frame;
begin
    repeat;
    get the desired ES from the user;
    define the ES as part of series;
    define the message (data) needed for the ES;
    get the desired data from the user;
    transfer data to ES; get answer from ES;
    if ES is not last in series;
    execute another frame in series;
    else give user the answer of series;
    until answer is a solution for user;
end;
procedure parallel;
open a parallel frame;
begin
    repeat;
    get desired ESs from the user;
    define all messages for each ES;
    get data from user;
    transfer appropriate data to each ES;
    obtain answers from each ES;
    if there are answers then
    begin
    ask user to select an answer;
    define the subset as a parallel;
    give answer message to user;
    end;
    until user desires;
end;
begin /\* multiple answers \*/
    repeat;
    save set of answers;
    give each ES the appropriate user reply;
    obtain answers for the ESs;
    display the set of answers to the user;
    until user is satisfied with an answer;
end;
