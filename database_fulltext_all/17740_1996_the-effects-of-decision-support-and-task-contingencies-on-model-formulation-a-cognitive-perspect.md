# The effects of decision support and task contingencies on model formulation: A cognitive perspective

Izak Benbasat $^{a,*}$ , Peter Todd $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC, Canada V6T 1Z2 $^{b}$ School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Ont., Canada K7L 3N6

## Abstract

This paper takes a cognitive cost–benefit approach to understanding model formulation. Work in the behavioral decision literature on the role of effort and accuracy in choice tasks indicates that effort, or cognitive cost, is a key factor in understanding decision behavior. However, the model formulation literature does not discuss how effort interacts with other factors, such as task complexity and decision aids, to influence model formulation. In this paper, based on the work on the cost–benefit theories of cognition, we posit that two types of effort, namely that associated with building or formulating a model and that associated with utilizing that model in the solution of a problem, will influence model formulation. We then examine how the methods used in the behavioral decision making literature and the reported findings concerning the interaction of effort with task and decision aids can be utilized to understand model formulation.

Keywords: Model formulation; Decision support systems; Cognitive cost-benefit approach; Behavioral decision making

## 1. Introduction

The term model is described in various ways by different authors in diverse disciplines; as Wagner ([31], p. 11) notes it has “several shades of meaning.” According to the editors of this special issue a model is an abstract representation of the essential elements of a real world phenomenon. There are many different types of models, including verbal, schematic, analog and mathematical models among others. Often these models represent different degrees of concreteness or abstractness. For example, in building an information system we often start with a fuzzy and highly abstract model based on a user view. This might be translated through the process of systems analysis into a refined logical model of what function this information system will perform. Next, during systems design a more concrete model will be used to represent how the system will work. Later at the implementation stage we may begin to build physical prototypes that provide a concrete representation of the system. At each stage the purpose of the model is to provide us with the essential details required to advance our understanding of a problem, to eventually lead to a solution. In other words, models guide us through problem solving:

Constructing a model helps you put the complexities and possible uncertainties of attending a decision-problem into a logical framework amenable to comprehensive analysis. Such a model clarifies the decision alternatives and their anticipated effects, indicates the data that are relevant for analyzing alternatives, and leads to informative conclusions ([31], p. 10).

While the process of model formulation is undeniably important, we currently lack a full understanding of how and why models are formulated in different ways. For example, how do individuals decide what kind of model is needed at any point in time; and what factors influence the processes by which models are formulated or used to solve problems in an organizational context? In part, this lack of understanding is due to the fact that modelling is often studied from a normative perspective. Descriptive questions about the behavior of modellers, such as those posed above, are asked less often.

To begin to address these questions Vinze et al. [30] describe a number of approaches that have been used in model formulation, including construction of linear programming models, logic-based approaches, hierarchical planning, and case-based planning approaches. They take the position that in its basic form “the process of model formulation is a human-problem solving activity” and can be studied as such. Thus, in order to better understand this activity, they asked expert modellers to work on model formulation tasks while thinking aloud. The verbal protocols generated provided data about the processes these experts went through. The protocol data was then analyzed to develop a cognitive model to categorize and understand approaches used by the experts to formulate models to represent problems.

In essence, Vinze et al. [30] describe the types of model formulation approaches that an individual might employ or alternatively, the strategies that are used in model formulation. However, they do not examine how approaches to model formulation might be contingent upon factors such as problem type, problem complexity, abilities of the model builder, and the type of decision aids or model formulation tools available to the model builder. Such factors have long been identified in the behavioral decision making literature as important determinants of how individuals process information and ultimately make decisions (e.g., [1,10,20]). In order to develop a more complete understanding of the model formulation process it is important to examine the influence of such factors. Moreover, it is important to develop a theoretical perspective on how they might influence model formulation. Work by Payne [17] suggests that a cognitive cost–benefit or effort–accuracy model can be used to understand how various task, environmental and decision aiding variables influence strategy selection in decision making and choice problems.

Over the past several years we have been engaged in research which uses this cognitive cost–benefit approach to examine how the selection of decision strategies is influenced by the interaction between tasks and decision aids which are designed to alter the effort required to utilize various decision strategies $[25–29]$ . We have examined the factors that affect the strategy selection process primarily in the domain of preferential choice. While this domain is different than the problem structuring domain related to model formulation, we believe that the lessons learned from studying the role of decision aids in influencing these choice processes can provide important insights to researchers interested in understanding and supporting model formulation.

This belief is based on our view that when experienced decision makers are solving a problem the challenge they face is not so much one of model formulation, but one of model selection. We argue that a decision maker has a series of pre-existing strategies which have been learned and stored in memory. Thus, to formulate a strategy, in effect, they make a selection from a repertoire of pre-existing strategies which may be applied or adapted to the problem at hand. Thus, a key aspect of the formulation process is a decision concerning which decision making strategy to employ in a given case. We argue that, this determination is made by taking into consideration the characteristics of the problem, capabilities of the decision maker, and the types of tools or decision aids that might be available to support different strategies. Incorporating the cognitive cost–benefit perspective, the process of model formulation becomes one of selecting a model which will provide a reasonably accurate solution while managing the level of effort to be expended by the decision maker. Since this behavior is also evident in the choice domains that we have studied, we believe that our research should provide some insights into the model formulation process.

In the remainder of this paper we describe this research and its implications for supporting model formulation. The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section we discuss our general view of decision strategies, model formulation and cognitive effort. Section 3 discusses examples of the type of strategies utilized in preferential choice problems and how to model the types of effort involved in their execution. Section 4 describes how we design decision aids to reduce or eliminate such effort. Section 5 summarizes the empirical evidence suggesting that when decision aids reduce the effort of implementing certain strategies, as expected, strategy formulation is influenced. The last two sections discuss the implications of the theoretical and empirical evidence presented in this paper on our understanding of model formulation.

## 2. Models, strategies and effort in problem-solving

In this section of the paper we outline our perspective on the study of model and strategy formulation and identify the assumptions underlying our analysis. Researchers studying decision making behavior have concluded that decision makers are highly adaptive [20]. That is, they will formulate different strategies to deal with a given problem as a function of task and environmental demands. Subtle changes in problem formulation can lead to significant changes in decision strategy [6]. What causes these changes in strategy?

In our view, when deciding how to solve a problem or building a model an individual is weighing two major factors. The first is the benefit to be derived from the strategy. This can be assessed in terms of the quality of the decision that would be obtained from the use of the model formulated. The second influence is the cognitive effort associated with: (a) building or formulating a model, and (b) putting that model to use, i.e., utilizing it in the solution of a problem.

We argue that individuals make tradeoffs between these benefits and costs, or effort and accuracy, when formulating and subsequently utilizing a model. The empirical support for this assertion is based the general behavioral decision making literature (see [20] for a review) and specifically, on studies examining the second type of effort, namely the use of models and decision aids to reduce the effort of utilizing a particular model or strategy (e.g., [28,29]). Empirical evidence suggests that when the effort required to use a strategy is reduced the individual is more likely to utilize that strategy. Furthermore, we have empirical evidence which suggests that by managing the effort required for different strategies, decision makers can be induced to use more normatively oriented decision strategies. Thus, we will demonstrate that the effort required to use a strategy can have predictable influences. We do not have direct evidence which indicates how effort considerations might influence the model building process, though we assume, for this paper, that the same logic would apply.

## 2.1. Strategies for preferential choice problems

The empirical evidence we will present is based on the study of preferential choice problems, a task where a decision maker chooses one from among a set of alternatives, each of which is described by a common set of attributes. For example, alternative apartments may be described by a set of attributes, such as number of bedrooms, size, distance to work and rent.

A number of approaches have been identified by researchers for solving preferential choice problems, primarily by observing decision makers while solving problems. These approaches have been called models of decision making [16,24], decision rules, theories or models of choice, heuristics, and strategies [20]. Payne et al. ([20], p. 9) define a decision strategy as “a sequence of mental and effector (actions on the environment) operations used to transform an initial state of knowledge to a final goal state where the decision maker views the problem as solved”; “each strategy can be thought as a method (sequence) of operations for searching through the problem space” (p. 23). In this paper, we will be using the terms strategies and models interchangeably; the former refers to the approach followed by a decision maker and the latter a representation of that strategy. A key assumption made in this paper is that what we have learned about the factors that influence the use of strategies in preferential choice can be extended to and provide insight for researchers studying problem formulation.

## 2.2. Decision aids and effort of strategy execution

The work we will discuss in this paper deals with the way decision aids influence strategy formulation. Our contention is that decision aids influence the selection of a particular strategy, through their influence on the cognitive cost of using that strategy. This contention is based on results from several studies $[25–29]$ in which different decision aids were made available for reducing the cognitive effort of dealing with choice problems of differing complexity. In these studies decision makers were observed to alter their strategies in response to the set of decision aids made available to them. They did this in such a way as to minimize the level of effort required to solve a problem. Thus, understanding the role of cognitive effort appears to provide insight into how decision aids influence the formulation of problem solving strategies by altering the effort relationships among the component operations of such strategies. It is based on three notions:

1. that decomposing strategies into their subcomponents offers estimates of the relative cognitive cost of using them [10,22],

2. that these same decompositions can be used to identify the decision aids needed to support a particular strategy [10], and

3. that these decision aids can reduce the cognitive effort associated with the various mental operations that are subcomponents of one or more choice strategies by automating storage, retrieval, and computational tasks.

The central proposition of this work is that specific features can be incorporated within a set of decision aids that will alter the relative effort required to implement different choice strategies, and that this will influence strategy formulation. The ability to manipulate effort provides an opportunity for the “engineering” of choice processes in such a way as to influence decision makers to utilize models which could lead to a high level of value or utility but which are otherwise cognitively too demanding to implement. Encouraging the use of such normative decision models has been an elusive goal in the design of computer-based decision support systems to date [14].

## 2.3. Strategy formulation versus strategy selection

To reiterate our basic argument, when experienced decision makers approach a problem we assert that the challenge they face is not so much one of model formulation, but one of model selection. We assume that a decision maker has a series of pre-existing strategies which have been learned and stored in memory. Thus, to formulate a strategy, in effect, they make a selection from a repertoire of pre-existing strategies which may be applied or adapted to the problem at hand. In essence, strategy selection becomes a multiattribute choice problem in itself [20]. In other words, the key aspect of the formulation process is a decision concerning which decision making strategy to employ in a given case. This determination is made by taking into consideration the characteristics of the problem, capabilities of the decision maker, and the types of tools or decision aids that might be available to support different strategies. Taken together, these factors influence the effort and accuracy associated with implementing different strategies. From this perspective, the process of model formulation becomes a process of selecting a model which will provide a reasonably accurate solution while managing the level of effort to be expended by the decision maker.

This process of strategy selection implies a top-down problem solving approach. According to Payne et al. [20] the view that model selection decisions are only made top-down, based on the information extracted from the original problem definition, is overly restrictive. An alternative view is that such decisions are opportunistic [8], which is in line with Vinze et al.'s [30] view of model formulation as an opportunistic problem-solving activity. This view posits that during the course of problem-solving, individuals may alter their strategies based on what they have learned about the structure of the problem. In our work on preferential choice problems, we have encountered examples of both top-down and opportunistic strategy formulation processes. In both cases we believe that this behavior is guided by a desire to balance trade-offs between effort and accuracy in decision making, and that furthermore decision aids can be used to alter the balance in these relationships. The ways in which effort and accuracy trade-offs are made in the choice domain are explored in the next section.

## 2.4. The role of cognitive effort in strategy selection

Studies in the choice domain indicate that decision makers are highly adaptive in strategy selection $[10,20]$ . Researchers have proposed a variety of mechanisms that influence strategy selection $[7]$ . Among these, effort and accuracy have received considerable attention. Payne $[17]$ proposed a cost–benefit framework of cognition which incorporates the notion that decision makers focus on tradeoffs between accuracy and effort in various decision making tasks. A subsequent series of both empirical and simulation work undertaken by Payne and his colleagues, among others, is largely supportive of this notion (see, e.g., $[3,5,9-11,18,23]$ ). According to the cost–benefit framework, the joint objectives of a decision maker are to maximize accuracy (or decision quality) and minimize effort. As these objectives often conflict, tradeoffs are made between the two. A series of conceptual, simulation-based and empirical studies suggest that managing effort may be the key consideration of decision makers in managing these trade-offs.

Beach and Mitchell [1] provide a contingency model which links various personal and task characteristics to strategy selection. They argue that the decision maker is motivated to pursue the least effortful strategy which will provide an acceptable solution, given the various contingencies. Others have also argued that strategies are evaluated in terms of effort subject to some constraint on decision quality [15,22]. Effort may be weighted more heavily than accuracy because feedback on effort expenditure is relatively immediate while feedback on accuracy is subject to both delay and ambiguity [6,13].

Monte Carlo simulations of decision strategies demonstrate that heuristics will save decision makers considerable effort relative to a normative approach, while sacrificing little in terms of accuracy $[10,18,19]$ . In addition, numerous empirical studies have concluded that decision makers attend more to effort reduction than to accuracy maximization $[2–4,11,21]$ .

Overall, the empirical, simulation and conceptual literatures indicate that effort is an important factor influencing strategy selection. Given this, it should be possible to manipulate the processes of strategy formulation by explicitly changing the effort required to pursue various strategies. One way to do this is by introducing decision aids which alter the cognitive costs of executing different strategies. To design such design aids it is important to have a detailed model of decision strategy which outlines the actions and effort involved.

## 3. Strategy formulation and effort in the choice domain

In this section, we illustrate how preferential choice problems can be modelled in terms of their elementary information processes. Whether or not the individual decision makers perform such a modelling exercise prior to strategy formulation is not known. However, such models are useful for predicting how the decision maker will behave by estimating the effort associated with implementing different strategies and assessing how decision aids that alter the effort associated with particular strategies influence strategy formulation.

In order to better understand how decision strategies can be supported we introduce the notion of elementary information processes (EIPs). EIPs can be considered the basic building blocks of a decision strategy. They are low level cognitive operations such as reading a value, comparing two values or storing a result in long term memory. EIPs have been used to model a variety of decision processes (see for example $[10,18]$ ). The EIPs used in preferential choice strategies represent three basic classes of decision making effort:

(i) processing effort associated with comparison and computation within and among alternatives,

(ii) recall effort which is associated with the retrieval of information about attributes, such as importance weights and threshold values, from long term memory, and,

(iii) tracking effort which is associated with the storage and subsequent retrieval of information about alternatives such as the status of various alternatives prior to choice, e.g., whether an alternative is discarded in the case of elimination strategies or what the current sum of the weighted values are in the case of the additive strategies.

Note that the first category, processing effort, focuses on the manipulation of information. The second two categories both involve memory operations. They are distinguished by the fact that attribute recall requires the retrieval of information that is assumed to exist in memory prior to the choice process whereas tracking effort involves the storage and retrieval of information created during the choice process. Strategies differ in terms of the demands they place on the three components of effort. This is because strategies differ both in terms of the number of units of information that are considered during the decision making process and the number of EIPs that are applied to each unit of information. In discussing the effort in terms of EIPs we will assume, for simplicity, that each EIP requires the same amount of effort. While this is an oversimplification, it does not alter the overall thrust of our analysis about the relative differences in effort between strategies.

In order to illustrate how EIPs can be used to model a decision strategy and then suggest ways to support that strategy, we will describe the EIPs required for two commonly employed preferential choice strategies: additive difference and elimination by aspects and then, in the next section, suggest some decision aids based on those models. We will discuss the EIPs required for the two strategies in the context of an $r^{*}c$ matrix, where r represents the number of rows/attributes and c the number of columns/alternatives (as in Fig. 1). Thus the term $r^{*}c$ would represent the number of units of information (or operands) to which EIPs (or operators) are applied.

The Additive Difference (AD) strategy is based on a comparison between attributes for two alternatives at a time. To make one such comparison for an attribute pair involves a set of ten EIPs: move to attribute value a, read a, move to attribute value b, read b, subtract a from b giving difference d, retrieve weight indicating the importance of the attribute, combine difference d and weight, retrieve the overall score, add weighted difference to overall score, and store total difference. Once all attributes have been evaluated for a pair of alternatives, the preferred alternative is retained while the other is rejected. This process is repeated until one alternative remains (i.e., c - 1 times for c alternatives). Thus, the total number of EIPs required to implement an AD strategy for a problem with c alternatives, each described by r attributes is $(10^{*}r)^{*}(c - 1)$ .

The relatively effortful AD strategy can be compared to the less effortful Elimination by Aspects (EBA). EBA is based on a comparison of attribute values to some threshold level and the elimination of any alternative which does not meet the threshold level for any one of its attributes. The sequence of four EIPs required to evaluate a single attribute is: move, read, compare, and eliminate. In addition, as each attribute is considered, the attribute threshold must be retrieved once. Furthermore, EBA requires additional effort to keep track of the status of alternatives to avoid reprocessing those that have been previously eliminated.

The total number of EIPs required to follow an EBA strategy for an $r^{*}c$ problem set is $(1^{*}r)+(1^{*}(r-1)^{*}c)+(4^{*}(r^{*}c))$ . The first value “ $1^{*}r$ ” represents the number of memory retrieval operations, one for each attribute, needed to access attribute threshold values. The constant 4 represents the four processing operations referred to above for each comparison of a cell value to a threshold. The factor $1^{*}(r-1)^{*}c$ represents the tracking effort required to maintain the status of alternatives in the matrix. This is required since the attribute-wise evaluation must take into account whether or not an alternative has been eliminated based on the value of a previous attribute in order to avoid unacceptable alternatives being reconsidered as potential choices. This tracking operation must occur for each attribute after the first one is examined since some alternatives may have been previously eliminated.

It should be noted that the numbers provided in Table 1 are estimates of EIP load and are meant to illustrate the relationships between the strategies. In general, AD requires more effort than EBA, regardless of factors such as information load, alternative similarity or other task characteristics. In large part, the additional effort required for AD relates to processing. Typically, both the number of units of information processed and the number of operations (EIPs) performed on each unit of information will be greater for AD than for EBA. In addition, it can be argued that the type of operations required by AD are more effortful in that they tend to involve computation rather than simple comparison [18]. On the other hand, AD should lead to a better choice being made than does EBA since it considers all available information, weights attributes according to their importance to the decision maker and is compensatory, allowing high values on some attributes to offset low values on others [12]. The effort-accuracy trade-off would suggest that decision makers would prefer EBA since it typically generates reasonable solutions at relatively low cognitive cost. However, by providing a decision aid which automates some of the processing associated with AD, and consequently reduces the amount of effort to implement it relative to the elimination strategies, it may be possible to induce decision makers to switch from elimination strategies to the more effortful, but more accurate, AD strategy. ## 4. Supporting preferential choice strategies

This section describes two decision aids employed in our research to give the reader an understanding of the influence of such support tools on reducing the effort associated with strategy implementation. The discussion here is not intended to be a full coverage of all decision aids, but rather illustrative of specific ways to support the AD and EBA strategies.

To support the AD strategy we designed the COMPARE command (see Fig. 2). This command takes differences between any pair of alternatives, indicates the preferred alternative for each attribute, and displays the magnitude of the difference between alternatives. Thus, COMPARE eliminates five of the processing operations associated with AD, namely operations: move to $a$ , read $a$ , move to $b$ , read $b$ and subtract $a$ from $b$ . However, it does not eliminate the other five operations associated with AD (retrieve weight, combine difference $a - b$ and weight, retrieve overall total score, add weighted difference to total score and store total score). With the use of COMPARE, the total effort required of the decision maker for AD is $((c - 1)^* (5^* r))$ or half of what is required in an unaided condition. The bottom part of Table 1 shows the effort estimates for following the AD strategy when support is provided in the form of the COMPARE function (indicated as HIGH AD in the table) and when it is not provided (LOW AD).

CONDITIONAL DROP was developed to support the EBA strategy. The CONDITIONAL DROP command automates the move/read/compare/eliminate sequence of operations associated with EBA, automatically comparing attribute values to a threshold specified by the decision maker and eliminating any alternatives which violate the threshold. The user only has to enter the threshold values into the CONDITIONAL DROP command (i.e., perform “r” attribute recall operations, one for each attribute, as shown in Table 1). All processing steps are automated. Since undesirable alternatives are automatically dropped, the memory requirement associated with tracking the current status of an alternative is also eliminated. In this way the CONDITIONAL DROP supports both processing and tracking effort. Table 1 shows the effort estimates for following the EBA strategy when support is provided in the form of the CONDITIONAL DROP function (indicated as HIGH EBA in the table) and when it is not provided (LOW EBA).

The effort argument suggests that decision makers will alter their strategy when provided with one, or both of these commands. In short, when a COMPARE command alone is provided we would expect to see greater use of the AD strategy since it is made relatively easier. When CONDITIONAL DROP is provided then we would expect to see EBA used more prevalently. When both commands are provided together, it is important to examine the relative effort required to use each strategy. In this case the CONDITIONAL DROP provide almost complete automation of the EBA strategy, while COMPARE still leaves the decision maker with significant effort required to implement the AD strategy. Thus, the effort minimization argument would suggest that decision makers would opt for the EBA strategy when provided with both tools. This suggests that it is important to consider the relative level of effort that different decision aids impose in strategy formulation. The data supporting these contentions is reviewed in the next section.

## 5. Summary of empirical findings

Our basic proposition is that, all other things being equal, if a decision aid reduces the effort associated with employing a particular strategy relative to other strategies, a decision maker will be more inclined to employ that strategy. The bottom part of Table 1 shows how the level of effort required to use each of the three strategies outlined above changes with the different levels of support for each experimental condition. The total effort is decomposed into three components: attribute recall effort, alternative tracking effort, and processing effort. The entries in Table 1 provide estimates of the number of EIPs that would be required under each strategy assuming that the decision aid was employed in an appropriate fashion to support that strategy. It is important in examining Table 1 to note the relative changes in effort between strategies that occur when the different decision aids are provided.

To test this proposition, we conducted a series of experimental studies. The details of the experimental procedures used are available in $[28,29]$ . The general approach used in these studies was to observe the strategy formulation/selection process used by subjects who were asked to make apartment selection decisions. Subjects were assigned to various treatment groups; each group had access to different types of decision aids. For example, the COMPARE decision aid was used to support the implementation of the AD strategy, whereas CONDITIONAL DROP was provided to support EBA.

In a series of five studies dealing with preferential choice problems, we found support for the proposition stated above $[25–29]$ . In general, the findings indicate that decision makers tend to adapt their strategy selection to the type of decision aids available in such a way as to maintain a low level of effort expenditure. In two experiments comparing the behavior of aided and unaided decision makers $[26]$ , a decision aid which reduced the relative effort required to follow the EBA as compared to the conjunctive strategy, both elimination type strategies, led the subjects to select the EBA strategy in making a choice. This occurred even though without the aid, subjects were more likely to employ a conjunctive strategy. A third experiment $[26]$ provided limited evidence that when support for an additive strategy, which has a higher expected accuracy, was provided subjects were more inclined to use that strategy even though it required a higher level of effort to implement. A fourth experiment looked at this same phenomena while designing the experimental treatments in such a way as to focus more clearly on support for specific choice strategies (in this case AD and EBA) and using a more refined form of data analysis [28]. Consistent with the previous studies, this fourth experiment also showed that strategy selection could be influenced by the availability of different decision aids. More importantly, it reinforced the finding that the availability of decision aids can induce additive strategy formulation when they reduce the effort required to implement such strategies.

We also tested the robustness of the findings in the fourth experiment $[28]$ by examining the use of similar decision aids under conditions of higher cognitive loads, under conditions where the number of alternatives in the choice matrix were tripled compared to the ones used in earlier studies $[29]$ . The formulation of filtering and elimination strategies, which require less effort but do not necessary lead to high quality outcomes, are commonly observed when the cognitive load, typically operationalized as information load, is increased (see for example $[16]$ ). Thus, it is useful to test if the ability of a decision aid to induce the formulation of additive strategies, which leads to higher quality outcomes, persists under conditions where information load is increased. If this is the case, it will provide insight into whether a decision aid can override the influence of task variables, such as problem size, on strategy selection. Such a finding would be particularly important since it has been commonly observed that even minor changes in task characteristics can lead to significant changes in information processing behavior.

The results of the fifth study [29] show that the results of previous studies still hold under conditions of high task complexity. There was an interaction effect caused by the behavior of subjects provided support for an additive difference but not for an elimination by aspects strategy. These subjects' behavior is consistent with the utilization of an AD strategy. These results are consistent with our earlier studies in indicating that decision aids influence strategy formulation in such a way as to allow the decision maker to maintain low overall levels of effort expenditure. They further indicate that decision aids can be designed to overcome task limitations that may constrain strategy formulation behavior.

## 6. Implications for strategy formulation

In this paper, we have provided a summary of the studies conducted in the behavioral decision making literature on the costs and benefits of cognition and discussed our work which extended these studies by investigating how the use of decision aids could alter the costs associated with particular strategies, or their subcomponents, thus inducing the decision maker to shift strategies. We also described the method used to estimate the costs of implementing given strategies by decomposing them into elementary information processes. We then demonstrated how to predict the effects of different decision aids on strategies by examining their impact on effort reduction through the automation of some of these elementary processes.

Our contention is that model formulation can not be considered independently of the costs and benefits of utilizing the model. In this section of the paper we will offer some ideas for designers to consider when building tools to support the model formulation and decision making process.

In our studies on choice problems, we assumed that strategies which make fuller use of information, such as additive difference, lead to more accurate decisions. Such an assumption is supported by the normative work in this area (e.g., [12]). In model formulation, the benefits to be gained from developing “better” models could be viewed from the perspective of both the person who will use the model and the person who develops models for others to use. For the former, the benefit to be derived is a function of the level of accuracy which results from using the model. For the latter, the benefits might be the same, i.e., the user and formulator of the model have the same objectives, or the formulator might be more interested in the sophistication of the model to be developed, creating an agency problem.

We do not know if individuals can predict the amount of benefits to be derived from the use of a model yet to be formulated, nor do we have any research evidence as to how individuals form perceptions about the value of particular models. This issue is not explicitly addressed in the choice literature, although simulations can be used to assess the benefits of the model to be formulated and then test them against actual outcomes. For research purposes, model quality/benefits could also be estimated using a panel of expert judges.

The effort associated in formulating a model will depend upon the cognitive abilities of the developer, the size and complexity of the task or decision problem being modelled, and the tools available to the developer. In addition to the costs of formulating a model, there are costs associated with utilizing the model, that is putting it into use in solving a problem. Several types of cognitive effort were identified for choice tasks: (i) processing effort associated with information in the choice matrix, (ii) memory operations associated with recall and tracking, as discussed in Section 4, (iii) planning effort related to determining tradeoffs between attributes, and (iv) effort devoted to understanding and using the decision aid [26]. These cognitive costs could be reduced by the use of computer-based models and other decision aids to process the information in the model. However, there will always be some elements of effort that can not be automated, such as the cost of determining the weights and utilities in multiattribute-multialternative problems.

These costs, especially the cost of model utilization, are more salient to the persons who are formulating a model for their own use, but should be equally important to the designer who is developing a model for another. There are factors unique to each individual such as cognitive abilities or experience with similar models which will reduce the cost of utilizing a model, as well as other factors, such as the availability of decision aids, that would make such utilization easier. If perceived or actual costs of putting a certain model into operation goes up, then the individual is likely to formulate a simpler model, that costs less to utilize. This would be true under conditions where the task becomes more complex or when decision aids are not available. Our research and other studies show that effort is a highly influenced tial factor in strategy selection. To the extent that such effort is reduced, through the use of decision aids, as we investigated in our studies, or through other means such as training and experience, we would expect that the model builder will be more willing to formulate more complex (and effortful to operate) models which are expected to lead to better quality outcomes.

For the person formulating models for the use of another individual the goal is to move the decision maker towards a normative strategy which presumably provides a more accurate outcome. The key question for a model developer is how to design a system which effects this shift in problem solving process. Our results indicate that effort may be a key factor in facilitating or inhibiting this shift. The effort minimizer is likely to move towards the normative strategy only if it is as easy as or easier than some competing alternative process that is available. Thus the model formulator should focus on this effort objective, otherwise the model may either be rejected or used counterproductively. The developer must consider both the effort required of the decision maker to interact with the model and the effort required to process the information generated by the system. If these factors are properly taken into account then it is possible to formulate a model for another person that would lead to higher quality decisions. Thus it is important for the model formulator to understand both (1) the link between the features included in a model and effort reduction for problem solving, and (2) the effort to utilize the model. It is likely that model features which provide the greatest benefits (in terms of pursuing a course of action that is most effective) are more difficult to use. This is a common argument for the low usage of many management science models. Therefore, the designer has to think about the user making a tradeoff where cost is the effort to employ the model or its particular features and the benefit is the quality of problem solving achieved by its use. If the features provided are difficult to use or their potential value in reducing effort is not evident to the user, then the designer has failed to provide an adequate model to use.

Lastly, we discuss two issues of interest to researchers who would like to study the cost–benefit issues related to model formulation. We have illustrated a methodology to estimate the costs of model utilization, and to predict the impact of decision aids in reducing such costs, for choice problems by focusing on the impact of decision aids on processing and memory operations. The extent to which this process is also directly applicable to model formulation is an open research question. In our analysis, we used approximate estimation methods such as assuming that all elementary information processes required the same amount of effort. We recommend that researchers studying model formulation problems to do so in the preliminary stages of their work on the impact of effort.

The issue of calculating the costs of model formulation, and how model development tools would reduce such costs is an interesting and challenging research topic. Are individuals and model formulators able to estimate such costs, and are these estimates influenced by factors such as problem complexity? Another issue that needs to be investigated is whether or not individuals building models for their own use are capable of considering, and distinguishing between, two types of cost, namely the costs of building and the costs of utilizing these models.

To conclude, studies in multiattribute problems have shown that normative models lead to higher quality decisions but are typically more difficult, or costly, to understand and utilize. The message we would like to leave with researchers interested in model formulation is that cost/benefit considerations are important in model formulation and utilization. It is therefore important to understand how decision makers trade off both (i) the cost of developing a model versus the benefits from using the model, and (ii) the cost of using the model versus the benefits expected from it. Furthermore, to effectively support such processes it is important to study how task conditions and decision aids interact with effort to influence model formulation processes.
