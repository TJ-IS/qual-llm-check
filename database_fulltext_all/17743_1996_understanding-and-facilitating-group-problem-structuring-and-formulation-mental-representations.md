# Understanding and facilitating group problem structuring and formulation: Mental representations, interaction, and representation aids

Anne P. Massey $^{a,*}$ , William A. Wallace $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Business Management, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC 27695-7229, USA $^{b}$ Department of Decision Sciences and Engineering Systems, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, NY 12180-3590, USA

## Abstract

Many issues facing the organizations of today are complex and ill-structured. The complexity of these issues do not lend themselves to structuring and formulation by quantitative models, nor simple intuitive problem solving. Rather, making sense of these situations necessitates considering and oftentimes negotiating alternative models of the ill-structured situation. The use of groups is one way to gain access to alternative perspectives. In this paper, we argue that individual mental representations are the underlying basis of group interaction and communication. And, that these internal representations can be formalized in external representations that may be useful to facilitate interaction and communication as a group strives to create a shared problem perspective. Based on this theoretical foundation, we explored these ideas by examining small groups using representational aids during the process of structuring and formulating a problem. While descriptive in nature, this study provides insights on the successful use of representational aids and suggests that these aids can positively impact problem structuring and formulation by facilitating the sharing of alternative perspectives.

Keywords: Problem structuring and formulation; Cognitive models; Mental representations; Representation aids; Model visualization; Group interaction; Group communication

## 1. Introduction

Problem solving is an integral part of the management process. It can be described as human thought that consists of both sense-making and action-taking $[61,69]$ . Using a phase or stage description, the problem solving process consists of identification, definition, alternative generation, choice of a solution, and implementation and testing $[21,20,1,66]$ . The problem definition phase is the process by which a model is developed by assembling components and relationships from the stimuli that led to the recognition and identification of the problem [20,66]. During this phase, the attempt is made to make sense of the situation and understand the nature of the problem, before attempting to generate a solution [1,9,62,69]. The resulting problem statement or model is a problem definition [71].

Many issues facing organizations today are messy and confusing. Because these types of problems lack clear boundaries and have no definitive formulation $[27,66,67,34]$ , they do not lend themselves to structuring and formulation by quantitative models, nor simply intuitive problem solving $[51]$ . Making sense of these ill-structured problem situations in order to define the problem to be solved is complicated by the difficulty in specifying and understanding the relationships between relevant problem variables or elements [13,66,67]. However, because the definition of the problem ultimately affects the direction of all the succeeding problem solving activities, it is crucial that a “good” problem definition is developed [1,41–43,66,67].

Unfortunately, an additional characteristic of ill-structured problems is that oftentimes the structuring and formulation of these problems are confounded by the multiplicity of individuals and groups with various and sometimes conflicting interests. These individuals and groups may have very different perspectives about the nature of the problem to be solved $[14]$ . Therefore, these individuals and groups — based on these perspectives — may make sense of a given situation in very different ways thus leading to potentially different definitions of the problem to be solved. A limited field of view is the greatest threat during the activities of structuring and formulation. An attempt should be made to broaden the problem solvers' perspective by considering alternative ways of perceiving and ultimately defining an ill-structured situation $[66,67]$ .

The use of groups to define problems is one way to gain access to alternative perspectives for problems with no definitive formulation. Turoff and Hiltz [65] stated that “when the problem is ill-structured, one needs to communicate with others to determine what information is relevant”. It is argued that the interaction process allows the group to produce an outcome which is superior to that which should have been produced given the particular characteristics (knowledge, skills, resources) of the individual members [24].

Representational decision aids are purported to facilitate groups in this process of information exchange as members attempt to find relevant elements of the problem situation, structure the relationships between these elements, and formulate the problem to be solved $[72]$ . The aim of these approaches is to at least partially model previously unstructured situations. Additionally, they help overcome limitations in mental resources and extend human processing capabilities $[49,50,52]$ by providing, as Larkin $[33]$ suggests, an “information storage resource”. These decision aids should assist individuals and groups in representing and communicating problem perspectives.

In the first part of this paper, the theoretical linkage between the cognitive view of group interaction, the activities of problem definition, and the use of representational aids to facilitate these activities will be presented. The second part of this paper explores this linkage by examining eight small groups engaged in defining an ill-structured problem situation. These groups utilized representational methods, specifically cognitive maps and influence diagrams, as the means to present and manipulate the elements they felt were relevant to defining the problem in the given situation.

The study reported here is descriptive in nature, designed to explore how representational aids may be used to facilitate the exchange of individual perspectives and the development of a shared group understanding.

## 2. Cognition and interaction

As the previous section detailed, a cogent argument is not needed to highlight the importance of problem defining and the necessity of entertaining alternative perspectives in order to develop a richer, shared understanding of the problem to be solved. In order to fully comprehend a problem definition, one must understand the perspective — mental representation — that an individual uses to make sense of the situation $[15,29]$ . Thus, perhaps more importantly than the definition itself, is how one arrived at that definition. In order to more fully explore and understand alternative definitions, one must be able to explore and understand the “whys” behind the definition — that is, what is the mental representation used to develop a given definition. Constructing groups of individuals to gain access to alternative perspectives is not, however, devoid of difficulties as group members may not utilize fully or efficiently knowledge members contain. In order to be effective, members must be able to identify relevant concepts and relationships, point out erroneous assumptions, and use each other as resources to share and explore information contained in the mental representations they have of a problem situation.

Mental representations serve to organize the beliefs that individuals hold about a situation $[11,28,39,40,46,55,68,23]$ . While these representations may not be completely accurate, they are used by the individual to make sense of and ultimately define a problem in a given situation. These internal representations are constrained by such things as the individuals technical and social background, previous experience with similar systems, and the structure of the information processing system ([22], p. 8; [14], p. 81). Interaction with the system and others can modify the mental representation as new information is incorporated, and old (perhaps erroneous) information is deleted.

Cognitive science provides theories on individual and social cognition and attempts to explain how knowledge is stored, retrieved and processed using these internal representations. Newell and Simon's [46] information processing theory suggests that problem solvers map out the task to build an objective representation of the situation. This is done by identifying relevant concepts and relationships and using these to build a mental representation.

The relationships between concepts results in the formulation of networks, or models, to describe situations. The notion that the organization of concepts in memory can be conceptualized in network form is no longer new. Minsky's [39,40] frame construction and Newell and Simon's [46] are two based on nodes (concepts) and arcs (relationships). Others include semantic nets [56], schemes [4] [6], schemata [53], and cognitive maps [5,17,19].

This internalized process of building a mental representation or perspective parallels the notions of problem structuring and formulation found in problem solving literature. The processes of structuring and formulation call for finding relevant elements and variables, and investigating the relationships between these in order to develop and explore alternative problem definitions [1]. Internally, these processes are more automatic and parallel than suggested by the phase descriptions of model formulation theory, but no less relevant.

## 2.1. Sharing mental representations: The underlying basis of group interaction

When individuals communicate in a group they are, essentially, sharing their individual mental representations. Fig. 1, adapted from Carley [10], attempts to explain the co-construction of the cognitive world of the individual and group through interaction and communication. When confronted with a situation, an individual constructs a mental representation or cognitive structure in order to make sense of the situation. These mental representations are brought to the group and potentially communicated through group interaction. Through interaction and communication, as members acquire and transmit knowledge, individual representations can be altered. A group cognitive structure is formed as individuals find and align themselves with other members who have similar cognitive structures or models of the situation. The group structure reflects shared and conceivably new knowledge. Further interaction and communication is facilitated as the group and individual cognitive structures recursively co-evolve. Thus, the higher the level and ease of communication the greater the potential for sharing knowledge [10].

The effectiveness of the group process (interaction and communication) and the quality of the group output (the model) is dependent upon the sharing of individual representations and the ability of members to identify deficiencies in these representations. The nature of ill-structured problems suggests that no one perspective is “right”, nor sufficient to make sense of the problem situation. Rather, the purpose of forming groups of stakeholders should be to gain as many alternative perspectives as possible in order to achieve a richer, shared understanding of the problem in order to define it [65–67]. It is vital that the various perspectives be taken into account and, when there is a difference, a mutually acceptable definition must be agreed upon [71].

However, because communication typically revolves around verbal interactions, miscommunication of perspectives can occur. Assuming others will understand, individuals often speak in generalities. Miscommunication — the difference between perceived and real understanding — results in a reduction of shared knowledge and potentially group effectiveness [12].

Thus, the next section will discuss how visual depictions of internally held mental representations may be a useful means to facilitate interaction and communication between individuals. Facilitation of this process may promote the development of a shared group perspective and ultimately a mutually agreed upon problem definition.

## 2.2. Using visual representations to aid communication

The most difficult part of modelling an ill-structured problem situation by a group is establishing a common framework to facilitate communication [3]. Any methodology that can improve interaction and communication within a group should improve the sharing of perspectives and reduce miscommunication. One method of improving communication is the use of representational decision aids. Representational aids provide ways of visually presenting and manipulating a problem situation [72]. The depiction of a mental representation in a network form, as described previously, provides in a very literal sense a visual representation. These diagrams of concepts and relationships can display intricate networks of influence, causality, similarity or compatibility [52]. Rosenhead [52] suggests that representations capturing understandings of a situation can be comprehended quite easily. Even those without previous experience with a particular graphical notation are often able to adopt the language readily and use it to formalize a model, thus making lay participation possible [52].

When internalized mental representations can be explicitly represented in some graphical form (e.g., diagrams, maps, pictures or flow charts), the graphical form can be used as the common framework through which members can interact with each other and communicate their perspectives. Larkin and Simon [32] demonstrated that a graphical representation can shorten the time it takes for an individual to verbalize their problem interpretations. In a group setting, these visual representations provide each member access to a snapshot of others' mental perspectives. Attention should be focused on perceiving the visual representation, rather than relying solely on logical inferences based on interpretations of language [32]. By comparing individual models, members may note and discuss what they feel is absent and important and what is present and unimportant [3] as they strive to communicate in order to create a shared group vision.

In the study presented in the second part of this paper, the above relations will be explored using an ill-structured situation, that does not lend itself to a quantitative formulation, as the stimulus. The exploration is based on the following theoretical foundations discussed earlier in this paper: (1) individuals create mental representations in order to make sense of a situation, (2) these mental representations can be conceptualized as consisting of concepts and relationships, and formalized in graphical depictions in terms of nodes and arcs, and (3) these mental representations form the underlying basis of group communication. The research is descriptive in nature and explores the following questions:

\- Does the use of representational aids to communicate mental representations result in better group problem definitions?

\- How do groups use the representational aids to facilitate interaction and communication?

## 3. Research design and methods

## 3.1. The representation aids

Two representational techniques were chosen for this study. Both techniques are based on the underlying notion that a situation can be represented graphically. In simplest terms, these techniques result in networks consisting of nodes (concepts) and arcs (relationships) that may be useful in making sense of an ill-structured situation. The techniques chosen were influence diagramming [7,26,57,58] and cognitive mapping [5,16,18]. Given that cognitive maps and influence diagrams both rely on the fundamental building blocks of nodes and arcs, we do not posit a difference between the two. Rather, we are interested in exploring their use simply as alternative means of visually structuring and formulating a situation.

A cognitive map is a representation of the relationships which are perceived to exist among the elements of a given environment [30,31,44]. It is a way of representing a person's assertions about their beliefs with respect to a limited domain and is a form of a directed sign graph. Points (or nodes) represent concepts and arrows (or arcs) represent the causal assertion of how one concept variable affects another. Taking any two points, the concern is whether the state or movement of one point is perceived to have an influence on the state or movement of another point. For each of the concepts the individual or group is asked to think about the satisfactory alternative to this circumstance [17]. Axelrod [5] proposed that a person, with some direction, can write their own cognitive map and that analysis of someone else's map can assist in understanding how concepts are used and individuals view situations. An example of a cognitive map is shown in Fig. 2.

![](/api/attachments/XD67V9PF/fulltext/images/37cb48f1692ccc37eb8893513cc7b8e3a783ab1eaffe86c7e91a64d7bd7b3b5c.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Example of an influence diagram (adapted from Ref. [7]).

An influence diagram also represents someone's perception of the world, although with a different notation structure. It is a singly connected, acyclic, directed graph with three types of nodes (decision, intermediate, and outcome) and two types of arcs (certain: represented by a straight arrow connection; uncertain: represented by a squiggly arrow connection). An example of an influence diagram is shown in Fig. 3.

## 3.2. The experimental setting

Forty-five students from an organization behavior class at the University participated in the study. Students received extra credit in the class for participating. Additionally, due to the relevancy and nature of the problem situation (described below) it was felt that the students would be interested and would have alternative perspectives. A review of the literature $[2,35]$ and related studies $[25,38,54]$ was conducted to gain ideas concerning the types of ill-structured situations that were used. Of paramount importance was the interest of the participants and the ability to develop, discuss, and redevelop perspectives in a short time. Therefore, the situation developed had to be relevant to the student community.

The establishment of the 21 year-old drinking age in New York State has considerably affected university and fraternity/sorority regulations, and at the time of this study, a fatality associated with excessive alcohol consumption at a fraternity had made alcohol abuse a major campus issue. A faculty member in the Department of Psychology, whose research is in the area of alcohol and drinking patterns among student communities, assisted in the preparation of the problem situation. It was felt that using this issue would allow for varying perspectives depending on the demographics of the students (e.g., Greek membership, age, club or team involvement). The subjects were expected to have various degrees of familiarity with the issue. The problem situation, as presented to participants, is shown in Fig. 4.

From the forty-five students, twelve groups of three or four students were formed prior to scheduling the experimental sessions. Of the twelve groups, four each would be assigned to the cognitive mapping and influence diagramming treatments. The last set of four would be using brainstorming [47] to generate alternative problem perspectives. Brainstorming, popularized by Osborn [47], is perhaps the most well known tool for encouraging divergent thinking. However, unlike cognitive mapping and influence diagramming, it is not a visually oriented technique. Rather, it is used here simply for a basis of comparison to the chosen visual approaches.

![](/api/attachments/XD67V9PF/fulltext/images/1eabb76e91488ff2c58dce0b7aece36ee069f6e94f04b516a80a79991154e98c.jpg)  
Fig. 4. The problem situation.

Students were assigned to groups based on demographic data and a shortened version of the Myers-Briggs personality test. Participants were first assigned to groups based on their cognitive styles (i.e., sensation/thinking (ST), intuitive/thinking (IT), sensation/feeling (SF), and intuitive/feeling (IF)). Based on the distribution of cognitive styles, each of the 12 groups consisted of at least one individual representing ST, IT, and TF. For the three groups that consisted of three individuals, only these three cognitive styles were represented. In addition, demographic characteristics (e.g., fraternity membership, athletic or club participation, elective office, class year, age, and major) were also used to rearrange and assign participants to groups. The assignment was conducted to help ensure that there was as much diversity as possible within each group to encourage differing perspectives while at the same time maintaining homogeneity between the groups. Fig. 5 provides a summary of the group compositions. The resulting nine groups that consisted of four individuals were randomly assigned to one of the three techniques. The three groups that consisted of three individuals were also randomly assigned to one of the three techniques.

Prior to the experimental sessions, the participants received a training session (approximately forty-five minutes) on the purpose and use of each of the three approaches. For those using cognitive maps or influence diagrams, they were instructed that these techniques may assist them in structuring their thoughts concerning the situation. Participants were allowed to refer to these instructions and guidelines during the experimental sessions. At the scheduled sessions, the subjects developed their own individual problem definition and, for the eight groups using a visual approach, a cognitive map or influence diagram representing the concepts and relationships they felt were relevant to define the problem. They were allowed thirty minutes for this activity.

After a short break, they reconvened in their assigned group to develop a group problem definition and, if applicable, a group cognitive map or influence diagram. The problem definitions and associated visualizations prepared by individual members were available to the group for use (or not) as they desired. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the objective of learning more about how representational aids may be effectively used by lay participants, these groups were allowed to conduct their sessions as they desired. The brainstorming groups were instructed to generate as many concepts as they could that they felt were relevant to understanding the problem situation. They were then instructed to select a subset of this list — the most relevant concepts — and use these to develop their definition of the problem.

## 4. Presentation of findings

## 4.1. Definitional quality

The textual outputs, i.e., the problem definitions, were assessed by three independent raters following a Multi-Attribute Value (MAV) model $[54,36]$ . The raters in this study were instructed to evaluate the definitions along four attributes of the model: degree of structure, consideration of stakeholders, solution implication, and level of focus. Each attribute is scored along a continuous scale (0 to 100), where lower (higher) scores indicate lower (higher) quality. The aggregate measure, or overall score for a given problem definition, is the sum of the weighted scores of these attributes. Fig. 8 presents a description of each attribute, its weight, and the corresponding scale used for evaluation.

It is not our purpose here to consider the merits of the MAV model further (details of the model and its validity may be found in [36,45,54]). However, Spearman's Coefficient of Rank Correlation was computed for each pair of raters and a high degree of inter-rater reliability was found, warranting its use for further analyses. For example, the rating correlations for all pairs of raters for the aggregate scores were 0.287, 0.234, and 0.721 — all significant at a level of 5 percent. Therefore, the scores subsequently used reflected the average scores of the three raters.

![](/api/attachments/XD67V9PF/fulltext/images/b02e068b8ba3e4568c314a3b62f622e8ca60c0137a71ee538023dd954015a262.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Sample group influence diagram.

To illustrate, Fig. 9 shows two definitions and the MAV scores they received. Definition 1 was evaluated by the MAV raters as being narrow and having little regard for structural considerations. This definition did not clearly identify stakeholders and their roles or concerns. Additionally, the raters felt the definition was solution oriented as it implied that increased awareness and harsher penalties were needed. Conversely, Definition 2 received higher scores for structure and level of focus, as the definition attempted to identify the 'whys' associated with the problem situation. Definition 2 was less leading in terms of solution implication. In terms of understanding the nature, or types of problem conceptualizations, that were evidenced, a majority of the definitions focused on the "whys" of the problem situation, although directed differently. Smith [59,60] would refer to conceptualizations such as these as “causal diagnosis”, where one is concerned with specifying the causes of the situation, whether resulting from a larger systemic deficiency or historical development. The problem situation used here, and the resulting definitions, would fall in this category.

The use of output quality as a surrogate for process or treatment quality is not uncommon [37]. However, it is not sufficient to describe the quality of the processes employed during the group sessions. For the Brainstorming treatment, only one of the four groups improved.

The superior performance of the individuals in the brainstorming treatment requires comment. The individuals assigned to this treatment had no constraints in developing their individual problem definitions, and thus the resulting definitions tended to be much longer than with group brainstorming or the other treatments. In combination with the MAV model, the length of the definition may have contributed to a perceived or actual increase in quality (i.e., the brainstorming individuals were able to include more in their definitions). It was left to the groups to generate a proper succinct definition. Thus, we do not posit that the experimental results are evidence that individual brainstorming is better, in any sense, than group brainstorming. However, to assess the impact of brainstorming as a group technique one must compare groups using it to groups not ([37], p.131), as done here.

The main purpose of this study is to examine whether representational techniques assist groups define ill-structured problems. The conjecture was that if individual models could be made explicit in some graphical form and were available at the start of the group session, they would allow group members to assess and access knowledge other members had to contribute, reduce miscommunication, and facilitate interaction. Additionally, the process of developing a group network would assist the group in identifying relevant concepts and relationships and legitimize the result as the group converged to a visual representation and textual group problem definition. The relative comparisons shown in Fig. 10 provide the impetus to examine more closely the processes by which the groups arrived at and achieved their final outputs. As such, the following section details the analysis of the videotapes.

## 4.2.1. Process quality

In order to gain a richer understanding of how the cognitive mapping and influence diagramming groups utilized the techniques and conducted their respective sessions, the videotapes of these sessions were examined. For each of the eight groups, one of the authors reviewed the videotapes and developed process flowcharts that indicated the sequence of activities that each group carried out. The second author then proceeded with a more indepth analysis of each group following these general flowcharts — confirming the sequence of activities — and identifying who was involved (i.e., individuals A, B, C, D), and the time at which the activity occurred $^{1}$ .

As described in the previous section, the performance scores of the eight groups will be used as a means to potentially identify better or worse group processes. Table 1 presents the ordering of these eight groups according to their performance scores, i.e., $G_{k,mav} - A_{k,mav}$ , from the best (Group 12) to the worst (Group 3).

Following this, we more closely examined — in this order — the processes of the groups as reflected in the flowcharts. This examination lead to an interesting finding: there existed three relatively distinct processes within the eight groups and these processes were “clustered” according to the performance scores. For example, Groups 12 and 4 (the two best groups in Table 1) conducted their sessions in very similar fashions. Likewise, Groups 14 and 3 (the two worst groups) conducted their sessions in a similar manner. Groups 9, 1, 11, and 15 — that rest in the middle of Table 1 — also exhibited similar processes, reflecting aspects of both the best (Groups 12 and 4) and worst (Groups 14 and 3) groups. Fig. 11 summarizes the three processes derived from the eight group flowcharts. Based on the performance scores, these processes are characterized as most effective, least effective, and ineffective. In the following we will discuss and examine these more closely, beginning with the ineffective process.

4.2.1.1. Ineffective process. Analysis of Fig. 11 indicates that the two visual groups that exhibited a degradation of performance, i.e., Groups 14 and 3, did not follow the same process as the six visual groups that improved. While both groups began their sessions by presenting their individual viewpoints — Group 3 using the individual problem definitions and Group 14 using the individual visualizations — the key difference between these two groups and the other six is that Groups 14 and 3 elected to develop the group problem definition prior to developing the visual representation. Rather than the group discussing a strategy to conduct their respective sessions, this decision was the result of one member suggesting that they develop the group definition first and then build the visual representation to support the definition.

Once Groups 14 and 3 made the decision to develop their group definitions first, analysis of the videotapes indicated that one member wrote down a preliminary definition for the group. Members of each group then proceeded to discuss this definition, making changes and asking for clarifications, before agreeing to a final group definition. Therefore, al-

though one member wrote a preliminary definition, the final group definition was the result of a collaborative effort. However, during development of the definition, neither group directly referred to the individually developed definitions or visual representations. In fact, during their discussion of the definition, members of Group 14 attempted to verbally understand the relationships between specific aspects of their definition.

Once both groups had completed their definitions, they proceeded to develop the group visual. Members of Group 14 (a cognitive mapping group) circled concepts on the individual visualizations that they felt were relevant to their group definition. They then proceeded to copy these concepts to the board, while simultaneously adding linkages between the concepts. Group 3 (an influence diagramming group) first discussed and identified the outcome that they desired for their group visualization. Individual members then proceeded, without referring to the individual visualizations, to simultaneously add concepts and linkages between concepts to support this outcome and their group definition.

![](/api/attachments/XD67V9PF/fulltext/images/918f109dfb67f1bb8c37b2cf882734e34bc008484b114b8efe5e5bc798ece76b.jpg)  
Fig. 11. Process summaries.

In summary, we can characterize the key event for these two groups as the decision to develop the group problem definition before development of a group visual. Clearly, by doing this these groups did not use the group visual to facilitate their understanding of the problem situation. Conversely, and to be described in the following section, the groups that improved average performance developed the group visual first and used the visual to assist in the development of a group problem definition.

4.2.1.2. Effective processes. Table 1 identifies the six groups that exhibited group performance improvements. Unlike Groups 14 and 3, Fig. 11 indicates that all of these groups elected to develop their group visual first and then use it for discussion and development of their respective group problem definition. However, even between these six groups, distinct process differences were observed. Firstly, Groups 12 and 4 — unlike Groups 9, 1, 11 and 15 — began their sessions by presenting their individual perspectives as reflected in their individually developed visual representations. In addition, members of Group 4 read their individually developed problem definitions to each other and then directed the group to examine the visual to, as one member put it, “understand where we are coming from”. Both of these groups spent the first part of their sessions discussing the similarities and differences in the various visual representations. While Groups 9, 1, 11 and 15 examined the individual visualizations, no discussion transpired.

Secondly, the two groups that showed the most improvements in definitional quality (Groups 12 and 4) developed their group visual in a much different fashion than the four groups (Groups 9, 1, 11 and 15) that showed less improvements. More specifically, as Fig. 11 indicates, Groups 12 and 4 decided that they should first generate a list of concepts that they held to be most relevant. Only after the group had agreed to this list did they proceed to explore the possible relationships (links) between the concepts. This process is in marked contrast to all the other groups (whether they showed improvement or not) that simultaneously added concepts and relationships as they developed their visual representation.

Analysis of the videotapes indicated that the individual visual representations played a key role for these six groups. The two best groups used them for both presenting and discussing individual perspectives at the start of their sessions, and for reference and clarification purposes during development of a group visual representation. The four groups that improved performance to lesser degrees used them primarily for reference during group visual representation development.

Following development of the group visual representation, all six groups paused to reflect and discuss what their representation implied. Then, specifically referring to the group visualization for guidance, these groups proceeded to develop a textual group problem definition.

While these six groups all improved on the average quality of their individual members, the similarities between Groups 12 and 4 — the most successful groups — and their differences with the other groups suggests that their overall process was the most effective. The presentation and discussion of individual perspectives at the beginning of the session for Groups 12 and 4 proved to be a valuable endeavor for these two groups. Given that the groups in this study had not worked together previously, this activity immediately set the focus of the session on understanding the problem situation from different perspectives. The visual representations were immediately and subsequently used as the common framework through which members interacted and communicated with each other as the group worked to a shared visualization and, ultimately, a group definition of the problem. In addition, engaging all members at the beginning of the session appears to have set the foundation that all subsequent group activities were to reflect a collaborative effort.

## 4.4. Contrasts of visual techniques

Contrasts between the two visual techniques merit mention at this point. Although we had posited no difference between cognitive maps and influence diagrams, on average the influence diagrams tended to be more complex than the cognitive maps. Group influence diagrams averaged five more concepts and eight more linkages between concepts than the cognitive maps. However, as Table 1 indicates, this increased complexity did not lead to greater performance improvements — in terms of the aggregate MAV scores — by those groups using influence diagrams.

The only major difference between the two techniques, with regard to the MAV model, was that the definitions developed under influence diagramming tended to be less open-ended in terms of the single attribute solution implication. This finding is not unexplainable, particularly when we consider how the influence diagramming groups began development of their group visual. Within each of the three major processes, characterized in Fig. 11, the influence diagramming groups diverged from their cognitive mapping counterparts at the point at which they began to build their visual representation. This step, noted in Fig. 11, is when each influence diagramming group decided on an “outcome” concept node for their visual. The outcomes were generally what the groups felt should happen, thus leading to a group definition with a greater problem solving tone (as measured by the single attribute solution implication in the MAV model).

The process employed by the influence diagramming groups directed the point of focus on the outcome. Conversely, cognitive mapping was not predominantly outcome focused. However, Rosenhead ([51], p. 342) suggests that these differences in formulation do not necessarily detract from the overall homogeneity of the approaches. Rather, the use of these techniques by the groups involved — as general problem structuring techniques — downplayed differences between the two approaches. Clearly, the focus for the groups was understanding and analyzing the relationships between the concepts that the group deemed as relevant. The alternative notation structure of the two techniques does not appear to be as important as the ability of the participants to interact using a common framework. Given this, we posit that a simple graphical form consisting simply of nodes and directional arcs may be just as effective.

## 5. Discussion

In the first part of this paper, we raised several questions for exploration in this study. The first was whether better group problem definitions are derived as a result of the use of representational aids. Assessments of relative group performance improvements (or degradations) in definitional quality — using the MAV model — suggested that, perhaps, these aids were having some impact. However, these assessments provided no insight into how the representational aids were used or what would, in fact, constitute an effective process for their use — the two principal questions raised. Thus, using the output assessments principally for direction, we turned to a process examination of the eight groups that employed visually based representation aids. Clearly, although six groups exhibited performance improvements in terms of definitional quality, it is the approach taken by Groups 12 and 4 that appears most promising in terms of establishing an effective process.

The process depicted in Fig. 14 is based on what appears to be the successful activities employed by Groups 12 and 4 and guidance found in the problem solving literatures. First, based on this study, we posit that there exists value in having individuals attempt to formalize their perspective in a visual representation — that is, identifying concepts and relationships perceived as relevant to the given situation — prior to any group interaction. Once the group level activities begin, the focus shifts to sharing these individual perspectives. As Carley [10] suggests, the construction of the cognitive view of the group begins.

As Groups 12 and 4 illustrated, this construction should begin with a presentation and discussion of the visual representations. This activity immediately sets the focus of the group's attention on the visuals and serves to engage all group members. Stasser and Titus's [63] [64] found that when discussions are based solely on unstructured verbal interactions, the discussion will be devoted to a reiteration of information that is most common to all participants. Given this, groups will be unaware of unshared information that could potentially expand the group's perspective and lead to a richer problem understanding. However, the explicit attention to the visuals — called for at this point — should serve to alleviate this obstacle. These visuals serve as the impetus for identifying the common aspects of each member's perspective. And, perhaps more importantly, they serve as a means to highlight the aspects that were not common, but not necessarily less relevant.

Once the group has attained a sufficient understanding of the perspectives that each member holds, the group should proceed to identifying concepts that they feel are relevant to understanding the nature of the problem. However, while the individual visual representations may serve as a useful starting point for identifying concepts, groups should be encouraged to think beyond this initial set of concepts in order to facilitate divergent thinking and expose concepts that members had not previously considered. This divergent step is consistent with theories associated with the first phase of problem structuring in which groups are encouraged to find and make explicit elements of a situation $[20]$ $[48]$ .

Following the generation of a concept list, the group should proceed to explore the possible relationships between the concepts they deem to be most relevant or interesting. The individual representations may facilitate this discussion. The development of the group visual representation is an iterative process as the group adds concepts and relationships between concepts, discusses the resulting representation, and makes alterations. However, unlike the approach taken by Groups 12 and 4 — generate the concepts and identify linkages between this set, Fig. 14 depicts this activity as a recursively convergent and divergent process. While generation of an initial set of concepts provides a starting point, theories related to cognitive science suggest that the exploration of linkages between concepts may stimulate extraction of further concepts or reconsideration of concepts previously deemed as irrelevant or uninteresting [70], thus supporting this depiction in Fig. 14.

The resulting visual representation serves to portray the group perspective or cognitive view. As indicated by the successful groups in this study, the individual visuals and the development of the group visual provide a common framework that can facilitate interaction and communication among group members. It is the group's task to converge to the concepts and relationships that they feel are most relevant to making sense of the situation. As illustrated with Group 12, the convergent nature of this stage of the process suggests that the group perspective will be a more succinct and focused interpretation of a given problem situation than the individual perspectives. This proposition, however, warrants further exploration.

After the group has agreed to a visual representation that they feel is most relevant, they should proceed to develop a group problem definition. The success of Groups 12 and 4 suggests to us that analysis and discussion of the group visual representation facilitates the development of a textual definition. The Osborn–Parnes Creative Problem Solving (CPS) model [48,20] denotes this activity as “problem-finding” in which tentative definitions are created — here through interpretation of the visual representation. From this, the group may proceed to derive a mutually acceptable definition.

The process in Fig. 14 is prescriptive in nature and is intended to set the foundation for further explorations of the questions and preliminary findings presented in this study.

## 6. Summary

Organizations faced with ill-structured problems require consensus concerning the definition of the problem to be solved. In these environments, the use of groups to make sense of and define a problem are a necessity, rather than an option. However, while it is recognized that problem solving begins with some mental representation used to make sense of a situation [49], normally these representations go unexplored or unquestioned ([14], p. 81). The intricate relationships of concepts in a mental representation are oftentimes difficult to verbally communicate. Therefore, techniques that assist individuals and groups in formalizing and making explicit these intricate networks may facilitate the sharing of individual mental representations and the development of a group representation. As evidenced in this study, the visualizations become the focus of attention. They were successfully used as both information resources and vehicles of communication. Thus, when conceiving of individual and group support systems, designers should keep in mind that users must have the ability to structure and view a situation in a way that supports their cognitive processes and, when working in a group, enables them to communicate and work together effectively.

In summary, this paper first develops the theoretical foundation that mental representations are the underlying basis by which individuals make sense of problem situations, and through which individuals ultimately interact with others. Second, we discuss how these mental representations can be visually conceptualized and how they may be useful facilitators of interaction and communication in groups charged with structuring and formulating a problem. In the second part of this paper, these assertions were explored using individuals and groups engaged in these activities. Following analysis of the group processes, we established what may constitute an effective process that may enable lay use of these aids. Additionally, the detailed analysis of the content of the visuals contributes to an understanding of the basis for and emergence of the group perspective. While the study reported here was exploratory and descriptive in nature, it forms the basis to generate hypotheses for future research. We plan to explore further the process that was derived from the most successful groups in this study. For example, we plan to compare this process to a non-visual group structuring technique, such as Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing (SAST) [35].
