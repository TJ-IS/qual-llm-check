# A heuristic job scheduling decision support system A case study

Marc J. Schniederjans $^{a,*}$ , Donald A. Carpenter $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, College of Business Administration, Lincoln, NE 68588-0491, USA $^{b}$ Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Nebraska at Kearney, Kearney, NE 68849, USA

Received 1 January 1995; revised 10 November 1995; accepted 5 March 1996

## Abstract

The daily activity of establishing a job assignment schedule for each individual employee in a manufacturing setting can be quite a complex management task. Work rules, special work load requirements and absenteeism all act to compound the scheduling activity. Many organizations have tried to solve their scheduling problems by developing software systems to handle their daily scheduling decision making. The purpose of this paper is to describe the development of a unique heuristic-based scheduling software system. The scheduling system is presented in the context of a real world case study for a major US manufacturer who sponsored the scheduling system's development.

Keywords: Decision support system application; Heuristic methodology; Relational database problem; Production systems; Scheduling methodology; Microcomputer application

## 1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSS) are used to support decision making in almost every area of business. Based on surveys by Byrd [4] and Kusiak and Chen [12], there exists considerable interest in DSS in the domain of scheduling manufacturing activities.

The DSS used in scheduling manufacturing activities can be segmented by the type of decision methodology used in its architecture. As noted by Kendall and Kendall ([10], pp. 320–329), all DSS methodology can fall into the two categories of analytic or heuristic. These categories of methodology are both presently used in the development of DSS for scheduling manufacturing activities.

Analytic DSS that use optimization procedures have been used to support the scheduling of personnel, equipment and even value chain activities [20]. Unfortunately, the size and time requirements to generate even small scheduling problems using an analytic method can be prohibitive. For example, in a DSS study by Gopalakrishnan et al. [8], analytic methods used to generate work shift assignments were compared with heuristic assignments. The results of the study showed that time requirements to generate a solution increased exponentially when using the analytic method, when compared to the heuristic method. The time required for daily scheduling using analytic based methodology would virtually be impossible except for the most simplest of assignments. By comparison, they found heuristic DSS generated near optimal schedules using in some cases less than 1/8000th of the time of the analytic DSS.

Heuristic DSS's quickness in generating a near optimal solution is particularly important at the shop floor level of planning. Successful day-to-day scheduling at the shop floor level requires a daily schedule to be generated, taking into consideration such items as absenteeism, skill availabilities, and flexibility for changes in daily demand. These considerations and other dynamic environmental variables of the shop floor can violate analytic method modeling assumptions. These violations of model assumptions can overwhelm and render an analytic DSS scheduling system impractical. Hence, a near optimal heuristic DSS schedule may be more practical as an aid in scheduling. Indeed, heuristic DSS have been observed to generate as optimal a schedule as those generated by analytic DSS [8] without violating any necessary or sufficient mathematical conditions required of analytic methodology.

In addition to generating schedules efficiently, heuristic DSS are also ideal for the microcomputer based management information system environments that dominate the shop floor in the 1990's ([14], p.750). Research by Adler et al. [2] and Franca et al. [7] have shown that heuristic methodology can be applied to model very complex manufacturing environments with relatively little computer storage space or computational requirements. Applied research by Ferland and Fleurent [6], Gopalakrishnan et al. [8] and Sivakumar et al. [21] have also shown the ease and efficiency of using a heuristic DSS with a microcomputer interface.

The purpose of this paper is to present an application of a heuristic employee job assignment DSS. (Hereafter referred to as the HEJADSS.) The HEJADSS is a microcomputer scheduling system designed to support shop floor daily personnel assignments to jobs for a manufacturing operation. In addition to the application a discussion of the HEJADSS architecture and in particular its application specific heuristic methodology will also be presented. The difficulties in the implementation and development of the HEJADSS, as well as the impact of the system on the host organization will also be discussed.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Theoretical foundation

The basic theoretical foundation for HEJADSS represents applied extensions of research developments in heuristic screening methods, heuristic ranking systems, microcomputer based heuristic scheduling systems and the unique nature of the type of scheduling problem. The fundamental heuristic screening research supporting the development of HEJADSS can be traced back several decades to what some artificial intelligent experts consider the origins of expert systems. The 1960's DENDRAL expert system employed heuristic programming to systematically screen out chemical structures that did not match a particular profile [15]. Similarly, HEJADSS seeks to use a set of scheduling criteria to systematically screen out employees who would not be qualified to perform a particular job.

Once ineligible employees are screened out, the remaining eligible employees are ranked for possible job assignments by a modified version of the heuristic procedure by Leachman and Gascon [13]. While the Leachman and Gascon [13] ranking system was based on the timing of jobs in a schedule, HEJADSS ranks employees for possible work assignments based on their qualifications to do a particular job.

Similar to Bechtold [3], the modified ranking method of HEJADSS permits multiple criteria to be integrated in the ranking of employees for work assignments. Bechtold [3] proved that a variety of criteria (i.e., location and size of labor pool) could be included in a heuristic work scheduling system utilizing a microcomputer that effectively performs the needs of shop floor scheduling. HEJADSS differs from this prior research by including such criteria as work rules, ergonomic restrictions, skill limitations, work rotation and other governmental restrictions limiting the assignment of employees to jobs.

While there are dozens of job assignment scheduling DSS in the literature, none exactly fit the unique nature of the scheduling issues faced by the management of the host corporation in this applied study. Like most scheduling problems, the issue the shop floor management faced is how to make a daily assignment of an uncertain group of workers possessing a variety of skills to a fixed number of known jobs in the least amount of scheduling time. Simplifying the problem is the fact that work assignments are only made at the beginning of day, and if shortages of skills are observed, the jobs requiring those skills can be delayed. Sequential work load assignments (that might cause bottler-ck problems), group assignments and teams where not utilized in this scheduling environment. Complex heuristic scheduling systems to deal with issues like bottleneck problems [1] and work flow [19] where judged unnecessary for this type of work environment. Making the scheduling problem more complex is the fact that it has to match up the job requirements with the skills of potential assignees, while recognizing assignment restrictions and employee limitations. In addition, the DSS has to include a memory component that keeps track of previous job assignments over a period time. Research by Martel [17] on memory based heuristic scheduling was partially incorporated into HEJADSS to include consideration of prior work assignments. Research by Queyranne [18] on “worst fit” scheduling was inverted to achieve a “best fit” heuristic as the fundamental heuristic logic used in HEJADSS.

Based on this prior research HEJADSS's methodology can be characterized as a fairly simple DSS. While different terminology may be used to describe a DSS, we will describe the HEJADSS in terms of four basic components: a data storage file structure (i.e., database), hardware, user interface and a model base ([23], p. 835).

## 2.2. Data storage file structure

Four input files are required for the HEJADSS data storage file structure. The “daily required jobs file” defines the required jobs available for assigning each day. This file is updated each day by the user (i.e., management) for all new jobs being loaded into the system for production. The “daily available operators file” defines the available employees (called “operators” since they operate machines) that show up for work that day and are available for assignment. Again, this file is updated each day by management. The “jobs file” defines the individual work task requirements for each job. This file is based on standard time estimates of work required for each job. This information is obtained from the organization's production records for use in the HE-JADSS microcomputer application. The “operators file” defines the individual operator's qualifications to perform work task requirements for each job. This information is obtained from the organization's personnel files and includes any ergonomic restrictions, labor work rules and any requirements by the Americans with Disability Act that limits the operator's ability to be assigned jobs.

In summary, the data storage file structure for the HEJADSS consists of four files. Two files are input each day, and the other two files were basically permanent. Two files pertained to jobs and two files pertained to operators.

## 2.3. Hardware and user interface

In seeking a maximum amount of autonomy in the use of this system, the HEJADSS is structured within a single Intel 80386 IBM PS/2 model 70 system. The software system used in the development of the computer programs that comprise the HEJADSS is Borland's Paradox 3.5 relational database system.

The interface requirements for HEJADSS required management to perform a daily update of the “daily available operators file” and the “daily required jobs file”. At the end of the previous day the jobs file would be updated by management or their staff based on the jobs not completed that day and the addition of new jobs that were sent via their marketing computer order system. At the beginning of the next day the managers would load the employee number for each operator who showed up for work into the “daily available operators file”. HEJADSS could then generate the operator-to-job assignment schedules from its microcomputer printer. The job schedules would be individualized by operator. The operator names obtained from the “operators file” were nicely formatted on the printouts to make the distribution to personnel easy.

In addition to the daily interface with files, managers also were optionally permitted to review and edit as-need-be the schedules while running HEJADSS. This editing involved directly altering the heuristic ranking to permit the input of preferential assignment criteria (e.g., seniority considerations) that are not considered in the files.

The only other types of interface occasionally required by HEJADSS were performed by the personnel department. These updates were to the “operators file” when the operator qualified for additional types of skilled jobs, new operators joined the organization or old operators left, and when operators became disqualified or physically unable to perform certain types of jobs.

## 2.4. Model base

The architecture of the heuristic used in the HEJADSS is presented as a flowchart in Fig. 1. The system starts by taking the data from the four input files that make up the data storage file structure. The program initiates a screening process to determine and eliminate ineligible operators for job assignments. The HEJADSS takes such screening criteria as the operators' qualifications, ergonomic constraints, absenteeism and if they are already assigned to a job. Other work rule based screening criteria includes the number of days that must elapse between times an operator can work at a job, the maximum number of days an operator is permitted to work at a particular job and all combinations of prohibitions on multiple job assignments. The outcome of this screening process is to create an operator relation or temporary operator file that represents a listing of all available operators who are qualified, unconstrained and available. These operators are considered “eligible” to be assigned to jobs for that particular day.

The HEJADSS also screens the jobs files, pulling the data on job requirements for each of the daily required jobs. This information is placed in a jobs relation or a temporary jobs file that represents a listing of all available jobs and the individual job requirements that need to be completed for that particular day.

The HEJADSS takes the temporary operator file of eligible operators and ranks them in ascending order by the number of available jobs they are qualified to work on. The heuristic is simply an inverse listing of the number of jobs the operators are qualified to handle. The fewer the jobs they are qualified to work on, the higher the rank. The ascending order ranking places the operators who are the least qualified to be assigned first, or saves the most flexible eligible operators until last. This ranking of the operators is used to restructure the temporary eligible operator relation to reflect the relative value of each operator at that moment in the job assignment process.

Fig. 1. Flowchart of HEJADSS heuristic architecture.

The HEJADSS also restructures the jobs relation by ranking the jobs in ascending order. The heuristic used for the ranking of the jobs is based on the numerical difference between the number of eligible operators available to handle the required jobs and the total number of operators required to complete each job. The smaller the numerical difference, the less likely it will find enough operators to complete the job, or the less flexibility there is in making the job assignment. By placing the highest rank to the job with the smallest difference, the jobs with the least flexible assignments will be made first. If a job does not have a sufficient number of operators available for assignment, the job will be marked for manual scheduling as shown in the question box (i.e., Operators available?) in Fig. 1. If there are sufficient operators available for a job, the HE-JADSS simply takes the highest ranked job and assigns to it the highest ranked eligible operator. That job is then marked as “filled” in the jobs relation and the match of operators to jobs are reflected in an assignment relation.

If all the jobs are not yet assigned, the system repeats the earlier screening activity and revises the rankings to reflect both the operators' standard time assignments and the removal of the previous job. This iteration process of job/operator assignments and the revision of the rankings continues until all the daily jobs have been assigned. The HEJADSS then makes available a preliminary assignment schedule from the assignments relation to permit the user to review the schedule for final approval. If, in the judgment of the user, the preliminary schedule needs to be revised, the user can do so simply by making whatever manual revisions are necessary. Once the assignment schedule is approved by the user, a final operator assignment schedule is made available in either printed or electronically displayed format. The assignments are then transferred to a historical relation to be used in determining eligibility for the next assignment period (i.e., the next day's shift).

## 3. Application

The application of the HEJADSS took place at the Square D Company plant in Lincoln, Nebraska. Square D Company is one of the largest manufacturers of electronic fuse components in the US. The plant where the HEJADSS was deployed manufactures miniature circuit breakers and electronic component parts. The assembly departments where job assignments are made are organized into four areas, by product type. The plant operates on a three shift basis, with 15 to 45 process operators assigned to each of the four plant areas per shift. Typically each area in the plant has several job types, and each operator is trained on anywhere from one to seven or more distinct job tasks. The management of this plant intended to use the HEJADSS to prepare daily schedules for operator assignments to the machines and automated equipment required for production at the facility.

The initial prototype of the system took only about one month to complete. Difficulties in using the Paradox system slowed development of the system. Basically the problem was bigger than the Paradox system's application generator had been designed to handle. Also, the changing environment of the plant and the need to reformat output required more analysis and design work than originally expected. Compounding the difficulty, the newly enacted Americans with Disabilities Act required changes in the operator data storage file structure to allow for additional job assignment constraints. The final system took a total of nine months to prototype and install.

## 3.1. Observed benefits of HEJADSS

Square D management did not set any specific criteria on which to judge the effectiveness or efficiency of their new DSS. They simply wanted a reduction in the time required of management to establish daily job assignment schedules. To help assess the significance of the HEJADSS and make a judgment on the system's effectiveness and efficiency a comparison of three months observations on both manually generated schedules and the HEJADSS generated schedules were undertaken.

Based on a comparison of a wide range of scheduling problems using a variety of specific selection criteria, the HEJADSS reliably produced the same or better quality assignment schedules than those generated by using the manual methods. Quality was measured on the basis of four criteria: convenience (i.e., ease of using system), correctness (i.e., schedule not violating ergonomic constraints or labor work rules), usability (i.e., avoiding irregular or illogical schedules), and conformance (i.e., how well the available resources were fit into a final schedule). All staff members who were familiar and used both the manual scheduling system and HEJADSS scored them based on the four criteria using a 1 (low quality) to 5 (high quality) scale. The resulting average scores for the prior manual system were all below 1.5, while the average scores for the HEJADSS were all above 4.3. Based on these comparisons the HEJADSS was adjudged as an effective means to generating improved quality operator assignment schedules by the system developer and the management of Square D.

Based on a comparison of the scheduling effort, the results of using the HEJADSS were also judged as highly efficient. Changing from the previous manual assignment scheduling system, the HEJADSS saved considerable management and operator time. Management had been used to spending over an hour a day per plant area and per shift on developing an operator assignment schedule. The delays caused by the manual scheduling effort occasionally delayed operator assignments, wasting their time. Moreover, the manually generated schedules occasionally failed to observe all the ergonomic constraints and labor work rule limitations required in labor/management agreements, thereby violating labor/management agreements. The subsequent rescheduling effort to correct schedules for the assignment constraints also wasted time.

By comparison, the HEJADSS took only 15 to 20 minutes per shift in each plant area to generate an assignment schedule. The HEJADSS also eliminated the risk of accidentally violating ergonomic, work rules and labor contractual limitations. The relational database system used in the HEJADSS makes it impossible to violate the ergonomic or work rule limitations unless management would willfully override the system by editing the resulting assignment schedule. Moreover, the HEJADSS generated output provided an assignment schedule that detailed the operators' names, the jobs they were expected to be perform and the amount of standard time allowed for the job. This helped to clarify operator assignments and this specificity was observed to motivate operators to reduce idle time. Management were also given listings of jobs or operators that were not assigned or that had partial assignments. This permitted management to efficiently reallocate human resources to alternative work assignments. The HE-JADSS printouts also made it easy for operators to estimate the number of days they would be working on a particular job. While such information was likely to change because of absenteeism, it was viewed beneficial by both management and the operators because it let everyone know about their future assignments. Thus it helped to minimize job reassignment shocks that had been a common occurrence in the prior manual job assignment process.

## 3.2. Implementation difficulties

It was observed in the beginning of the implementation phase of the HEJADSS that managers using the system tended to use the assignment schedule exactly as generated by the computer. As they became more experienced with the system, they increased their use of the editing and customization features of the system. This continued to increase their efficiency in generating assignments and their satisfaction in the resulting assignments. The HEJADSS customization features allowed them to add their own personal and subjective judgment into the assignment process. Editing and customization features of the system included the ability to override the schedule and make preassignments of jobs to operators. Mallett [16] observed that the need for human behavioral concerns and considerations where critically important in scheduling applications. The editing features helped the HEJADSS to mimic the original real world behavior of managers in preparing job assignment schedules for the exception cases that needed such special consideration.

One difficulty that was observed early in the implementation of the HEJADSS was its habit of making the same set of job/operator assignments for the same daily set of jobs. Inherent in the algorithm used to rank the eligible operators is the risk of the same operators being placed at the same rank and therefore being assigned to the same jobs repeatedly. Fortunately, this problem tended to disappear after a few months into the implementation of the HEJADSS. The reasons why the problem disappeared were observed to be a function of three factors:

change in product demand, absenteeism and increased use of editing. Square D was seeking to reduce production lot size and move to a more Just-In-Time philosophy of manufacturing. Bechtold [3] observed that changes in management procedures can act to improve heuristic scheduling systems by altering inherent consistencies in human behavior. Changing the demand of products altered the daily set of jobs, which increasingly altered the rankings of jobs each day. The somewhat high absenteeism, which helped to motivate the need for a daily scheduling HEJADSS in the first place, also helped to keep the same set of operators from being repeatedly assigned to the same set of jobs. Finally, the behavioral change of increased use of editing by management helped to alter the assignment schedules. It was observed that after only a few months the repeating job/operator assignments that had troubled management and added to job monotony for some of the operators, had virtually been eliminated.

Finally, it should be mentioned that the HEJADSS required little in the way of file maintenance work. Other than the daily input files, the system recorded and updated all necessary files as it processed jobs and made operator assignments each day. The electronic exchange of information improved accuracy by eliminating data input errors over manual exchange methods that invariably involved human error in data transmission. With only a rare update on the operators file as operators became qualified to operate additional pieces of equipment, the HEJADSS is self-contained and self-maintaining w.r.t. its own data requirements. Similar accuracy and efficiency in use were observed by Kendall and Schniederjans [11] in their application of a microcomputer based DSS.

## 4. Conclusions and further research

Like many organizations that make the change from manual to computer based job scheduling, Square D did not choose to set any specific cut off values for the success of the HEJADSS. Square D management simply wanted to reserve judgment on evaluating the entire system's impact until they had given it a chance to work. They did communicate one primary criteria under which HEJADSS's success was to be determined, namely if the system could reduce the amount of time required to develop daily job assignment schedules. A reduction of 66 to 75 percent of the time (i.e., from an hour to 15 or 20 minutes) was viewed by management as a successful reduction daily scheduling time. Based on a comparison with other applications of heuristic scheduling systems [3,9], even much more expensive knowledge based systems [5], it can be concluded that HEJADSS has substantially reduced job scheduling time.

Based on the three months of observations between manually generated and HEJADSS generated schedules there was a substantial qualitative improvement in scheduling. It was observed that about ten percent of the schedules generated manually resulted in a violation of one or more work rules or ergonomic limitations. Under HEJADSS no rules or limitations were violated in the resulting schedules. Also, the detailed scheduling information provided in the computer generated schedules was reported as helping to make job assignments more clear to those using the schedules than manually generated schedules did. Similar to other computer based applications of DSS [9,11], the introduction of computer based reporting that accurately details important decision making information can assist in goal accomplishment in any area of an organization's operation. Based on these comparative reports it can be concluded that HEJADSS improved the accuracy and information efficacy of scheduling for Square D.

One of the areas being explored for further research with this system is a comparative study with optimization scheduling systems. Originally the management of Square D wanted an optimization based scheduling system created instead of the heuristic system contained in HEJADSS. Resources at the time and the urgency to implement a new system did not permit the development of an optimization based scheduling system. Now the HEJADSS is working, time permits an opportunity to compare this system with possible future improvement based on optimization theories. Mallett [16] has suggested a number of small algorithm improvements that could be incorporated in HEJADSS to augment the existing heuristics. These types of algorithm based improvements have commonly appeared in the literature as a means of improving heuristic based systems [22].

## 5. Summary

A heuristic decision support system (HEJADSS) is described in this paper as it was applied in scheduling operators to jobs for an electronics organization. The heuristic methodology used in the HEJADSS in this application was observed to reduce management scheduling time and effort, while generating an assignment schedule that adhered to the real world work constraints required by law and labor contract.
