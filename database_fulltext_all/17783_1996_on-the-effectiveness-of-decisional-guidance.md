# On the effectiveness of decisional guidance

Ali R. Montazemi $^{a,*}$ , Feng Wang $^{b}$ , S.M. Khalid Nainar $^{a}$ , Christopher K. Bart $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Michael G. DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada L8S 4M4 $^{b}$ Michael G. DeGroote School of Business, Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada L8S 4M4

## Abstract

The objective of this study was to determine the impact on decision makers of two forms of computer support – suggestive guidance and informative guidance – on the problem formulation stage of judgmental tasks. Three hypotheses were postulated regarding the effectiveness of these two forms of guidance. The three hypotheses were tested in two experiments comprising two judgmental tasks of differing complexity. The task performance of 282 subjects provided the basis for the assessment of the two forms of computer support. In the first experiment, which had the less complex task, we found that the availability of DSS improved task performance. In this scenario, suggestive guidance was found to enable subjects to perform significantly better than those subjects using informative guidance. However, in the second experiment, which had the more complex task, subjects using informative guidance outperformed those who were either supported with a suggestive guidance or who received no-DSS support at all (i.e., only pencil and paper was used). No significant difference was demonstrated to exist between those who were supported with suggestive guidance and those who were not supported with DSS. The results are analyzed with respect to current findings in decision research.

Keywords: DSS impacts and evaluation; Decision processes; Cognitive cost-benefit theory

## 1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSSs) are computerized systems that have as their objectives the following: (1) to decrease the cost of searching for and solving a structure, or (2) to increase the effectiveness of searching for and solving a structure for an unstructured problem [40]. When selecting an appropriate form of DSS to fit this definition ex-ante, system developers face a dilemma [11]. The reason is the variety of conceptual designs that a system developer can adopt when building a specific DSS, often with little information on the efficacy of a chosen design. To address this issue, in this paper, we focus on the effectiveness of DSSs.

A traditional assumption in the literature on DSS is that, if decision makers (DMs) are provided with expanded processing capabilities, they will use them to analyze problems in more depth and, as a result, make better decisions. However, recent empirical investigations suggest that, in some cases, the conservation of effort by the DM may be more important than an increase in decision quality [70]. Although improved effectiveness is an important goal of a DSS, we must also seek to understand the process of human decision making as it is, not as it ideally might be [52].

Two lines of thought provide the basis for the above conclusion. First, evidence shows that human decision behavior is contingent upon the characteristics of a given task environment $[4,48,53]$ . Second, when engaged in performing tasks of any complexity, human decision processes involve several components that interact both with each other and with the task environment to help determine a final outcome $[2,3,18,43]$ . Additionally, as the complexity of a decision situation increases, the decision maker adopts strategies that reduce cognitive strain $[51,52,62]$ . In the literature on information systems, the question is how we might effectively support decision makers when they are faced with problems of varying complexity.

In theory, an assortment of modeling techniques can be adopted to design and develop an effective DSS $[11,30,57,58,60,74]$ . However, the outcome of empirical investigations on the topic is inconclusive $[6,19]$ . This could be due to a lack of cognitive fit between the nature of the task environment (e.g., task complexity) and the form of DSS used in these investigations. Cognitive fit can be defined as a cost-benefit characteristic that, for the most effective and efficient problem solving to occur, allows optimal problem representation and employs appropriate tools and aids to support these strategies (methods or processes) required to perform a given task $[2,3,71]$ .

The objective of this investigation is to examine the effectiveness of two types of decisional guidance of use in structuring the problem formulation stage of judgmental tasks: suggestive guidance and informative guidance. To this end, the remainder of the paper is structured as follows: in Section 2 the current literature is reviewed. In Section 3, the basic theories on the effects of cognitive feedback and reinforcement learning are used as the basis to postulate three hypotheses on the effectiveness of suggestive guidance and informative guidance. Next in Section 4, because the central proposition of this paper is that nature of feedback due to environmental factors (such as task complexity) may influence the effectiveness of these two forms of decisional guidance, a justification is presented. This justification takes the form of two experiments. The methodology used in these experiments is described in Section 6. Section 7 provides our results, and a discussion closes the paper.

## 2. Decisional guidance effectiveness

Decisional guidance can be defined as the manner in which “a DSS enlightens or sways its users as they structure and execute their decision making process – that is, as they choose among and use the system’s functional capabilities” ([57], p. 107). A variety of structural designs for decisional guidance are available [57–59]. The focus in this paper is on isolating the optimal type of guidance for structuring the problem formulation stage of the decision making process. Of the two types of guidance considered here, suggestive guidance makes judgmental recommendations (what to do, what input values to use) and, in contrast, informative guidance provides pertinent information that enlightens the decision maker’s judgment without suggesting how he/she should act [47]. The decisional guidance tested in this research could be categorized as the corrective model for reinforcement learning [35,56].

The corrective model of DSS provides feedback to the user for repairing his/her misconception about issues pertinent to “appropriate” decision processes. A DM needs this type of feedback to reach a “correct” decision outcome. Unlike informative guidance that does not provide the user with corrective feedback, in this context suggestive guidance identifies differences between a DM’s understanding and the “correct” knowledge required to guide the problem solver toward the solution of a specific decision problem. One form of suggestive guidance implementation is a critiquing system. Critiquing systems exist in a variety of forms, ranging from spell/grammar checkers to case-based reasoning and intelligent tutoring systems $[58,60]$ . For example, an expert system computes its own solution and then informs the user of its output (i.e., it provides informative guidance). In this situation, the expert system neither examines the user’s solution nor does it provide feedback specific to the error made by the user. However, for a critiquing system the solution is part of the input and the output is criticism and explanation to the user. The question raised here is whether

DSS in the form of suggestive guidance or informative guidance can improve a DM's task performance.

Feedback should improve a DM's performance when dealing with judgmental tasks [2,33]. Therefore, one would expect that decisional guidance would improve a DM's task performance. However, empirical investigations of the effect of DSS availability on a DM's performance have been inconclusive [6,19,36].

On the one hand, some evidence suggests that DSS availability does not affect performance [1,26]. The work of Aldag and Power [1] attests to this in that they report that the availability of assisted decision analysis does not affect subjects performance. Additionally, Goslar et al. [26] demonstrate that DSS availability neither influences subjects performance, the amount of data considered, nor the time required to complete the decision task. On the other hand, several studies have found that DSS availability does have a positive effect on decision quality [5,15,42,54]. For example, Dos Santos and Bariff [15] found that users receiving system guidance when charged with the task of manipulating model variables were better at finding and prioritizing problems than users not receiving such guidance. Premkumar et al. [54] also demonstrated that computer-assisted instruction improved the performance of its users.

In view of this review of the current literature, it is safe to conclude that no strong theoretical basis exists that can predict how decisional guidance will influence decision making. Therefore, in the next section, we will postulate three hypotheses and provide their justification.

## 3. Hypotheses development

Of great concern to decision researchers is the extent to which the strategies are used by a DM when making a judgment vary across task environments $[17,32,53]$ . This concern provides the basis of this investigation: simply put, our question is “does task complexity impact the effectiveness of DSSs in improving a DM’s task performance?” Obviously, this question is too broad to be addressed in a single research undertaking. Therefore, we will elaborate our interpretation of “DSS effectiveness” in a specific task environment.

Four task types are possible: decision tasks, judgmental tasks, problem tasks, and fuzzy tasks [9]. In addition, three major categories of problem-solving activities can be identified [41]: (1) representation, (2) formulation/choice of approach, and (3) selection of final solution (see Fig. 1). Our focus of attention is on the problem formulation stage of judgmental tasks. Research in cognitive psychology indicates that in uncertain environments, DMs have limited ability to process information [49], notably so with respect to judgmental tasks [28,33,72]. Judgmental tasks require DMs, to first consider and then integrate diverse sources of information. Second, they must make a judgment or prediction about the likelihood of future events occurring. Thus, an accurate judgment or prediction requires the DM to (a) determine which pieces of information to pay attention to, (b) to weight these pieces of information appropriately, and (c) to combine this weighted information to arrive at an overall judgment.

We make two basic assumptions when postulating the effectiveness of DSSs. First, we assume that the DSS adequately represents a subset of the decision problem. This assumption is based on the definition proposed by Marsden and Pingry [40] that

In order for a decision model to be useful, it must be solvable. A necessary, but not sufficient, condition for a decision model to be solvable is that there exist mapping from action-state conditions to goals. Further, if the solution is to be utilized, the individual and organization must be satisfied with the decision model, that is, it must be acceptable as adequate in representing the problem (p. 185).

Our second assumption relates to the positive effect of feedback on performance $[2,3,68]$ . In the fields of cybernetics, engineering, biology, economics, organizational theory, and psychology feedback is an important construct. Technically, feedback is the process by which an environment returns to a DM that part of the information in their response output necessary to compare a present strategy (state) with a representation of an ideal strategy $[13]$ . That such feedback enables DMs to understand and improve their judgment, improve their expertise in the judgment task, and reduce commitment to incorrect judgment strategies has already been asserted $[3,28]$ . The positive effect of feedback on performance is due to reinforcement learning (i.e., the process of acquiring new knowledge based upon evaluative feedback), received by the DM through interaction with the world. Ideally, the result of such updating (reinforcement) is an optimized action map that assigns action to incoming input so that cumulative reinforcement is maximized $[35]$ .

We adopt the reinforcement learning behavior scheme proposed by Kokar and Reveliotis [35] to assess the effectiveness of decisional guidance provided to DMs in the form of feedback media. The general mechanism according to which DMs adapt their behavior to information incoming from the world (i.e., to reinforcement values and world-state relevant information) is called a learning behavior (see Fig. 2). This behavior consists of three parts: the internal state $x$ , the update function $u$ , and the evaluation function e. The internal state x expresses the level of knowledge that the DM possesses about the world; it does not relate explicitly to the world states. At every cycle, the evaluation function e determines the DM's response a to the received input i, based on internal state x. The selected action brings the world to a new state, which results in a new input $i'$ and a reinforcement value r. The DM's internal state x is then updated so that it better approximates his/her knowledge about the world.

Fig. 2. The learning behavior scheme [35].

The feedback received by a DM can be interpreted as a corrective action associated with his/her internal representation of the learned concepts. Based on this learning behavior scheme, suggestive guidance takes the form of a DSS tool that provides feedback based on a DM's action as well as on an evaluative function (i.e., an expert's knowledge of the task environment). Informative guidance, however, is a DSS tool that provides feedback based on the DM's action.

Our basic proposition, based on above discussion, is that feedback provided through decisional guidance should have a positive effect on a DM's judgmental task performance. Also, the evaluative function included in suggestive guidance adds value to the feedback toward corrective actions on the part of DM. Therefore, suggestive guidance should be more effective than informative guidance in assisting a DM perform a judgmental task [8]. Based on the above discussion, we can postulate the following three null hypotheses as follows: When a DM is involved in the problem formulation stage of judgmental task:

Hypothesis 1. There is no difference between suggestive guidance and no guidance with respect to task performance of DMs;

Hypothesis 2. There is no difference between informative guidance and no guidance with respect to task performance of DMs; and
Hypothesis 3. There is no difference between suggestive guidance and informative guidance with respect to task performance of DMs.

## 4. Investigational goal

Keeping in mind that the nature of feedback from suggestive guidance may influence its effectiveness, our goal in this investigation was to examine the validity of the three above stated hypotheses. Feedback relating to problem formulation takes two forms [3]: task information (TI), and cognitive information (CI). TI provides information about the task environment or, in other words, how subjects should perform a judgmental task (e.g., provides information about the causal-relationships existing among attributes), and CI provides information about the subject's cognitive strategy and reflects relations between the cues and the ensuing judgments that subjects make when they are actually working on a task (e.g., provides information about the variance between their perception of the value of an attribute and its real value).

Feedback in the form of TI is possible for problem formulation of tasks with a known structure; however, provision of TI for the complex and highly configural judgmental tasks is not possible [3]. In this latter case, feedback can be provided only in the form of CI. For example, rule-based critiquing systems provide feedback in the form of TI, whereas case-based reasoning systems support feedback in the form of CI. We tested the stated hypotheses for both types of feedback.

## 5. Methodology

Because the objective of this study is to determine the impact of two forms of computer support on the performance of DMs, our criterion variable is the measure of DM performance, and the predictor variable is the type of computer support required to problem formulation.

## 5.1. Environment

Two experiments, which simulated real-world judgmental tasks were selected to test the stated hypotheses. Tasks were in the form of case studies, and subjects who participated in our study were senior level business administration students. The first case study required subjects to analyze the financial statements of a firm (called CRI) and to assess from this analysis whether the firm was in a good position to raise money through an issue of equity. Since, the answer could go either way, subjects were faced with an ill-structured decision problem. However, based on background knowledge gained from their enrolment in a financial accounting course, subjects did have exposure to a knowledge of the relationships among the various dimensions pertinent for making an assessment of this case situation. The nature of problem formulation for this task made possible the provision of feedback in the form of TI (see Appendix A). A total of 134 students, who had registered in four sections of this financial accounting course taught by the same instructor, participated in this first experiment. Their participation was in exchange for up to a 10% course credit proportional to their performance on the case.

The second judgmental task, required subjects to develop a competitive strategy for a second firm (C&C yachts limited [22]). This second case reviews the history of a relatively small, yet highly successful yacht builder. The company had only recently embarked on a new venture: lunching a new manufacturing facility in the U.S. The company was also planning for expansion into Europe. The subjects involved in this second experiment were challenged to develop and defend a set of recommendations to the company's senior management that evaluated the company's current corporate strategy and suggested changes for the future. Problem formulation for this task was complex and highly configural; therefore, problem formulation could only be supported by CI feedback (see Appendix A). A total of 148 students, who were registered in four sections of a policy course taught by the same instructor, participated in the study in exchange for up to a $25\%$ course credit proportional to their performance on the case. The reasons for the difference between the course credits assigned to the CRI and the C&C case studies were that (1) the C&C case study was more complex than the CRI study, and (2) the course credit for both the CRI and the C&C case study had to be made proportional to student effort expended on other course requirements.

Both cases represented ill-structured problems, and both could be categorized as having information unstructure [40]. To justify selection of alternative solutions, DMs could only deal with the unstructure by acquiring adequate information to assess the different dimensions of the decision problem. The task complexities of the two cases were, however, different.

Three characteristics of a problem may affect task complexity: the number of alternatives in the choice set; the number of dimensions of information used to define an alternative; and the amount of time available for making a decision $[53]$ . The CRI case comprised three alternative solutions with seven major dimensions, and the C&C case three major alternative solutions with 16 dimensions. The information provided for the CRI case study was in the form of 1-page description of the decision environment. Additionally, four exhibits related to the financial statements of the firm and its organizational structure were given. The information provided for the C&C case study was in the form of 12 pages of text. Additionally, four exhibits related both to the financial statements of the firm and to the organizational structure were given. Therefore, a different amount of time was required to complete the two assignments. All subjects were given 2 hours for CRI and 4 hours for the C&C case studies to complete the task. This was based on the fact that historically the time allotted had proved reasonable for completing the two case studies when students used pencil and paper. Evidence suggests that DSS availability does not influence time to complete a decision task $[26]$ . Consequently, the effect of time on task completion was kept constant for each case study.

DSSs, in the form of suggestive guidance and informative guidance were developed for each of the two cases (see Appendix A). Subjects were assigned to three groups to perform the required analysis. The first group used suggestive guidance (DSS(SG)), the second group informative guidance (DSS(IG)) and the third group pencil and paper only (no-DSS). We controlled for instructor-effect, a significant determinant of performance as observed by Kulik et al. [37], by using the same instructor for all three treatment groups. The variability due to student-specific factors was controlled by random assignment of subjects to treatment groups.

In both cases, the subjects' grasp of course material was tested (i.e., financial accounting and policy). The two cases were among many that the subjects had been required to analyze as part of the requirements of the courses in which they were registered. Therefore, they were familiar with the decision task. Any variability due to bias in performance evaluation was eliminated by initially setting up a clear benchmark for the objective evaluation of the subjects' case analysis, and then by carrying out two independent evaluations of the subjects' case analysis. Inter-rater reliability was verified before the two scores were averaged to get a final score.

The timing and sequence of feedback built into the two forms of DSSs were based on the findings of Te'eni [68]. To ensure compatibility of task complexity among the three treatment groups and to avoid the potential trap of evaluating the superiority of computer versus calculator, all subjects personally computed the pertinent ratios for the CRI study. The C&C case study required no major computations.

## 5.2. Test

Each subject was provided with a hard copy of the pertinent case study. Since the computer experience of the subjects in the two DSS groups could affect their use of the systems, subjects were required to complete a questionnaire to assess their computer knowledge and years of computer experience. To assess computer knowledge, we adopted the instrument developed by Rockart and Flannery [55].

Next, subjects were provided with a brief description of the two DSSs while in the micro-lab setting. Subjects used a sample test to familiarize themselves with the operation of the DSS. They also had access to a help key that provided assistance in using the system. A research assistant was also available to answer any questions concerning the system's operation. At the end of the test, subjects were told that their marks would be adjusted (normalized) in relation to the group with better performance.

## 5.3. Reliability of performance evaluation

The response of each subject for each case study was assessed by two independent raters. Inter-rater reliability was measured using the correlation between the two evaluations. The results show that correlation is highly significant (r = 0.871, p < 0.001 for Experiment 1, and r = 0.813, p < 0.001 for Experiment 2), permitting the evaluations of the two raters to be averaged to obtain an overall measure of performance.

## 5.4. Statistical model

One way ANOVA was used to test the performance of the three groups of the subjects. The three factor levels (treatments) consisted of the following: (1) the group that used DSS(SG), (2) the group that used DSS(IG), and (3) the group that did not use DSS. The final score for the case study was assigned as the response variable. Our experimental design was unbalanced because the number of replications per cell was not the same for all cells because of the limit on the number of available microcomputers. Therefore, the general linear model was used for computation.

## 5.5. Test for random assignment

To test for the random assignment of subjects to the three groups, the following analysis was performed. For the first experiment, ANOVA was used to assess subject performance for the mid-term and final exam (see Table 1) to find out if there were any differences among the three groups regarding their domain knowledge. The result of the ANOVA test demonstrated no significant difference among the three groups of subjects (F = 1.169 (p = 0.314) for the mid-term and F = 2.076 (p = 0.130) for the final exam).

In the second experiment, the subjects' class participation performance and the final exam score were used to reveal differences among the three groups regarding their domain knowledge (see Table 2). The result of the ANOVA test showed no significant difference among the three groups of subjects ( $F = 0.156$ ( $p = 0.86$ ) for class participation and $F = 0.129$ ( $p = 0.88$ ) for the final exam score).

## 6. Results

## 6.1. Experiment 1

The average performance for the DSS(SG), DSS(IG) and no-DSS was 48.14, 42.60, and 29.38, respectively. The result of an ANOVA test among the scores of the three groups showed a significant difference (see Table 3). The Newman–Keuls method ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ) was used to investigate the difference among the three pairs of the groups. This analysis also revealed a significant difference between each group pair. The group who used DSS(SG) did significantly better than those who used DSS(IG), and subjects who did not use DSS performed significantly less well than the other two groups.

The two groups of subjects that used DSS(SG) and DSS(IG) explicitly assessed the causal relationships among the important dimensions in the CRI case. The results of ANOVA confirmed that the group using DSS(SG) assessed the causal relationships more accurately than DSS(IG) ((F = 104.739, p = 0.000) for the number of incorrect selections of the causal relationships among the dimensions and (F = 11.137, p = 0.001) for the incorrect rating of the relationships).

The above analysis provides sufficient evidence that the difference in the scores of the three groups of subjects is due to the type of DSSs used. Therefore, based on the results of this experiment, all three hypotheses can be rejected.

## 6.2. Experiment 2

The average score for the DSS(SG), DSS(IG), and no-DSS groups was 36.33, 44.68, and 32.73, respectively (Table 2). The result of an ANOVA test among the scores of the three groups suggests a significant difference (see Table 4). The Newman–Keuls analysis ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ) was used to investigate the differences, and showed a significant difference in the performance of the group that used DSS(IG) and the other two groups. Although the average score of the DSS(SG) group was higher than the no-DSS group, the difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, it can be stated that the group using DSS(IG) outperformed the other groups. The DSS(SG) and no-DSS group performed similarly. The ANOVA test of the number of alternative strategies analyzed by the subjects suggested a significant difference among the three groups (see Table 5). The results of the Newman–Keuls analysis ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ) showed that the group using DSS(IG) assessed significantly more alternative strategies than the other two groups. There was no significant difference between the DSS(SG) and the no-DSS group.

The above analysis provides sufficient evidence to conclude that the difference in the score of the three groups is due to the type of DSSs used. Therefore, Hypotheses 1 and 3 are supported, and Hypothesis 2 is rejected.

## 6.3. Summary

Informative guidance was superior to no guidance with respect to task performance in both experiments. Additionally, the first experiment findings also support the positive effect of cognitive feedback in the form of TI. Suggestive guidance enabled subjects to perform significantly better than those supported by informative guidance. The results of the second experiment, however, are mixed. The group using suggestive guidance received feedback in the form of CI. This group had a significantly lower performance rating than did the group using informative guidance. This observation is in line with the findings of Balzer et al. [3].

Balzer et al. [3] found that subjects who received TI feedback showed significantly better performance than subjects receiving no feedback. These investigators also noted that the performance of subjects who received CI was similar to the performance of subjects who received no feedback. In our investigation, the CRI case study environment made possible the specification of causal-relationships among the dimensions of the decision problem. TI modeling could therefore be performed. The C&C case environment, however, was highly configural; non-linear relationships existed between the dimensions and alternative solutions. In this environment, the only possibility was to develop CI to provide information about the subject's cognitive strategy by reflecting the difference between a subject's assessment of the dimensions and their correct value. When this interpretation of structure of the two DSSs is imposed, our results with regard to the efficacy of suggestive feedback are consistent with those of Balzer et al. [3]. However, the findings reported by Balzer et al. [3] are based on a relatively simple judgmental task that included only linear cue-judgment relations. A reassessment of the effectiveness of informative guidance and suggestive guidance in other, more complex, judgmental context is therefore necessary.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Contingent decision making

The overall decision making process can be broken down into three interrelated subprocesses $[18]$ : information acquisition, evaluation/action, and feedback/learning. Based on normative theories of human behavior (i.e., objective rationality), DMs are motivated to use DSSs in any one, or all, of these related subprocesses toward providing an optimal solution to a decision task. Our second basic assumption suggests that optimal use of DSS(SG) would require subjects to adopt the following strategy: first, subjects should develop an accurate internal state of the decision environment by a thorough study of the case materials, and next, to update their internal state of knowledge to better approximate their knowledge of the decision environment, subjects should use the corrective feedback provided by the DSS(SG). If followed, this strategy provides the optimal means of selecting appropriate alternative solutions for decision problems. However, in the test situation, subjects who used DSS(SG) did not exhibit this type of normative decision behavior when working with the C&C case. Descriptive theories of human behavior shed light on this phenomenon $[32,39]$ .

Decision makers do exhibit rationality, but only within the constraints of their perception of a decision-problem environment $[61,67]$ . Simon $[61]$ suggests that DMs attempt to compensate for their limited cognitive abilities by constructing a simplified representation of the problem and by behaving rationally within the constraints of it. A bounded rationality results. Bounded rationality can be attributed to the operation of a psychological phenomenon, which has been labelled “cognitive strain”, where cognition is those attributes (elements) of which a person is aware in relation to himself (i.e., self schema), to persons other than himself (i.e., person schema), and in relation to the typical sequence of events in a given situation (i.e., event schema) [7,38]. Additionally, the structure of schema used to handle each decision problem is influenced by environmental factors. Cause-effect relationships are therefore myriad. Consequently, a variation among the structure of schema used by DMs facing similar decision problems can be created. However, as decision problems become less structured, lack of explicit guidelines create an environment in which the DM has to interpret or refine the structure based on his own beliefs [7,14,25]. Therefore, based on our definition of DSS, the informative guidance provided to the DM should enable refinement of his/her appreciation of the problem structure (i.e., problem representation) based on individual beliefs. Both experiments support this hypothesis. The performance of subjects using DSS(IG) was superior to those without access to DSS.

A fundamental and critical component of human judgment, however, is the level of elaboration brought to bear when interpreting a specific domain of knowledge that is encoded in his/her schemata. This elaboration is achieved through two schema-related adjustments: assimilation and accommodation. Assimilation involves the alteration of a perception to fit an existing schema, whereas accommodation is the alteration of a schema to fit current knowledge. The retrieval operations of schemata vary in their extensiveness; habitual encodings are evoked spontaneously and automatically by the decision problem. This occurs when there is a good fit between the available schemata and the situation at hand [7,65]; further elaboration processing is evoked only if directed by task demands or by feeling of partial recognition of the decision problem [7,38]. In this latter case, construction of meaning and understanding of the decision problem involves an association of experience with existing knowledge; alteration of existing schemata by incorporation of new knowledge, and occasionally, a dramatic restructuring or creation of knowledge (i.e., learning) [20]. Thus, learning is a primary phenomenon on which secondary, reflective reasoning is based [10]. This phe nomenon provides insight into our findings on the effectiveness of decisional guidance.

The basic assumption of our research is that suggestive guidance provided by a DSS tool enables the DM to update his/her internal state (i.e., schema) to better approximate his/her knowledge of a decision problem. The subjects who used DSS(SG), in the first experiment, took the information from the case material and used it to reflect on the corrective feedback of the DSS. The result was a restructure of their knowledge of the decision environment. As a result, their performance was superior to the other two. This learning process, however, was notably absent from the problem-solving process of the subjects using the DSS(SG) in the second experiment. These latter subjects bypassed the first phase of the problem solving process (i.e., problem finding) and relied on the feedback from the DSS to create their problem-schema. Reflection on corrective feedback was not possible because they did not have the basic schema on which to reflect. Consequently, they came up with a set of correct data, with the help of the DSS(SG), but this could not be processed via a correct problem-schema.

## 7.2. Implications for research

Although its importance to information science has only just been realized, human cognitive processes have long been of interest to philosophers, psychologists, and behavioral scientists [47]. The role of information scientists then must be to identify those dimensions that can affect the quality of the decisions. Here, we show that increased functionality of a DSS (e.g., suggestive guidance) can, in fact, have an adverse effect on the DM's judgmental task performance. This finding is in line with those of other researchers [34,70,71] who are concerned that the concept of cognitive costs and benefits plays an important role in the development of an effective DSS. We concur with Todd and Benbasat [70] that provision of tools that alter the relative balance associated with desirable and undesirable strategies for problem solving stage is necessary.

The decisional guidance used in this study supported problem formulation. To provide feedback, we used lookup tables in our DSS(SG), which has an architecture similar to that used in spell/grammar checkers or in help modules installed in a wide range of commercial software [60]. Of note is that interest is growing in DSSs designed to provide dynamic feedback during information retrieval processing [23,24,64]. The objective of these DSSs is to support all stages of the problem solving process (i.e., problem representation, formulation, and selection of final solution). For example, case-based reasoning system support problem formulation for highly complex and ill-structured decision problems [27,63]. The addition of intelligent agents, to a case based reasoning system, can support problem representation as well as problem solution [45,66].

The proliferation of these types of systems raises two important questions. First, does the functionality of the components of a DSS improve a DM's performance enough to justify their cost? Second, what form of feedback is the most effective for different types of decision tasks? This latter question is particularly important in the attempt to enhance the effectiveness of tools such as suggestive guidance. First, the amount of time invested in absorbing feedback in the form of CI to an erroneous input could be greater than the time required to become knowledgeable about the decision domain. Second, a user may simply accept the default guidance of the system without the feedback having any effect on his/her misconception of the relevant decisional model $[3,18]$ . Assessment of these conjectures could be pursued in future investigations. Whether or not feedback in the form of task information is more effective in improving a DM's performance than the provision of cognitive information needs further substantiation. Confirmation of the efficacy of the feedback phenomenon would be valuable to investigators involved in the design and development of decisional guidance.

## Appendix A

## A.1. First experiment: CRI case study

Embodied in it, this study had both structured and unstructured elements. Subjects were asked to study and analyze the CRI case. The task involved the application of some relatively standard techniques of financial statement analysis, plus the consideration of some case-specific attributes. The financial statement analysis constituted the structured components of the case, which consisted of determination of relationships amongst variables such as high debt implying greater interest expense and higher risk of insolvency. These factors rendered the problem structure in a micro-sense. The unstructured component comprised case-specific attributes/factors needing consideration in the overall domain, and these could go either way. Therefore, although, the overall decision involved judgment, that component of it in the form of a “cognitive map” (see Fig. 3) consisted of familiar structures.

An appropriate solution to the task required subjects to follow the strategy outlined below. First, subjects needed to study the case materials with some care. Only this would enable isolation of the various important economic dimensions relevant to case analysis. These dimensions consisted of the following: (1) liquidity; (2) solvency; (3) profitability; (4) leverage; (5) technology obsolescence; (6) research and development success; and finally (7) exchange rate risk. Dimensions one to four are commonly used when analyzing a firm's financial health. Dimensions five to seven are case specific. Dimensions five and six are critical because CRI is a high technology company. Dimension seven has importance because CRI is in the process of planning an equity issue for its intended expansion overseas.

The second step in the analysis of this case required quantification of the above seven dimensions, and the third step required the identification of causal relationships among the seven dimensions. These causal relations are essential to an assessment of whether the proposed equity issue would be successful. The causal relationships among the first four dimensions are general and were made available in conventional accounting and finance courses. The inclusion of the dimensions five to seven, however, requires judgment of the subjects when they assess the case-specific environment. Successful execution of these three steps enables subjects to perform the final step, which is the justification of one of the three possible alternative solutions.

## A.1.1. First possible solution

The CRI company is fairly liquid and enormously profitable, does not carry a heavy debt load, and appears to have a better than industry average with regards to its research and development success and the “quality” of its patents as measured by the economic years remaining on them. Additionally, the exchange risk vis-à-vis the current market trend seems minimal. Therefore, Mr. Smith, the controller, should recommend that CRI is well placed to go to the equity market and raise the \$50 million needed for the company’s overseas expansion.

## A.1.2. Second possible solution

CRI does not have a heavy debt load, and this implies room is available to “leverage gains” and capitalize on its “underutilized” debt potential. This state of underutilization can be reasoned to be pronounced because the firm is very liquid and profitable which makes the serving of debt easy. The advantage of increasing debt load under these conditions is the potential profit that further investment could make available to current stockholders. These profits might be reduced to the individual shareholder, in case of an equity offering which leads to an increase in the size of shareholder base. Hence the attractiveness of getting additional funds by way of debt. Therefore, CRI should not go to the equity market, rather it should raise funds by means of increasing its debt, especially since other risks, such as the exchange risk, seem minimal.

## A.1.3. Third possible solution

Depending on interest rates and equity markets projections, CRI could remain indifferent to the choice between an equity issue or an increase in debt load. This position could be argued based on the firm's current liquidity and highly profitable situation that make financing of the proposed overseas expansion by use of its internal funds possible. This option would be most attractive in a macroeconomic climate characterized by high interest rates that could impact unfavorably on the stock market and mandate against the favorable reception of an equity issue by stockholders.

## A.1.4. DSS structure

The cognitive mapping technique was selected as appropriate means to assisting the subjects analyze the financial statements of this case study. Cognitive mapping has been applied in information requirements analysis [44], decision analysis [31], and strategic planning [16]. We developed two computer-based DSSs to assist subjects develop the causal relationships (cognitive map) among the seven major dimensions of the case. The first DSS was designed in the form of “suggestive guidance” (DSS(SG)), which prompted the user regarding the causal relationships among the seven dimensions (Fig. 3). The causal relationship between each pair of dimensions (e.g., profitability and solvency) could be identified as a positive influence, a negative influence, or no influence at all (e.g., an increase in profitability has a negative influence on solvency). In addition, toward the solution of the case, subjects were asked to identify the degree of importance of dimension pairs. The rating between two dimensions (e.g., A and B) could have any of the following forms:

1. A and B are equally important.

2. A is moderately more important than B.

3. A is strongly more important than B.

4. A is very strongly more important than B.

5. A is extremely more important than B.

For example the rating between solvency and profitability is as follows: solvency is moderately more important than profitability. The reason supporting this choice is that, while solvency is a pure long-term concept, the notion of profitability is only partly so.

The correct relationships between dimensions and their degree of importance toward the solution of the case were built into this DSS. If the subject's selection (i.e., relationship between a pair of dimensions and their degree of importance) was correct, the system accepted the input and continued assessing another pair of dimensions. Otherwise, the system displayed the correct answer with its justification. The information given about the selected dimensions was saved in a database that subjects could access by means of a button at the bottom of the screen. In other words, the DSS provided “suggestive guidance” to help the DMs develop the “correct” causal map of their decision environment. This system was named DSS(SG). Feedback took the form of corrective prompts and information about choices made.

The second DSS for this environment, the “informative guidance”, was similar to DSS(SG). However, this DSS did not offer suggestions regarding the correct answer to the relationship between the dimensions and their degree of importance. Subjects could only access pertinent information about the case and their response to the nature of the relationships that they had already selected. We call this system DSS(IG).

However, only three segments (alternatives) had sufficient data to support a meaningful analysis. Three dimensions needed assessment for each business segment. They were as follows:

Having decided what posture to take in the company's product-market environments, any outstanding unfavorable environmental condition and/or firm weakness that might interfere with the fulfillment of company goals still needed to be addressed. Although the company has done well in its traditional markets, for the first time it may be faced with competing in a market that is not growing at 25–30% per year. ## A.2.1. DSS structure

Several models have been proposed for the design of strategic decision support [12,21,29,46,69,73]. To develop the two DSSs that assisted subjects assess the C&C case study (see Fig. 4), we adopted a revised version of the model proposed by Thierauf [69]. The first DSS design was in the form of “suggestive guidance” (called DSS(SG)). DSS(SG) prompted the user with a series of questions to help them assess the 16 dimensions for each of C&C’s three business segments. For example, one of the segments was “Custom Boats and North America”. To assess the dimension related to economic factors for this segment, items such as “GNP trend”, “money supply” and “currency exchange rate” needed consideration. A three-point scale (favorable, unfavorable, and no impact/not applicable) was used. The DSS(SG) accepted the correct response to a question and then displayed another. In the case of an incorrect response, the DSS(SG) displayed the correct one with a brief justification. For example, if the “exchange rate” was assessed as either “favorable” or “not applicable”, then the corrective feedback from the DSS(SG) was displayed as follows: “There is a threat of rising Canadian exchange rate. This is unfavorable for the C&C.”

All user responses were saved in a file. Incorrect answers were highlighted with an asterisk. This file could be displayed on the screen by activating a button at the bottom of the screen. The user could also browse through another file that contained information relating to the system's correct answers to selected questions. The DSS(SG) was designed to guide the subjects by assessing all relevant strategies. Subjects could end their assessment at will.

After entering relevant data for the selected alternatives (segments), the DSS(SG) used correct data to construct the position of the selected strategies on a SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats) grid. A series of What-If Analyses, to assess the effect of different strategies on the firm's position on the grid was available to subjects. Next, subjects used DSS(SG) to develop a strategy for the C&C and to defend their selected strategy. The DSS(SG) provided “suggestive guidance” to help the DMs assess relevant dimensions and to develop the correct SWOT grid. However, to defend a course of action (strategy), subjects had to use the proffered data together with their experience in analyzing similar case studies, and as well, their overall knowledge of the C&C environment. Student’s performance was assessed on how logically the selected strategies were defended.

Informative guidance (i.e., DSS(IG)), was similar to DSS(SG), except that the former did not correct a subject's incorrect answers, and it did not guide the user to select relevant strategies (segments). DSS(IG) also saved all user's responses in a file that could be accessed at all times. However, unlike the file for the DSS(SG), the DSS(IG) offered users no means of distinguishing between correct and incorrect answers – they only had access to their own responses. Additionally, DSS(IG) allowed the users to end their analysis when they thought it appropriate. The resulting SWOT grid was, therefore, based on the individual user responses.
