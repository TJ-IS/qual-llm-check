# Marketing the MIS

Charles D. Schewe

School of Business Administration, University of Massachusetts/Amherst, MA 01003, USA

James L. Wiek

Weyerhaeuser Co., Tacoma, Washington, D.C. USA

and

Robert T. Dann

Pharmatech Systems, Inc., Ambler, Pennsylvania, USA

The promise of management information systems has not yet been realized; low utilization rates by line decision-makers may be one cause. This paper suggests that the effort of marketing the MIS is as important as the work of both the MIS design and implementation phases combined. Various MIS “markets” are segmented, and marketing mix strategies which could encourage greater MIS usage are developed. An application of these marketing strategies for an advanced MIS is provided as an illustration.

Keywords: Marketing, usage strategies, MIS, Management Information Systems, broadening marketing.

## 1. Introduction

After nearly two decades, the true potential of computer-based management information systems (MIS) has not yet been realized. In its simplest form, a management information system is a carefully developed master plan for the orderly flow of relevant and reliable information through the organization to enhance managerial decision-making. An MIS is composed of three key components – people, procedures, and machines, all of which operate in a structured and interacting fashion. People are the mainstay; top managers, middle managers, and systems support personnel (programmers and statisticians, for example) all work together to design, implement, and control a continuous flow of pertinent and accurate information for decision-making at all corporate levels. The design of such a system entails formal procedures by which information obtained from both intra- and extra-firm sources is provided to the appropriate company managers. In addition, the process usually relies on various devices to effect this flow. While this machinery need not include a computer, contemporary use of the term “management information system” implies heavy reliance on the computer for data storage and processing.

When a computer is an integral part, an MIS can be conceptualized as consisting of a data bank, a statistical bank, and a model bank. The manager accesses the system through an input/output subsystem — a card reader and printer, or a cathode ray tube (CRT) with keyboard. Given an information need, data pertinent to the problem can be assessed directly from the data bank or the data may be transferred to the statistical bank for massaging by appropriate statistical subroutines. In more sophisticated systems, the manager can request that data (either raw from the data bank or refined through a statistical program) be input to powerful mathematical models that stimulate the decision environment and provide the decision-maker with optimal or near optimal decision alternatives. Such systems sound like a dream come true, but recent literature indicates growing disenchantment with corporate MIS [1]. A major factor has been the lack of impact on top management [2]. Research into life insurance industry applications found very little usage of statistical analysis of data, and that usage that was found was for the greatest part clerical [3].

Middle managers in food-processing companies which reputedly have very sophisticated information systems, were found to request information from their interactive systems almost exclusively during the short, annual budget planning period. And on top of that, most managers only requested five or less pieces of information per month in this “peak period” [4].

This paper presents a marketing approach to the development and use of management information systems and is intended to inspire more effective utilization of information systems by managers. It begins by focusing on the MIS user and usage situation in order to provide an understanding of the different managerial motives for system usage. Next a series of marketing strategies relating to the MIS product development, physical distribution, communication, pricing or costs to user, and feedback will be discussed; these are the elements of a marketing program designed to meet the needs of specific groups of MIS users. Finally, an example is provided of how this approach can be utilized to encourage the more effective use of a sophisticated pharmaceutical industry MIS by product managers and marketing research personnel.

## 2. MIS in a marketing context

MIS is a resource which serves to improve the decision-maker's efficiency and effectiveness by reducing uncertainty in decision-making. While great organizational rewards can potentially accrue from effective MIS usage, a wide range of factors serve to limit its utilization and its resulting benefits. Most organizations are plagued by, but probably do not recognize, a low return on resources invested in their MIS by failing to properly tailor their product and/or service (the MIS) to meet the needs and desires of the consumer (the information system user). This MIS marketing process should logically begin by determining both the technological and information requirements of the system (product specifications) and the full set of needs of the information system user (market demand specifications). Once these specific product and market demands have been identified, a marketing mix which establishes parameters for product, distribution, communication, and cost/price variables can be developed. The sequence of this MIS marketing strategy formulation process will be explained, beginning with an analysis of the MIS user/consumer.

## 3. Analyzing the MIS user/usage situation

The benefits derived from a management information system are limited by the skills and sophistication of its users. The information system is merely a machine full of potential information whose value is realized as the system user puts the system to use. Thus a market research approach that will help understand the system user's relationship to the system is a logical first step; such research should attempt to clarify the differing needs and characteristics of the several parties in the MIS usage process.

## 3.1. Multiple roles in the MIS usage context

Successful marketing plans require the precise identification of potential consumers and the determination of their identifying characteristics. In addition, there may be other parties in the usage context whose influence must be considered in the formulation of the MIS marketing plan. Kotler [5] has identified five roles in the buying process: initiator, influencer, decider, purchaser, and user. The nature and structure of this consumption process and the individuals involved in it should be fully understood so that the MIS can be designed and managed to operate effectively in the face of the complex set of influences that may be exerted on the system. Demographic segmentation. Information users/decision-makers at different managerial levels tend to be of different ages. Top executives naturally tend to be older than middle managers, and older individuals may be more resistant to change. This may help explain why, as reported earlier, the impact of MIS has not been strongly felt among top executives.

Educational level also helps to differentiate among buyers of the MIS service. Previous research among food processing firms indicates that better user education is needed to reduce inadequacies in the system user's knowledge of MIS capabilities [6]. Furthermore, an executive in a large and well-known beverage company indicated that some of their worst offenders with respect to system usage were recent M.B.A. graduates from the top business schools in the United States. This would lead one to suspect that formal training is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the effective interaction with a computer system.

Benefit segmentation. MIS users differ substantially in terms of the benefits sought from their systems. Such differing usage motives are illustrated by the following purposes for which MIS's are frequently employed:

-- retrieval, manipulation, and statistical analysis of sales data.

\- monitoring performance of statistical control units (products, customers, territories, etc.) relative to predetermined objectives.

\- allocation of economic resources such as expenditures for sales personnel or advertising.

-- handling manufacturing schedules, inventory levels, or transportation systems.

\- simulation prior to market tests to ask "what if" questions to test the assumptions underlying a new marketing program.

Volume of usage segmentation. Experience with MIS usage has shown that often a small proportion of consumers is actually responsible for a major portion of MIS utilization; for example, in one large MIS with which the authors have worked, less than 30 percent of all system users have requested more than 60 percent of all data displays for information. Marketing efforts directed toward this heavy user segment

might seek to:

(a) motivate a wider variety of uses of the system;

(b) generate even greater rates of usage; and

(c) create favorable word-of-mouth communication from high volume to lower volume MIS users.

## 4. Marketing strategies to increase MIS usage

Having first gained a better understanding of the differences that exist among MIS user groups through the use of segmentation, the system designer/manager should then offer them unique marketing mix packages. Various segments may respond favorably to changes in one or more product dimensions such as: data format, timeliness, accuracy, or level of aggregation;

level of sophistication of the tools in the statistical bank;

types of models available; or

special input/output components.

Beyond this, greater cultivation of alternative user segments may require special communications, distribution, and cost/pricing strategies.

## 4.1. Product development and management strategies

MIS product strategy should begin with an understanding of the benefits expected (in terms of product and system usage attributes) by system users. From this base, questions regarding the management of system timeliness, physical form, and information reliability may be addressed.

MIS benefits. The major benefit delivered by MIS is the information input for improved decision-making. While many step-by-step procedures for MIS design and development have been offered [7], information needs determination should begin by mapping the managers' perception of their decision environment, their information needs, and specifying the information sources. Most executives cannot indicate their information needs when directly questioned. Consequently, an indirect approach of leading them through a series of decision processes is necessary in order to specify both the kind of data they require as well as the extent to which it should be held in aggregated form.

## 4.2. Physical distribution strategies

Physical distribution involves the delivery of product benefits through time and space from points of production to points of consumption or use, thus providing time and place utility to the user. This leads to a set of needs as follows.

System usage to maximize time and place utility. Physical distribution considerations bearing on MIS usage include the number and location of points at which users may access the system, system response time, and the mode of data transmission through the system to the user. Batch processing systems require the use of punch cards which are submitted to a card reader; interactive systems, on the other hand, use direct access to the system through remote terminals. The means of transmission greatly affects the time necessary to retrieve the desired information from the system. Systems may necessitate the preparation of card decks and submission of the program to a clerk who inputs the program to the computer, such a procedure delays receipt of the output, while at the other extreme, many companies can obtain the desired output almost instantaneously.

MIS users' time utility may be increased by distribution strategies which reduce the time necessary to actually access the MIS. Where remote terminals are the mode of access, there often is a backlog of users waiting to communicate with the system. This may cause a substantial delay in information retrieval and result in considerable anxiety and aggravation of the system user.

If terminals are part of the system, their location could influence usage too. The authors surveyed over fifty-five middle managers who use an interactive information system and found that eighty percent of the managers preferred the terminal located in a private or closed area rather than in an open area. The major reason, they indicated, was because privacy afforded less noise, fewer distractions from passersby, and a greater opportunity for concentration. Those few who liked the open area location did so because it gave them greater company visibility and such placement reminded them that the system was there and that they should use it.

Organizational location of MIS. Yet another area where location takes on meaning within the MIS context is the placement of the MIS within the organizational structure of the company. Many writers [8] have indicated the importance of top management support, both financial and psychological, given to the design and implementation of the system. Where the system is located in the organization can be symbolic of the importance top management attributes to the use of computer-based information. A high position can signify heavy commitment and corporate prestige accruing to its utilization.

## 4.3. Communication strategies

Marketing communication should be directed at present MIS users to increase both the frequency of usage and the variety of decision situations to which MIS analysis is applied. Communication may also be directed toward potential MIS users in order to move them from awareness of MIS to interest, mental evaluation, trial, and full usage of MIS.

Recognizing that the realization of effective MIS usage is a long-term goal, the communication program should be continuous and should utilize reinforcing media, such as system experts (change agents), usage workshops and instructional sessions, and non-personal messages.

Role of the system expert. Probably the most important single element in the MIS marketing communication strategy is the inclusion of a staff person who will serve as a facilitator between line managers and the system itself during actual use of the system. This “system expert” should know the capabilities and limitations of the MIS and be aware of any changes made in the system. That person should also be available to assist any manager who may be eager to use the system but lacks the skill or initiative to effect the information transfer.

Workshops and instructional sessions. Workshops, conferences, and training sessions should be made available to overcome the problem of inadequate MIS knowledge and to facilitate the flow of feedback from system users to system personnel. Beyond this, brush-up sessions should be conducted on a regular basis to maintain and strengthen user skills and to disseminate information about changes in system operation and capabilities.

Non-personal communication media. Advertising media such as company newspapers or house organs should be used to communicate noteworthy MIS developments. Bulletin boards strategically placed within the company could display information promoting or assisting computer usage. Direct mail pieces could even be placed in pay envelopes.

One of the most important MIS sales promotion tools is the user's manual. To be effective, such a piece should be attractive, concise, and readable to those who know little about computer utilization.

## 6. Pharmatech Systems, Inc. - A marketing example

All good marketing programs begin with the recognition of an opportunity in the marketplace, and MIS design and implementation should be no different. In 1961, a Director of Marketing Research (initiator) felt that existing data already being purchased from information suppliers could allow pharmaceutical companies to isolate from among a variety of alternative marketing programs the one that would produce the most long range profit. The idea was experimented with until 1968, at which time it was presented to pharmaceutical companies at a meeting of their marketing research groups (communication). Subsequently each of the major pharmaceutical companies was individually contacted to uncover their information needs (market segmentation) as well as to solicit their interest in developing such a system (personal selling). A printed brochure (communication) was presented outlining the MIS concept and the development costs (pricing) to be borne by subscribing companies. The initial concept of the system was to present to marketing managers bar graphs printed on microfilm (product) of sales response to various promotions. The system was launched into operation in 1970 and was called "Pharmatech" (brand name). Seven companies (purchasers) initially subscribed. Many meetings with these companies took place to define and redefine (product development) the system's boundaries and insure that the MIS as developed reflected each of their needs (benefit segmentation). Reports (communication) and brochures (advertising) were also distributed to keep them up to date on developments and to reassure them that progress was being made.

In September, 1970, the first report of five hundred pages (packaging) appeared. One-half were graphs and the other half were computer-produced tables (products). The initial reaction of users was very negative. The document was too formidable and complex (product attributes) and was rarely used because of this perceived effort to get the data (cost). Interviews (market research) with users revealed that some data supplied by the system was not what was wanted or needed, that summary tables would be most helpful, and that analytical tools could be used to improve the data's use in decision-making (benefit segmentation). It was thought that to avoid these problems, a greater variety of tabulations (product attributes) would be necessary and that this could not be provided in printed form because of the already prodigious report. Thus, the idea of an "on-line" system (distribution) to directly access the data base using CRT's (packaging) was developed. It allows for data retrieval, use of regression analysis, and use of a simulation model (product/benefit segmentation). This new and improved system is called MIRIAD (Marketing Information Retrieval with Interactive Access and Display) and responds to user commands by displaying the output on a screen within five seconds after request (distribution) and then, at the user's option, printing the data in hard copy form (product). A manual (communication) aimed at clearly presenting the system's capabilities and instructions for its use was developed and distributed to all potential users. Before the system became operational, a seminar (communication) was held for thirty prospective users of MIRIAD to instruct them in its use. These individuals then became liaison men between the pharmaceutical firm and MIRIAD (personal selling) to facilitate this new "on-line" system. When MIRIAD began being used in 1972, "Pharmatech," the original computer graphics system, became outmoded (product life cycle) and was eliminated.

Since the implementation of MIRIAD, more exploration into system usage has been undertaken (market audit). Because the system is on-line, it has been possible to monitor the usage rates of companies (volume segmentation); across companies, usage rates have varied from less than 100 requests for data displays per month to over 2,200 requests (product usage attributes). Workshops (communication) have been held to help determine the reasons behind these variations. Questionnaires (market research) have been used to obtain information on system users' perceptions of MIRIAD (product attributes) and then relate these data to individual usage rates to determine what MIS dimensions may be changed to improve MIRIAD usage. The usage patterns of various subprograms such as the simulation model and regression analysis program have also been monitored to see what system benefits have been most often sought (benefit segmentation). Usage behavior has been looked at within groups differentiated on the basis of education, position in the company, and age (demographic segmentation). Finally, terminal location preferences (distribution) have also been explored.

In summary, Pharmatech Systems, Inc., started with an idea and the development of a product to meet the basic information needs of its market. Specific strategies to market this product were knowingly undertaken to insure product purchase – first by the pharmaceutical firms and subsequently by the decision-makers/system users within those firms. It has continuously monitored its market after the sale to insure effective calibration of the product to meet not only the technical information needs of the users but also to insure that users' psychological needs are met. The end result is that MIRIAD has become a highly successful product in its marketplace.

## 7. Conclusion

A prerequisite to more effective decision-making through usage of MIS-generated information is actual usage of the system. MIS usage is best enhanced through the conception and implementation of an effective marketing plan, a set of strategic decisions with respect to the marketing mix offered to systems users. This marketing plan can be effective only through a thorough understanding of the needs of the consumer that is being served. Too many MIS design plans begin with an analysis of computer hardware offerings and neglect the importance of the user's needs – especially the psychological needs. System usage holds the key to unlocking the true potential of the computer, but this is likely to happen only if system designers adopt the marketing concept in their approach to system development.
