# An Investigation of Information System Utilization

Stephen R. Barkin

College of Business, University of Utah 84112, USA

and

Gary W. Dickson

College of Business Administration, University of Minnesota 5.5455, USA

This investigation focuses upon the information utilization process; i.e., what it means for a decision-maker to utilize an information system. A definition of utilization is adopted that states that an information system is utilized if the output from the information system is included in the Human Information Processing system of a decision-maker. The definition of utilization is further refined by segmenting utilization into two distinct subsystems, a Human Information Processing system and a Data Selection system. As an initial investigation of the utilization process, it was decided specifically to study the relationship of the Data Selection system to the Human Information Processing system. The primary aim of this investigation was to determine whether factors that are internal to a decision-maker may affect the data selection process. "Cognitive style" was chosen as representative of internal factors. Measures of data selection and cognitive style were created with particular emphasis placed on the development of an instrument to measure cognitive style. An experiment was designed to investigate the effect of this factor on data selection. The results of this experiment indicate there is a strong relationship between cognitive style and data selection.

Keywords: Utilization process, human information processing, information system design

Each of the above statements has been extracted from a research study that has dealt with man-machine interactive decision-making. Despite their common focus on this topic, each study was distinctly different. The objective of one study was to analyze the conflict between users and data processing departments. The objective of a second study was to explain the failure of the installation of a large Management Information System. The objective of a third was empirically to identify variables which may help to describe and predict how computer-based reports are perceived by decision-makers. The objective of the fourth study was to identify the principles that result in effective project design and implementation. Although dealing with a wide variety of man-machine interaction problems, each of the statements quoted above has one attribute in common, the inclusion of the word “use” or one of its variants.

It is evident that those doing research in the field of Management Information Systems (MIS) feel that the utilization of a system is one of the “key” variables associated with the success or desirability of the system. To support this point one needs only to scan the ten-plus years of literature in the MIS area. Although acknowledging the importance of utilization as a key determinant of system success, MIS professionals have not adequately defined this seemingly elusive variable. If MIS professionals are to deal with system utilization, it must first be defined. The problem then is to understand what is meant by the utilization of a system. The following questions illustrate the nature of this problem.

Is a system utilized if a decision-maker receives its outputs?

Is a system utilized if a decision-maker receives its outputs and examines these outputs in detail?

Clearly, even if a decision-maker receives system outputs, they may not be examined. Even if examined, there is no guarantee that any actions or decision-making have been or will be impacted. This problem of defining system utilization is further compounded by the difficulties of measurement. If the premise is accepted that an important criterion of success in MIS design is to produce “utilized” systems, a measure of utilization must be developed to insure this objective. Unfortunately, neither the MIS researchers or MIS professionals to data have provided the designers of information systems with much insight as to what it means to utilize an information system or how to measure the phenomenon of system use.

This paper discusses a definition of information system utilization that can be operationalized within an information system environment. A means of measurement is proposed. The definition and measurement methods are tested in an experiment which also explores some facets of utilization.

## 1.2. Utilization defined

Designers of information systems want to insure that the systems they develop are utilized. These designers, however, have not adequately defined utilization or a means for its measurement. Unfortunately, the term utilization may convey one idea to the designer of one MIS, but something quite different to another. As an approach to dealing with this problem, the following definition of utilization is proposed:

The utilization, usage, or use of a system refers to the inclusion of system generated data by the decision-maker in their Human Information Processing system.

The Human Information Processing (HIP) system is the cognitive system that has the capacity to organize, manipulate, and integrate data for decision-making. An information system is therefore utilized if the output from the information system is organized and/or manipulated and/or integrated by the decision-making process. One potential advantage in accepting this relationship between HIP and utilization is that the research undertaken in HIP can now be related to utilization. HIP research has provided theoretical models (e.g., [14,16,19,24,25]) upon which operational models of information systems utilization can be built and thus allow MIS designers to consider the user as a variable in the design process.

## 1.3. Theories of Human Information Processing

To appreciate the definition of utilization adopted for this study it is useful to look at current theories of HIP. The three theories to be presented represent contemporary research in the HIP field.

The theory of HIP developed by Simon and Newell [25] is best described as structural in nature. Man is characterized as a serial information processor with limited short-term memory, infinite long-term memory, and a stored repertoire of programs called operators. The main thrust of this theoretical approach deals with how the Human Information Processor internally represents task environments. Simon and Newell call this internal representation a problem space. It is the problem space against which the stored programs (operators) are applied. Behavior to resolve a task, as this task is portrayed in the problem space, can best be characterized as tree-like in nature; that is, the problem-solver can widen the search for alternative paths or deepen the search down one particular alternative until coming to a dead end or a solution.

A different approach to HIP has been undertaken by Schroder, Driver and Struefert [24]. Rather than describe the structure of the Human Information Processor they describe the way this processor organizes information for the problem-solving process. This organization they call the level of information processing. The crux of the theory is tied to a construct called “integrative complexity”. This term describes how a person combines information perceived from the environment as well as information generated for internal purposes. The greater the ability to combine data elements and therefore create new data the greater the “integrative complexity”. Most significant about this work of Schroder et al. is the development of an instrument to measure integrative complexity.

A third approach to HIP theory does not describe the structure of the Human Information Processor or describe the ability to combine and create new information but rather classifies individuals by decision-making type or “cognitive style”. McKenney [19] provides a good example of this approach to HIP theory (for others see refs. [15,27]). Decision-making type, according to McKenney, is characterized by an individual's communication mode and appraisal manner. Communication mode refers to the way an individual perceives the environment and acquires and creates new information on the basis of this perception. Appraisal manner refers to the degree to which an individual plans for information processing activities.

Although the above theories take differing approaches to explaining HIP, each provides an explanation as to how a decision-maker structures and organizes information. Unfortunately, each also fails to elaborate upon the process by which data is selected for inclusion in the HIP system. The failure of these theories to provide a description of a data selection process leaves them incomplete. An analogy to this situation can be found in the computer area. A description of a computer system cannot be considered complete by just describing the nature of the central processing unit (CPU); the nature of the input/output (I/O) devices must also be described. These researchers have described the nature of the CPU. The inclusion of a description of the nature of and I/O device, the data selection process, will further complete the description of the HIP system. Fortunately, psychologists interested in the area of attention have dealt with this issue.

Much like Human Information Processing Theory, theories of attention differ in their descriptive approaches. All, however, attempt to explain four basic phenomena [11, p. 288]:

The focus of attention on a very limited aspect of the external world;

The broadening and narrowing of the range of attention;

Retrieving rejected messages from a short-term store;

Responding to important messages in a non-attended channel.

As in their Human Information Processing counterparts, theories of attention disagree more than they agree as to how the mechanism operates, from the "filter" theories of Broadbent [6], Triesman [26] and Deutsch and Deutsch [9] in which messages are filtered or attenuated prior to admission to long-term storage, to the time-slice multiplexing theory of Schmidt and Kristofferson [23] in which attentional phenomenon is a function of periodic switching from one channel to another. Regardless of their similarities, however, these competing theories of attention disagree on a key point: Is attention influenced by factors internal to the individual or external to the individual [6,9,23,26]? Restated in terms more familiar to MIS practitioners: Are attentional phenomena influenced by factors internal to the decision-maker (i.e., HIP), and/or external to the decision-maker (the information system)?

## 1.4. A model of utilization

By combining HIP models and attention theory the deficiency of failing to deal with data selection can be eliminated. Thus, the model of information system utilization decomposes the HIP system into subsystems, a HIP subsystem and a Data Selection subsystem. A diagram of this model of utilization is shown in fig. 1. This pictorial model depicts utilization of an information system as a multistep process: the information system is shown as producing data which is provided to a decision-maker. From the data available certain elements are selected and employed in decision making by the Human Information Processing System. Thus utilization is suggested as a combination of the latter two stages: data selection and HIP.

## 2. The research strategy

## 2.1. The problem defined

To adequately explore the model of utilization presented in fig. 1, substantial resources in terms of time and personnel are required. As an example, one would have to determine how data are selected and then how the processing of these data takes place. Breaking the utilization process into two parts allows for options in researching utilization.

As one step in the exploration of the above utilization model, an investigation of only the Data Selection subsystem was undertaken. Controversy exists among the theories of attention as to whether or not attentional phenomena are influenced by factors internal and/or external to an individual. In terms of the model of utilization presented in fig. 1, the issue is whether data selection is affected by the information system (external factors) and/or the HIP system (internal factors). Due to the interests of the principle investigators, it was decided to focus at this time on whether or not the HIP system (internal factors) affects the Data Selection system.

## 2.2. Cognitive style—the independent variable

The cognitive style approach to HIP was chosen as representative of factors internal to the decision-maker. This particular factor was chosen for two reasons. First, cognitive style has been a successful factor in experimentally affecting variables that may be associated with utilization $(4,5,13,15,20]$ . Second, cognitive style is an intuitively pleasing variable when extended into a business environment. Unfortunately, much controversy exists as to how cognitive style is measured. As a result of this controversy, a great deal of time and effort was spent developing a means of measuring cognitive style.

A cognitive style paradigm similar to that employed by Barrett et al. [4] was adopted. Individuals are measured along a continuum of cognitive styles. At one extreme of this continuum is the Type I or "trial and error" individual, at the other extreme, the Type II or "quantitative" individual. A description of the problem-solving strategies of these different cognitive styles is presented in Table 1 [4, p. 3]. Type I individuals are roughly heuristic types as analyzed by Huysmans [15], whereas Type II individuals are roughly analytic types as employed by Mock [20].

## 2.3. Data selection—the dependent variable

The major models of attention reported in the literature vaguely deal with the issue of whether or not persons, are cognizant of their attention process. To avoid this vagueness, the data selection process is therefore defined as that process that discriminates among data elements and admits specific ones to the HIP system such that the decision-maker is conscious of the admitted data selected. This awareness helps insure that the data have entered the HIP system. The definition of data selection therefore can be viewed as a restricted definition of attention. There are two advantages to accepting this definition. First, the measurement of data selection is simplified. Second, it is more easily applied in a non-experimental environment and is therefore more operational.

## 3. Hypotheses

The objective of this research was to determine whether factors that are internal to a decision-maker can affect the data selection process. Several hypotheses were posed to investigate this issue. It should be noted at this point that a risk was taken in choosing only one variable representative of internal factors (cognitive style). Should the effect of cognitive style on data selection be null, we know only that this specific representative of internal factors does not affect data selection, yet other representatives of internal factors might exist that do affect data selection. However, if the reverse is true, confirmation would exist for the proposition that data selection is affected by decision-maker characteristics. The specific relationships hypothesized between data selection and cognitive style are presented in Table 2. The main hypothesis of these four is H1: Type I decision-makers will select more data than Type II decision-makers. This hypothesis was predicated upon the attributes of the Type I and Type II decision-makers. That is:

1. The search procedure of the Type I decision-maker is one of trial and error. This type of person draws inferences which are a hunt for situational differences which vary over time. The lack of an ordered plan with which to isolate the situational differences should result in the processing of more data, at least until several differences are found.

2. Type II decision-makers tend to utilize a modelling process which will simplify the environment and remove the need for data.

## 4. The experimental setting

The ideal experimental setting to investigate the questions raised in this research should have several properties. First, to aid in extending the results of the research into the business world, subjects should be placed in an environment that is a reasonable replicate of real-world decision-making [2, pp. 12–21]. Second, to minimize the risk of the confounding of data due to differential learning rates among subjects, a series of repetitive decisions should be made in a reasonable time frame. This procedure allows an averaging of data over time.

The experimental vehicle chosen to serve as the basis for this research was a large-scale interactive production simulation written in FORTRAN and executed on a time-shared CDC 6400 computer system with Model 33 teletypes. This simulation fulfilled the necessary requirements. The setting, provided by a written case, was real-world in nature. Subjects were required to make several decisions, a decision covering a week of simulated operation at a manufacturing facility. Each decision-making session required about an hour of time on a subject's part. The weekly decision consisted of three parts:

## 1. Hiring/firing

## 2. Ordering raw materials

## 3. Scheduling production

To assist in this task, for each simulated week, subjects were provided with a Production Summary Report reflecting the impact of last week's decision. The experimenter identified the critical data on this report employing an expert judge technique [7, pp. 141–144]. The Production Summary Report contained 198 data elements, 87 of which were critical to the decision and 111 of which were non-critical to the decision.

## 5. Research method

## 5.1. The measurement of cognitive style

A basic problem in measuring any construct such as cognitive style is the inability to observe what is being measured. It is improbable that cognitive style can be measured by monitoring brain waves or other physical phenomena, although some basic research has begun [12]. One is therefore left with the alternative of observing behaviors and attitudes which should result from differences among individuals of differing cognitive styles. A survey of existing cognitive style measurement instruments yields unsatisfactory results. Leaving aside the validity issue, few, if any, researchers publish reliability or consistency data [8,15,17,19,27]. The failure to publish psychological testing statistics left current cognitive style measurement instruments unsuitable for use in this study. A substantial effort was therefore expended in developing a suitable test instrument to measure the Type I/Type II cognitive style continuum. The resulting instrument was a 17-item preference test. This instrument went through four phases of refinement during which it was administered to over 500 subjects. During each phase, refinements were made on the following criteria:

1. Inter-item correlation no higher than 0.49 (most item intercorrelations were far lower);

2. Item-total score correlation no lower than 0.4 (most item-total correlations were higher);

3. At least 60 percent of the Type I subjects had to score opposite on an item than did Type II subjects [21, pp. 65, 86, 106].

The Kuder-Richardson 20 reliability [22, pp.196--197] of the resultant cognitive style measurement instrument was 0.85.

## 5.2. The measurement of data selection

Psychologists have employed varied means of measuring attention. Some have attached specialized cameras to subjects' heads and monitored the scanning motion of the eyes. Some have had subjects repeat back nonsense syllables or streams of text. Others have had subjects identify objects after a determined period of time. No studies, however, could be identified that investigated attention at a level associated to the report-oriented nature of business information systems. A method of measurement, therefore, had to be developed.

Many measures of data selection can be postulated. On could administer one of several varieties of \*ests to a subject at differing time intervals after the subject scanned a report. Unfortunately, this measure would bring such confounding factors into data selection as memory decay. To avoid this confusion, a memory measure cannot be used. After discussing this issue with several professionals, both psychological and business, it was decided that a satisfactory instrument for measuring data selection did not exist. A simple real-time protocol methodology was therefore employed. Subjects were issued an accenting pen and asked to "highlight", as they read a report, any data elements they felt were:

1. Utilized in their decision-making process;

2. "Eye catching": or

3. Interesting.

In brief, any data elements that subjects thought about as they scanned a report in the decision-making process were to be accented.

As most business system reports contain not only data elements but groupings of data elements (headings or paragraphs) and as the method of measuring data selection had not been previously tested, it was decided to obtain two measures. First, each element highlighted by a subject was counted. Second, if an element within a paragraph was highlighted, it was assumed the entire paragraph was selected. This procedure provided two measures of data selection: one of elements, and one of paragraphs.

## 5.3. Subjects

To increase the probability of finding differences between the independent variable (cognitive style) and the dependent variable (data selection), subjects whose scores were at the extremes on a cognitive style measurement instrument were selected. Twenty-six subjects, 11 Type I decision-makers, and 15 Type II decision-makers, were chosen from an upper class undergraduate level business administration class. Each subject participated in two consecutive experimental sessions, a three-hour “learning session” (Session I) and a four-hour “live session” (Session II).

## 5.3.1. Session I

Initially, subjects participated in a three-hour experimental session in which a simplified learning environment was created by the production simulation. Subjects made about four decisions each. The major purpose of this session was to familiarize subjects with time sharing, teletypes, and the experimental setting. All subjects received the same starting position for the firm as presented by the Production Summary Report.

## 5.3.2. Session II

One to two days later, subjects reported for a four-hour experimental session. At this time, all were given a new (but common) starting positon and presented an environment that was more complex than that presented in Session I. Subjects were required to make five decisions covering five weeks of production.

In studying tables 3, 4, and 5, a pattern emerges. When support for a hypothesis is evident, this support is statistically quite strong for experimental Session II, negligible for experimental Session I, and less strong for experimental Sessions I and II combined. A possible reason for this phenomenon is the lack of a complex decision environment in experimental Session I. When the environment is complex, the relationship between cognitive style and data selection is operational. When the environment is not complex, this relationship is apparently non-operational. This is consistent with the findings of Schroder et al. [24].

A second possible reason may be learning effects. Due to the newness of the environment, greater variability among subjects' data selection may not be sufficient to produce a relationship between cognitive style and data selection. Once subjects reach a similar threshold point on the learning curve, the impact of newness is less, reducing subject variability, and thus a main effect can be demonstrated.

## 7. Conclusions

The experiment and results show that the HIP system as represented by cognitive style affects the data selection process of a decision-maker. Subjects exhibiting a Type I cognitive style selected more data than did subjects exhibiting a Type II cognitive style. Coupled with the findings that Type I subjects selected more data non-critical to the decisions than did Type II subjects, this research suggests that many data elements and/or paragraphs selected by subjects of differing cognitive styles were not the same and depended upon cognitive style. In terms of the model of utilization presented earlier, one can conclude that as differing data were selected, differing data entered the HIP system; i.e., decision-makers exhibiting differing cognitive styles utilized an information system, for the same task, in different ways. Additionally, within each group of subjects exhibiting a similar cognitive style, utilization, in terms of data selected, was “somewhat homogeneous” in that many data elements and/or paragraphs selected were common.

The designers of information systems have the problems of tailoring systems to meet the varied needs of individuals. At present, the assumption usually made by designers is that all individuals are the same. One of the reasons underlying this assumption is that little evidence exists to show that decision performance can be enhanced by tailoring the systems to meet individual needs. A second reason is the lack of adequate measures of individual differences to use in research or practice by the system designer. Finally, assuming performance differences exist and individual measures can be constructed, it follows that there may need to be as many information systems as there are decision-makers using these information systems. The cost of such a design strategy would presently be prohibitive.

In order to include features in an information system to support the varied needs of individuals, the needs must first be identified. The conclusion of group differences in utilization presented offers a practical solution to tailoring information systems for users. By identifying groups possessing a few common characteristics, e.g., Type I versus Type II decision-makers, as opposed to the many differences possessed by individuals, information systems can be constructed to support these common traits. Designing information systems to support intra-group traits should reduce the amount of design features necessary to provide an information system that can support differences among decision-makers. Although this practice would be a compromise to building systems tailored to individual needs, designing a system to support groups of users is perhaps the more practical approach to constructing "utilized" information systems.

## 8. Future directions

This research has presented an explicit view of the utilization process; i.e., utilization is a dichotomous phenomenon: Data selection and Human Information Processing. Evidence has been presented that HIP systems may significantly affect the selection of data by the user of an information system. Future research employing this model of utilization may follow two paths, one conceptual and the other operational.

Conceptual research would be aimed at refining the model of utilization. This research should include replication of the study and an exploration of the broad range of factors that may impact the utilization process such as system response time, aggregate data, detail data, psychological traits, etc. Operational research would aim to extend the results of the conceptual research into the business area. Such research could study the affect of specific information system features on the utilization process as well as attempt to extend the use of such tools as the data selection measurement method into the analysis and design of information systems.

This study has answered a few of the many questions we have about utilization. Hopefully, future research will answer more. Research is continuing and we can expect to further refine our knowledge in this area. Perhaps one day it will be easier to build "utilized" systems.
