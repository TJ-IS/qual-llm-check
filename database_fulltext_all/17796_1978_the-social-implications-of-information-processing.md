# The Social Implications of Information Processing

Hans H. Brune

Department of Communications, Government of Canada,
300 Slater Street, Ottawa, Ontario K1A OC8, Canada

This paper suggests that the growing use of computer and communications technologies has serious long and short term implications for Canadian society. Of immediate concern to a broad spectrum of Canadian society is the inadequacy of many of today's information systems to meet the test of social acceptability. In many cases, the interfaces between people and machine systems are underdeveloped, causing problems in error correction, human frustration, misuses or abuses of personal information, and, to a lesser extent, invasions of personal privacy. The paper suggests that the designers of today's machine-oriented "data processing" systems must accept a large share of the responsibility for these problems because the human component, which should be present in every "information processing" system, had frequently been overlooked.

Serious long term social and economic dislocations are likely if Canada fails to align overall social and economic policies with the on-going transition towards the “information economy and society”. The paper examines the growing confrontations between labour and consumers, sellers and buyers, state and society and among the regional, national and international economies arising out of the pervasive use of computer/communications technologies. The social implications of national productivity and job exports are examined. The point is made that the social implications of the use of these technologies are much broader than privacy and encompass economic, political, legal and other social issues which cannot be resolved in isolation. It is suggested that in analysing these issues full consideration be given to technological, economic and social aspects.

A number of different approaches are outlined to initiate broader discussion of these issues; among these a data protection approach. Three basic requirements are introduced in an attempt to formulate guidelines for dealing with national and corporate data protection requirements. Collaboration and co-operation among Canadian government, industry and professional associations is proposed as the most desirable approach to meeting requirements in this area. The avoidance of premature legislative measures of the kind visible in the United States is recommended. The paper concludes with a summary of unresolved data protection issues.

Keywords: Information economy and society, information policy, data protection, man-machine-communications systems.

Most discussions of the social implications of information processing focus either on specific aspects (e.g. privacy) or on the long-term implications measured in terms of decades. Neither approach is entirely satisfactory. Given the rapid convergence of computer/communications technologies and the substantial growth in the use of these technologies in Canada and elsewhere, three questions which must be squarely dealt with are:

"Is there a potential during the next decade for major economic discontinuities which may be caused by the use of these technologies and which would lead to major social problems?"

The answer is obvious; the potential does exist. We will address this issue when dealing with economic implications.

"What likely social conflicts could be generated by the use of these technologies?"

"What must be done, and when, to alleviate these potential problems?"

The answers to the last two questions require attention from all of us and cannot be given without careful analysis. In doing so we must avoid the pitfalls of past efforts, viewing the issues with an emotional frame of mind, or failing to take into account the economic realities or the broader social needs of a changing society.

The “when” of the last question is the easiest to answer. The systems which will be in use at the turn of the next decade are already designed or are in the design stage. Their economic impact will be felt in the early eighties. But the social implications will become visible only after extensive use, perhaps in the mid-eighties. Corrections at that time may be almost impossible to achieve. The investments in hardware and software may be simply too significant and the resulting functional structures too far advanced to allow for major modifications. The process may have become irreversible. The right time to begin the development of appropriate safeguards and policies is now.

## 2. A framework for analysis

In attempting to answer these questions, we have no choice but to address the issues within the framework of the triangle - Technology, Economy, Society.

First, we must attempt to understand fully the potential of the new technologies to be able to recognize social and economic opportunities and threats. Second, we must examine the uses made of these technologies within our economy to identify actual and potential sources of social conflicts. Third, we must examine the nature and magnitude of these social conflicts and threats to be able to develop appropriate solutions.

## 3. The technological perspective

Perhaps we could agree that technology provides the “means of production”. But every major change in the means of production, from the invention of the wheel through the steam locomotive age, to the harnessing of nuclear energy, has caused major changes in the social organization of production, the economy. These changes have in turn modified our social structures, the society. Social and economic pressures have in turn forced the technologist to design the means of production in such a way that the final product is both economically feasible and socially acceptable.

Somehow, this sense of “technological” responsibility and accountability seems to be lost when it comes to the application of computer technology. The user bears the responsibility for economic feasibility alone; society is left to its own devices to deal with the social impacts. The reasons become apparent when viewing the scope of work of today’s computer technologists – the data processors.

## 3.1. The current state

In 1977, the Informatics Institute of Canada (IIC), a Canadian professional association, undertook a survey of some 111 computer-user organizations in the public and private sectors and firms in the Canadian computer services industry. Of these, some eighty to eighty-five percent could be classified as “data processing organizations”. The functional scope of these organizations could be described as: capturing data in a machine-processible form; processing and storing of data by machine; and printing output documents. A further task is the development of computer programs. All tasks are performed in accordance with user requirements. What we see is a kind of “data processing factory”.

There should be no misunderstanding. Most, if not all, of the surveyed organizations perform their tasks efficiently, profitably and with considerable ingenuity. But as yet the machine system and the user's information systems are two distinctly separate entities; separated not only in the physical but also in the organizational sense. The user controls the information system, the technologist the machine system. Man/machine interactions are usually under-developed, often ignored.

## 3.2. The social implications of data processing

The bulk of today's machine-processed documents is produced in a "data processing factory" environment. To identify social concern resulting from this type of processing, we do not need to look further than the technologist, the designer of these systems. He alone must bear the responsibility for society's concern.

Computer systems usually work well if both the user organization and the recipient of those documents behave precisely as predicted by the designer and no errors occur in the chain of human and machine processing. Any deviation from this assumption spells trouble.

People are used to errors in manual data processing. Prompt correction and a personal apology followed swiftly the discovery of an error. Computers, on the other hand, do not make errors, and the user organizations, encouraged by data processors, are doing their utmost to maintain this myth. In principle, the recipient is found to be wrong until proven otherwise. This reaction is understandable considering that the user organization usually possessed little knowledge about the functioning of the machine system nor about error correction procedures. In some systems, error correction routines seem to be lacking entirely. We know of a case where a vice-president of a major department store had to draw cash out of petty cash and physically hand it to the cashier in order to settle a customer account charged in error.

One of the best reports addressing social concern of this type in Canada is the 1972 survey report entitled "The Public Locks at Computers". This document was prepared by the Social Research Centre, a subsidiary of Canadian Facts Ltd, for the federal Department of Communications. The survey covered some 1030 households. Unfortunately the report created little response in the Canadian data processing community, and in government. Only now are we re-examining it in the light of our increased, although still inadequate, understanding.

While the survey reflects public opinions of some five years ago, there is no reason to believe that substantial changes have taken place. The problems are still with us and present indications suggest even a hardening of public attitudes towards computers.

De-personalization because of unresponsive or poor systems design is a related problem. Preservation of human dignity is essential in any relationship, between buyers and sellers, employees and employers, government and citizens. Most of the systems failures are caused by designers who designed the human elements of such relationships out of the system.

With regard to the fair information and privacy issues, it is well to remember that the public in general does not object to the collection and storage of personal data. The real objections are the uses, or more precisely the misuse of data. Computer-stored data can, however, not be used without a program. The ultimate responsibility for the misuse of data is therefore that of the program designer.

We believe that the social problems outlined above cannot be resolved within the framework set by the computer room. An upgrade of skills from data processor to information processor is essential. We deal with these aspects in the following sections.

## 3.3. The technological evolution

Some fifteen to twenty percent of the organizations responding to the IIC survey could be considered as having reached the stage of "information processing". The tenor of these responses suggests that the mission and scope of those organizations is oriented towards meeting corporate or equivalent information systems needs. Entrepreneurial goals and expected user impact appear to form the foundation of the information systems design specification. Technology is viewed as the servant, not the master, of information systems development. Man and machine form an integrated information system. Data are used as an information resource, shared by many. Man and machine interactions are optimized, making use of the unique capabilities of both.

Machines process, store and communicate data, characters, symbols, and other representations like bits, bytes and packets. Machines are superior to man in the repetitive processing of data in terms of speed, accuracy, reliability and the ability to transfer data instantaneously over distances.

Man is best in processing information. Man can both use and create information (knowledge, messages) by applying both quantitative and qualitative reasoning and by associating seemingly unconnected data or thoughts. Man can seek and formulate goals by processing information. Machines can only convert information from one medium into another, or communicate information in the form of spoken or written messages or visual images.

Some seventy to ninety percent of all respondents to the IIC survey expect to reach this stage by 1985. What can technology offer them to meet this goal? To answer this question, we must examine the trends in hardware and methodology development.

## 3.4. The hardware evolution

We assume that the current technological trends in hardware development will continue during the next decade. Improvements in the price/performance ratio of computer hardware by a factor of ten should be expected. Somewhat smaller improvements in price/performance will be made in telecommunications services (other than the basic telephone services) by a factor of two or better. New data services such as electronic mail, electronic funds transfer and remote word processing and storage facilities will become operational during the next decade. The cost of information and data storage, handling and transfer by physical media other than paper (tapes, disks, laser, microfilm, facsimiles) will continue to decline, perhaps causing massive replacements of paper-based information systems before 1985. The combined effects of these hardware developments will be a gradual collection from data processing to information processing; man, machine and communications together from the information system.

## 3.5. Ti - methodology evolution

Computers and modern communications hardware are useless devices without software and corresponding methodologies for their application. The existence of an appropriate methodology is a critical factor in giving economic purpose and useful social values to our information systems.

A most pressing problem to date is the quality of information systems management. There is no training in this area and little research is being done. Some managers seem to avoid this problem by attempting to remain “data processing” managers. But it remains to be seen whether this approach will survive the eighties. We are witnessing a gradual change in the functional scope of the senior position; from the EDP manager to the Director, perhaps Vice-President “Information Systems Management”. The increased complexity of tasks performed is forcing greater specialization in the labour force engaged in this field. In response to these trends, the federal Department of Employment and Immigration is currently undertaking an analysis of occupations in this sector. The resulting Occupational Profiles will likely be available during 1978.

Perhaps the best illustrations of the ongoing transition in methodology are the changes encountered in the components of a typical information system.

## 3.6. The components of the information system

An information system usually contains six elements:

Hardware represents the physical component of the system. Hardware is the most visible but least interesting part of the system in the context of our discussion. The initial manifestation of hardware was the central computing facility. Tomorrow the systems designer will deal with distributed computer systems, mini-computers, terminals, communications facilities, new microfilm, printing and other information and data handling machinery.

Software refers to the computer programs and allied documentation which supply the logic of the processes undertaken within the system and govern machine input/output functions. In the past, applications software was a home-grown product. Today, pressures on programming productivity and usability have made commercial applications software a more acceptable alternative. Major improvements in applications software development and maintenance productivity and in the quality of the produced software development should be expected during the next decade through a combination of new methodology, new programming languages, and new compiler and performance monitoring hardware.

Data refers to the information elements and structures which the system is designed to process. In the past the management of the data base was the responsibility of the user organization for which the data were processed. By 1985, more than two-thirds of the surveyed organizations are expected to assume the management responsibility for the flow and storage of data throughout the enterprise.

Telecommunications services refer to the hardware/software facilities and the services offered to transmit data, messages or images over distances, including input, conversion, and output devices. More than half of the surveyed organizations are expected to assume management responsibility for the use of such services within their organizations by 1985.

Systems design refers to the functional and organization structure of the system, the combination of human and machine resources and procedures, which give form to the information system. In all but a few exceptional organizations the overall responsibility is ill-defined and fragmented. The most likely scenario for the next decade is that the need for corporate survival will force senior management into making major changes in the assignment of this responsibility.

End-users represent the coupling of the system with the outside world. We differentiate between two kinds of users. Direct end-users (management and staff) have a certain degree of control or influence over the development and operation of the system and the system is designed for them. Indirect end-users, the participants in the system (customers, vendors, employees) have to interact directly with the system, but have little or no control over the development and operation of the system. We deal with the conflicts of the two groups in later sections.

## 3.7. Conclusions

In viewing the technology evolution within a Canadian perspective, the tools required to sustain the pace of automation of information processing and information transfer activities are already in place. The major problems likely to be faced during the next decade will be the development of appropriate skills, methodologies and a broad management infrastructure necessary to raise the economic feasibility and social acceptability of the on-going automation effort.

## 4. The economic transition

The existence of a sound economic base is fundamental to the survival of society as the ability to obtain and to keep a satisfying job is fundamental to the social well-being of the individual. An assessment of the social implications of computer, communications and information technology would be incomplete without an understanding of (a) the growth of computer/communications in Canada, (b) the structural changes in our economy, and (c) the resulting social conflicts generated by the use of these technologies.

## 4.1. The growth of computer/communications in Canada

One very useful task accomplished by Al Shackleton of the Computer/Communications Secretariat was the development of a method and model for estimating the magnitude of computer/communications spending in Canada and the subsequent production of spending estimates and forecasts for the period 1970 to 1985.

## 4.2. Structural changes in the economy

The economies of most Western nations are currently undergoing a structural change towards the "information economy". An increasingly larger share of GNP is generated by information-related activities. "Information workers", people in clerical, administrative, management, technical and scientific occupations, account for a growing share of the national labour force. The application of computer/communications technologies played a major part in this economic transition.

The major reasons for these structural changes are:

\- The change in the kinds of products sold today;

\- The higher growth rate of the service sector relative to the manufacturing and primary sectors;

\- The ongoing process of the "division of labour" i.e. specialization within the labour force;

\- The need for better management information;

— The utilization of emerging economies of scale.

The most visible sign of the information age is the variety of products in the market place to satisfy increasing expectations of the consumer. For example, some twenty-five years ago the consumer choices of automobiles were limited to a few models, colors and options. Today it has been said that the chance of seeing an identical automobile, the same model, color and same range of options, is less than one in a million. Yet, the great variety of models is produced in an assembly-line environment geared for mass production.

Gone are the days where the seller provided his goods to the buyer for cash or certified cheque. Today we buy on credit, expect a variety of after sales services, and perhaps a product recall if certain design deficiencies are discovered. Higher costs of information-related activities (research and development, production control, sales services) as a component of the price paid by the consumer are the natural outcome of this change. We also experience a change from information "poor" to information "rich" products; from hydro dams to nuclear power plants, from simple appliances to computer-controlled dishwashers, automobiles and airplanes.

Another manifestation of the “information economy” is the growth of the service (tertiary) sector relative to the manufacturing (secondary) and resource (primary) sectors. Financial, legal, medical, educational and government services are taking an increasingly larger share of the national labour force. Most of these services relate directly to the manipulation of information rather than the manipulation of materials.

The “little black book” of the shop foreman of a bygone era has been replaced by modern information systems. Modern production divorces more and more workers from the direct manipulation of materials, yet the same workers perform information service functions, essential to the production process. We are experiencing a growing specialization of our labour force.

In the management process, information and knowledge represent economic power. Knowledge about markets, consumer habits, competitors, about risk potentials and profit opportunities has become more important to economic survival and growth than mere possession of production plant or technological skills. Management is beginning to understand the real value of information.

Computer/communications technologies have made it possible to utilize effectively certain economies of scale in production, distribution, marketing, finance, administration, and research and development previously unexploited because of distance. The same technologies have increased the ability of management to implement their management styles, to centralize corporate key functions, to organize to take better advantage of national or regional differences in labour costs, market conditions, taxation load, currency values, and to respond more rapidly to short-term profit opportunities.

## 4.3. The socio-economic implications

In summary, we are in the midst of a major transition of our global and national economies brought about by the invention of new tools – computer/communications. From the socio-economic viewpoint, we have not yet begun to master these tools effectively. The uses of these tools may result in social and economic dislocations as drastic as those encountered during the transition from the agricultural to the industrial society. Proven policy tools of a bygone era seem to have lost much of their former effectiveness. Especially in the high technology area, the cost of physical production processes, expressed as a percentage of the price of goods sold has declined drastically during the past two decades. Indirect costs related to the production, processing and distribution of information and to the provision of services have become the more important cost elements and also sources of employment. Information processors of the present and future generations will have to come to terms with the economic realities and social impacts which they have created.

## 5. The social implications of information processing

It should now be evident that the major structural changes in national economies are not merely the results of applying known data processing techniques but rather the fruit of carefully planned strategies combining both information processing and new management techniques. Data and information can now be collected, processed and distributed instantaneously irrespective of distance between originator, processor, or user. Information-related activities have become highly transferable, no longer dependent on physical closeness to markets, consumers, manufacturing plant or distribution point. For example, the goods ordered from a Canadian sales office could be designed by an American, using components produced in Taiwan, assembled in Europe and shipped to the Canadian customer. But what are the effects on Canadian society?

## 5.1. Consumer-labour conflicts

We are witnessing a growing divergence of interests in the Canadian market place. National and corporate interests are becoming increasingly divergent as corporations take on a multi-national character. Consumer, farm and labour interests appear to be on a collision course.

Consumer and corporate interests call for efficiency in the market place; unrestrained access to low cost goods and services; use of newly emerging economies of scale; more reliance on the free market place as an effective regulator of economic activity. Pressures are mounting to optimize industrial productivity and to keep prices to consumers in line with those paid elsewhere.

Responding to the new economic reality, Canadian and foreign-based producers are faced with the option either to go international and to rationalize their operation on a global basis or to fold. Global rationalization implies, however, not only cost-effectiveness in physical production processes, but more importantly, cost effectiveness in the production, processing and distribution of information. This cost-effectiveness determines in the last instance the most desirable location of the corporate headquarters, research and development activities, administrative functions and, indirectly, the structure and distribution of computer/communications networks.

The interests of Canadian labour are oriented to different goals. Job security is an important topic today. Wage and salary levels seldom respond to the reality of international competition and technological progress but rather to assumed social needs. Rising wages without corresponding productivity gains bring labour into conflict with employers. The result is more imports of goods and services, more exports of jobs, more automation. The consequence is a growing imbalance between demand and supply in the labour market. Labour-management, and ultimately, political confrontations are the natural outcome of such developments.

The usual response of national governments to such situations is the imposition of tariff, quota and other trade barriers. Yct computer/comunications and the effect of the economic transition have reduced the effectiveness of such barriers as instruments of economic protection. Certainly these barriers help to preserve jobs in low technology industries such as textiles, shoes and certain low-cost consumers' goods or in industries with products of a low "information content". However, this type of protection does little to preserve the jobs of more highly skilled Canadians, the engineers, accountants, managers, in short, information workers.

This problem, however, is not strictly a Canadian phenomenon; European governments appear to have developed a partial solution to this problem. Restrictions are being imposed on the transborder flow of personal data within the framework of national data protection policies. In effect we are witnessing the emergence of a new kind of trade barrier. Barring more suitable international agreements, these barriers could be necessary to avoid major economic dislocations, protect jobs, maintain national security and the integrity of rational societies.

## 5.2. Economic policy conflicts

Labour and capital productivity improvements are the best instruments to ensure long-term corporate survival. In all but a few exceptional cases, a corporation must grow quickly from local into international markets and at the same time rationalize its operations. Today's sophisticated products and services require access to large markets in order to recover the cost of their design. Modern inventory and production control, cash management, billing, purchasing, accounting and market research techniques are needed to remain competitive. Labour-saving automation of production processes in manufacturing and in the provision of services are essential tasks to compensate for the rising proportion of indirect costs in the price of the product or service. In toto, these requirements generate a need for large investments in information systems. Adequate profits are a necessity to attract investors in the first place. Availability of highly skilled manpower in information processing-related occupations is also an important prerequisite.

Governments have essentially three options to meet these needs: (a) reliance on immigration and foreign investment; (b) generate labour and capital resources internally; or (c) isolate the country from the effects of international competition.

Importation of skilled labour (immigration) and capital resources (foreign investment) reduces the need to encourage savings and the need to reduce consumption to provide for the required public and private investments in human and production resources. People simply live better. Other countries had to forego consumption in order to create the investments. As foreign investment as a share of total national investment mounts there is no need to encourage domestic capital formation. With assured markets for domestic production, the taxation load can bear on production rather than consumption with little impact on the national economy. Education and training become of secondary importance as long as skilled labour from foreign countries is able and willing to immigrate to make up the deficit.

Internal generation of labour and capital resources is a painful process, usually reserved for the most affluent countries, like the United States, or for those countries which lost a war. In the latter case, the choice for an economic policy was rather narrow. Investments had to be financed from current production. Capital formation had to be encouraged by taxing consumption heavily and savings lightly. Education and training facility development had to take a higher priority in public spending than income re-distribution. In the more affluent countries, monetary policies -- easy credit -- helped to finance current investments from tomorrow's earnings. But this policy is ringed with traps. The current weakness of the U.S. dollar on international money markets is one manifestation of spending too much of tomorrow's earnings today.

The insular (protective) approach to economic policy may have many attractions, at least for the short term. Tariff, taxation and data protection policies and increased government regulation of industries could help to protect the country from major economic and social discontinuities caused by international economic imbalances. But given the nature of capital, corporations and the higher skilled labour force, emigration of productive resources would be a distinct possibility, avoidable only by adopting drastic measures of a kind usually restricted to countries behind the iron curtain.

It was mentioned before that the loss of employment opportunities caused by the ongoing automation of information-related activities ranks high on the scale of social concerns with Canadians. Given the continuation of existing technological and economic trends, it may become increasingly more difficult to avoid this negative social impact and other related problems such as growing public and international payment deficits. The development of an economic policy which takes into account the reality of the information age and is supported by a more enlightened public may become an urgent national necessity. Appropriate manpower development policies geared to the achievement of constant productivity gains in information-related activities should be viewed as an essential component of overall economic policy.

## 5.3. Buyer-seller conflicts

There is also a growing conflict between buyers and sellers, the consumers and merchandisers in the market place. Most of the conflicts to date are the result of poor or inadequate systems design, an issue already discussed above. Most of tomorrow's conflicts stem from other causes: violation of established rights to personal privacy, threats resulting from unfair information reporting, and conflicts arising out of ill-defined rights and privileges in a buyer/seller relationship.

Our economy is now geared to easy credit purchases and to market efficiency. Risk minimization is an important requirement for economic survival. Such an economy must necessarily have a transparent society as a counterpart. Easy access to personal information is an essential prerequisite for the functioning of the market place. Credit risks need careful evaluation to avoid excessive losses. Manufacturers and merchandisers require access to customer profiles, information on product preferences, buying and spending habits. Market efficiency demands end-user-directed advertising, electronic payments and credit transactions. The alternative spells inefficiencies, risks and losses, ultimately to be borne by the consumer in the form of higher prices.

If the 1972 survey is an appropriate reflection of public attitudes, there is considerable hope that the existing concerns of consumers can be alleviated with a minimum of government intervention. Few consumers take exception to the legitimate collection of data. The main concern of consumers lies in use, or more precisely, in the misuse of personal data. The indiscriminate use of personal data and the collection of personal data from third party sources is viewed by many as an invasion of personal privacy. The denial of legitimate access to stored personal data is viewed as an infringement of the right to fair information reporting. There is fear of potential use of electronic checkout facilities in supermarkets and retail stores to mislead consumers. The potential misuse of electronic payment information for third party commercial exploitation is another social concern.

These concerns imply a need for appropriate data protection policies and an update of the social and legal rights and privileges arising out of buyer/seller, employee/employer relationships. There is a need to define ownership rights to personal data. The existing imbalances related to the use of and access to personal data need to be redressed. Restrictions on third party uses of personal data and the use of personal identifiers (Social Insurance Numbers) for other than tax purposes will be required. Secrecy provisions may have to be imposed on personnel handling sensitive data. The laws governing unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data and the use of computer/communications facilities in criminal activities may need to be strengthened. Legal rights and privileges of both buyers and sellers need updating to fit the reality of the electronic age.

## 5.4. State-society conflicts

The maintenance of the division of powers between the different institutions of the state (Parliament, Government, Judiciary, as well as federal, provincial and municipal jurisdictions) and between the state and its citizens has been the cornerstone of our democratic society. The careless application of computer/communications may well result in the creation of major power imbalances, if:

\- the tools of information processing are available to one institution but not to others. For example, neither Parliament nor the Judiciary is making extensive use of information processing;

\- the organization of the information processing function does not reflect accurately the existing allocations of power, or if the control over data and programs is excessively centralized;

\- acquired rights and privileges of both state and citizens are violated in the performance of information processing activities. For example, the use of arrest data by provincial Crown insurance companies could be a violation of such rights. The disclosure of electronic payments, health care and banking information without appropriate search warrants could be viewed as another example of violating citizens rights.

Data protection in the public sector is an important, but also a very difficult task to be faced. The boundaries between legitimate access to information and unwarranted invasion of privacy are blurred. Information related to a parent-child relationship is privileged but society desires information to prevent child abuses. Society demands law and order in our streets but also insists on the preservation of the rights of the accused.

Computer/communications technology is neutral. It can be used for the benefit of society or to oppress society in the fashion described in Orwell's book "1984". Data protection policies cannot be developed in isolation. They must be founded on social policies which take into account the true needs of society and the realities of an information economy.

## 6. Options in public policy

In the previous sections we reviewed the actual and potential impacts of computer/communications uses within the triangle, technology, economy and society, in order to become more aware of their wider implications. We are not facing a few unrelated “black and white” issues, each resolvable at its own pace, but rather an array of interrelated, often conflicting socio-economic needs and wants. We also should be able to avoid making technology the “whipping boy” for our own inadequacies in coming to terms with the newly emerging social and economic order, even if the new order is not of our own making.

The question has been raised many times whether there is a need to do anything. Would the natural course of history not take care of itself? Doubtless this is a valid policy option provided we are willing to accept the consequences of growing confrontations in society and between competing societies, be they regional or national.

This section attempts to highlight the range of public policy options available to resolve the issues. The basic requirements of policy development and implementation are stated as a foundation for further discussion. Next, the two principal options – government legislation versus collaboration between private and public sectors – are discussed. Finally, the unresolved issues in data protection are identified.

## 6.1. Basic requirements

The defence against the misuse of data, programs and facilities is “data protection”. Essentially, this means that data, programs and facilities must be protected against socially undesirable use. Data protection insures the citizen and society against many possible undesirable effects that could be generated by the new technology. For this reason the term “data protection” should be viewed in its widest interpretation. It should not be confused with the term “data security”; data security protects the data, programs and facilities against accidental or deliberate misuse.

The basic requirements of data protection can be expressed as follows:

First, data protection measures must not restrict unnecessarily the efficiency, versatility and innovative capabilities of the public and private sectors in the fulfillment of their socio-economic mission. The welfare of citizens depends on the efficient performance of both sectors.

Second, the private and public sectors must be permitted to adapt their functional and organizational structures to the ongoing automation of information processing and information transfer activities.

Third, these two basic requirements must be reconcilable with social, legal and economic realities. The result of ongoing changes must satisfy constitutional and legal requirements, be politically desirable and should not harm citizens or the institutions of society.

## 6.2. The basic policy options

The application of computer/communications technology is changing society and the economy in a fundamental manner. This raises two basic questions:

"What kind of society do we want to see emerging?"

"What should be the future shape of our economy?"

In regard to the latter question, we see a need for a broad discussion of the basic options among government, industry and labour. What are the advantages and disadvantages, short and long term, of each economic policy option? What secondary measures will be needed in support of economic policy in such areas as tariff and taxation policy, data protection policy, education, training, retraining and manpower development?

In regard to the first question, we must begin to question the wisdom of calling for government intervention to reshape those social habits and customs we tend to disagree with. The trend in the United States, and to a much lesser degree in Canada, is to cure the real or imagined ills of society by means of the law book. One of the many manifestations of this trend is the growing amount of privacy and fair information reporting legislation in the United States on both the federal and state level. These legislative initiatives are often in reaction to boisterous pressure groups and yellow journalism, a trap which Canadian legislators so far have been able to avoid.

Almost eighty percent of the respondents to the IIC survey expressed the belief that the legislative approach is an inappropriate tool for solving social problems arising out of computer/communications uses. Only seven percent indicated support for a legislative approach. Some eighty-nine percent felt that a collective approach, based on the premise that information processing is a professional responsibility with technological, social and economic implications, would be more appropriate. Most see the role of government as providing leadership – in the identification of the issues, in fostering the emergence of non-governmental social institutions dedicated to a co-operative and collaborative solution approach, and in setting national objectives governing computer/communications uses.

In terms of solution approaches, almost eighty percent of all respondents would support the creation of a national data protection forum, working closely with local computer (or consumer) ombudsmen, as the social institution dedicated to the resolution of social conflicts. The same percentage would support the development of data protection guidelines as standards for acceptable social behaviour.

On July 11, 1977 the Parliament of Canada passed the Canadian Human Rights Act. Part IV of this Act provides the legislative framework for the protection of personal information under the control of the federal government. The Act provides for the appointment of a Privacy Commission with wide investigative powers and enables government to make regulations in the field of information processing, storage and transfer within federal jurisdiction.

A more comprehensive approach is taking shape in Europe. The German Data Protection Act, for example, calls for the establishment of a "Data Protection Commissioner" on the federal level, functioning both as an "ombudsman" on the federal level and as an advisor in data protection matters on the national level. The same Act calls for the establishment of State Supervisory Boards, taking into account the jurisdictional responsibilities of states (provinces) for social aspects. Furthermore, the Act requires the appointment of a Data Protection Officer with the power and responsibility of a corporate officer in each private enterprise employing a minimum of five people in automatic data processing.

## 6.3. The unresolved data protection issues

There is a need for more precise and more comprehensive definitions of the concept of “personal privacy” and its counterpart “the right of access to information”. Privacy is a most ill-defined concept. “Which part of the privacy sphere of every citizen must be protected against any intrusion by society”? In what situations has the citizen a right to refuse the provision of information? “What are the obligations of a judge, divorce court lawyer, law enforcement officer, tax collector, physician, personnel manager in preventing third-party access to personal information obtained in the exercise of their legitimate duties”? “Is it realistic to impose these obligations on people when the data are stored in computers”? “that is the scope of protection – data, programs, uses, or a combination of all three”?

A second need is to reach an agreement on the scope of data protection within the framework outlined above.

A third need is to come to terms with the scope of data protection within the public sector. Aspects of this matter include organizational measures to preserve secrecy within the hierarchy of government (what the social worker knows is of no concern to the mayor); the information rights of the state versus the citizen; and constraints on the flow of data across jurisdictional and administrative boundaries (disassociation of administration).

Finally, but perhaps most important, there is a need to define the scope, responsibility and accountability of the information processing “profession”. The process of occupational profile development should be brought to a successful conclusion. Changes to curriculum development in colleges and universities should be initiated. Training programs need to be revised. The formation of institutions supporting professional development should be encouraged. The sole alternative to professional responsibility is state legislation and regulation.

## 7. Conclusion

This discussion of the social implications of computer communications is at best an incomplete, first-cut attempt to come to terms with the social and economic complexities generated by the growing use of these new technologies. Much more work will be required. The challenges faced are the business of every citizen, either as consumer, employee, employer or policy maker. Whether we will have the courage to face the issues collectively is another matter.
