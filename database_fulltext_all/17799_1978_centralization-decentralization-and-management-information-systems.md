# Centralization, Decentralization and Management Information Systems

Phillip Ein-Dor and Eli Segev

Faculty of Management, The Leon Recanati Graduate School of Business Administration, Tel-Aviv University, Ramat-Aviv, Tel-Aviv, Israel

This paper suggests a format for conducting case studies on the issues of centralization and decentralization in the context of MIS. The problems are stated, evidence presented and questions are posed for which operational measures of the variables have been presented. It is hoped that many organizations will be motivated to perform such case analyses in order to generate a body of data on these issues.

Keywords: Centralization, decentralization, management information systems, MIS, system organization.

## 1. Introduction

Few concepts are used, misused, and confused as much as centralization and decentralization in the context of management information systems (MIS). The degree of centralization of a system is the degree to which one element or subsystem plays a major or dominant role in the operation of the system [8]. In regard to MIS, there are at least five different elements, the positions of these should be considered on the centralization/decentralization axis. These five elements are: (1) the degree of centralization of the organization in which the MIS is implemented, (2) the degree of centralization of MIS development and implementation efforts, (3) the degree of centralization of the MIS unit, (4) the degree of centralization of the computer hardware resource, and (5) the degree of centralization of data in the organization. The five elements of centralization are not mutually independent and are rarely differentiated with clarity.

It is the purpose of this note to define, operationalize, and discuss each of these elements prior to posing one or more questions on each element. These questions form a framework for case analysis of the effects of centralization and decentralization on the success of MIS. We hope that this presentation will stimulate organizations to describe and analyze their experience regarding the various elements of MIS centralization; the authors would be happy to receive and synthesize such case reports.

## 2. Centralization of the organization

Definition: The scope of strategic decisions within the competence of sub-units.

Operationalization: Number of product-market units; number of profit centers; number of divisions; number of groups.

Problem: The feasibility of constructing comprehensive MIS in decentralized organizations.

Evidence: Decentralization of the organizations in which MIS are implemented has been found as a source of inconsistency in data due to problems of synchronizing updates and reports [18]. The major problem is to ensure that systems built by different divisions can communicate with one another; if this is not done, the firm may find itself with a number of unconnected systems which cannot be aggregated or compared. This would imply that the organization can then no longer produce a consolidated picture of itself with consequent danger of loss of control.

A noted authority on the management of decentralized corporations states that information technology has been of little help in controlling decentralized companies [2]; there are, on the other hand, reports of successful MIS efforts in such companies [12,19].

Question: To what degree is your organization decentralized and what has been the effect of this on the development and success of MIS?

## 3. Centralization of the MIS development and implementation

Definition: The degree to which MIS plans and projects are formulated and executed centrally.

Operationalization: Number of independent development and implementation groups; percentage of systems implemented by each group.

Problem: The relationship between the degree of centralization of the organization and the degree of centralization of MIS development and implementation efforts.

Evidence: While calls have been made for more centralization of information systems planning [14,17] and other claim such centralization is impossible [2], the general attitude towards this issue seems to be highly pragmatic.

Roark notes that from the systems point of view, centralization is invariably called for, but political issues may militate in favor of decentralization [19]. He argues, therefore, that there is an optimal degree of centralization in any organizational context and offers indices of over and under centralization. This approach is supported by the finding that companies generally organize computer systems in conformance with their traditional plans of organization. Those that do no experience abnormal difficulties [5].

The tendency to configure systems in accordance with general corporate philosophy has led to the development of a “hybrid” organization with shared control. This is currently the most popular form as evidenced by a survey in which 32.5% of the organizations had highly centralized control of information systems, 20% were highly decentralized and 46.5% exhibited the hybrid form of organization [11].

Questions: To what extent does the organization of the MIS development unit parallel your overall organizational structure? What difficulties, if any, are caused by differences in the plans of organization?

## 4. Centralization of the MIS unit

There are two aspects to the centralization of MIS units - the internal organization of the unit and the degree of centralized control of the unit.

## 4.1. Internal organization

Definition: The degree to which resources of the MIS unit are controlled centrally or diffused to project teams.

Operationalization: Existence of project teams; number of project teams; number of hierarchical levels of projects.

Problem: The relationship between the organization of MIS units and success of MIS.

Evidence: There are two major forms of MIS unit organization -- functional and project team [15,21]. Under functional organization, personnel resources are organized by skill areas or functions, assigned to tasks, and controlled by central management of the unit. With project team organization, personnel are assigned to projects and then report to project leaders rather than to MIS unit management. Stuart reports an unsuccessful case under functional organization which was transformed by adopting the project team approach [21]. Gibson and Nolan have found sudden changes from one form of organization to the other [6]. A survey found both types of organization in companies with successful computer programs [15].

Questions: How is your MIS unit organized and how diffuse is control of resources within the unit? Have you found any connection between the organization of the MIS unit and its success?

## 4.2. Centralization of control

Definition: The degree to which decentralized MIS units are centrally controlled.

Operationalization: Existence of a corporate computer staff; size of the corporate staff group; level of interaction between the corporate staff and MIS units; existence of standards for MIS.

Problem: Corporate MIS strategy and consistency of levels of performance.

Evidence: It has been claimed both that a corporate staff is necessary to guide the systems effort [9], and that centralizing the control of a company's information system in a staff group creates insoluble problems and is therefore infeasible [2]. In practice, Garrity found that, among decentralized companies, corporate staffs played a stronger role in the successful companies than in those with only average performance [5]. Those capabilities which have been found to distinguish successful staff groups are superior technical knowledge and effectiveness in working with non-technical personnel.

Questions: Does your organization have a corporate computer staff? If so, how big is it, how does it operate and what is its impact on the success of MIS?

## 5. Centralization of the computer resource

Definition: The degree to which computer equipment is geographically concentrated.

Operationalization: Number of computer centers; mean and variance of investment in each center.

Problem: The effect of computer resource centralization on MIS success.

Evidence: The three main possibilities for distributing computing in a geographically dispersed organization are combinations of: (1) a large processor at company headquarters, (2) smaller processors at regional or plant locations, and (3) terminals connected to a central site and/or to regional computers [10]. There are long lists of advantages and disadvantages of each solution [6,10,12,17,19]. Distributed minicomputer networks are being proposed, and implemented, with increasing frequency as alternatives to large centralized systems [3,4,16].

While some authorities consider hardware centralization to be mandatory in the more advanced stages of MIS development (e.g. [6]), it appears that there is an optimal balance of advantages and disadvantages which determines the best level of equipment centralization. This leads to an observed tendency to regionalize rather than centralize data processing capacity; thus computer facilities are pooled in centers as large as are consistent with good service, regardless of where the system development is done [9,19]. A number of different combinations of computer resource and system development resource centralization have been discussed by Milutinovich and Kanter [17].

Garrity found no correlation between large scale centralization of business data processing and degree of success. Nor was the centralization of business, engineering and scientific applications on the same computers found to have any bearing on success [5].

Questions: How is the computer equipment distributed in your organization? On what basis are computer location decisions made? Does; the distribution chosen have any bearing on the success or failure of MIS?

## 6. Centralization of the corporate data base

Definition: The degree to which data are concentrated in data bases.

Operationalization: Number of data bases; percentage of data incorporated in data bases; mean and variance of proportion of data in each data base.

Problem: The contribution of data base centralization to MIS success.

Evidence: When data for each application are on separate files, updating problems result and different reports which should be identical often are not [13, 18]. The data base assists in keeping data current [20]. Redundancy is also minimized by use of a centralized data base [19] and lack of data element standardization as a reason for bad reports is reduced [1].

The concentration of data in a corporate data base permits the separation of data from specific applications and the sharing of data by different programs [17]. It thus answers (in part) the problem of decision models which generally do not have their own data bases, require collection of new data for each run, and consequently fall into disuse [20].

A strategic decision with respect to data base development concerns the question whether existing files should be built on and transformed into data bases or should continue undisturbed while the data base which will eventually replace them is built [10]. Nolan suggests the use of existing files while adding an ad hoc response capability and augmenting reliability and the ability to reconstruct lost data [18]. Grindlay and Cummer, on the other hand, believe that it is a mistake for systems designers to try to build an MIS on a data base designed for other purposes [7].

Questions: Does your organization have data bases? If so, how many and how centralized? If you have data bases, how were they constructed? Did the method of construction have any impact on the success of MIS using them?
