# Planning MIS Investment and Expense Levels

P.J. Dixon

Massey-Ferguson Ltd., 200 University Avenue, Toronto, Ontario M5H 3E4, Canada

Multinational Data Processing Equipment and Software Planning and Organisation present unique problems and opportunities. This paper describes how one multinational company managed to increase its world-wide data processing capacity while substantially decreasing its costs per unit of processing by imaginative use of the combination of centralized negotiations, treasury style cash flow analysis, and leveraged leasing applied on an international scale. The paper also touches on general management conditions, attitudes, and style which need to be present to make any Data Processing Investment effective.

Keywords: Centralized negotiations, EDP investment management, multivendor environment, leveraged leasing

Asking for capital for data processing and telecommunications equipment is not exactly popular with most Boards of Directors in most companies.

In 1975 the Corporate Management Systems Division of Massey-Ferguson submitted a formal capital request for some \$13 million to be spent in 1976 as part of a four year data processing equipment program which was expected to cost \$21 million. The Board approved the request which was, in the history of this company, the first request for data processing capital of any substance. Why did the Board accept the proposition?

Firstly, Massey-Ferguson's world-wide capacity needs for data processing facilities were analyzed against present loads by major application area and, perhaps more important, against the company's business systems development strategy as we could see it over the next 5 or 6 years.

The business systems development strategy itself was anchored in our understanding of company strategies in the supply, distribution, market planning, and financial planning and control areas, as we expected them to evolve between 1977 and 1982.

Our business systems strategies were (and continue to be) based on the following priorities:

1. Improvement in the process translating world-wide demand, by market, for our major powered products, into factory production programs by factory. The improvements being sought were: quality of forecasts, accuracy of operational data, speed of communication, and overall system reaction time to business environment changes.

2. Improvement is the process of translating our production plans into work-in-process; in other words, improvement of materials planning, and materials and work-in-process control world-wide.

3. Wholegoods pipeline planning and control systems world-wide.

4. Improvement in the accounting methods of operating companies, and in world-wide financial planning and control, financial analysis, and in the financial planning processes.

In all these areas the financial impact was either quantifiable or self-evident - self-evident to general management, that is, rather than to data processing technicians.

Next, we have established a world-wide inventory (register) of all data processing equipment, together with costs, method of acquisition, and unexpired portion of our commitment in respect of each leased or rented item.

Third, we have translated our business systems development plans into anticipated future workload for our data centres, in common capacity units.

Finally, we have translated this information into equipment configuration needs, by data centre, for the next six years. The requirements over the first three years were translated into schedules of equipment acquisition by specific units.

Then the real work started! What we had done to this point was equivalent to factory capacity planning based on future expected load with respect to products currently in production, as augmented by incremental capacity needed to support product upgrades, and new volumes from new product lines being planned.

Our data centres at that time were loaded to capacity and technologically ageing. We now proceeded to do our staff work; this involved estimating the capacity that we needed to implement our systems modernization program at a facilities cost the company could afford:

1. We priced our equipment requirements stream (based on our hardware and software strategy) over six years in several ways:

a) By taking our existing mixed equipment acquisition strategy, estimating the equivalent rental cost if all current equipment were so funded, and extending these figures to reflect our proposed equipment configurations.

b) Assuming all computing equipment was rented at current prices, and extending these prices to required configurations by data centre, by year.

c) Mixed central processors and peripherals from different suppliers; purchasing central processors; negotiating centrally all peripherals for a world-wide buy, then leasing back under a world-wide operating lease arrangement.

2. We then carried out series of financial analyses: a) Lease versus purchase analyses using the quasi financing method of evaluation.

b) A comparison of the future equipment payment stream, comparing centrally negotiated and leased method of acquisition, with the same equipment stream acquired by current mixed rental/lease methods.

c) A comparison of the future equipment payment stream, comparing centrally negotiated and leased method of acquisition with current equipment complement, acquired by current methods.

d) Comparison of the future equipment payment stream, comparing centrally negotiated and leased method of acquisition, with straight manufacturers rental.

All analyses were made over six years, and net benefits of the most favourable over least favourable method of acquisition were tested as follows:

1. Cumulative benefits were discounted at 10, 20, 30, and 40 % to give net present values under varying interest assumptions.

2. The company net return interest rate was calculated by discounting the cumulative benefits to zero for the project.

The key numbers arrived at were:

1. Central purchasing combined with operating leases resulted in over 200 % increase in data processing capacity at nc increase in cost.

2. Computer supplier straight rental for the planned equipment over 6 years was computed at: \$84 million.

3. Planned equipment at a combination of lease/ rental rates as paid in 1975 for the next 6 years was estimated at: \$53 million.

4. Central purchase of CPUs with leases of peripherals at negotiated prices, on an operating lease, for the next 6 years was computed to be:

\$26 million.

Net present value of the difference of 3 minus 4 at 20 %:

\$10 million.

The company net return varied from location to location from as high as 68.8% per annum to as low as 31.8% per annum.

As can be seen, approaching data processing equipment investment as a capital proposition based on the company's business strategy, can pay handsome returns, provided it is supported by:

1. An equipment and software evolution strategy;

2. Competent central purchasing negotiations;

3. Competent treasury style lease/purchase analyses and comparison of alternate cash flows.

The strategy outlined here was executed and the leases negotiated. Technically, the strategy will also position us (in 1981–82) to take maximum advantage of new data processing, telecommunications, and software technology, which we expect to be proven and available in the marketplace between 1979 and 1981.

Our operating leases give us sufficient flexibility to move faster -- if need be -- for cost effectiveness reasons.

Now, I would like to elaborate on aspects of data processing investment and expense levels, which cannot be readily measured or quantified, but which, to my mind, from a general management point of view, are at least as important.

A company's success and growth, along with those factors which the investment community uses in valuing its equity, are as much based on historical performance, perceived ability to innovate, approach to organization structuring, and (above all) style of management, as they are based on actual financial results at any point in time.

Information Management must support that approach, and that style, otherwise its management will become largely irrelevant to participating in the general management of the business – and the data processing strategy would also become largely irrelevant to the evolving general management strategy. Let us use a few examples to illustrate the point.

If the approach to driving a company forward is based on judgments wholly based on financial analyses there is not much point in emphasizing marketing support systems.

If the approach of general management to growth is through sustained growth of revenue and net income, systems strategy emphasizing asset control to optimize the balance sheet may not be welcome.

If the approach is to operate through decentralized subsidiaries, a systems strategy for integrated manufacturing control may be irrelevant, regardless of demonstrated profitability.

If personnel strategy leads to average management age in the upper fifties, it is useless to plan information systems expense levels, which can only be effectively utilized through widespread innovation and willing absorption of rapid rate of change.

If information systems and their use are not perceived as relevant to the evolution and deployment of the company's internal power structure, it is likely to be a waste of time trying to concentrate on decision support and planning and control systems, regardless of how well the company's reaction time can be improved.

Once an Information Management executive has attuned himself to the style and strategies of his chief executive, he will almost instinctively set basic guidelines for business systems and expense levels in the direction usable and absorbable by the chief executive officer, and by his colleagues. The rest becomes a financial planning refinement.

If, however, the company power structure is not understood;

If organizational strategies are not participated in; If style of management is not deferred to;

then no amount of logic, professional excellence, technological soundness and financial analysis can produce an information systems strategy relevant to the business at any investment and expense level.
