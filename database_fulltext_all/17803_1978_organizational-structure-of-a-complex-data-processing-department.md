# Organizational Structure of a Complex Data Processing Department

Niv Ahituv and Michael Hadass
Faculty of Management, Tel-Aviv University, Israel

The traditional organizational structures of D.P. departments do not generally suit modern requirements. A new structure is suggested, followed by a description of its functioning through a project development procedure. Conversion stages from a traditional structure to the suggested structure are described.

Keywords: Data processing units management, data processing projects management, organizational structures of D.P. departments, managing data base environments, project life-cycle.

## 1. Introduction

The organizational structure of a D.P. department is widely discussed in the professional literature. The traditional methods are summarized by Gildersleeve [5] and [6, pp. 63–67], and by Brandon [2, pp. 195–205] and [3]. The latter discusses briefly a future structure of a data base environment, a topic which is mentioned also by Davis [4, pp. 373–374]. This article attempts to present a conclusive discussion of the issue taking into account the large spectrum of problems to be solved by a modern D.P. department.

The main objective of a D.P. department is to supply computerized information services to its organization. This can be achieved by fulfilling three functions:

1. Systems development: analysis, design, programming, documentation, implementation and project management of new computerized information systems.

2. Systems maintenance: maintenance, modification and enhancement of existing systems.

3. Production: day entry, operating of mainframe and peripheral equipment, scheduling, job setup, clerical control, production control, supplies procurement, etc.

The accomplishment of these objectives needs the support of two additional functions:

1. Administration: coordination, budgetary planning and control, costing, personnel, secretarial services, etc.

2. Technical support: systems programming, data base administration, communications network control, hardware control, etc.

The D.P. department is structured into functional units according to its size and complexity. A primary partitioning of the department is usually done into two units: development and production. When it is justified by the needs of the department, a refining of this partitioning can be done up to five or more units, according to the functions mentioned before.

The systems development function is considered the most problematic one and we shall emphasize it in our discussion, without neglecting all the other functions.

We shall first briefly review the traditional approaches towards the organizational structure connected with the systems development function. The first approach may be called the applicational organization: every unit is intended to develop applications for a specific department of the organization. In this type of structure there are a number of units, such as finance, marketing, production, etc. Each unit is responsible for all the stages of its projects' 'life cycle' except for routine production. It consists of project managers, system analysts and designers, and programmers. The members of each unit have specialized knowledge of its departmental counterpart.

The advantage of this structure lies mainly in the specialization it creates. Its main disadvantage becomes evident when integrated data is needed for managerial purposes. This structure is normally found in the early days of the use of computers in an organization.

The second type of structure may be called the functional organization: every unit of the D.P. department consists of experts of various fields. There is a systems analysis unit, a systems design unit, a programming unit etc. A project is transferred from unit to unit according to its progress from phase to phase. Advantages of this type of structure are the specialization of people within their profession, and the standardization which is enforced on every project. The development process, however, takes longer time and is not controlled by a project coordinator. This approach is usually adopted by organizations with substantial experience in computer utilization.

The third approach is the matrix organization: it is based on the functional organization, but is adds project coordinators to main projects or groups of related projects. The coordinator is responsible for controlling the project development process and for representing the department vis-a-vis the end user.

This approach eliminates some disadvantages of the first two approaches, but it creates new problems of defining lines of command and may result in severe conflicts of priority.

## 2. What is a complex D.P. department?

A complex D.P. department has most of the following characteristics:

1. It deals with large and modern computer systems involving communication, data bases, complicated operating system, etc.

2. It consists of a large number of employees with different professions, such as systems analysts, programmers, clerks, key punchers, etc.

3. It supplies information services to a wide spectrum of users with different requirements.

4. It processes and produces a high volume of data.

5. It operates on large data bases, which serve many different application programs.

Obviously, the department must be economically viable, and conform to the common management practices of the enterprise. (A description of the relations between a D.P. department and its environment is given in Milutinovich and Kanter [7].)

## 3. The problem

The organizational structures already discussed do not answer all the needs of a complex D.P. department. There must be central control over the common data bases which requires specialists in advanced technology, but there must also be specialists in users' areas. There are different kinds of production needs, but there must also be central control over production scheduling and procedures.

It seems that the traditional approaches cannot provide a satisfactory solution to a complex D.P. environment. An applicational organization can cause difficulties in:

(a) integrating data from separate systems;

(b) analysing the benefits of projects according to the point of view of the whole organization rather than a single user;

(c) coordinating the production resources;

(d) implementing general standards and procedures; and

(e) balancing the workload among the units.

On the other hand, a functional organization can present:

(a) lack of knowledge of users' requirements;

(b) bureaucracy and inflexibility in project development; and

(c) complicated control project progress.

The matrix organization can avoid some of the difficulties which the other two approaches may create, but it leads to severe managerial problems in responsibility coordination.

## 4. The suggested organizational structure

Any solution to the above problem must be consistent with the following principles:

1. User oriented applications are developed by professionals who specialize in user's areas.

2. The users do not have to be aware of the different functions of the D.P. department. For each user, there is one person in the department who serves the needs and problems of that user.

3. Production is centrally managed.

4. The staff is divided into two categories:

a. specialists in sophisticated fields who provide technical support are managed by a highly skilled professional manager who is aware of the problems of managing 'superstars'.

b. administrative staff, who support the manager in knowledge and decisions improvement, report directly to the department manager.

## 5. Major components of the suggested structure

The D.P. department should contain five major components:

1. Managerial (administration, secretarial services, etc.).

2. Economic: preparing and controlling the yearly budget; cost accounting and pricing; supervising feasibility studies and cost/benefit analyses. This unit should report directly to the D.P. manager.

3. Production: operations, key punching, I/O control, libraries, etc.; employs industrial engineering team to plan and check the production activities.

4. Information Services: in charge of applications which serve a specific user or a group of related users; responsible for all phases of a project development, including maintenance and modification. Each unit employs systems analysts, systems designers and maintenance programmers, while application programmers are taken from the programmers' pool. Standards and procedures are coordinated by the chief systems analyst.

5. Internal Resources: managing all internal resources, involving computer utilization analysis, computer capability analysis, computer capacity planning, and the evaluation, planning, development and implementation of operating systems, other major software systems, and telecommunication and data communication equipment; managing data base utilization through the Data Base Administration (DBA) group which plans, designs, establishes standards and practices, controls data names, maintains the data base, enforces security and integrity controls, etc.; maintaining a software facility through the chief programmer in charge of programming standard and the application programmer pool.

## 6. Stages of project development

The concepts of the previous section are clarified by representing the role of each unit during the common life-cycle of a project. (See for example Blumental [1, pp. 111–112].).

1. Initiation stage: A new project is formally initiated by the user who produces a working paper which is transferred to its application group in the information services division.

2. Primary study: The application group analyses the working paper and transfers it to manager's preliminary decision.

3. Feasibility study: This is performed by an ad-hoc team consisting of representatives of the user, the application team and the economic unit, which is in charge for this stage.

4. Managerial decision and control: This phase is performed by the D.P. manager and the user who consult with the economic unit which approves budget and prices. The final “go/no go” decision is done by the user.

5. System analysis: This is the work of the application oriented group of the information services division; technical support is given by the internal resources division.

6. System design: the same as 5.

7. Programming: the same as 5, except that the assignment of programmers from the pool is ad hoc for every project.

8. Implementation: coordinated by the application oriented group, in cooperation with the user, the production division and the internal resources division; consists of system testing, conversion, training, documentation etc.

9. Production: performed by the production division; software maintenance and modifications are supervised by the application group, using its maintenance programmers.

10. Post-evaluation: performed by the economic unit.

It should be noted that quality control is performed by the chief systems analyst, the chief programmer and by the industrial engineering unit during the whole life-cycle according to well-defined standards and criteria. Such a procedure allows effective control over the project from both an economic and professional view point. The problem oriented group will develop the application by utilizing technology specialists of the internal resources division.

## 7. Conversion from traditional to the new structure

Two questions pertaining to the conversion into the new structure should be answered: When does an organization decide to introduce the change? and: How should this be done?

The conversion of the D.P. department from its traditional structure to the new one should be considered when the organization decides to install a data-base management system (DBMS) and/or a communications network, provided that the department has most of the other characteristics which define a complex D.P. department.

The conversion to the new structure should be evolutionary. The first stage towards conversion is to establish two teams:

1. Procedures team: This team will review the existing systems and establish standards and procedures for development and production activities. The team will compile a manual of procedures, containing documentation standards, timing, management practices, file organization, etc. At a later stage, the team will conduct the modifications of the existing systems according to the new rules. The main task of such a team is to convert the D.P. department from its 'enterpreneurial' behavior to more formal methods of working.

Subsequently the members of the team will assume the positions of industrial engineers in the Production Division, chief programmer in the Internal Resources Division, chief systems analyst in the Information Service Division, and members of the Economic Unit. 2. Data base and/or communication team: This team will conduct the feasibility study for installing the new technology of data base and/or communications network. The members of the team will later take over the functions of data base administrator, systems programmers, and hardware coordinator. When the second team has completed its work, the new structure may be adopted. Allocation of employees will be made according to their skills.

## 8. Conclusions

The paper proposes one solution to the problems of current DP departmental structures. The proposed organisational structure is based on the concept of employing highly skilled people specialized in advanced technology, while not neglecting the specific requirements of every user. The cooperation of these groups is achieved by implementing formal procedures and checkpoints.

The proposed structure lacks none of the disadvantages of the matrix approach and is much better defined in terms of responsibilities, lines of command, and relationship between user and D.P. department. It provides the manager with effective tools to run and control the department.
