# Information for Strategic Planning: An Analysis\*

William R. King

Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Criteria for the assessment of the practical utility of strategic information are developed and applied to a variety of strategic information systems. The systems which are explicitly evaluated are real-world ones with which the author has been associated, but the evaluations are held to be generally applicable to the broader classes of systems which each represents. Few strategic information systems are found to meet the criteria, and it is concluded that the area of strategic planning needs much development of both computerized and non-computerized systems of informational support.

Keywords: Strategic planning, strategic information, information system evaluation.

William R. King is Professor of Business Administration at the Graduate School of Business of the University of Pittsburgh and a principal in the management consulting firm of Cleland-King, Inc. He is the author of eight books and more than 60 technical papers in the fields of planning, information systems and management science. Currently, Dr. King's research is focusing on the development and evaluation of planning information systems.

## 1. Introduction

The need for better informational support for strategic decision making is becoming more critical as more firms develop more sophisticated planning systems. The heavy and active participation of business firms in such activities as The PIMS System – which provides users with comparative evaluations of their performance relative to that of other firms – is evidence of this growing need for, and awareness of, strategic information [1].

Survey studies also depict an increasing awareness on the part of business managers of their need for systematic technological, competitive, and regulatory information which has in the past not been available through formal information systems [2].

This growing need is for truly strategic information rather than just more information. Most companies generate studies and reports as informational backup for strategic planning. Yet, the state of the art, as applied to strategy and planning, is well characterized by a plethora of data and a lack of critical relevant information [3].

## 2. Criteria for strategic information

Some firms that have tried to develop information systems for support of strategic decision making have encountered difficulty in assessing the relevance of the wide variety of data which might be useful in the determination of business strategy. The strategy concept is so encompassing that almost any business-related data can be argued to be potentially relevant to the support of strategic decision making.

For a strategic information system to be cost-effective, it must be developed according to specified criteria which ensure its relevance and usefulness. There are three such criteria which have been applied in a number of firms which have successfully developed such systems.

The first criterion for strategic information involves the recognition that much of it will emanate from new sources—often external to the firm. This implies that environmental information must be collected and processed in ways to render it into a form which is suitable for the support of strategic choice. For instance, volumes of papers describing emerging technologies or stacks of competitive annual reports are not suitable for direct use in the planning process, except to answer occasional specific questions. As far as guidance for strategy formulation, data in such raw form are almost useless.

The second criterion for relevance is inherent in the difference between data and information. Information is evaluated data. Of particular concern in strategic planning is that the data be evaluated for some specific strategic purpose or use [4]. Too often, the information developed for the support of strategic planning is so unfocused as to be of little use. Illustrative of this is the competitive data system developed by a large business equipment firm which compiled and disseminated one paragraph intelligence statements using inputs provided by sales and technical personnel. These intelligence assessments were prepared after visits to client firms and contacts with competitive personnel. Items in the data base were retrievable only through a dump of all items related to a particular competitor or a specific time period, or both. A system audit showed that the system was not utilized to the degree expected when its development was undertaken, that it was not integrated into any decision-making process, and that the lack of evaluation and assessment of input led to a great likelihood of erroneous conclusions being drawn by managers on the basis of the information supplied by the system. As a result, the system was deactivated.

The third criterion for relevance is the degree to which the information is collected, analyzed and disseminated in a fashion that is integrated with the decision-making processes of the organization. This generally means that the use of information will be integrated into the annual planning cycle in identifiable ways that influence strategy. However, strategic comparative information is important to the process of strategy implementation and control; i.e., ensuring that plans are carried out and produce the desired effect. Many companies have strategic information "systems" that failed because they do not meet this criterion. Often, these systems are developed on the basis of a "data bank" mentality which says, "Let's collect all of the data that we can concerning competitors, or technology, (or whatever area is currently faddish). This will obviously be of great use to us in formulating our plans." The results are usually voluminous, unmanageable, and unelifying since the data have little real value.

Many firms have found, to their dismay, that unless information systems and processes rate well by these three criteria, they are not worth the effort required for their development. It is the purpose of this paper to illustrate a variety of operational strategic information processes which do rate well in terms of these criteria and by the evaluations made by their manager-users. In doing this, we demonstrate the feasibility of strategic information systems which can produce something other than the ad hoc “special study” which is currently the primary element of most strategic information activities.

## 3. Non-computerized strategic information systems

When the term “information system” is used, one naturally thinks first of computerized systems. The three criteria for strategic information do not imply the need for computerization. To emphasize this, we begin by considering non-computerized systems.

## 3.1. Assumption-generating strategic information processes

While many firms use computer models to develop forecasts, predictions generated by simple and more familiar processes are easier for managers to comprehend. They are therefore more likely to be used for strategic decision-making. Such forecasts are really assumptions which are used for more sophisticated forecasts.

For example, one construction-industry supplier routinely performs analyses of its cost structure and develops simple five-year projections based on known, or extremely likely, changes from current circumstances. The forecasts take account of recent trends in materials costs and uncontrollable wage increases dictated by union contracts, cost-of-living escalators, anticipated statutory and contracted retirement contributory requirements, and a variety of other “hard” bases for projecting the future. The aim is to analyze those cost changes on the basis of current “knowns”, rather than to assess the future using statistical or other formal methods.

The results of this simple routinely-produced analysis are often illuminating; e.g., in a memo to division controllers explaining the results of one of these analyses the corporate controller said, "I am sure that you and your general manager will be shocked, as we were, with the financial impact of these factors." His reference was to pension costs which were known to be escalating over 100% because of changes in laws and contracts, liability insurance costs escalating at 15–20%, and mandated fringe benefit increases on the order of 40%, as well as to other "certain" cost escalations of lesser magnitude.

## 3.2. Image information systems

One of the most neglected varieties of strategic information is that which reflects the attitudes of the clientel (customers, suppliers, employees, and stockholders). Yet, this variety of information may be as important to the firm in defining its current situation (the first step in a strategic planning) as data on sales, profits, market shares and other status descriptors.

Experience suggests that there are great discrepancies between a firm's image of itself and the image held by its customers. Often these discrepancies are not very significant in the firm's current operations but they can be critical in terms of their impact in the future. For instance, the firm that feels it is technically superior in an era when cost is becoming more significant, may find that its image of being "high priced" is more important to its future success than its presumed quality image.

A firm's image may be assessed in two general areas: product image (price, quality, reliability, etc.) and organizational image (quality of personnel, responsiveness, integrity, etc.). Industrial products manufacturers have used image surveys which involve both structured and unstructured interviews of key customer personnel. A questionnaire which serves to guide for these interviews can be developed and tested within the organization. Such in-house testing can be used as a basis to define the important "dimensions" of product and organizational image. In one such survey conducted by the author and his associates for a large aerospace contractor [5], the interviews included the following product and organizational image categories:

General characteristics,

personnel image,

ability to communicate with customers,

project management skills and capabilities,

ability to meet normal customer requirements,

responsiveness to customer's special requirements, negotiating skills,

special capabilities, and

product characteristics.

The results of the image survey were sufficient to suggest that the firm had potential problems with the degree to which top management was interacting with customers and the freedom given to project managers in selecting personnel who interfaced with the customers.

Overall, the survey developed an image that was surprising to the executives. It depicted an honest and technically competent organization that lacked marketing aggressiveness. This was reflected in the customer's perceptions of virtually all aspects of customer contact. The survey pointed out the failure to communicate key personnel changes to customers and deficiencies in technical proposals. The firm's products were rated high in terms of operating characteristics (performance, reliability, and ease of maintenance) but low in their management of technical product development effort within stated costs and schedules.

The same image survey was also conducted within the firm. The contrast between customer perceptions and internal perceptions led management to take a number of specific actions designed for short-run impact on the image and incorporation of image considerations into the strategic planning activities. This led, for the first time, to specific concern with the image that the company wished to project and discussion of the strategies that it could take to reach this goal.

The economic impracticality of continuing large-scale surveys led the firm to integrate continuing image-monitoring activities into other information subsystems where image-related surrogate measures (such as customer complaints) were tracked and assessed; the overall image survey is to be updated at two-year intervals.

This situation illustrates how subjective judgmental data can be incorporated into the planning process to complement objective information. Moreover, the collection, analysis, and dissemination of such information can be performed systematically and routinely.

## 3.3. Strategic data bases

Virtually all textbook descriptions of strategic planning suggest the important roles to be played by strength-and-weakness assessments, competitive capabilities, and assessments of environmental opportunities and risks. However, few go so far as to develop operational procedures for generating such information. Moreover, none do so in a fashion which meets our criteria for strategic information

One process, developed by the author in collaboration with a colleague [6], uses the concept of a “strategic data base” to implement these criteria for important varieties of environmental information. Strategic data bases are concise statements of the most significant strategic items related to various clientel groups or environments which affect the organization’s strategic choices. As such, they are the mechanisms through which the current situation and future opportunities are assessed. They reflect the influence of various forces (competitors, top management, the business and industry in which the organization operates and the organization itself) on the strategic options that are available.

The evaluation of the large quantities of raw data in these diverse areas can be performed by task forces, or teams representing diverse interests. In this way, the organization can be assured that the evaluation does not represent only one point of view of only the parochial viewpoint of analysts.

These teams of managers, supported by assigned staff, are to draw conclusions in terms of from 10 to 15 of the most important factors affecting the future of the organization in a specified area. In fact, developing a 20-page list of strengths and weaknesses is relatively easily, but reducing this to the 10–15 most significant involves significant evaluation, analysis, and negotiation.

Table 1 shows a strength and weakness strategic data base similar to that prepared by a defense contracting firm. It shows the most important company strengths and weaknesses which a task force recommended as the basis for future corporate strategy.

It is useful to contrast the way that a task force develops strategic informational inputs with the process that is more commonly used to provide these informational inputs. The common approach relies on staff analysts, who gather data and prepare documents which are to serve as background information to support planning activities and choices. Because the planners and analysts often have neither the managerial expertise nor the authority to make significant choices, the output of such an exercise is often a voluminous document which “leaves nothing out”. Thus, managers are provided with a plethora of unevaluated data rather than strategic information.

In contrast, the managerial task force forces managers who represent diverse interest groups to gather and evaluate data in each of a number of areas and to choose (through the consensual process) those that are most important.

Thus, the strategic data bases meet the strategic information criteria: they represent useful information rather than unevaluated data and there is a good likelihood that information which has been widely regarded as most significant will be used in developing the firm's strategic plans.

4. Computerized internal strategic information systems

Computerized internal systems are those developed and operated within the organization. Subsequently, we consider externally developed and operated systems.

## 4.1. Strategic model-oriented information systems

Strategic information systems which purport to answer "What if?", questions are much discussed in the literature [7] but there is ample evidence to suggest that such systems do not usually meet our criteria [8].

One system, developed by the author and his associates, will be used both to illustrate the practical uses of such “model-oriented” systems and the way in which they may be designed to meet the strategic information criteria. The system is shown schematically in Fig. 1 as involving a number of inter-related modules. These modules were sequentially developed in conjunction with a strategic planning process in a manufacturing firm specializing in electrical equipment for installation in new and renovated buildings. This “business model”-based information system has, as its core, a “profit module” which takes the interrelated projections of the other modules and makes profit, return on investment and revenue projections that are displayed as output in the form of typical operating statements.

Three submodels make up the marketing-oriented module. The first depicts sales and service relationships in the business, which involves both the sale of capital equipment and the subsequent sale of parts, service, and service contracts. This sales-service module utilizes historical patterns to project the distribution of future revenues to be anticipated because of current sales. It also uses inputs from the market and competitive modules to project future service revenues to be expected on competitor's products.

The second submodel, the competitive model, uses past and projected competitive practices and patterns in the industry to predict how the total market will be divided among the participants.

The other modules are used to analyze specific strategies—e.g., the capital investment model is used to project the profit consequences of a proposed production process investment intended to reduce production cost. The outputs of these models are fed into the profit model through other submodels.

In the firm's planning process, the model-oriented information system was used for three purposes:

(1) The testing of proposed strategies: "What if?", questions.

(1) What would happen if we went out of the X segment of our business over the next two years and began producing redisigned products for the Y market as soon as possible?

(2) How important is the combined effect of our proposed improved warranty and the anticipated improvement in our parts and service sales generating capacity?

The system presents answers for each of these questions in terms of financial projections of revenues, profits, and ROI on a year-by-year basis in both actual dollars and present dollars (corrected for inflation). These projections are made for various business lines for both domestic and international markets.

A base case representing a projection of a “surprise free” or “business as usual” situation was prepared in each instance. Each projected consequence was then compared with this base case. Such comparisons facilitate the use of “what if?”-strategy questions to evaluate proposed strategies, and even if a manager is unwilling to use the model as the final basis for choice, projections often delineate poor strategies (saving further effort) and highlight potentially good strategies for further analysis.

Sensitivity questions show what factors are important and unimportant. This is, in fact, one of the prime values of business models: other analyses seldom isolate a single factor or sets of factors, in terms of their relative importance.

One use of this model was particularly revealing. A corporate study group presented a report, to top management which resulted in a morning-long discussion concerning three strategy elements—production cost improvements, material utilization and the potential sale of a production facility. At lunch, the chief executive said, "Let's see what the model says!" Various runs were made with the result that all three strategy elements were shown to have relatively little overall impact on the firm's future. Thus, the model was able to show the managers that the real question was not the validity of their proposed strategy elements, but the impact that they were likely to have.

The third use of the model is in forecasting. In the company, forecasting provided insights which resulted in the development of a regional strategy for the company's marketing effort. Such a strategy was never previously contemplated, but differential construction activity was forecast for various construction segments in different parts of the country and this, coupled with marketing and distribution cost analyses, showed that a uniform nationwide strategy would be clearly inferior to one which took advantage of low distribution costs for some products in some areas.

The system is not very different from systems developed in other firms. However, the process used to develop the system was designed to address the issues of the three basic strategic information systems criteria. The business model, the heart of the system, was developed from issues raised in the first annual cycle of a task force planning effort. The task force, as it sought to develop a strategic plan, identified 'the need for critical market, competitive, and financial information from the external environment. This identification of a specific need for environmental data resulted in the collection of data such as product "end-use" information compiled through a telephone sample survey. Then, the data were incorporated into the Market Module to depict the segmentation of the market. This facilitated the incorporation of market forecasting into the Market Module using Dodge Construction Forecasts as a takeoff [9].

Competitive issues which the task force identified as necessary for the planning process led to the development of a Competitive Module which used the judgment of participants in assessing the likelihood and nature of competitive responses and counter-strategies.

The model-development process contributed directly to the development of new environmental information sources, because various kinds of environmental data were found to be necessary for strategic decisions. The strategic information system was then developed, but because the need for every element of the model emanated from an ongoing planning process, the integration of the model into the decision-making process was naturally achieved.

## 4.2. "Strategic question" information systems

Another internal computerized system currently under development by the author is a so-called “strategic question” competitive information system. The ways in which such a system can meet the strategic information criteria are best illustrated by contrasting it with information retrieval systems which are often developed in the competitive context.

A pure retrieval system is one which permits the user to request any item or combination of items in the data base: for this, the user must know in advance exactly what is wanted and how to ask for it. But strategic managerial needs are not so clean cut; thus such systems seldom measure up to one critical criterion.

A strategic question competitive information system has a data base which is structured in terms of strategic business issues. This permits inquiries to be made in terms of the use to which data are to be put, rather than in terms of the data. Thus, a manager using such a system asks questions such as, "What is the capability of Competitor X to introduce a new product in Line Y next year?". The system then presents responses which successively identify subclasses of data in which the manager may be interested; e.g., such a query could produce a response that would identify Competitor X's financial capability, production capability, marketing capability, and technological capability. The user could then identify specific areas of interest, or ask for all information related to the initial question. A user indication of interest in marketing capability would then produce a system response indicating the availability of data on competitive distribution channel capacity, field sales capability, service capability, technical sales expertise and a variety of other marketing-related areas. The system would also indicate its ability to provide projections of future market growth. Indication of interest in other areas would produce similar system responses which would, after each user response, indicate successively-more-detailed sets of available data.

Of course, such a system is limited to strategic issues and questions which have been preprogrammed.

However, if the system is designed on the basis of strategic issues and questions identified by user-managers as those most urgently needed to support their strategic choices, the system is likely to be effective, and therefore used. Such a system meets the strategic information criteria in ways that a pure retrieval system cannot.

## 5. Computerized external strategic information systems

Commercially available information system services are not normally strategic in nature. However, if these systems are effectively integrated into a planning process, they can meet the strategic information criteria.

## 5.1. PIMS as a strategic planning information system

The PIMS (Profit Impact of Market Strategies) system, as described by Schoffler et al. [10], has been used by many firms to identify areas of strategic deficiency and to suggest strategies based on the strong and weak points of the business as identified by PIMS. PIMS consists of a large structured data base to which many firms have contributed data on over 750 businesses. A multi-variate regression model is used to compare businesses in terms of a variety of criteria as they relate to ROI. The criteria include market share, total marketing expenditures, product quality, R & D expenditures, investment intensity, and the degree of vertical integration.

The PIMS system provides a par ROI—”... an estimate of the pre-tax return on investment (ROI) that in 1971–73 was normal for businesses facing market and industry conditions equivalent to those of your business and occupying a similar market position” [11]. It also identifies the “chief factors acting to increase ‘par’ ROI” and the “chief factors acting as a drag on ‘par’ ROI.” The PIMS output gives numerical estimates of the effect of each of the factors of the ROI equation and the sensitivity of par ROI estimates to changes in each factor.

One firm, a major food processor, used the PIMS drag factors to suggest strategies in a planning process. These strategies were then evaluated using PIMS data, and other models and forecasting tools which were available. The process served to distinguish those businesses which could reasonably be managed as cash cows (or milked of their cash flow) and those that could be managed for growth. It also pointed out particular problem areas which, if rectified, could have positive impact on ROI in each of the business areas.

## 5.2. Commercial retrieval systems in strategic planning

While most commercial information retrieval systems do not match our strategic information criteria, it is possible to utilize such systems in a manner that effectively supports strategic planning. For instance, one producer of sophisticated electronics systems used a system similar to that of the Lockheed Corporation and Systems Development Corporation to identify the technical publication history of the engineering-trained managers who had recently become prominent in a competitive firm. The identification of the technical accomplishments allowed the firm to infer its competitor's technical product strategy, which led to an effective and successful counter strategy. The firm has now instituted an ongoing competitive intelligence function, drawing on clipping services and other commercial information sources, to maintain competitive profiles for strategic planning.

This use of commercial retrieval systems meets the strategic information criteria because it involves new environmental information needed and used as a part of the planning process.

## 6. Summary

While strategic information systems are not widely regarded, there are some such systems which have proved to be useful in supporting strategic decision making. The key to their successful development is that they be designed for specific strategic managerial purposes rather than to fulfill the wishful thinking of analysts. Some firms that have focused their systems development efforts on specific strategic purposes have been able to provide important support to that most critical area of managerial activity—the determination of the firm's overall strategy.
