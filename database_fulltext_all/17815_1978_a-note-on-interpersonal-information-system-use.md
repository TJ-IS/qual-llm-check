# A Note on Interpersonal Information System Use\*

E. Burton Swanson

Graduate School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles, California 90024, USA

Two major design alternatives for the use of a management information system (MIS) have been advanced in the literature to date. The first calls for the direct interaction of the manager with the computer-based system by means of a remote terminal, i.e., for the personal use of the system. The second calls for the employment of an intermediary, or "chauffeur", i.e., for a special form of interpersonal use of the system. Results from the case study of one MIS suggests that both of these alternatives may be inadequate as a design basis. An adaptive interpersonal use model is advanced as a new alternative, and the concepts of self-use, other-provision, and other-use are suggested for the description of various usage roles which may be assumed by the individual within the organization.

Keywords: Management information systems, MIS, personal use, interpersonal use, chauffeur-driven systems.

## 1. Introduction

A popular design concept for a computer-based management information system (MIS) is one in which managers have ready access to remote terminals permitting on-line inquiry into the organizational data base. The use of security codes defining selective access to portions of the data base is commonly thought to provide an MIS based upon personal use in such situations. Each manager receives access to data tailored specifically to his or her own decision making needs, and in the privacy of his or her own office, the manager manipulates the terminal to extract the information required, given the problem situations which arise.

The personal use model is portrayed in Fig. 1. Its hallmark is the direct interaction of the manager with the computer-based system by means of a remote terminal. As a design approach, it has been much advocated by proponents of computer and communications technologies. (See, for example, Miller [4].)

Fig. 1. The personal use model. Each manager (M) interacts directly with the computer-based system.

Where the personal use MIS model is employed, design emphasis is understandably placed upon improving the “man-machine interface”, especially in terms of making terminals and computer-based languages easy to use by managers. (Alter [1]) Characteristic of this emphasis is the conclusion reached by Montgomery and Urban in their discussion of interactive marketing decision-information systems: “... the manager-system interface is critical ... since its effectiveness in large measure will determine the usage level of the system.” [5: p. 233]

Recently, however, the personal use model has come under strong criticism. From his study of 56 different systems of the interactive type, Alter finds that "most interactive decision support systems simply don't do anything which would entice an executive to use them at all." [1: p. 11] Taking issue with the belief that direct use of computer-based systems by managers ought to be encouraged, he concludes finally that, "the goal of developing better man-machine interfaces in order to entice direct usage by busy managers may be entirely inappropriate." [1: p. 12]

![](/api/attachments/NT6CR2CB/fulltext/images/740d8267e752c8762fa58460cf9c8617ca5540e236c7fe6c7b3cbee102ec2f22.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The chauffeur-driven use model. Each manager (M) interacts indirectly with the computer-based system, by means of an intermediary (I).

Keen [3], in an articulate critique based in part upon Alter's studies, suggests that managers might be better served by interacting with technically skilled and managerially trained intermediaries in the use of computer-based systems: "the unwillingness of executives to use a computer terminal is legendary." [3·p. 4]

An alternative to the personal use model is thus what Keen terms the chauffeur-driven system, which may be portrayed as in Fig. 2. Its essential feature is the reliance of the manager on the expertise of the intermediary in the use of the computer-based system.

The chauffeur-driven system represents one example of what we will term here the interpersonal use of information systems. Interpersonal use is understood to differ from personal use in that use of the system takes place in part directly between persons, rather than solely by direct interaction of each person with the computer-based system. In the case of the chauffeur-driven model, the manager's use of the system is strictly interpersonal, i.e., the manager makes no direct use of the computer-based system. In Keen's words: "We hide the computer. The manager who works through the intermediary need not know if the 'system' is a library of (computerized) routines or a roomful of clerical slaves." [3: p. 14]

In summary, the previously-described personal use model and the chauffeur-driven use model represent the two major design alternatives for MIS usage which have been advanced in the literature to date. The purpose of the present paper is to present research results which suggest that both may be inadequate, and to offer a new model of system use in their stead.

In an earlier report by this author of user involvement with and appreciation of one terminal-based MIS [7], it was observed that user involvement assumed important and unique interpersonal forms. In particular, it was observed than an individual's involvement with the MIS was typically based in attempts to influence others in the organization. Some of the flavor of this influence-motivated involvement was further conveyed in a sketch of the development and use of the system studied [6]. However, no numerically-based analysis of interpersonal user involvement was reported. The present paper extends the results previously reported, concentrating on the nature of the interpersonal use of the information system.

To provide the context for the results, the study setting will first be briefly described.

## 2. The study setting

SQC Engineering is a department consisting of more than 200 employees of a large international manufacturer of complex electronic equipment. The department, referred to as a “group” within the formal organizational structure, is responsible for the production quality control of a manufacturing plant employing several thousand individuals. The Group itself consists of three “second-level” departments, each of which consists, in turn, of four or five first-level departments. The personnel of the Group are primarily engineers and technicians, supplemented by various forms of clerical support.

SQC Engineering employs an internally-developed "Activity Reporting Information System" (known as "ARIS") as a vehicle for self-management. The ARIS system gathers data on the planned and actual work activity of Group members, and makes it available to management on a "need to know" basis. Each department manager has access to the data within his or her formally defined responsibility; that is, access is limited to the departments and personnel reporting to the specific manager. In addition, certain engineers, termed "product managers" have access to work activity data pertaining to their product responsibilities. These data often cut across formal organizational lines.

ARIS makes two data files available for management inquiry from remote terminals. The first, the Activity Status File, contains work activity data from the most recent 13 weeks, at a level of detail which includes activity records of individual Group members. The second, the Activity History File, derived from the first, maintains an 18-month summary of work activity at the department level. For inquiry purposes, a query language is employed which allows the user to specify the format and content of ARIS reports according to individual need.

Inquiry into the ARIS data base takes from terminals scattered throughout the plant. Security provisions restrict inquiry privileges to those defined to have the "need to know." A prospective inquirer must supply the system with his or her security code.

This code allows the inquirer to access the ARIS files according to the desired restrictions. During the course of the study, 46 individuals had personal access to the ARIS data base.

## 3. Interpersonal system use

As one part of the study of ARIS, copies of a questionnaire were distributed to its users. $^{1}$ The principal use of the questionnaire was for the measurement of the user's involvement in the design and development of the system, and of the user's appreciation of the system. This is discussed in some detail in Swanson [7]. Several ancillary questions were also asked in the questionnaire, and it is on the basis of one of these, that the present analysis is principally drawn. Thirty-nine (39) responses were received, and thirty-five (35) were complete enough to be used in the present analysis.

Central to this analysis are the users' answers to the following five-part question:

How do you make use of ARIS?

1. I make inquiries for my own decision-making purposes, using the terminal(s) myself. Yes or No.

2. I make inquiries to produce reports for my management. Yes or no.

3. I make inquiries to produce reports for persons other than myself or my management. Yes or no.

4. My subordinates make inquiries to produce reports for me. Yes or no.

5. Persons other than my subordinates or myself make inquiries to produce reports for me. Yes or no.

The answers to this question permit a cursory portrayal of the pattern of personal and interpersonal ARIS use. (Specifically, an answer of "yes" to Part 1 of the question indicates personal use of the system; an answer of "yes" to any subsequent part(s) of the question indicates a form of interpersonal use.) The pattern of ARIS use found was validated insolar as possible by cross-checking the consistency of responses between individuals, by interviews, and by the record of use maintained automatically by the computer-based system.

ARIS users were classified according to the characterization scheme suggested, and the relationships among self-use, other-provision, and other-use were examined by means of a contingency table analysis. One significant relationship emerges, as indicated in Table 1: self-use is positively associated with other-provision. It may be interpreted as follows: if one uses the terminal, one tends to use it both to inform oneself and to inform others in the organization.

No significant relationship between other-use and self-use or other-provision was found. Thus, whether one uses the terminal or not offers no basis for predicting whether one will receive ARIS reports produced by others. This is especially noteworthy. Self-use and other-use do not emerge as mutually exclusive modes of system use. Managers do not choose between personal use of the system (as suggested by the personal use model) and use of the system by means of an intermediary (as suggested by the chauffeur-driven use model). Rather, managers may be active in both roles.

In the case of ARIS, it is seen that neither the personal use model nor the chauffeur-driven use model is adequate as a basis for the description of system use.

Recalling that ARIS users are of two types in terms of managerial responsibility, department managers and "product managers," one is inclined to conjecture that usage patterns might vary accordingly. The relationship between managerial responsibility and self-use, other-provision, and other-use was thus examined by means of another contingency table analysis. Interestingly, one significant relationship is found, as indicated in Table 2: other-use is significantly and positively associated with being a department manager in SQC Engineering. An interpretation of this finding is thus needed.

Note that department managers do not choose only to be other-users, i.e., avoid using the terminals themselves. No significant relationship between being between a department manager and self-use or other-provision was found.

A breakdown of other-use by department managers in terms of subordinate-source and other-source (reflecting question items 4 and 5, respectively) indicated that 15 of 16 other-users receive reports produced by subordinates. A lesser number, 10, receive reports from other sources (including, in three cases, based on comments, their own managers).

Again, neither the personal use model nor the chauffeur-driven use model is rich enough to account for the patterns of usage reflected in this data.

An interesting question is the extent to which department managers as other-users initiated the production of the reports they received, as opposed to being simply the recipients of reports initiated by others. In the first case, the motivation for the inquiry would be more truly managerial, while in the second, managerial involvement would be, in a sense, more passive. Instances of both cases were found in interviews; however, no statistically-based conclusions can be drawn. Whichever interpretation is favored, ARIS is seen to have successfully “penetrated” the management hierarchy of SQC Engineering in terms of other-usage. Overall, the pattern is consistent with previous research findings that written messages tend to be pushed upward through an organization’s formal channels of communication (Guetzkow [2]).

Again, that other-use is pervasive within the SQC Engineering management would seem to underscore the necessity of studying the use of an MIS from an interpersonal perspective.

Several other questions relative to forms of system use were addressed, based upon data obtained by means of the questionnaire. First, it was hypothesized that self-use might be explained in part by the amount of classroom training in ARIS received, or by prior programming experience. The results of this analysis are not shown to conserve space. No statistically significant relationships were found. Thus an explanation for self-use remains elusive.

In particular, self-users and other-providers are more likely to be appreciative than are those who are other-users only.

## 4. Conclusion

In terms of this single study, our conclusions may be summarized as follows:

(i) A substantial variety exists among forms of individual use of the information system.

(ii) Self-use is significantly and positively associated with other-provision. Those who make personal use of the information system tend also to make interpersonal use of it, in terms of providing reports for others.

(iii) The department manager role is significantly and positively associated with other-use. Other-use is thus the means by which the information system penetrates the management hierarchy.

(iv) Self-use is not significantly associated with the amount of classroom training received or with prior programming experience.

(v) Forms of individual use are significantly associated with system appreciation. Self-users and other-providers are more likely to be appreciative than those who are other-users only.

Strictly speaking, no generalization is warranted from this sample of one information system. Rather the conclusions should be treated as as hypotheses generated for further testing and exploration in a variety of MIS settings.

An open question is the extent to which system characteristics peculiar to ARIS, e.g., the query language, may explain any of the conclusions. For instance, is it perhaps the case that self-use of ARIS with little or no classroom training received may be explained in part by the simplicity of the query language? A similar question exists with regard to the organizational environment of ARIS. Is it perhaps the case that the substantial variety in forms of individual use of ARIS may be explained in part by the technical, engineering orientation of the department? Might a less technically inclined management tend toward other-use only?

Nevertheless, the concept of the terminal-based inquiry system as an information system based upon strictly personal use appears now to be a dubious basis for an MIS design. Indeed, it may be that interpersonal use is the key to success for systems directed toward management.

Nor does the chauffeur-driven use model appear fully adequate as a design basis. Patterns of interpersonal system use may tend to assume more sophisticated forms than those reflected by the simple notion of the “intermediary” role.

To guide future research, a new model is suggested: the adaptive interpersonal use model, shown in figure 3. The essential feature of this model is the notion that each manager adapts uniquely to a computer-based MIS. Certain managers use the system directly. Others choose to be other-users, receiving the reports provided by intermediaries or other managers. Still others are active in both roles. By means of interpersonal linkages, a few managers emerge as dominant users, e.g., M $_{1}$ and M $_{4}$ in Fig. 3.

In the case of ARIS, two individuals were identified as particularly dominant users. One, a second-level manager, provided aggressive leadership in both the development and use of the system. This is described in story form in [6]. An excerpt from the story, a recollection of a staff assistant, provides an illustration of the decidedly interpersonal approach of the manager, referred to here as K.H.:

"I sat in on one of his staff meetings. He asked me to sit in and operate the terminal for him. And he had on his sheet of paper the manpower estimates by product by each of his managers. So he'd say, 'O.K., key in for X product by department.' Then he'd get a printout, and he'd say, 'O.K., L.W., you said you'd promise so much manpower in this area, and it shows you're over. Why?" So L.W. would say, 'Well, that extra effort is there because of failure analysis problems. R.M. has spent a lot of time on failure analysis,' (K.H. would) say, 'Oh, yeah? O.K., A., you key in detail for Department Y, and find out what activities were done by whom,' and I'd key in, and ... it'd come out, and R.M. wasn't on there at all. So K.H. told him, 'Look, now you guys are all managers here, we're not picking on any one of you, but you should all know what your manpower are doing. That's one of your main jobs, keeping in touch with your manpower.' [6: p. 94]

Thus, the total pattern of usage of any MIS reflects the diversity of personalities, and formal and informal roles and relationships within the organization. The successful design of MIS requires an understanding of the nature of this diversity in the prevailing setting.

For researchers, the implications deserved emphasis. No study of a terminal-based MIS and its use should be based solely on an examination of direct terminal use. The use of an MIS is a significantly complex phenomenon, and must be observed within an interpersonally-based framework. The concepts of self-use, other-provision, and other-use are intended to provide a basis for such a framework.

One approach to this framework which should be explored is the application of graph-theoretic techniques to the portrayal of patterns of interpersonal information system use. Considering each individual as a node in a network of use, the representations of Fig. 4 might be employed. In this manner, a complete directed-graph representation of the network of use may be constructed. This was attempted in the case of ARIS, but lack of complete data as to sources for other-use and targets of other-provision rendered the result only marginally successful. Dominant users were identified, but it was not possible to complete the representation of the use network.

For practitioners, it is suggested that the design of any new management information system be guided by an analysis of the prevailing patterns of interpersonal reporting in the organization. Such an analysis should be based not merely upon a questionnairing technique such as that illustrated here, but upon interviews designed to uncover the subtleties in established organizational relationships. Through an understanding of these prevailing patterns and relationships, the design and implementation of the MIS should be greatly facilitated.

Practitioners might also conduct post-implementation audits of MIS usage by means of the same techniques. In this fashion, patterns of interpersonal system use might be studied in terms of their development over time, providing valuable insights into tendencies toward system success or failure.

Finally, in the study of interpersonal information system use, both researchers and practitioners must be particularly sensitive to the processes by which MIS use is initiated. Knowing who produced what report for whom does not get necessarily to the why of the matter. But it is precisely the why of the matter that is of greatest interest in the empirical study of MIS use. For it is only through an understanding of these “whys” that the full impact of information systems upon individual and organizational performance can be assessed.
