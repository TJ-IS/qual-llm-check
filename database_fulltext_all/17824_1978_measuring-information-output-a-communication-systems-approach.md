# Measuring Information Output: A Communication Systems Approach

Richard O. Masen \*

University of California. Graduate School of Management.
Los Angeles CA 900, USA

We live in an "Information age," an era in which the production and dissemination of information has become more important than the production and distribution of goods and services. Information activities have become a substantial part of the United States Gross National product. Nearly one-half of the U.S. workforce is currently employed in information related jobs. Despite the prevalence of information activities little is known about measuring them and determining their overall performance and productivity.

This paper presents a conceptual framework, with examples, for measuring the output of an information system. Drawing or communication theory four approaches to output measurement are developed: technical level output, semitic level output, functional output and influence or pragmatic level output.

Keywords: Information output, information productivity, information measurement, communication theory, information value.

## 1. Information as output

Defining and measuring the output of any system is always difficult, especially if the output is rather intangible. Much of the original development of output measures and productivity analysis occurred in industry where such tangible measures such as "automobiles produced per man-hour" or "ton miles moved per man-hour" are reasonable, if not wholly satisfactory, output/input indicators of productivity. Services, of course, present a more difficult problem. If the service is addressed to people then such outputs as "number admitted", "number served or treated", "number screened", "number immunized", etc., may be acceptable indicators of output.

There is an important class of systems in our society, however, for which output measures are more difficult to define. These are systems whose primary output is information. Essentially, information (and knowledge and communication) involves the means by which one system (a mind or a machine) affects another (including itself at a different point in time). So an information system is a very important component of any system and a critical element in the linkage between many diverse systems. Systems which produce information as an output include accounting, data processing, research and development, education, communication media, and entertainment. Also included are statistical centers of government which produce economic statistics (e.g. GNP), labor statistics, social indicators, census, social security statistics, and educational data. These systems not only produce information as an output but also, of course, consume resources in the process. Consequently, our concerns for efficiency require that the output of these systems be related to the input resources consumed. Only then can managerial judgments be made as to the appropriateness of the allocations. This measurement requires understanding the fundamental nature of information and of alternative ways of measuring it.

## 2. Toward a theory of information

Information, as an output, is represented in symbolic form. Thus, information finds its theoretical basis in the theory of signs, a theory with a long history. In the closing pages of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), John Locke devided all human knowledge into three parts - Physica, the knowledge of things as they are in their own proper beings, their constitutions, properties and operations; Practica, the skill of correctly applying our own powers and actions for the attainment of things good and useful; and, Semiotica, the doctrine of signs. Semiotica encompasses the nature of the signs that the mind employs for the understanding of things or for conveying its knowledge to others. Thus, this concept of signs is central to both information and communication. It is the key link in the way one system affects another and thus involves the system's context as well as the sign itself.

It was the American pragmatic philosopher, Charles Sanders Peirce who first pointed out the t iadic nature of the relationships inherent in semi- otica. A sign for Peirce is "something that stands to somebody for something in some respect or capa- cty". For any situation, he said, there is (a) a real world "designatum", (b) a sign, and (c) a user. The user is the receive- of the sign and is a critical part of the relationship. Every sign, to be "informative" must signal, influence, persuade, indicate or otherwise affect the user. So the user becomes the sine qua non of any sign.

Later, drawing on Peirce's insights, Charles Morris identified three types of rules for operating on signs:

## (1) Syntactic

rules of syntax which govern the relations between signs and the arrangement and manipulation of signs.

## (2) Semantic

rules governing the relationships between signs and the things, actions, events, qualities, properties and other relationships they represent.

(3) Pragmatic

rules governing the relationship between signs and their users.

These distinctions served to guide further developments.

In his review of Claude Shannon's, The Mathematical Theory of Communication, Warren Weaver places Shannon's contribution into context [16]. Reiterating that communication involved "all the procedures by which one mind may affect another", Weaver classifies the problems of communication into three hierarchical levels:

## Level A

How accurately can the symbols of communication (i.e., signs) be transmitted? (The technical problem.)

## Level B

How precisely do the transmitted symbols convey the desired meaning? (The semantic problem.)

## Level C

How effectively does the received meaning affect conduct in the desired way? (The effectiveness or influence problem.)

Weaver points out that Shannon's mathematical theory applies in the first instance only to level A. However since Levels B and C are dependent on the performance at Level A, it has implications for these levels as well. Each of the three levels is relevant to the information communication process. Hence, a fully developed theory ought to deal with all three levels.

This brief review of the doctrine of signs leads us to spell out several distinctions which are necessary for defining information as an output:

(1) Communication is the process by which one system, P, (e.g., a mind) affects another receiving system, R. The affected system, R, is the receiver. The producing system, P, performs the functions of observation, recordation, collection, storage, analysis, processing and transmission.

(2) An information and communication system, P, produces and transmits signs.

(3) A sign is a sensible (sight, hear, smell, taste, feel)

## 3. Measures of information output

Weaver's distinction between the technical level, semantic level and influence level suggests three different senses in which an information system's output can be measured. The technical level refers to the physical manipulation of data and media. The semantic level refers to the logical (and psychological) units of meaning handled by the system. Finally, the influence level refers to the units of change in behavior, attitude or cognition created by the system. In order to develop a framework for measuring output at each of these levels we must have a concept of the producing unit P.

Structurally an information producing system P consists of hardware, software and people with special telents and skills. The object upon which work is done in an information producing system is data and media.

Viewed as a work element, data is a representation of some thing in the form of digits, characters, letters, numbers, symbols, words, et al., whereas, media is the means, agency, or instrument for carrying data. Examples of media include paper documents, punched cards, computer magnetic tapes, human voice, books, newspapers, movies, television, radio, telephone, projector (slide, overhead or opaque), teaching machines, phonograph records, sound tape, computer graphics output (e.g. CRT), computer printers, lectures and plays or drama. The hardware, software, people, data and media are coordinated and controlled within an organization by means of rules and procedures.

Information, on the other hand, is data interpreted within some problem context or view-of-the-world. Information exists at the semantic and pragmatic level. The ultimate purpose of any information producing unit is to convert data to information.

Producing units receive inputs of data and media, process them and produce outputs in the form of data and media which are communicated to the receiver. Consequently, the work performed by a producing unit may be measured in terms of (1) the amount of physical activity accomplished (media and data processing), (2) the amount of logical activity accomplished (data processing) or (3) the amount of conversion or processing activity accomplished. Physical activity refers to the technical level. Logical and conversion activity refer to the semantic level.

## 3.1. Technical level output

Technical level output is defined as the number of physical units of media or data (signs or symbols) handled or transmitted during a time period. It is an indication of the producing system's ability to handle volume. Some units for measuring output at the technical level are given in Table 1.

Basic productivity measurements maybe obtained at the technical level by comparing the number of units of output as measured by the units (described in Table 1) to a measure of resources consumed by the system; for example, bits processed per dollar of expenditure, characters keypunched per man-hour, lines printed per printer hour, checks sorted per man-hour, blocks moved per main frame hour, or documents handled per dollar of expenditure.

## 3.2. Semantic level output

Semantic level output refers to the number of units of meaning handled or transmitted by the producing unit during a given time period. Meaning refers to the logical or psychological properties of the data especially as they signify something to the receiver. Thus a unit of meaning is derived by looking at data from the point-of-view of the receiver or information system user. Burton Swanson [18] calls this the perspective of the inquirer who would make use of the data. Table 2 summarizes some suggested ways of measuring the semantic output of a producing system. ## 3.3. Functional output

An information producing system, P, receives inputs of data and/or media and converts them into outputs by changing them in a systematic way. It is generally useful to conceive of P in the following way.

There are one or more streams of input data and/or media which come into P. The streams are divided into distinguishable units called items. Items in turn, contain written messages. Data is the general term for the written message contained in the item. In many scientific, statistical or accounting systems a written message is formed as a structured datum because the message is expected to be evaluated as true or false. (Poets and writers, say in the Joycian tradition, create written messages rather than data because their messages are not necessarily intended to be evaluated as true or false.)

Written messages (and structured datum), in turn, are comprised of identifiers of objects and events (i.e. subjects) called keys and descriptions of properties or points in time (i.e. predicates) called values. An input stream may be ordered in terms of its keys and/or the values of its items.

The information producing unit accepts inputs and performs operations on them. The operations are conducted under the direction of a control function which prescribes the operation to be performed on the input according to a series of rules called a program. The information producing unit also has a memory in which items of data and/or media may be placed in addressable locations according to their key. The locator of each location is called an address.

The information conversion process may also be subdivided into a series of basic or unit processing operations which are executed according to the program. Each unit operation is a generalized self contained segment of an information processing system which accepts data and/or media. It then performs a function (executes an instruction) which changes the state of the data and/or media in a systematic way, and places the result in an output stream. A unit operation is not necessarily a processing step, an elementary time and motion study movement, or a particular job, nor is it a particular role in a specified information processing system. Rather a unit operation is a basic function common to all information producing systems and is defined independently of the industry, organization, hardware, software, data, media and people with which it is associated.

The results of the information conversion process are placed into one or more output streams in the form of processed items. An output stream may be ordered in terms of the keys and/or values of its items.

Table 3 summarizes and defines some of the most common unit operations employed in an information producing unit.

In principle, the execution of any of the unit operations defined in Table 3 constitutes a unit of work for the producing system P. A count of the units of work completed becomes a simple functional measure of output.

Productivity in turn, maybe measured as a ratio of the useful output to the total number of executions of a unit operation. For comparison purposes a more useful measure is obtained by defining standard sizes or limits for the data and/or media processed by a function. Illustrative standard operations are: writing a line of 120 characters, coding a \$500 alpha-numeric character text into a binary coded decimal format, sorting 1000 items with 80 character written messages on a 9 character key, retricving a 100 character written message item on the basis of a 12 character address, and adding two 10 digit numerals and producing an 11 digit command.

One level above primitive unit operations described in Table 3 are macro-unit operations called routines. A routine is an ordered sequence of primitive unit operations which is designed to accept a specified type of input data and media and to produce a specified type of output. A standard routine is defined for a standardized volume and size of inputs and outputs. An illustrative standard routine is: Reading 20 items with 10 digits each, calculating the sum into a 12 digit summand, formatting the result into the form \$X.XXX.XXX.XXX.XX, and writing the result in a prescribed box in the lower right hand corner of an $8\frac{1}{2}$ by 11 inch sheet of paper. This routine could be performed in a variety of contexts by means of a manual pen and pencil system, an adding machine, a transistorized pocket calculator, a personal computer, or a large scale computer.

A critical aspect of any primitive unit or routine is that it can malfunction. That is, it can produce an error of deviation from the intended result. These deviations produce variances in the information producing system. Variances are departures from the ideal of the process. They may be created by the hardware, software, data, media, people or procedures involved in the operation or by the environment in which the operation takes place. If detected (say by means of verifying) variances may be absorbed (returned to a normal or standard state) by the hardware, software of people or by means of rules and procedures. Otherwise a variance is incorporated in the data and/or media and passed on to another operation or system.

One approach for studying the possible implications of variances in an information producing system is to identify the unit operations or routines which comprise the system and to establish the dependence relationships among them in the form of a matrix. The normal and deviate state characteristics of the data and/or media are identified for each unit operation. The variances (i.e., deviate states) are identified as to which are absorbed in that unit operation or passed on to another unit operation. Each variance exported from a unit operation is tracked through the system to the subsequent unit operation where it is either absorbed, magnified, reduced or passed on unchanged to the next operation. In this manner all variances are tracked throughout the system. Variances which are not absorbed by the system are either passed on the receiver (e.g., in the form of an erroneous report, typographical error, noisy signal, or perhaps, a dull passage of prose) or passed to the environment (e.g., in the form of a frustrated clerk).

Variances which materially affect the successful functioning of the system in terms of the quantity of output produced, the quality of output (e.g., timeliness, accuracy, reliability, validity, clarity, readability, entertainment value, surprise value, etc.), the financial cost of the system or the social cost of the system (e.g., human stress, frustration, safety, motivation, absenteeism, alienation, self-concept, friendliness, health, etc.) are identified. These are often called key variances.

Variance analysis may be used to construct more informative measures of the functional output of an information producing system. For any unit operation or routine there are four basic things which can happen. An operation may:

1. Receive a normal input and produce a normal output (successful normal operation).

2. Receive a normal input and produce a deviant output (internal creation and passing on of variance).

3. Receive a deviant input and produce a normal output (the absorption of variance).

4. Receive a deviant input and produce a deviant output (the passing through of variance). Pass through variances may be:

a) A reduced diviant of the same type.

b) An unchanged deviant of the same type.

c) A magnified deviant of the same type.

By identifying each of these possibilities for a given unit operation or routine, the number of each type can be counted and perhaps weighted according to its materiality to form total functional output measures. These functional output measures are defined at the technical or semantic level depending on the particular functions and the nature of the data and/or media involved. To the extent that normal or deviant output is traced all the way to its impact on the receiver the output approaches the influence or pragmatic level.

It should be pointed out that the functional measures developed here are extensions of efforts undertaken to study productive systems more generally. See refs. [1,5–7].

## 3.4. Influence level output

The output of an information system has a pragmatic impact on the receiver. It produces practical results which have some ultimate value to the receiver. These results and the value placed on them constitute the influence level of output.

The influence level includes the personal, psychological, organizational and social context of the receiver and the receiver's habit or "disposition to respond" to output in a predictable way. "A disposition to respond to something in a certain way," according to C.W. Morris [14], "is a state of an organism which is such that under certain additional conditions the response in question takes place" (p. 81). Following this notion measures of output at the influence level focus on the response of the receiver.

## 3.4.2. Accept

Once received, data must be read and understood by R. Perhaps the best measure of this is a count of self-reports by R on the number of items read and judged relevant, useful or acceptable by R.

In addition, the degree of acceptance can be measured. For example, in a study of receivers of MIS data in a large electronic manufacturing firm, Swanson [17] had the receivers evaluate the data on 5 point Lickert scales according to its attributes such as: timely-untimely, relevant--irrelevant, unique-redundant, accurate-inaccurate, instructive-misinstructive, concise-diffuse, clear-ambiguous, and readable-unreadable. Swanson inferred a degree of acceptance from the receivers' responses to these scales. (He termed it aptly "appreciation"). He concluded that receivers who were involved in the design of information producing systems "appreciated" the data more and rank it higher in terms of acceptability.

Another example of an attempt to measure $R$ 's acceptance of data is Belzer's study [2] of medical researchers who submitted search queries to a literature retrieval system. The receivers were first given one of five surrogates for the actual documents which satisfied the query. The receivers were asked to rate the expected relevancy of the document on the basis of the surrogate they received. The surrogates were (a) citation only, (b) abstract only, (c) first paragraph of document, (d) last paragraph of document, and (e) both first and last paragraph of document. The receivers then were given the full document and rated it as to its actual relevancy. Belzer used the responses for each type of surrogate responses to calculate the probability that a surrogate rated relevant would yield a relevant full document. The entropy function applied to this probability (i.e., the probability that a full document is relevant given that the surrogate was judged relevant) yields a measure of the bits of error-free information per full document for each surrogate method.

Belzer obtained the following results: citation only, 0.0953 bits; abstract only, 0.123; first paragraph, 0.1630; last paragraph, 0.1659; and, both first and last paragraph, 0.3013 bits. The results show that P's ability to produce acceptable data for R is a function of the surrogate method employed by P. Further they suggest that since all of the paragraph methods outperform the abstract method and do not involve the costly extra operations of abstracting, summarizing and rewriting, the paragraph methods may be the most cost/effective in terms of R's acceptance of the data.

## 3.4.3. Retain

The most appropriate measure of the extent to which R stores and retains data is a test of recall in which R is asked to produce the data upon demand. This is the method use in most classrooms. Unfortunately, examinations of this type are generally used primarily as measures of R's ability to memorize and recall and not as measures of P's ability to produce information output at the influence level. That is, they assume that the information system is functioning properly and are intended to test the amount of student learning.

## 3.4.4. Integrate

Again classroom examinations are the best example of measures of the extent to which R integrates the data received and relates it to other data. The typical “compare and contrast . . .,” or “trace the evolution or history of . . .” question, or a problem set with calculations to be completed, are cases in point.

## 3.4.5. Evaluate

The affect of data on R is often divorced from considerations of the cognitive or intellectual impact of the data on R. Yet it must be the case that R has received, accepted, retained and to some extent integrated the data before the data evokes a change in attitude or worldview. Attitudes can also effect the way receivers solve problems or change their behavior so it seems proper to place attitudes at this level in the hierarchy.

Allport defined an attitude as "a mental and neutral state of readiness, organized through experience exerting a directive or dynamic influence upon the individual's response to all objects and situations with which it is related." Psychologists following in the tradition of Thurstone, Lickert and others have devised scales upon which attitudes can be measured. Also communication researchers such as Hovland, Lumsdaine, Sheffield, Janis and Kelly have constructed methods of measuring the amount of change in attitude or opinion resulting from the receipt of particular items of data. There is a vast array of possible measuring techniques to employ at this level. Essentially, however, they all reveal the amount of change in the affective domain of the receiver with respect to some idea, object or event. This amount of change may then be related to an intended level of change or the consumption or resources required to produce it in order to obtain performance measures on $P$ .

## 3.4.6. Apply

Data which has been integrated and at least has not evoke debilitating attitudes may be applied in the problem solving or decision making process. To be applied the data must be "taken" to be true or false. That is, it is a statement, as E.A. Singer pointed out, in the imperative mood. In a sense, the information system, P, "commands" the receiver, R, to accept the conclusions as true or false until they are replaced by new imperatives resulting from further evidence, analysis or inquiry. Thus, all data is tentative and can be changed in the light of new evidence.

The imperative and tentative nature of statements implies that other statements exist. As the receiving system acquires data it changes the status of the statements it held previously. The receiver begins an engagement with an information producing system with a series of a priori beliefs or conclusions about the problem or decision he or she faces and attaches credibilities to these beliefs. By means of an engagement with an information producing system the receiver intends to increase or decrease the degree of confirmation or credibility held in these beliefs. In the process the a priori is converted to an a posteriori belief.

A formal measure for this kind of application of data is found in the information theory concepts developed by Shannon [16] and extended by Theil [20]. The theory measures information exchange in terms of negative entropy. It assumes a set of n mutually exclusive beliefs or conclusions, $E_1, E_2, \ldots, E_n$ . During an information exchange process the a priori probabilities (credibilities) $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$ for this set of beliefs are transformed to a posteriori probabilities $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ . A measure of the amount of information exchanged is given by the formula

$$
I (p: q) \equiv \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {i = n} q _ {i} [ \log_ {2} (q _ {i} / p _ {i}) ].
$$

$I(p \cdot q)$ is a reasonable measure of the output in terms of the application of data to problems at the influence level of an information system. It essentially measures the amount of change in belief or the part of the receiver. In an operational sense the use of this formula reduces the problem of measurement to that of estimating two sets of subjective probabilities.

An important question emerges as to whether it is possible or feasible to estimate $I(p:q)$ for real information systems. In a study of changes in scientific beliefs held by mean scientists, Ian Mitroff and the author conclude that the measure is applicable and generally reliable.

## The influence of scientific information: an illustration

A study was made of 42 lunar scientists from universities, NASA, U.S. Geological Survey, other federal agencies, and from private industry [12,13]. Twenty-two were either principal or co-principal investigators on NASA lunar research grants and received lunar samples directly. Ten more had access to the lunar samples. The remaining ten had indirect, but no known direct, involvement with the samples.

However, this latter group was chosen on the basis of leadership and prestige in the field. As a group this sample of scientists was considered to be representative of the influential leadership in the lunar research field. The study was designed to reveal the change in degrees of belief held by these scientists on hypothesis in four research areas: (a) the cause of mascons \*, (b) the temperature history of the Moon, (c) the origin of tektites, and (d) he origin of the Moon.

The 42 scientists were the receivers in this study and Apollo program was the information producing system. The research question was "Did the data obtained by the Apollo program have any measurable effect on the scientists problem solving ability?"

The scientists were asked to record their opinion on each of the 4 hypotheses as of three points in time before Apollo 11 (that is prior to the receipt of moon rocks), before Apollo 15, and after 15, and after Apollo 15. This data was covered to probabilities and used to calculate the amount of information exchanged using the formula $I(p:q)$ above. In order to gain an insight into how $I(p:a)$ can be used as a measure of information exchange at the influence levels it will be useful to explore the results obtained on one of these hypotheses, the cause of mascons, in some detail.

Scientists have offered two major explanations as to the origin of the mascons on the Moon. (1) Meteorites: The mascons are due to large meteorities which are buried beneath the lunar surface. (2) Lava: The mascons are due to large lava plugs which have welled up from the lunar interior.

Prior to Apollo 11 lunar scientists believed these explanations to be about equally credible (as indicated by the credibilities of 0.544 for meteorites and 0.465 for lava). See Table 4. They based this on the observed fact that the mascon's tended to be near the surface (implying a "cold" rigid substructure of the Moon) and that studies on the magnetic fields of the solar wind indicated that the lunar interior did not conduct electricity. Collectively this evidence tended to argue against the mascons being caused by terrestrial-type lava flows deriving from the interior of the Moon. Consequently more substantial credibility was accorded the meteorite hypothesis.

## 3.4.7. Change behavior

A measure of information output in terms of changes in behavior requires the direct observation of receivers and the construction of a scale in which changes in the receiver's behavior may be recorded. The simplest measure is to record whether or not a receiver engaged in a prescribed behavior. The recordings can be made in binary, yes/no, or 1/0 scale. A count of the frequency of engaging in the behavior before the receipt of an after would serve as the basis for constructing a measure.

The most frequently used measures of change in behavior are measures of ability to perform problem-solving tasks and tests of skills, although most measures of change in behavior are unique to each situation and are difficult to identify in a complex situation.

## 3.4.8. Change in systems performance

Changes in total system performance are the ultimate pragmatic intention of the act of generating and communicating knowledge, however, this is the most difficult level at which to operationalize measures. The difficulty stems from two key problems. One is the complexity of forming an overall measure of performance for the relevant impacted system to which the receiver is related. The other is the difficulty of attributing the changes in systems performance to the receipt of specific data by the receiver. There are many alternative causes and other contributory factors to be taken into account.

Several research studies have attempted to measure the output of an information system at the pragmatic level in terms of the changes in the overall performance of a system. Lucal [8,9] for example, investigated the use of periodic reports which were designed to support managerial and sales personnel. Lucas hypothesized that user performance was a function of (a) use of reports and (b) personal and situational factors such as decision style, attitudes, etc. His performance measure was an accounting figure which summarized the sales or economic performance of the receiver of the report. Questionnaires were used to obtain a measure of the extent to which reports were used by the receiver to support specific managerial or sales activities and also to obtain measures of decision style, attitude, situational factors (sales territory, bank locations, etc.) and personal factors. These data were formed into a multiple regression model with overall performance as the dependent variable. The estimated coefficients for each factor are indicators of the relative contribution of each independent variable to the receiver's performance. In the two studies Lucas found: rather weak relationship between receipt and use of the information systems data and the receiver's performance in the system.

A similar approach was taken by Tierney in a recent Ph.D. dissertation [21]. Tierney studied 8 private liberal arts colleges with a total of 43 academic departments. Two of the colleges (14 departments) used a straight manual system to obtain instructional cost and workload information. Three (18 departments) had placed the elementary data in a computeized databank system. Three (16 departments) had installed a predictive information system in which a model is used to form various ratios (such as student/faculty ratio) and cost analyses on the basis of varying assumptions. (For a discussion of the difference between databank and predictive systems see ref. [11]). Tierney's question was whether the use of these more sophisticated information systems resulted in better overall performance in the colleges. He took as his measure of performance the departmental dollar expenditures per F.T.E. student for 1975–76.

A stepwise multiple regression model was employed. For control purposes two variables representing contributory factors were permitted to enter the regression first. These were (e) prior departmental expenditures per F.T.E. student for 1972–73 (under the assumption that future expenditures are in part a function of past expenditures) and (b) the college's selectivity on incoming students as measured by SAT scores (under the assumption that expenses per student would be affected by the quality of the students). The results were a coefficient of 0.78 for prior expenditures per F.T.E. student, 0.38 for selectivity, -0.30 for predictive systems and -0.16 for databank systems. The first three variables were all significant at the 0.01 level whereas the coefficient for the databank systems was not significant at the 0.05 level. The significant, negative coefficient for the predictive systems suggests that use of these systems, indeed, aid in the reduction of departmental expenditures per student. Thus, it represents a form of measure of the output of the information system at the pragmatic level in terms of changes in the performance of the system in which the receiver of the data operates.

## 4. Summary

Information, as an output, involves the means by which one system, the producer P, affects another system, the receiver R. The means, in symbolic form, consist of signs. Drawing on the theory of signs, three levels were identified at which an output flew of signs could be defined and measured – technical, semantic, and pragmatic. A fourth level – functional – was also added in order to analyze information output in terms of the processes which produce it.

Information output at the technical level consists of the number of signs accurately transmitted between P and R. Several levels of aggregation ranging from the "bit" to a "physical databank" for measuring at the technical level were suggested.

Information output at the semantic level consists of the amount or number of units of meaning flowing from P to R. This measure requires a linguistic approach. Words, sentence expressions, written messages and text represent units of utterances intended to communicate meaning. Statements, datum, data records, files, database (logical), reports and queries represent units of meaning based upon "true"/"false" evaluation. Datum, it appears, is the basic semantic unit for most information systems.

Functional output of an information system consists of the successful completion of an information processing function such as sorting or calculating. Twenty-three basic functions, called unit operations, were defined. The execution of a function, however, can result in an error or deviation from the norm of the function. These deviants may be passed on throughout the processing system as variances. Consequently, a full functional level measure of information output must also include some tracking and recording of the variances.

Information output at the influence or pragmatic level consists of the amount of change in $R$ occurring as a result of receiving signs from $P$ . Changes in the receiver require that $R$ receive, accept, retain, interpret, evaluate and apply the data (all mental operations). Also $R$ must change his or her overt behavior which in turn results in a change in the performance of the system in which $R$ operates. Suggestions were made for measures at each step. In particular, the information exchange equation $I(p:q)$ was proposed as a measure of $R$ 's application of data. In terms of a method for measuring the impact of the producing system's information on overall system's performance, regression models which relate a measure of performance to a series of independent variables (including a measure of the receipt or use of information) currently seem to be the most fruitful approach.

The paper offers a family of information output measures, each of which measures a particular aspect of the system. All are potentially useful. In a given circumstance one would want to choose one or more of these measures to describe the key aspects of the system under study. These output measures may be related to a variety of input measures to form productivity measures for the information system as a whole.
