# Designing Information Systems for Organizational Control: The Use of Psychological Tests

Martin L. Bariff and Edward J. Lusk
14 Washington School, University of Pennsylvania,
Philadelphia, Pa 19104, USA

Information systems should be designed in recognition of their potential effect on organizational control. The information system/user interface, e.g., report mechanisms, thus becomes a critical element for achieving control in organizations. Psychological tests, as compared to interviews, are proposed as a structured approach for designing user reports, hence improving control systems. A framework for this methodology is proposed, and a field study is described.

Keywords: MIS, management control, information requirements analysis, personnel training, psychological testing.

## 1. Introduction

As purposeful systems, organizations undertake activities to achieve predetermined objectives. Coordination and control of these diverse activities require formal planning and control systems which, in turn, require supportive information systems. The development of these information systems should be considered within the general context of organizational design [12,15].

Systems analysts, as organization designers, need to recognize the interdependencies between the information systems and the primary organizational design factors, e.g., tasks, technology, decisionmaking, organizational structure, people and reward systems [12]. Since these factors must all be considered in the design of an information system, a contingency approach is suggested for different organizational contexts. Thus, systems analysts must anticipate information systems impacts upon all the design factors during system development and subsequent modifications.

The design and administration of information systems have two primary effects on organizational control. First, the information systems subunits, similar to other subunits, must be administered in accordance with organizational budgets and policies. Second, information systems support other organizational subunits, and thus influence their planning and control decisions.

## 1.1. Management control of information systems.

In recognition of major investments in hardware, software and personnel, the information systems subunit requires formal planning and control systems. To capture and provide information systems subunit cost data at an appropriate level of detail, a separate chart of accounts should be developed for the administration, development and operations activities. This detailed data may be aggregated and reclassified into a general ledger chart of accounts format for corporate reporting.

Within the information systems subunit, this information supports budgetary control and planning activities. Another important use of this data is the transfer pricing of information systems services to other organizational subunits. Partial or full cost chargeback or profit center pricing for the information systems subunit services may be selected. Nolan [19] has studied the effects of chargeout systems in a variety of organizational contexts. His findings indicate that the introduction of a chargeout system provides better organizational control of information systems subunit resources. Complementary to the control of information systems services, a second organizational control issue is the impact of information systems on the primary activities of other organizational subunits.

1.2. Influence of information systems on organizational control.

The services provided by the information systems subunit support management operations, planning and control of organizational activities. Information systems exhibit varying degrees of influence on organizational control. Greater influence is represented by computer-embedded, automated decision models, e.g., production and inventory control. Lesser influence is represented by user requested reports which support human information processing and decision-making.

Institutional decisionmaking is affected by information systems in two ways: (1) the quality of information systems input and output, and (2) information systems impacts on organizational task and social structures. The first type of influence relates to data entry and report generation activities. The quality of report content is directly dependent upon the design and operation of data entry systems. Thus, systems analysts should develop paper forms and CRT dialogues which minimize the entry of incorrect data [11,16]. The indentification of data elements for entry and subsequent report generation is performed within the information requirements analysis process.

The second type of influence relates to information systems implementation effects on job design and enrichment, interpersonal contact, and redistribution of power (e.g. [4,13,18,21]). These organizational impacts must be considered during systems development in order to minimize potential user dysfunctional behavior after system implementation.

This section has suggested various ways in which information systems influence organizational control. In this paper, the primary objective is to suggest a structured methodology (i.e., by use of psychological tests) for designing the report generation interface between an information system and users. The next section discusses the role of information system/user interface within the context of information requirements analysis.

## 1.3. Information requirements analysis.

The development of information systems to support decisionmaking and to facilitate organizational control requires knowledge of (1) decision models supported by the organization and their associated information requirements, and (2) channels of communication explicitly and implicitly available to transmit information to requiring users. Typically, systems analysts formulate judgements of information requirements and available channels of communication by conducting interviews and evaluating existing reports.

Information requirements analysis is a complex process, for information needs are seldom known or clearly articulated [1]. Various methodologies have been developed to facilitate the determination of information requirements [3]. The focus of this paper, however, relates to the design of report presentation modes to communicate identified information requirements effectively. Both psychologists and systems analysts suggest that reports generated by an information system be congruent with users' information processing capabilities. This individualized information systems design strategy is similar to an organization designer's choice of a control system to fit a manager's style [8]. Presently, many of these psychological attributes are inferentially considered by the systems analyst. This subjectivity, however, may promote a dysfunctional system's design which may render the organizational control system ineffective. Thus, the authors propose that generally accepted psychological tests be used by the systems analyst to evaluate user preferences for report presentation modes.

The problem of ascertaining the existing behavioral sensitivities to provide information germane to the design of the information systems component of the control system has an important analogue in clinical psychology. The clinician (like the systems analyst) comes to the case with a set of biases and predispositions formed by professional experiences. In order to rationalize a treatment plan (that is, a plan of action required to reorient the patient's behavior based upon a diagnosis), the clinician uses various psychological tests which have meaning in developing a diagnosis and structuring a treatment plan [17]. We suggest this analogy only to indicate that in order to rationalize their activity, clinical psychologists use psychological tests. The systems analyst faces the same basic problem when analyzing individuals within an organizational system in order to develop the information systems component of an organizational control system. The systems analyst would use the test results to develop user psychological profiles which enable the systems analyst to infer user report presentation mode preferences. These profiles and inferred user report preferences are then used to design reporting systems which facilitate effective information systems-user communication.

The following sections of the paper discuss (1) a behavioral framework for organizational controls, (2) the processing of psychological test data, (3) a field study in which some behavioral dimensions were evaluated with the methodology to be suggested, and (4) organizational control implications for the information systems design.

## 2. A behavioral framework for the design of organizational controls—An information systems perspective

An information system has the capability to provide feedforward, realtime status and feedback reports to users. The primary objective for making this information available is to motivate individuals to behave in a manner which is consistent with organizational objectives. Economic forecasts, production schedule status and budget variances represent reporting mechanisms which are intended to minimize potential differences between expected and actual behavior.

The design of information systems in conjunction with reward systems, structural configuration, and technology jointly influence an organization's ability to control its members' behavior. Thus, the capability of an information system to communicate clearly and effectively with users represents a critical linkage in an organization's control strategy. For example, economic forecasts, production status reports and variance information can only provide useful information if individuals receiving this information can effectively utilize it. In complex organization structures exhibiting task differentiation and integrating mechanisms, the communication linkage becomes increasingly important.

There are many frameworks within which information systems elements of organizational design and control may be studied [5]. The authors suggest the framework represented in Table 1. These classifications have been constructed recognizing the information systems aspects required in control analysis (that is, provision of usable information and data to the appropriate decision makers in order to motivate effective corrective actions).

To develop reports which correspond to user requirements, the systems analyst must consider both the hierarchical level and functional specialization of the users. Furthermore, user behavioral factors, e.g., cognitive style and implementation apprehension (discussed below), should be evaluated to aid the systems analyst in developing and implementing the information systems project. If a concurrent organizational change is also planned, situational factors and organizational characteristics also should be evaluated. The evaluation of individual and contextual characteristics provide guidelines for selecting attri butes of report characteristics, e.g., content, format, and presentation mode. Lastly, criteria which may be used to evaluate the information systems project should be identified. The authors propose that the factors represented in Table 1 delineate the more important behavioral variables for information systems design. A brief discussion of these factors follows.

The first user behavior variable set, cognitive style, refers to users' preferences for perceiving and processing information. Individuals differ in their abilities to use reasoning, intuition, feeling and pattern recognition for interpreting data and formulating judgments. The information from psychological tests provides useful input for matching report content, format and presentation mode with users' psychological structures.

The second user behavior variable set, implementation apprehension, is defined by resistance to change, defense mechanisms and stress level. These variables provide insights to users' predispositions toward the acceptance of an information systems modification and thus may suggest a need for differential amounts of consultation with users to relieve their apprehension and facilitate the implementation.

For information systems engagements which include organizational change, situational and organizational characteristics should be considered. Psychological tests provide a means for assessing users' predispositions toward job creativity, ambiguity, security and satisfaction. Relocation of personnel will require adaptation to a new subculture. More extroverted users will adapt more easily to new environments. An information system also may influence changes in the distribution of power and interpersonal trust. Thus, psychological tests can provide pre- and post-implementation evaluations of task and social structure information. To achieve institutionalization of planned information systems and organizational interventions, systems analysts, as change agents, should be familiar with successful termination techniques used in organizational development [14].

These individual and organizational variables represent relevant dimensions for designing an information system as a control mechanism. The matching of appropriate report characteristics with user behavioral variable diagnoses helps to minimize the potential for dysfunctional behavior. However, user satisfaction with report characteristics should only be considered as an intervening variable. User satisfaction only represents a measure of information systems production quality rather than decisionmaking performance. Thus information systems projects must be evaluated in terms of decreased costs and increased benefits. In a complex environment, however, it may not be feasible to associate changes directly in performance criteria with an information systems modification. Thus, user evaluation of outputs or the administration of an information system as a profit center may be the only operational criteria. ## 4. Behavioral variables design methodology/

The collection of behavioral variable information can be accomplished (1) through unstructured or structured interviews and analyses of existing reports, and/or (2) by the administration of psychological tests. The former approach often allows users to misstate report preferences and requires a substantial preference elicitation for all anticipated reports which are prepared for the user. Subsequent to the determination of format preferences for an initial set of reports, interviews must again be conducted for the design of additional reports. The development of user behavioral profiles, however, may require more initial but less subsequent effort by the systems analyst. One guideline for adopting the psychological test approach is suggested: as either the number of initial reports, number of users, or projected subsequent report changes increase, psychological testing becomes more cost-effective. Furthermore, user preference solely developed from limited interviews are more subjective than results provided by established psychological tests.

Clinical psychologists, similar to systems analysts, evaluate and classify individuals into psychological types. Typically, their evaluations are influenced by the results from a series of psychological tests. Although the evidence is somewhat inconclusive, it appears that clinicians' use of psychometric data improves the accuracy of patient diagnoses [17]. Thus, the authors suggest that the use of psychological tests represents a movement to a more systematic basis of information systems design.

The process of utilizing psychological tests for report design and implementation planning is illustrated in fig. 1 \*. The objective of the processing of the psychological data is to ascertain explicitly the users' behavioral sensitivities either horizontally by specific organizational decision group, vertically by stylistic mode or some combination of the two. These insights become inputs to the information system design.

The first requirement, the selection of the psychological tests, is based on the scope of the engagement. For an information systems modification, the systems analyst should collect data on all relevant user behavioral variables. These psychological test results then provide a basis for formulating user psychological profiles. Further guidelines for using psychological tests are found in the appendix.

The second requirement is the partitioning of the individuals tested into groups based upon (1) the systems analyst's a priori insights to the organization's decision network, and/or (2) an algorithmic partitioning (e.g., cluster analysis) of the individuals.

The partitioned test data is then analyzed (e.g., discriminant analysis) to isolate statistically significant separations on the basis of the test scores. If the algorithmic partitioning does not provide significant group differences, the test scores should be analyzed independent of the organizational groupings. If no viable groupings or significant differences emerge, the systems analyst still has knowledge about the general level of psychological characteristics for the individuals tested.

The third requirement is the translation of behavioral profiles into design guidelines, e.g., types of report formats and implementation procedures. The systems analyst must have knowledge of potential effects of alternative behavioral profiles. Thus, as suggested, the systems analyst should have adequate training or access to a consultant with a behavioral science background.

The last requirement is the preparation of the report formats and the implementation procedures. Having described the methodological sequence, the next section summarizes a field study using this methodology.

## 4. Illustrative example

The organization to which the above methodology was applied was a community nursing service in a large metropolitan eastern US city which was in the design phase of a nursing evaluation information system. The community nursing service was having difficulty in evaluating and, therefore, controlling its service. The major problem was that the management information system in use failed to provide the appropriate management information. During the design phase, the authors identified seventeen individuals as constituting the organization's decision network. The authors, through the interaction with these seventeen individuals recognizing the purposes, nature and scope of the desired information system, proceeded with the study assuming that two behavioral dimensions were relevant to the study: Cognitive Style and Implementation Apprehension.

## 4.1. Test selection and results

Based upon the scope of the study and the availability of standard psychological tests, a series of psychological tests was selected: (1) Cognitive Style-Kelly REP test, Category Width and Field Independence, and (2) Implementation Apprehension-Authoritarianism, Dogmatism, Flexibility, Manifest Anxiety, MacDonald Tolerance of Ambiguity; Defense Mechanism Inventory (DMI) and Extroversion. If a concurrent organizational change had been planned, measures of situational and organizational characteristics would have been obtained.

Given the selection and administration of the test, the seventeen individuals constituting the organization's decision network were a priori partitioned based upon the organizational hierarchical level. There were seven individuals who were primarily administrative and ten individuals who were field nursing supervisors. The supervisory group was located in the agency's districts throughout the city. Therefore, there were seventeen individuals, fourteen test scores per individual and two a priori groups: (1) 10 supervisors, and (2) 7 administrators.

An analysis of the test results indicated equivalent responses by both groups for the REP test, Field Independence and the DMI. This, these variables were eliminated. The remaining seven variables were subjected to a discriminant analysis which disclosed a significant difference in the test results between the two user groups ( $\alpha = 0.01$ ).

## 4.2. Discussion

Two cognitive style test scores (Field Independence and REP) and the five DMI test scores did not disclose statistically significant inter-group separation. The cognitive style tests indicated that the users were field dependent as compared with national norms. Thus, the interpretation of nonsignificant test differences, which indicates homogeneous user characteristics, provided valuable insight for the information systems design strategy. Similarly, the DMI scores did not depart in a statistically significant manner from national norms. The remaining seven variabels were used in the discriminant analysis.

The resistance to change scores indicated that both user groups were flexible (above the national norms for all three variables), but that the supervisors were more flexible. This finding suggests that both groups should be receptive to the information systems modifications. The implementation process confirmed this prediction.

The stress dimension results disclose that both groups were below the national female norm for anxiety and the supervisors were less anxious. For tolerance of ambiguity, the results suggest that both groups are able to cope with high levels of ambiguity; the supervisors' results indicated that their were higher.

The overall implication for this information systems design is that reports with minimal complexity would be most suited to the user's cognitive structures and that anxiety and resistance to change should not provide obstacles during the implementation stage.

Given the small number of system users in this study, a quantitative cost-benefit comparison between the psychological testing and interview methods could not be performed.

These results later proved to be reasonably accurate given the actual systems interaction during the design and implementation phase. Although there was no follow-up evidence than the experiences of one of the authors who participated with those individuals designing and implementing the MIS, the following may be reported.

1. The nursing supervisors were asked to gather and report substantially different information and to process different reports. They did so willingly and with an unusual spirit of cooperation.

2. The administration seemed willing and able to analyze this new information, except for one individual who has since left the agency.

3. The consensus of those individuals in the decision network was so positive that a resolution was drafted and sent to the board of managers that the agency apply for a federal grant to continue the study and implement the MIS project.

A limited validation of the psychological test results was conducted since this was the first implementation of this methodology. The validation process was necessarily limited to the reports designed for the MIS. The authors asked the users to evaluate a set of representative reports on four dimensions: readability, completeness of data, ability to locate and abstract data, and amount of detail. The purpose of the validation was to compare the systems analyst's assessments of users' information processing capabilities with users' explicit report preferences. The results were not all statistically significant, but the directions of the user preferences were all consistent with the psychological test results.

## 5. Implications

The proposed report design and implementation process was tested in a specific organizational context. Ideally, the development of user behavioral profiles should have normative implications for design guidelines which generalize over many organizational settings. Research in the behavioral sciences and organizational behavior, however, indicates a need for a contingency theory approach to information systems design problems, i.e., consideration of person, task and environment, rather than generalized theories. Thus, the authors suggest that user behavioral profiles should be evaluated within the context of each organization rather than attempting to extrapolate decision types out of a particular context.

In contrast to conducting interviews with users, the authors used psychological tests for the development of user behavioral profiles to aid in the information systems design. The use of the methodology represented in Figure 1 is an attempt to enhance the effectiveness of communication between an information system and its users. If information appropriate to user task responsibilities has been identified and used in the design process, communications from an information system should better motivate a user toward behavior which is congruent with an organization's objectives. Thus, the psychological test methodology should promote both effective information systems-user communication and improved organizational control. The results of this exploratory field study are encouraging, and hopefully will encourage other information systems researchers and practitioners to further test this methodology.

## Appendix

The decision to use psychological tests to measure user information processing style, implementation apprehension and task/social structure attitudes is primarily dependent upon three considerations. The first consideration is user attitudes toward psychological testing. A systems analyst must first informally determine if users perceive such tests as personal threats or useless exercises. Neutral or favorable attitudes permit their use. A second consideration is the availability of expertise to select, administer and interpret tests. Systems analysts with appropriate behavioral backgrounds or consultants must participate in the process. The third consideration is the cost-effectiveness of testing as compared to interviews. The criteria suggested in Section 3 of the paper provide some guidelines for a cost-benefit analysis.

If the psychological testing approach is selected, a decision to use specially designed or standard tests must be made. Situation specific tests require design and pretesting prior to administration. This is a costly activity both in time and money. Thus, it is recommended that standard tests for identified psychological variables should be selected.

Standard tests contain closed end (e.g., true/false or multiple choice) or open end (narratives) responses. The former is preferable for machine scoring is less expensive and more objective. These tests are more amenable to group administration and thus promote a more efficient use of systems analyst time.

Each selected standard test should have acceptable validity and reliability [1,10,22]. Validity, minimization of systematic error, indicates the extent to which a given test measures an underlying construct. Further validity evaluation may be derived from the use of multiple tests which purport to measure the same psychological construct. For example, resistance to change may be measured by Flexibility, Dogmatism and Authoritarianism. Reliability, however, the minimization of random error, indicates the stability of test responses over (1) repeated measurements, (2) alternative test forms, and (3) subsets of items within the test. Most standard tests provide this information. Specimen sets are normally available from the test publishers.

The administration of tests includes many considerations $[1,7,10]$ . Materials must be secured, controlled and prepared for distribution. Verbal instructions and rapport with test takers should be thoroughly discussed with proctors.
