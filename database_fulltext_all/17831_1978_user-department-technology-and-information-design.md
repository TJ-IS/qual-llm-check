# User Department Technology and Information Design

Norman B. Macintosh and Richard L. Daft
School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Ontario,
Canada

New research findings are reported on the problem of tailoring information systems to user needs. User information requirements are found to be systematically related to the underlying technology of user departments. A typology of user department technologies and guidelines for information system design are provided.

Keywords: Work-unit technology, information system design, technology and information system model, concise, elaborate, cursory diffuse information, information utilization, information amount, information focus use.

## 1. Introduction

Information is an essential ingredient in management decision systems [2], and because information is so important, most authorities on the subject agree that information systems must be tailored to users' needs [1,11]. Indeed, if it is not so tailored, the information will not be included in decision making, and if information is not utilized even a technically well designed system may be considered a failure.

The real problem for information designers is how to identify and understand user needs. This paper reports the results of recent empirical research into the relationship between user needs and information system design. These findings are especially encouraging because they provide practical guidelines for analyzing applied information system problems. But before turning to answers provided by research, we shall consider three typical cases of information system failure.

Company Beta is a large public utility. They developed an extensive computer information system which contained large quantities of data on the cost and design of physical plant (buildings, switching centers, feeder lines, even size of wire). Based upon anticipated demand, the system could provide the best physical plant configuration for any given territory. It was installed for use in the technical sections that designed plans for construction personnel. After a while it became apparent that the information was not being used by technicians in the design of plans, but no one understood why.

Company Epsilon is a nation-wide advertising firm. The new controller spent two years developing and implementing a computer-based expense budget system that tracked both target and actual expenses for each client for about twenty-five expense categories. Every month, account executives were provided with gross billings and detailed expense print-outs for each client. A year after the system was installed the controller learned that account executives ignored these reports.

Company Omega, a clothing company, manufactured innerwear (underwear, socks) and outerwear (sweaters, jackets). The information systems group developed econometric computer simulation models to predict sales and establish production targets and schedules. The models were based upon a large data base of past sales, style changes, competitors' sales, economic conditions and other related information. The computer models worked well for the innerwear plant, but for outerwear the results were a near disaster, resulting in large unsold inventories of some items and large unfilled orders for others.

These three examples of information system failures illustrate the issue. Why aren't apparently sound information systems such as these more successful? Some traditional views may provide a partial answer.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Traditional approaches

The literature contains three types of explanations for information system failures like those cited above.

One explanation is that system designers fail to take into account the needs of individual managers. The recommendation is that a thorough user needs analysis is essential when designing new information systems. Does the manager prefer a lot of information or a little [7]? Does he have a high or low tolerance for ambiguity [6]? Is the style of leadership autocratic or laissez-faire [9,10]? What about the manager's age and educational background [4]? The logic is that we must tailor-make the system to the style of individual managers because they use the information.

Another approach focuses on the implementation of change. Information systems may fail because designers ignored the change dynamics [3,11,13]. The designer must see himself as an agent of change in order to succeed. The logic is twofold. First, users tend to resist change. A change implementation strategy should be part of the total design package. Second, a new information system causes operations to be seen in a new light, especially by people in authority who may begin to evaluate the existing operation in more critical and questioning detail. Since the status quo is threatened, users may be suspicious of new systems and may resist them. Consequently the information system designer must wear two hats—one as an information system specialist and one as a specialist at implementing organizational change.

The third traditional approach argues that problems are inherent in formal information systems. Formalized information tends to leave out tangible details, is slow in arriving, and often contains inaccurate data [12]. This argument is true to some extent of most information systems. It does take time to assemble and process data, much data has to be aggregated, and some noise inevitably creeps in. System designers should work to improve information relevance and timeliness and to decrease system noise.

These three views on information design are important. They shed light on the organizational setting of information systems. Manager's personal traits are important and should not be overlooked. New information systems often are linked to changes in behavior patterns, so change strategies need formulation. In addition, relevance, timeliness and accuracy should be considered by designers. Yet these ideas have been tried and information systems still fail. Why? Because these factors are only part of the story.

## 2.2. Work-unit technology

In our investigation of information systems in a variety of organizations we discovered a central factor that apparently has been overlooked in conventional thinking. And systematic study suggests the same result: work-unit technology places a critical constraint on the design of the information system. There is a relationship between the technology of a work-unit and 'the amount and type of information it requires to perform effectively.

Simon defines technology as the knowledge of how to do things and how to accomplish organizational goals [15]. Thompson defines technology along similar lines as the beliefs about cause/effect relationships to produce desired outcomes [16]. Technology, then, includes the knowledge, procedures and techniques used to perform a given organizational task.

Work-unit technology can be analyzed along two dimensions [14]: (1) the degree to which the task process is understood (task knowledge) and (2) the variety involved in the task. Task variety is the frequency of unexpected problems that occur during task activities. Task understanding pertains to the availability of concrete knowledge about work-unit activities. When the work-unit task is well understood, participants can typically follow objective, computational procedures that may involve the use of instructions, manuals, or standards. When work-unit activities are not well understood, there is no objective, computation procedure to follow. Participants will tend to rely upon accumulated experience and judgement.

The two dimensions of technology combine to form the basis for four categories of technology - programmable, technical-professional, craft, and research. These four categories are shown in fig. 1.

Programmable technologies are well understood and have little task variety. The work is typically routine. Examples include assembly line work, fast food outlets, clerical work in bank branches, and payroll departments in government agencies. Technical-professional technologies also tend to be well understood, but the work contains high variety. Examples include tax accountants, engineers, legal firms, medical diagnostic units, and computer programming. Craft activities normally require on-the-job experience and training because established procedures and techniques do not exist. Craft technologies include master chefs, fine glassware manufacturers, wire tasters, musical groups and much managerial work. Finally, research technologies are characterized by high variety but established procedures are absent, such as in space and missile projects, industrial R&D, social science research units, and government policy analysis groups.

## 2.3. Information requirements and technology

Two characteristics of information have been related to technology. The first is the amount of information processed during execution of organizational tasks and decisions. This characteristic has been studied by other researchers: Galbraith [8], for example, related the amount of information processed to changing levels of uncertainty; Driver and Mock [7] related information amount to personal decision styles.

The other characteristic is information focus, which refers to the precision of meaning conveyed by information. Single focus information is clear, specific and typically results in a single interpretation by users. Multiple focus information, on the other hand, tends to be imprecise and ambiguous, and lends itself to different and perhaps conflicting interpretations.

Two propositions describe how these two characteristics of information are expected to be related to technology. The first proposition is that task understanding will be related to information focus. Tasks that are well understood will employ single focus information whereas poorly understood tasks will tend to employ multiple focus information. The rationale is that when cause-effect relationships are well understood, information can be developed which is precise and unambiguous. Information about well understood work-unit activities will tend to be specific and accurate and will indicate the correct response or at least the correct procedure to use when a problem arises. However, when cause-effect relationships are not well understood, it is more difficult for participants to exchange specific, single focus information. Information will tend to be general and not specific. Multiple focus information typically will not specify a single correct answer or procedure to follow.

The second proposition is that work-unit task variety will be related to amount of information used by participants. The reasoning is that when problems or unexpected situations are few, work is processed with few decisions. Work can be preplanned [8], so day-to-day information can be relatively small and directed toward a limited set predictable applications. When variety is high, however, a greater need exists for information. The number of decisions increases and information processing requirements mount.

Many task related exceptions mean participants no longer make automatic responses to tasks [15]. Decisions tend to cover a range of problems so a broader information base is needed.

## 3. Research strategy

Twenty-four work-units in twelve organizations were surveyed with questionnaires. Work-units covered a range of departments including bank branches, engineering, industrial R&D, tax accountants, sales representatives, lawyers, clerks, social policy researchers, consultants, tradesmen, draftsmen, and auditors. The questionnaire included eleven technology questions and fourteen information system questions.\* Answers for respondents in each work-unit were combined into a work-unit score for task understanding, task variety, work-unit information amount and work-unit information focus. A third information characteristic--information massage--emerged from the data and was included in the study. I:formation massage describes whether participants used information in a quick and decisive manner or whether it was used with careful thought and deliberation.

## 4. Results

Partial correlation statistics were used to test the relationship between technology and information characteristics. This technique isolates the separate effect of each technology dimension by statistically controlling for the effect of the other dimensions.

Table 1 contains the partial correlation coefficients between task variety and work-unit information characteristics. These data indicate that a positive relationship exists between task variety and amount of information (r = 0.45). They also indicate a positive relationship between variety and information massage (r = 0.32). This latter finding indicates that variety is associated with thoughtful and careful use of information--a practice which probably reduces the uncertainty associated with frequent problem occurrence.

The findings concerning task knowledge and information characteristics are in Table 2. Information focus tends to be related to the degree of task knowledge (r = 0.46). Information in well understood work is precise and clear; information in poorly understood work tends to be imprecise and ambiguous.

Table 2 also indicates a strong negative relationship between task understanding and information amount (r = -0.61). This finding was not hypothesized and is something of a surprise: it means that participants in work-units characterized by well understood processes tend to use larger amounts of information. The logic is that when a conversion process is well understood it can be broken down into its component parts, and a body of knowledge builds up to handle problems that arise. So a relatively large information base which reflects this body of knowledge and techniques serves this type of task. By contrast, when the task is not well understood, an accurate information system is difficult to develop and process. Employees tend to rely on their own ability and experience to make decisions rather than on formal information.

## 5. Solving information design problems

One valuable aspect of the Figure 2 model of information system design is that it can be used by system designers to determine user needs for information. Let us return to the three examples of information system failure described at the beginning of this article. In each case there was a mismatch between the underlying technology and the information system imposed upon the users.

The problem in Company Beta, the public utility, was that the technicians job had been routinized. The technicians were designing plans for routine, small construction jobs that had few problems. The elaborate computer based information system was suited for large-scale engineering projects that contained new applications and many problems. After understanding this, the company removed the information system from the technicians. A new engineering planning unit was also created that used the computerized information system to prepare long range construction plans for each geographic territory served by the company.

The problem in Company Epsilon, the advertising agency where a detailed budget information system was used to monitor account expenses and revenues, was that a detailed information system was designed for a craft technology. Many aspects of advertising campaigns — consumer tastes, winning clients, creating new advertising themes — are not well understood. Account personnel have experience with these intangible factors. They know on the basis of training and experience how to win clients and create campaigns. They will tend to ignore budget targets, especially expense targets. Different personnel have different expenditure styles. The firm later dropped the detailed budget in favor of a general information system that reported actual costs and gross billings for each account. Gross billings were critical to agency success, and personnel were allowed to spend money in their own way to increase billings. The cursory information system, although less precise than the one it replaced, was successful.

In the case of Company Omega, the clothing firm, demand for innerwear was stable and well understood, so the computer simulation model could predict sales, establish production targets and schedule plant operations. The demand for outerwear, by contrast, was not well understood. Styles and demand varied continuously, and no one really understood why. The elaborate econometric model was useless because the true causes could not be identified and included in the model. In this type of situation it is better to trust experience and judgement, which is what the company finally did for outerwear. Key decision makers made personal contact with a few store buyers, and they visited stores to see what was selling. Based on this cursory information and their own experience, decision makers decided on styles and production levels. Forecasting outerwear demand as a craft decision yielded better results than the econometric models.

## 6. Design recommendations

Figuring out the best design for an information system is a challenging task. Our research, however, indicates that tailoring the information system to user needs can be aided by consideration of the following guidelines.

6.1. Focus on the technology of the responsibility centre rather than on characteristics of individual managers.

The relationship between the information system and individual managers is more complex than previously realized. Information systems which are tailored to meet the personal needs of managers will not necessarily be useful to the organization. But when the information system is congruent with technology requirements, it will serve managers well.

Technology is an enduring characteristic of a department, and technology represents the pattern of problems with which managers deal. Systems that are tailored to a single person or a single problem tend to have a short useful life. Managers shift around from position to position; specific problems come and go. Besides, managers frequently do not know what information they need. Managers in highly uncertain situations frequently will grab at any information to help them make a sound decision. But the highly uncertain situations are the ones where specific, detailed information will tend to be inaccurate; to rely on it would be a mistake. In these situations managers should rely on personal judgements, their own and others, and on informal information to reach a good decision.

One organization asked and gave each manager the information he said he wanted. The MIS department worked months processing information until they discovered most of the information went unused. Then managers received only what the MIS department thought they needed. Neither approach worked. An analysis of the underlying needs based upon technology was lacking.

6.2. Do not oversystematize by establishing large, data-based systems for technologies that do not need large information systems.

This seems to be the hardest lesson for many information system specialists to learn. Large information systems do not always help, and they can even cause problems if they are pushed onto the wrong technology. Overloading managers with poor quality information is recognized today as a major information system problem. When a task is not well understood, such as outerwear demand for Company Omega, a small amount of general information is appropriate. A cursory system worked well whereas an elaborate one did not.

## 6.3. At the same time, do not undersystematize.

This problem occurs less frequently in these days of information overload brought on by computers, data bases, and rapid information transmittal. But it does happen. When the technology is well understood, and when problems occur often, a high volume information system should be developed to support managers.

Most technical-professional technologies are suited to such large information systems. Engineering is an example of a technology that derives many benefits from an elaborate information system. Frequent problems arise in most engineering projects, whether the project pertains to new products, buildings or equipment. Having access to a large data base that represents earlier project experiences and known engineering relationships is useful, and such data will be used frequently by engineering personnel.

An example of a successful elaborate information system is QUICKLAW, developed at Queen's University, Canada. It is composed of 35 different data bases such as all Supreme Court of Canada reports from 1830 to the present and all International Legal Treaties. Modern information retrieval techniques were incorporated into the system so that subscribers, say international lawyers, could access and interact with the data base from computer terminals. By identifying the specific subject matter, such as demurrage, the international lawyer is provided with a list of all references on that subject. He can then select references for scanning and have pertinent information printed for future referral. This system provides precise information of an extremely large volume. The system matches well the technical-professional work of lawyers. \*

6.4. Technologies vary widely within organizations, so a uniform, company wide information system will not suit everyone.

The assumption that all responsibility centers are similar, or the temptation to ignore differences in order to simplify the information system reports, is a vital mistake. As Dearden [5] warned, so-called total management information systems are a myth. Companies should have different styles of information systems serving the requirements of different functions. The dual information systems used in Omega, the clothing firm described above, demonstrates this point. So does the successful use of the elaborate information system by planning engineers in Beta, the public utility, after the system went unutilized by technicians.

6.5. Try to match the information system to the user's technology, then worry about being a change agent.

Change dynamics are important, but an information system that is congruent with the technology of the responsibility center has an excellent chance of proving its worth, and there are differing methods of levering the system into the work-unit. Most managers are on the lookout for ways to improve performance, and a good information system will usually be adopted—if it works.

In one instance an information system was developed that could help branch managers of a North American office equipment organization reduce service calls by a significant amount. Branch managers had the option to take or leave the information system, but if they adopted it, they had to pay a monthly rental fee. The system was designed to help the branch manager analyze and improve service call performance. At first branch managers were reluctant to use this system because of the cost. The information system department made it a point to demonstrate that branches which purchased the system would reduce costs and make higher profits than those that did not. Not long thereafter nearly all branches bought the system. Implementation was a relatively minor problem once the right information system was developed. When an information system matches the technology and can thereby benefit the user-managers, resistance to implementation will begin to disappear.

## 6.6. Try to avoid the "big" mistakes.

Sometimes it can be difficult to determine precisely the nature of work-unit technologies. In our research, we spent considerable time developing accurate measurements of technology, but refined measurements are not necessary for most information system applications. A major use of the approach described here simply should be to avoid the big, costly information system errors: do not waste time and money imposing an elaborate information system upon craft activities; do not expect technical-professional tasks to struggle along without adequate information system support; do not use precise, voluminous information in a research setting; and do not provide invasive reports and information back-ups to people engaged in routine work.

## 7. Future research

In our research we attempted to explore the relationship between work-unit technology and information system requirements. The findings suggest that a technology focus may be a promising development for problems of information system design. It does seem to work. But many questions remain to be answered. For example, are there additional dimensions of technology that can be used to better understand information needs? How do styles of managers fit into the technology framework? And what affect do other organizational variables such as size and level of hierarchy have on the technology-information system relationship? At this point in our thinking it seems that the two dimensions of work-unit technology discussed here place important constraints on the information system. Within those constraints, however, the information system may be tailored to other factors, which may include manager traits and practical processing problems. Additional research is needed to learn how to balance these factors to achieve the best information system configuration.
