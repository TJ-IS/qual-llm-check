# Computer Usage by Small and Medium Sized European Firms: An Empirical Study

L.D. Neidleman \*

School of Business, San Jose University, San Jose, CA 95192, USA

The central concern of this study is: Why aren't more small and medium sized firms utilizing computers? A secondary point considered is: For firms that do have computer systems, what were the chief indicators and reasons for selecting a computer system? These questions were explored, primarily via questionnaires in France, West Germany and the Netherlands. A total of 267 valid responses were analyzed. A major conclusion of the study is that many small and medium sized firms do not utilize computers due to ignorance of their capabilities and a misunderstanding of the cost/benefit factors involved.

Keywords: Questionnaire, computer system, HAVES, HAVE NOTS, knowledgeability factor

## 1. Introduction

This paper presents findings from a cross-national study of computer use in small and medium sized business firms in three Western European countries. The central objective is to help explain:

1. Why some firms do not utilize computer systems, and

2. For those firms with computer systems, the selection criteria and the degree of satisfaction with the choice.

In examining the first question, there are a number of factors which come to mind, e.g., cost, ignorance, fear, lack of qualified personnel, lack of time, etc. However, the intent of this work is not to impose a solution but rather to learn the perceptions of the small and medium sized organizations themselves: What are the most important factors as they see them? Are there any common parameters by country or by activity?

The second question is a very practical one which confronts a diversity of organizations. It can be restated as: How do you choose a computer system for the particular needs of the organization? It is faced with any number of choices: a minicomputer, a time sharing service, facilities management, service bureau, medium scale computer, etc. Each salesman promises that his system is the answer. How does one choose? What parameters should be considered? What rules of thumb can be established: volume, dollars, complexity, timing, etc.?

This study examined the answer to these questions by using both questionnaires and interviews. The selected firms were grouped into two general classes: manufacturing and nonmanufacturing. The main variables considered were: size (employees), sales, amount spent on computers, and a knowledge factor (as discussed below).

For the more knowledgeable selector, there are sources that describe methodology and many methods to use; for example, there are professional publications such as Auerbach Special Reports, Data-pro Reports, Datamation, EDP Weekly, and EDP Analyzer. W.F. Sharpe [2] discusses the selection problem from an economics point of view. While his book is well written and contains a great deal of information, it is an advanced text and is, perhaps, only suitable for the most sophisticated potential user or owner of a computer system (Usually a large organization). Joslin [1] gives a more fundamental approach to the problem. In his text, he explains some principles which can be used and applies these to a particular case. W.A. Weiner [3] gives a very straight forward checklist approach which can be useful as a guide.

The above sources are fine for the selector who is aware of their existence and who has the skill to utilize them, but what about the person who is less knowledgeable, such as is generally found in smaller organization? The whole area of data processing and computers may seem too complicated and bewildering. Indeed, even for the person well versed in the field, it is difficult to be aware of the latest technologies and better products: hardware and software products change rapidly. There are also advances in peripherals, communications, and terminals. Today's equipment makes yesterday's obsolete in both capability and cost. Thus, for the neophyte and/or smaller potential user, the question is not how to select but should I select a computer system? While many firms have chosen to do so, many have not. Perhaps many have not even posed the question.

In 1976, an article appeared [4] about a survey conducted by Olivetti in Frankfurt. In a series of advertisements in a computer magazine, Olivetti posed the question: "Why don't you use a computer?" They received over 3,000 answers from companies of about 50 people (ninety percent were about this size). The responses were: costs are too high (61%), company is too small for a computer (54%), nobody in the company understands computers (27%), there is no computer available with the appropriate size for our company (24%), there would be no cost reduction with computers (22%), and a change to computer applications would be too difficult (15%).

Because microprocessors and minicomputers make the cost and feasibility of automation low to even the smallest of firms or organizations, it is important to examine this large segment of nonusers. It is important from a market view, of course, but also to the smaller user.

The findings of the study reported here are presented in three sections. First we describe the individual samples which were gathered in three countries: France, West Germany, and the Netherlands. Then we summarize the findings by country and as an entire group. Within each of these classifications, the data are further divided into HAVES (those with computer systems) and HAVE NOTS (those without). Finally we summarize the important points and make recommendations for future study.

## 2.1. French Responses

Since CNRS was the co-sponsor of the project, a major effort was made in France. A small fraction of the questionnaire returns were from the provinces, the majority, however, and all interviews were carried out in Paris.

The addresses for the mailing were obtained from four different organizations: AFCET, Control Data Corporation, the Association of Linen Suppliers, and the Chambre de Commerce et d'Industrie de Paris. Eighty-eight percent were provided by the latter.

AFCET is the major French professional computing society. All responders to this mailing were computer users, including such companies as Air France, Renault, and Fiat.

The majority of the Control Data respondents were small companies in the Paris area. About twenty responses were from Grenoble.

The Association of Linen Suppliers represents French firms which supply work uniforms and laundry services for a number of French organizations.

The Chambre de Commerce et d'Industrie de Paris (CCI) provides services and keeps records on the small and medium sized firms in Paris. Five major industries or activities were selected: fabrication of precision materials and instruments, fabrication of drinks and alcohol, the dairy industry, the textile industry and transportation. An attempt was made to study these same five activities in West Germany and the Netherlands but this was only partially successful. Since all five activities were not sufficiently represented in the returns (see Tables 7 and 8), the activities were grouped into two categories – manufacturing and nonmanufacturing.

## 2.2. Dutch Responses

The organizations which provided the mailing lists in the Netherlands are: STIM (Stichting Informatieverwerking Midden- en Kleinbedrijf), NIVRA (Netherlands Instituut van Registeraccounts), and the Nederlandse Orde van Accountants. Each contributed about the same number of addresses.

The STIM organization is concerned with small and medium sized firms in the Netherlands. It is somewhat analogous to the C.C.I. of Paris in function. The entire STIM mailing was to the textile industry throughout the Netherlands.

The remainder of the Netherland sample was distributed through the two professional accounting bodies – NIVRA and the Nederlandse Orde van Accountants. These two groups are essentially parallel to CPAs and regular accountants. It was originally hoped that a higher return rate would occur through these bodies, but this did not occur.

## 2.3. West German Responses

The German sample was provided by Control Data Corporation, Frankfurt. A total of 500 questionnaires were mailed with 56 (11.2%) valid returns. Although both users and nonusers of computer were contacted, only those who utilize computer systems responded. The German repliers mainly use IBM computers.

## 4. HAVE NOTS

In order to weigh the accuracy of the replies, a "knowledgeability factor" was computed for each firm. This was done by assigning weights to select questions such as: computers are unreliable, the biggest cost in a computer system is the hardware (physical equipment), the price of computer systems is increasing, and software is inexpensive.

These were then used to determine a single number which measures how accurately (i.e., knowledgeably) the firm responded. The mean for each sub-sample was calculated and used to separate the more knowledgeable.

The result for all nonusers, both separately and as a group, was clear — the more knowledgeable felt they needed computers and the less knowledgeable felt they did not. This point was one of the most significant of the study.

Table 4 shows the responses to several questions obtained from the nonusers. These questions were based on the replies of the Olivetti study [4]. It should be observed that in all cases the majority opinion reinforces the Olivetti survey. Although as a group the majority (54.2%) of nonusers did not feel they needed a computer system (see Tables 5 and 6), they did, as question two of Table 4 shows, recognize that their firms could utilize computers in carrying out their company operations.

## 5. HAVES

In 1971, the Diebold Research Program did a survey of 154 of the largest industrial and manufacturing companies in the United States. Their study found that the more profitable companies spend a larger percentage of their resources on the data processing function. For all the firms surveyed, they found that the average data processing budget was about 0.9% of corporate sales. Using the median amounts of Table 2, the percentages of dollars spent per month on computers to the total gross sales for these samples are: France 1.8%, the Netherlands 0.02%, and West Germany 0.1%. The French figure is the least accurate since fully 75% of these had sales replies which were in the greater than \$900,000 category. The Dutch and German figures are fairly reliable. The Dutch and German figures indicate what is probably true for a more general European population – the Europeans spend less on computers per month than do the largest industrial and manufacturing companies in North America.

Breaking this sub-sample down further revealed that 38.9% of the French respondents used IBM equipment. The next largest percentage (19.5%) was CII-HB (Compagnie International pour l'Informatique – Honeywell Bull), the (1976) French-Honeywell combine. Thirteen percent of the sample uses Burroughs, while about 8% uses Univac and SEMS respectively.

Datamation [5] pointed out that CII-HB currently has about 27% of the French main frame business while IBM has about 54%. Our data supports these numbers. It is a French national policy to increase the percentage held by French firms. Because of this, it is increasingly difficult for government bodies and firms to purchase non-French computers.

In the case of IBM users in France, the median numbers of employees was 775 and the average was 3169. The average monthly amount spent on computers by IBM users was \$ 57,200 versus \$ 34,000 for the CII-HB users and \$ 21,700 for the Burroughs users. The median and average number of employees for CII-HB and Burroughs were, respectively, 650 median, 2197 average and 370 median, 880 average. Thus, once again the size of the organization correlates with the amount spent on computers. These data also imply that the larger firms tend to by IBM.

Approximately 33% of the French respondees had minicomputers and 20.8% had micros. The questionnaire also asked for the acquisition mode: purchase, rent from the manufacturer, or third party lease. The IBM users were distributed: 57% rent, 17% buy, 17% third party lease and 10% no response. CII-HB users were: 40% buy, 13% rent, 33% third party lease and 14% no response. As a group, the acquisition modes were: 45% buy, 35% rent, 12% third party lease and 8% no response.

From Table 2, it can be seen that there was no significant difference in France between type of organization and amount spent per month on computers. However, the gross sales for nonmanufacturers was significantly higher than those of the manufacturers.

HAVES were asked: What were the overriding considerations in selecting the system? They were asked to rank order 17 factors such as: politics, benchmarks, costs, vendor reputation, available software, simulation, maintenance, etc. These factors were then ordered and the rankings averaged. The first seven factors ranked in order of most significance for the French sample are: (1) company policy, (2) respond to needs, (3) politics, (4) cost, (5) industry standard, (6) available software, (7) vendor reputation. The noteworthy thing here is that cost only ranked fourth.

As with the HAVE NOTS, the HAVES were asked questions which could be answered with general comments. About 64% of the french responded to the question: What were the main selection problems? The predominant answer was the difficulty in getting information. Equally significant was a concern for compatibility and conversion. This latter point forced many firms to stay with the same vendor. Other points mentioned were: problems with the salesman, difficult to evaluate performance, and manufacturers did not understand the firm's applications.

The following distributions occurred in West Germany: 59% IBM, 25% NIXDORF, 21% Digital Equipment, 14% Univac, 13% Siemens and 9% Hewlett Packard. In (5) it is stated that IBM has more than 60% of the West German market, Digital Equipment has, in numbers, 28% of the minicomputer market, and Siemens has 17.5% of the main frame market and 12% (in numbers) of the mini market. The distribution is thus very similar.

IBM users had a median number of employees of 3400 and an average of 4157. The average monthly cost of computers by the German IBM users was \$45,900. The median number of employees for Digital Equipment users was 1750 and the average was 2537. No other amounts were given for cost per month because the other systems are used in conjunction with IBM or else do not occur in sufficient numbers. Of the West German responses, 48.2% had minicomputers and 32.1% microcomputers.

The acquisition modes for several of the computer suppliers were: IBM - 16% buy, 79% rent, 3% third party lease and 2% no response; NIXDORF - 50% buy and 50% rent; Digital Equipment Corp. - 59% buy, 8% lease and 33% no response.

From Table 2 it is clear that all numbers (sales, monthly computer dollars, employees) are greater for the nonmanufacturers in this sub-sample.

Factors such as vendor support and vendor reputation were not viewed as significant as the above.

Sixty-eight percent of the respondents commented on the selection problems. The majority view was that it was difficult to compare systems through bench-marking or independent technical evaluation. Several of the firms, because of compatibility/conversion problems or simply because they were satisfied, stayed with their original vendor — frequently IBM. ## 6. Conclusion

Several of our conclusions are the same as those reached for nonusers of computers in the survey conducted by the German Olivetti Company, Frankfurt. Furthermore, our study indicates that these results appear to hold in other countries. The most significant finding is that small and medium sized firms that do not employ computers do so primarily out of ignorance of the economics and potential technical and operational benefits. The majority view of all nonusers in both surveys is that computers cost too much, and that they are too small to use computers.

Two other significant points for the nonusers of this study are:

1. A strong correlation exists between (a) the respondents' perceived need for a computer system and (b) their belief that computers are necessary to compete effectively now and in the future.

2. The vast majority did not feel that many jobs would be lost if their organization acquired a computer system.

From the French nonusers, there is the suggestion that volume of data processed and company growth are prime factors in a decision to automate. However, more study is necessary to verify this. It would be useful if this information could be quantified — by activity, for example.

While studying present users and owners of computer systems was not the main objective of this work, several points of interest were observed. As stated earlier, the main question addressed to the users was: What were the major problems encountered and criteria used in selecting your computer? The findings are:

— Cost, available software and hardware in that order were ranked as the most important selection criteria amongst the Dutch and Germans. The French ranked these factors high also but ranked politics and company policy ahead of software and hardware considerations. This may well be a reflection of French national policy.

\- A common problem in the selection process was in obtaining good technical information and evaluating what was available.

\- As a group, computer users spend far less of their gross sales on computers than some of their counterparts in the United States. A more exhaustive survey would be necessary to conclude that this is generally true throughout Europe.

It is apparent from this study that the "word" about data processing is not getting to the small and medium sized firms in the countries studied. Why is this the case? One could offer numerous reasons: not enough salesmen, insufficient incentives for salesmen, insufficient advertising, poor literature, lack of customer interest, etc. To pinpoint the causes, more detailed studies on a larger scale should be conducted.

Are there common parameters which can be determined which will assist both the seller and buyer of computer systems? Are these parameters independent or dependent of industry activity? Are they a function of an organization (sales, size, volume of paperwork, prc. itability or some other factors)? If such rules of thumb can be devised, they will provide an answer to the smaller firm which asks: Do I need a computer?

But this study has barely scratched the surface. In order to get comprehensive results, it is necessary to have sufficient resources (time, people and money). In the Olivetti survey, the magnitude of the untapped computer market for the small and medium sized firms is pointed out; for example, in West Germany only 7,500 of the 122,000 medium sized businesses (6.1%) and only 28,000 of the 1,870,000 small businesses (1.5%) have their own computer. It would be interesting to compare these with data from other countries.

Although the majority of nonusers did not feel that computers were necessary to compete (either now or in the future), I believe generally, however, that the following statement is closer to the truth. To compete effectively in the future (if not the present!) the small and medium sized user will no longer have the luxury of asking: Should I automate? Competitors will be automated and if he does not, he will either have to settle for a very narrow segment of his market, be limited in his growth and profits, or even be forced out of business.
