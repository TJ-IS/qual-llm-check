# Needed: Executive Awareness of Information Resources

Isaac L. Auerbach

President, Auerbach Publishers Inc., Pennsauken, N.J., USA

and

Vladimir Slamecka

Professor, School of Information and Computer Science, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, GA, USA

The authors argue that the development of international information markets requires a substantially heightened awareness on the part of executive decision makers of the characteristics of information and the potential of the information industry. They urge that the private and public sectors mount programs that will (1) educate executives in industry, government and academia on the value of scientific and technical information systems and services, (2) formulate strategies that view information and information systems as tradable commodities for national economic and diplomatic goals; and (3) develop marketing techniques for such commodities that take into account the social, economic and political environments of the clients. While these suggestions are aimed at the United States, they are believed to have an almost global relevance.

Keywords: Information industry, information policy, information markets, scientific and technical information, education and training

This growing awareness on the part of the U.S. can be attributed, at least partially, to the mounting pressure from several international resources.

The exchange of scientific and technical information is not a novel concept; it has long been an established part of the process of international communication in science. This international process, however, is assuming an added dimension, caused by the realization that the accumulation of knowledge appears to be a necessary condition for socioeconomic development [1]. To the extent that such accumulation is available in developed countries, the less developed nations naturally contemplate ways of acquiring it, not unlike a commodity one obtains through trade or barter.

Whether knowledge can be so acquired and purposefully applied by the buyer is not clear. What can be so acquired, however, is one constituent element of knowledge – recorded data and information, particularly scientific and technical information. Because so much scientific and technical information is a product of each nation's investment in R & D, a growing number of countries are beginning to view information as an important national resource. The precise value of this resource is difficult to determine and agree upon, however; partly because of this, the commodity is also assuming great importance in diplomatic negotiations and international relations.

In advanced nations, the perceived value and utility of scientific and technical information have spurred the formation of a new “information industry.” Members of this industry are concerned, generally speaking, with the development of information systems and with services for acquiring, organizing, managing, packaging, delivering and interpreting recorded data. There are already signs of impending international competition among the information industries of these countries; their members reason that insofar as economic development is tied to advanced science and technology, the potential markets for their products and markets are vast. This reasoning is strongly encouraged by the demands for scientific and technical information (as well as for other components of knowledge such as know-how) that emanate from the developing countries.

Although largely dormant for the present, the information markets are literally being defined and created in high level international discussions. The global planning for the 1979 U.N. Conference on Science and Technology for Development, at which the characterization of information trade is likely to be a central issue, is in full swing. At the same time a number of parallel issues have arisen which will have a direct effect on information markets; for instance, the Council of Europe has recently directed its experts to develop draft conventions on the international transfer of data. The U.S., which has no role in the Council of Europe, has formed an Ad Hoc Inter-Agency Task Force on Transnational Data Flow, under the auspices of the National Security Council, to consider this issue.

At the base of these developments is the critical problem of the evolution or development of socially prudent, economically viable, and internationally equitable information markets for our world.

## 2. The issue

A matter of primary significance to the development of global information markets is the degree to which decision makers in individual nations understand this area. The question being raised is the following: how aware are government and business leaders of the value of, and the opportunities for, information systems and services in international trade and diplomacy? Such awareness is crucial for both groups: for business leaders, in order to support and take advantage of the developing information markets; for government leaders, in order to promote the use of the national information resources to the greatest benefit of society. Such awareness is also crucial for both the developed and the developing nations' government and business leaders who will jointly fashion the principles of global information transfer.

Does such awareness exist among the government and business leaders of the United States? Based on our intensive involvement over a significant period of time with top management in government, industry and academia, we have a strong impression that:

\- Senior U.S. government officials do not appreciate the importance of scientific and, particularly, technological information as a significant national resource that might be used in international trade and diplomacy.

• Academicians, as well as members of the National Academy of Science and the National Academy of Engineering, are ill-informed about the major information banks and services that have evolved during the past decade to aid them in their work.

\- Top research, engineering and other industry executives display a similar lack of understanding of the value of information systems and services, and of their potential as marketable commodities.

\- Most of the top U.S. companies have no corporate policy on their information resources. A recent survey found that $84\%$ of the top corporate executives in companies with annual sales of over 90 million dollars would be hard-pressed to specify even the barest outline of a policy on information resources.

Our equally extensive involvement with international organizations, professional and governmental, and our firsthand knowledge of individual countries at all levels of development, lead us to suspect that a parallel situation obtains around the world. Thus we hope that the following suggestions addressed to the United States will be reinterpreted by others in a manner appropriate to their countries.

## 3. Need for action

We see a need for a sustained effort to educate business, government and academia on our national information resources. In our view, the broad effort of research and education should be conducted along two major lines.

One line of effort should seek to define the probable domestic ramifications of U.S. participation in international information commerce, and to formulate alternative strategies for the worldwide marketing of U.S. scientific and technological resources. These strategies should be based on the differing social, political and economic environments in potential recipient countries. Building upon the work already sponsored by the National Science Foundation and the National Academy of Sciences-National Academy of Engineering, a special task force of members from the information industry, government, business and academia should be brought together to analyze these environments and determine how best to address their respective needs. In this way, the U.S. will be in a better position to understand the utility of its services and products and to market them more successfully.

The other line of effort, by specially constituted groups within the information industry, should be directed toward creating and organizing appropriate informal and formal expositions, presentations, reports, and similar materials. These would be designed to alert directors and managers in government, business and education to the value of technological information systems and services. The educational effort must be broad and must incorporate the following elements: the basic terminology of the information field; the nature of the U.S. information resource, and the range of information services available; the properties of effective information transfer processes in countries of different socioeconomic and cultural levels; and U.S. strategies for worldwide information commerce.

## 4. Learning terminology

Tutorial material on information terminology should clarify certain basic concepts. This will enable executives to be more comfortable in discussing technological information as a commodity in trade and diplomacy. Examples of such basic concepts are:

• Data, information, knowledge

• Information as a concept, resource, and commodity

• Information sources

\- Processes of generating, acquiring, transmitting, transforming, and using information

• Information transfer

• Information banks

• Information systems and services

• Information use within various communities and clientele groups

• Information professionals

• Information industry

• The professional information societies

5. Inventory of information resources and commodities

In offering U.S. information resources abroad, executives must be aware of several factors that affect the marketability of these resources:

\- The types of information commodities (i.e., specialized information systems and services) that are available in the U.S. and abroad

• The present cost of these commodities

\- The means by which these commodities can be acquired by foreign countries or regional blocs

\- The communication media options that are available

\- The information banks and data bases that exist in these overseas market areas; the U.S. information systems, if exported, that could match these.

In order for executives to gain this knowledge, present efforts to maintain a worldwide inventory of information products and services – such as AUER-BACH Publishers' Special Report – Commercially Available Information Banks [2], Aslib and EUSIDIC's Data Bases in Europe [3], and UNESCO's Information Systems and Networks for Technology Transfer [4] – need to be significantly enhanced. If these inventories are maintained and expanded, overseas markets – and competitors – could be identified more easily.

## 6. Analyzing foreign information markets

It cannot be emphasized strongly enough that, for information services to be of maximum value and utility, the unique social, cultural, political, and economic environment of a recipient country must be taken into account. Information services that are not tailored to the environment of their users may appear valueless. Analyzing a country's level of development and meeting its unique needs is a difficult task for U.S. information systems and services – most are designed for a technologically advanced clientele; yet such analyses are indispensable.

In each foreign environment it is also essential to determine whether there are subject experts and information professionals who can use U.S. information effectively. The transfer of scientific and technical information is of little value unless there are people who know how to interpret, use, and extrapolate from this information. The number and quality of such people will have a profound effect on the value of the information services provided. If such native expertise does not exist, the U.S. may need to establish appropriate training programs for local personnel.

## 7. Conclusion

Clearly, the development of international or global information markets and customs has a very high priority in a world bent on achieving socioeconomic progress. In this communication we are concerned with an indispensable prerequisite to such a development – the raising of the appropriate decision makers' understanding of information as a resource and commodity, and of the potential for these markets. We believe these observations to be applicable to most countries.
