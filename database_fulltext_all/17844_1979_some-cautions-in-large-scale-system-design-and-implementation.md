# Some Cautions in Large-Scale System Design and Implementation

John A. Gosden

Vice President, The Equitable Life Assurance Society of the United States, 1285 Avenue of the Americas, New York, New York 10019, USA

Virtually all large-scale system implementations will benefit from the application of certain management techniques. Awareness (and avoidance) of erroneous assumptions as to system requirements and objectives, data reliability, human interface, and testing methods is important. Strategies such as phased implementation, use of pilots and packaged software, and categorizing and reducing risks are useful. Knowledgeable selection of applicable developments in computer architecture and serious use of planning and control techniques are advocated.

Today's trends include well-informed users, better hardware and networking facilities, analysis and study of alternatives, and heavy user involvement in the entire system design and implementation process.

Keywords: Large-scale systems, user requirements, data reliability, human interface, large system testing, phased implementation, packaged software, risk reduction, automomous subsystems, co-tingency planning, installation planning, system management trends, networking facilities, hardware

## 1. Introduction

First, this paper is not a survey. Most of the material I shall discuss comes from systems I have had something to do v'h, as designer, implementor, or manager. Second, I am only going to discuss automated systems. Third, I define a large system in this context as one whose development requires at least 20 people working fulltime for a year and whose costs run to at least a million dollars. Typically I'm talking about systems that were about three times that size; some began as million-dollar projects and ended up costing three million. Such projects involve many more than 20 people and well over a year and thus create many problems that one cannot deal with in a straightforward way.

I've tried to organize this discussion under a very simple set of headings. I shall begin with mistakes, or "what we all learn from". Then I shall discuss some of the strategies that one uses in building large-scale systems, followed by comments about architecture, methods and techniques, and finally, my prediction of trends.

## 2. Mistakes

Although it may seem trite, it is only by discussing mistakes that we can learn from our experience and perhaps avoid significant pitfalls the next time around.

The first, classic, mistake is the assumption that users know their requirements. When faced with the prospect of a very large system, even the cleverest users don't know what all of their requirements will be. The problem may simply be size, although additional factors may contribute. First of all, by the time the perceived requirements are written down, many of them have probably changed. Second, what user who's important enough to know what's important has the time to look through more than the table of contents and the executive summary? Third, the fact that we are building a system that will significantly change the way in which we work is likely to result in inherent but unpredictable requirement changes.

The second big mistake is the assumption that data is reliable. One of the major causes of system delay is insufficient built-in protection against bad data. People do not design for bad data, but they should. I sometimes suspect that in some systems we would be better off to assume values for the data than to collect it. There are many more ways for data to be wrong than right. Virtually all programs should contain input validation checks, even those that supposedly receive “legal” data from the immediately preceding program. It is probably a good idea to design a system with the philosophy that if everything is going wrong, the system should run at its best. Then if things go right (an unlikely event), the system can degrade somewhat.

Another mistaken assumption is that humans are predictable. The first group to make this mistake were those who thought that computers were going to put everyone out of work. That hasn't happened. The second group to make this mistake thought that on-line systems (using terminals) would be easy to use for anyone at all and that anything a programmer could dream up for somebody to type in could be typed in easily. And airline reservation system implementation stalled for two or three years while they re-engineered the human interfaces. That happened in the sixties, yet we're still doing the same thing; people are designing and building systems without carefully considering the problem of human interface, particularly for on-line systems where major updates are occurring. In summary, we get very different reactions from people than we expect, and there are ways to reduce the impact of such errors.

The fourth error is the assumption that specifications are complete. There is, in fact, only one complete specification for a new system: the complete system code (as compiled and running). Anything else is merely an incomplete translation or a summary, and there are holes in such translations for alternative interpretations by designers and implementors. And sooner or later, one of these holes is going to trap us.

We tend to assume that testing large systems is just an extension of testing small systems. But the fifth major error is in not realizing that testing a large system is very much more complicated. Error avoidance and recovery procedures can be especially troublesome; an error is the error detection mechanism can send you off into error recovery when there is no error, or avoid sending you there when there is one, or otherwise do the wrong thing. In such situations you don't even know what you're looking at for a long time. Many large systems go through a year of testing before they are satisfactory.

The sixth and last error is assuming that objectives remain stable. A system that takes longer than a year to implement is based on shifting sand; requirements don't stand still. Changes in laws, customer demographics, the competition, project personnel, etc., all contribute to this instability. The name of the game is change, and it is often quite dramatic.

## 3. Strategies

We must now consider the strategies used, beginning with phased implementation. Phased implementation consists of taking a big project and breaking it into formal stages so that you can recognize and evaluate milestones (requirements, design, coding, testing, etc.) as you go along. In a very big project these milestones may be three to six months apart and must therefore be re-evaluated, managerially, technically and financially, as you go along. You can – and should – reassess your cost benefits, your estimates, and your goals, and you can throw away some of the project if it's wrong. You have to look carefully at a big project every six months to decide whether the ground rules still apply.

Dividing a long-term project into stages does not necessarily address the problem of time passing and requirements changing. Thus at the start there may be low confidence that the end goal will be relevant if and when it is reached. So you try to observe a creeping commitment; that is, you try to find a way of going forward that spends small amounts of money, reserving most of the project budget until you have more confidence.

One good way of dealing with this is to begin to implement the system as a pilot, which then becomes version 1, version 2, etc. This is not the same as phased implementation, because you build your pilot. Pilots sound easy, but for very large systems they can be very, very difficult to devise in a useful way. Another good solution is to use a package. You attempt to get about 70 to 80% of the capability for about 30% of the investment. It may only do part of the job for half the cases, but you put it in and just start to use it. The objective is to get something running as soon as possible for as little money as possible.

As an example, two years ago management decided to organize the whole financial accounting system so it would be uniform throughout the company. First we did the data base design. Designers and users went through tables and accounts and identified all the data elements, reconciled the different names, designed the whole thing and found that it was going to take four years and a million dollars.

After studying the report, noting the milestones, and ascertaining that after two years and half a million dollars, our statements of account would still look the same, I offered the project team \$100,000 to build something that would produce results the first year. With this “seed money”, we used time-sharing and RPG to produce a standard year-end schedule which reduced a trial balance sheet procedure (done manually) from three days to about five minutes. The cost by the end of the year was \$90,000.

After that success, we gave them a quarter of a million for the following year. The project was so large that I didn't believe the specifications, so we changed the problem. We turned it into a creeping commitment, we got a pilot, and we are getting early payback and faster turnaround. The risk has been reduced enormously, we're moving toward our goal, and we're keeping our options open.

On one big project some time ago, we could not be sure of how our vendor's data management system software policy would change in two years. So we made three plans, actually one plan with three options, showing what we would do based on each of the vendor's three most likely actions, and we found we could defer our reactions until the vendor's choice became known.

So far I have really dealt with a very simple technique, which can be applied to anything: look for a creeping commitment; look for an early payback; look to keep options open; try to use standard packages and negotiate specific improvements. We use many packages; we spend about a million dollars a year on packages. Very few packages fit our needs closely, yet we will even buy a package for a hundred thousand dollars and spend a million to fix it up so that it does what we want. This may sound completely silly, but the alternative is often spending three million to do it from scratch and taking a year longer. The package is your pilot. It doesn't do exactly what you want, but it does something. You can run it. You can see what it does. You can show it to the user.

A good package solves problem one: the user requirement problem. If you sit down with nothing and ask what the user wants, you will get a long document. Or you can go to the user and say, I have this package, it costs a hundred thousand dollars, and if it doesn't do quite what you want, you tell me the things you really can't stand and I'll price each of the changes for you. Thus we can bargain with the users on each extra requirement separately. They may not know their requirements, but they can negotiate requirements quickly. If your system will cost five million dollars, it's hard to price all the parts. With a package, it is easier to estimate costs of additions and changes, and therefore to help the users make choices.

One of my biggest problems to date in putting up medium and large systems is the time between the business decision and its implementation. People really don't want to wait that long. Business opportunities slip by and are lost. It may be intangible, but it's a very important intangible to users. So by putting a pilot in early, we can help resolve two major problems. We can have early payback and solve the problem of getting the user to firm up on requirements.

As a manager, I adopt a very simple philosophy called protecting the downside. Simply stated, this assumes that the user will be responsible for risks related to business situations, while I assume responsibility for what I can control to provide as safe an environment as possible.

If you manage a big project with enough flexibility to leave room for choices, there are usually all kinds of marvelous opportunities. With every choice I have, I see if it will lift the bottom line (i.e., reduce risk). The “downside” is related to things I can control, and I spend time making sure no disasters occur. It looks like a very conservative way – but it works. Another way to look as it is: don’t take an unnecessary technical gamble. Suppose I have a three million dollar project and I take a 50-50 chance that will either save half a million dollars and come in early, or end in disaster. That is not really a half-million dollar gamble; that can be a billion-dollar-a-year gamble on all the business that’s residing on that system. If it’s a question of coming in early or staying within budget, it’s usually more important to come in early. So if you’re making a promise to deliver, especially on a system that will offer some sort of competitive edge, that’s what the user will remember. In such cases, it can be a terrible mistake to let the date slide in order to live within the dollar estimate; talk to the user about it beforehand, but protect yourself from failing.

## 4. Architecture

It is impossible to deal with very big systems as monoliths. We have some good examples: the operation of New York City or the Federal Government. After a while systems become so very large that there is just no way to coordinate and manage them. What we are starting to do now is not to build large hierarchical systems but to build sets of autonomous subsystems. Let me give another simple example. If I have a small company with five little units doing different things and I have a fire in one unit, the other four units will keep going, somewhat or another. The way we have been building big systems has been to lock the whole thing together, and, unfortunately, major computer architectures and operating systems almost force us into this.

With autonomous sub-systems, each one works as a stand-alone entity and will keep on running, in a degraded mode if necessary, even if the others aren't there. A good technical example is the telephone network. An early administrative example was the delegation of very wide power to governors in the Roman Empire. I sometimes wonder if the convenience of the telephone has destroyed our ability to run large systems because top managers can thus control at too fine a level of detail and time.

I had to build a system once in which three different computers would work together cooperatively. We were in a hurry, so I assigned it to three different groups. One group finished very early, and they built a shell simulating the other two so that they could start primitive testing. It was such a good idea that we built shells for each of the others; this allowed us to test each one independently before putting all three together. The next thing we found was that we had a beautiful disaster solution; if one part of the system failed while it was running, the others just locked themselves in their shells and ran until they absolutely needed the rest of the system, at which point they quiesced. Thus we serendipitously invented autonomous sub-systems with emergency stand-alone capability.

## 5. Methods and techniques

We're starting to use more and more "definable" techniques on big projects. The first is contingency planning. Systems people tend to get locked into one solution and develop it as far as possible. One of the biggest problems is to make people look at alternatives by asking a lot of "what-if" questions.

In a case involving a network, we were building a controller which was a modified version of a standalone system being developed by a vendor who might not deliver. I asked my people what we would do if the vendor did not provide the product, and they suggested talking to two other potential vendors. We did, and in about a month-and-a-half we had an alternate way. In fact, I liked the alternative so much that we scrapped the old way and adopted the contingency plan. In very large systems, it is very difficult to think of all the ways of protecting the downside. Contingency planning usually improves the testing strategy and the test plan because you think about problems: backup, recovery, etc. This leads to very thorough planning, right from the beginning.

By the time the system is close to installation, you really need a separate planning area, using techniques like PERT charts or Gantt charts, with a very detailed schedule of what people have to do. This schedule is needed because a new system is replacing something that is already running. It isn't until you start to assign sub-tasks and start to run through a very detailed plan that you realize what you can and cannot do. The installation that seemed to need a couple of weekends now requires a year – or can't be done at all.

I once installed a big system that originally was expected to be brought up over a weekend; in fact it took three and a half years. We had three million policies to cut over. To get the diverse records and merge and enter them into the system took time during which we had to freeze all activity on that part of the file. We could only afford to do this for four weeks, which only allowed time to input about 20% of the file. Then we found that our lead time between these system freezes was six months, so we could only put up 20% of the file every six months, which would take $2\frac{1}{2}$ years. In our first step, we only attempted to move 10%, because we weren't sure we could handle more, so the time stretched. What originally, three years earlier, was a “one-weekend switch”, turned out to be a $3\frac{1}{2}$ year exercise. We did not realize that it would happen until somebody sat down and did some very thorough planning.

Interface control is another method that is really becoming useful. The autonomous sub-systems that I discussed earlier could only communicate over a communication line with a one-thousand-bit block pattern. We had to agree on the format of that block, and that was all the freedom there was in the system. We sat down and negotiated the meaning of every bit and decided that people were free to do what they liked within their own sub-systems, but no one was allowed to a make a private deal with another module about some meaning of an element in the database. No deals. It was a very simple interface control: we split up the system and controlled the interfaces.

In a pilot, the other control is to limit the pilot: don't try and improve it if that means delaying it. The pilot allows people to give feedback as they see what it is, instead of reading the book and imagining what it is. And if the user reacts very negatively and cancels the project, you have spent only a small percentage of the total project estimate.

A large project usually involves several departments or operating areas of the company, and it is usually crucial in some way or another. It usually gets high priority and there are many kinds of people to be coordinated. The way to oversee all of this is to appoint a czar, somebody who has direct line authority for that project and for all the people who are working on it in all the different areas. The czar is responsible for all coordination. All department heads are responsible for providing the czar with resources: people cannot be diverted. The czar sits in a room and knocks head together and pontificates, ensuring that everyone comes to a decision instead of engaging in endless or protected negotiation. When projects get large, we usually resort to czar leadership: large systems won't run through negotiation alone; they need strong leadership.

At this point the user is very busy. The user has to get the data base ready for the new system, run the old system, and put up the procedures for installing and testing. The user must be involved in the cutover procedure. During much of its implementation the new system will impinge on current operations: it may change the organization; it will change the way people behave. There are many tradeoffs to be made, and often a pre-emption of, a discussion of trends in nical decisions are relatively easy – a staff consultant will help with them – but the toughest are business decisions. Somebody has to commit resources, realizing that this may impact other phases of the business. This is the essential problem in managing the development of a large system.

## 6. Trends

All of the foregoing discussion is a preliminary to, and often a pre-emption of, a discussion of trends in systems management. The most significant trend today is the fact that we are usually dealing with well-informed users. The major users know a great deal more about us; in fact, some of them previously had my job or were otherwise working in the department. They understand our terminology and our methods. We now have much less trauma than we used to have. You don't hear today about the big disasters in systems (which may be because our cover-up is much better), but after about fifteen years of EDP and great effort, we are now successfully putting in 3-to-20 million dollar projects.

I don't think I'm unique in protecting the downside – this is a second major trend. Perhaps natural selection has occurred. There are two kinds of people: those who protected the downside, and those who didn't, and the latter have disappeared from DP management – or they've been promoted out of the way. There is far less risky pioneering today. We tend to build systems with low technical risk but with high payoff.

A third trend is that we have much better networking facilities. At least, in the past few years, networking of big on-line systems has really started to improve. On a scale of zero to 10, we've probably improved our effectiveness from 0.5 to 2; that's a fourfold improvement, but we still have a long way to go. We're just starting to see that better networking control and operation can be achieved through peer relationships and autonomous sub-systems in the network. Networking nowadays usually implies a very strong hierarchical system. This is not a valid architecture, and I feel that it will take about five years to correct the current architecture. It has taken us about five years to recognize that the change is necessary, and it will take about five years to make the shift. We're coming up to what I call the Multiple-Mini-Maxi, a mixture of networking with large and small computer systems.

The well-infomed user wants to see some cost-benefit analysis. The fourth major trend is much greater analysis and study of alternatives, and the concept of trading. The users will trade facilities for earlier delivery time. They really understand the pay-offs, and they'll provide feedback fast. If you give them something they can use, even on a limited basis, they will react to it much faster: we have found that we can get a lot of feedback by using a rigged-up demo or a "quick-and-dirty" pilot. Well-informed users are to be ignored at your peril; in big systems you need all the help – and all the sympathy – you can get.

The fifth major trend is to creeping commitment. We do much more in the way of experiments, we run our testing early, we do phased implementation – pilots, we get the user very heavily involved. Nowadays we find that users want to be helping when we're starting to put up a new system. They're eager to be in on the early testing; they want to be the first ones up. Testing is less traumatic. They see a tradeoff by starting early.

There's a lot more emphasis on training nowadays. We use the pilot to run our first users through training courses. This provides us with feedback, and at the same time helps to get the training effort into the field. We educate training teams and, as we install systems across the country or through offices, a training team follows along giving an integrated program. This slows installation but ensures that everything is run in a series of easy steps (which may not be the same as were laid out at the beginning). We plan, but the detailed plan keeps changing to meet the new objectives.

The sixth major trend is the ability to increase system capacity by making hardware changes without major system changes. More powerful and reliable hardware is coming all the time. The only interesting thing about this, from my point of view, is that it's getting easier to use. You can just plug in most hardware and obtain its benefits immediately. But the biggest problem of hardware today is that we still don't have good ways to run multiple CPUs on the same problem. There was a classic example of a big system ten years ago where one CPU had the power of one CPU, two CPUs working together in a control program had the power of 1.2 CPUs, three CPUs working together had the power of 0.5 CPU, and four couldn't run. Even today, we would much rather deal with very large systems by putting in one bigger machine than by trying to get two or three working together. We try not to implement a system which needs more than one machine, and this means that we're limiting the system to the size of the CPU.

What are we looking for? It may take five years before we begin to realize what it is that we really need and then five more years to get it. I believe that we will end up with the big CPUs represented by an array of smaller CPUs. One of them will be scheduling the others, and each one will be doing only one task. We won't have fancy interrupts, we won't have fancy multi-interleaving, paging, etc. A whole lot of overhead and complexity will just disappear. Some computers will be idle some of the time. The stupid concept of trying to use everything 100% of the time will start to go away. Such systems will degrade gracefully; as one CPU fails we will still have all the others. If one breaks down, the scheduler will note the problem, initiate a restart and put the affected job in some other machine. This has been technically possible for about five years, but it really isn't practical today for the bulk of the big system users, because there's no easy way to get there except through migration. I wish it would come faster because it will make a big difference to the speed at which we can build big systems. I would much rather build a system that has ten computers in a row and add additional computers one by one, than try to convert a large system to run on today's multiprocessors.

## 7. Summary

In summary, while little of immediate dramatic impact or novel conception is happening in large-scale systems management, there are many important drives for change that I've tried to highlight:

\- the users are getting well informed, and they're really very different from the users you typically see ridiculed in the literature,

– there is less trauma for many reasons,

– better networking is coming,

\- more powerful hardware is being more easily used to advantage.

We are using different ways to handle and manage very large systems, and we are looking for more. These are not new or novel ways. They are management techniques that have been used for centuries, adapted to modern society and computing technology. The data processing community today has reached a high level of sophistication and evolution, and that direction is a trend that seems to be continuing.
