# A Case Study of User Evaluation of Information Characteristics for Systems Improvement

Seev Neumann

Associate Professor, Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University and Visiting Associate Professor, Graduate School of Management, U.C.L.A., Los Angeles, CA 90024, USA

and

Eli Segev

Senior Lecturer, Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University and Visiting Associate Professor, Graduate School of Management, U.C.L.A., Los Angeles, CA 90024, USA

This paper reports on a case study of user evaluation of information characteristics as a basis for the improvement of an information system. Branch-managers of a large bank were asked to evaluate the output of the system according to four information characteristics: accuracy, content, frequency, and recency. The findings of this study indicate that the users at the bank had a holistic perception of their information, and that content was a dominant characteristic which spilled over other characteristics. This case study casts doubts about the use of evaluations of users of information characteristics as the only basis for improving existing systems.

Keywords: Accuracy, Content, Frequency, Information Characteristics, Information Systems, Recency, Users, Users Evaluation.

## 1. The Organization and Its Information System

This study focused on a computerized information system serving branches of one the three leading banks in Israel. The bank is listed as one of Fortune's 500. The system was installed ten years ago and at the time of the study served 148 branches. Its configuration was based on two large third-generation computers and operated in a batch-processing mode. ## 2. Method and Rationale

Since the bank and its environment had changed considerably during the years, the bank's management considered changes in the information system. Two basic approaches for updating the system were considered: (1) initiation of a new design cycle ignoring the existing system, and (2) improvement of existing systems based on evaluations of their characteristics. The application of these two polar approaches (or combinations of the two methods) largely depends on the specific situation. When informational needs are transformed or largely changed, the initiation of a new design is usually considered. However, since the basic informational needs were not changed, management deemed improvements of the existing system to be the appropriate approach.

The paper reports on the ensuing study of user evaluation of information characteristics as a basis for the improvement of the information system. The rationale behind this approach is based on the now well-accepted definition of Mason and Mitroff, of an information system as “a PERSON of a certain PSYCHOLOGICAL TYPE who faces a PROBLEM within some ORGANIZATIONAL CONTEXT for which he needs EVIDENCE to arrive at a solution, where the evidence is made available through some MODE OF PRESENTATION” [14]. Thus, the user of the information and his needs are the key for the system design [4,13,21]. If the system does not fit the user’s needs, it will not be used. Extent of use serves in this case as an indication of the success of the system. The user satisfaction with the system is necessary for its use [8,20]; appreciation leads to the use of the system [22].

Improvement of an existing system is aimed at providing a better response to user needs, that is, increasing the use of the system by increasing user satisfaction from the system. In order to increase the user satisfaction from an existing system, it is necessary to first measure his current evaluation of the system, find out those aspects of the information which receive low evaluation, and then improve them [5,9].

## 3. Information Characteristics

The branch managers were selected as representatives of the various users of the information system at the branch level, and were asked to evaluate the output of the system according to the four information characteristics of accuracy, content, frequency, and recency.

Various significant information characteristics have been suggested (e.g., [2,15–17]). Davis [6] lists the following: purpose, mode and format, redundancy/efficiency, rate, frequency, deterministic or probabilistic, cost, value, reliability, validity. Gorry and Scott-Morton [10] suggest the characteristics of source, scope, level of aggregation, timehorizon, currency, required accuracy, frequency of use. Adams and Schroeder [1] studied the characteristics of accuracy or precision, age of data, repetitiveness, summarization, descriptive content, source. Taggart and Tharp [23] tabulate the extent of treatment of their surveyed literature of aspects of management information requirements analysis phase of MIS development and claim that the extent of treatment of information characteristics is considerably meager. This fact leads them to suggest that more specific research can be formulated around the user and the interaction significance of information characteristics.

Following Ein-Dor and Segev [7] we chose to focus on four information characteristics which disregard wider aspects of the system (such as purpose and cost), yet encompass all other previously discussed attributes of the information itself. These four information characteristics are: content, the information included in the information system; accuracy, the accepted percent of errors in the data; recency, the delay between the real occurrence and its presentation; and frequency, the length of time between two subsequent occasions in which the information is made available to the user. For detailed discussion of these characteristics see [7, pp. 163–165] $^{1}$ .

The initial stages in designing an information system entail making decisions concerning these information characteristics [12]. Once these characteristics are determined, it is up to the system implementor to produce an information system with these characteristics. When an information system is under evaluation and improvements rather than a new system is sought, it is obvious that the same characteristics should be considered.

## 4. Implementation of the Study

The original goal of the study was to cover the total population of 148 branch managers. A questionnaire was distributed among the branch managers during their first regional meeting in 1977. In order to assure common interpretation of concepts, an interviewer presented the questionnaire to the branch managers, explained its purpose. they way it was built, the concepts involved, and the way it should be filled out. The managers were asked to answer the questions in the first five to six pages during the meeting in the presence of the interviewer, take the questionnaire with them and answer the rest of it when they returned to their branches. (From page 5 on, the questionnaire is repetitious; there were 56 additional pages containing the same questions related to each of the system's reports.) The completed questionnaire was to be mailed back later. This study is based on the analysis of the data in the first four pages.

The branch managers were asked to evaluate four characteristics of information related to seven major activities of the branch. Each of the first four pages of the questionnaire related to one characteristic. On top of the page a heading defined the characteristic discussed on the page; Later, an identical question was repeated for each major activity of the branch and for all the reports. The managers' evaluation was based on a scale of five points. A typical page started as in fig. 1.

Out of 148 branch managers. 18 were not given questionnaires because the branches had operated less than three years. Eighty-five returned the filled out questionnaires, out of which four were unidentified. The other managers were not included in the data because of one or more or of the following reasons: They were unable to attend the relevant regional meeting, they left the meeting before the interviewer presented the questionnaire, they did not mail the completed questionnaire until completion of the fieldwork. Thus, the data consist of observations of 81 branch managers.

## 5. Utility of Method and Implications of the Study

We doubted the ability of the branch-managers to differentiate accurately among the various characteristics of the information system. Prolonged use of the system may create a holistic perception of the information made available to them by the information system (i.e., lack of differentiation among the various characteristics of information). If such perception exists it casts doubts about the utility of using the branch managers' evaluations of the information characteristics in improving the system.

Since by holistic perception it is meant that the managers do not differentiate among the various characteristics of information, one would expect highly positive relationships among evaluations of the different characteristics of information. Analysis of the branch managers' evaluations (see Appendix, Part A) showed that basically high positive interdependencies do exist among the evaluations of the information characteristics. However, it was clear that another effect, complementary to the holistic perception of the information, was also responsible for the structure of interrelationships.

Therefore, after the initial analysis, we tried to explain the possible reasons for the unexpected variations found in the correlation structures. One possible explanation is that there exists a dominant information characteristic which is perceived to be of a high importance by the users. This dominance is materialized by a split-over from the evaluation of the dominant information characteristic to evaluations of other characteristics. A strong split-over effect will cause high positive correlations among evaluations, but not similar structure of correlations in each matrix (depending on the area of activity of the bank).

We found (see Appendix, part B) that content is a dominant information characteristic and the assessment of this characteristic affects other evaluations. That is, there is a spill-over effect in which evaluation of content causes evaluations of other characteristics to be similar.

Although this is a case study of one organization, we believe that its findings may have implications for other organizations. They raise doubts about the use of evaluations of users of information characteristics as a basis for improving existing systems. They indicate that the user, at least in this bank, had a holistic perception of the information made available to him by the system, and that content is a dominant characteristic with a spill-over effect on other characteristics, which reduces the reliability of improvement methods based on users' evaluation of the information system.

The findings have no implications on the a priori users' definitions of their needs, only on perceptions of existing systems. Once an information system has been implemented and users are exposed to prolonged use of the system, it seems that users may state their needs in light of their experience. Thus, a branch manager who has faced lack of information, might also claim that there is a need to increase the accuracy of the information and change the frequency of the information supplied by the system. Note that whether the updating of the system is made through the initiation of a new design cycle, or through improvement of the existing system, the same bias may disturb the updating effort.

Furthermore, because of users' evaluations, the updating of information systems may be either delayed (in cases where satisfaction from one specific information characteristic, mainly content, prevents the identification of the system's problems), or a major redesign of the information system may be unnecessarily initiated (when specific information characteristics require attention). While awareness of the interdependencies among users' evaluations of information characteristics is important, tools for evaluating existing information systems have to be developed. Users' evaluations only seem insufficient for this problem.

## B. Search for a dominant Information Characteristic

In the search for a dominant information characteristic, means of correlations for each row (or column) of each matrix were calculated and added to Table 1. The next step was to examine the rank order of these means in every matrix $^{4}$ . It can be easily seen that, although the differences in the sizes of the means are small, there is a clear pattern in all eight matrices: the mean of the correlations of content evaluation with other characteristics is the highest.

Thus, content is the dominant information characteristic (as perceived by users) and there is a spill-over effect from users' evaluations of the content of information to their evaluations of the accuracy, frequency and recency of the information. Note that the most frequent pattern (in matrices 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, and 8) is that accuracy is second to content in the rank order. That is, the breadth, scope and reliability of information, at least in the bank, are more important than its timing and recentness.
