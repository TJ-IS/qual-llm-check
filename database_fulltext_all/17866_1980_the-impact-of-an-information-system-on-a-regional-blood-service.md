# The Impact of an Information System on a Regional Blood Service

Paul D. Cumming

Ph.D. J.R.B. Associates, 8400 West Park Drive, McLean, Virginia 22101, USA

Kenneth E. Kendall,

Ph.D. Associate Professor College of Business Administration, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lincoln, Nebraska 63588, USA

C. Carl Pegels

Ph.D. Professor School of Management, Crosby Hall, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, New York 14214, USA

J.P. Seagle

Ph.D. Associate Professor School of Business, State University of New York at Albany, USA

The impact of information systems on a specific environment, a regional blood service operating over a large geographic area, is reported here. Two related information systems have been designed and partially implemented to aid a regional blood service for a mixed urban and rural area of about two million people. The overall information system is discussed in the way it was designed and developed: by modules. The first subsystem provides inventory control, with modules for inventory tracking, short-term demand forecasting, and allocation. The other subsystem supports recruitment and blood collection, with modules for donor records, recruitment, and collections planning. We conclude with a discussion of the impact of the management information system modules on the blood service operations.

Keywords: management information system, regional blood service, blood inventory control, blood demand forecasting, blood a location, blood donor recruitment, blood collection, blood collection planning, management control, strategic planning, information system modules, bloodmobile scheduling, performance measurements, implementation.

## 1. Introduction

Much has been written about the development and planning for development of management information systems. However, little discussion has appeared on the impact of management information systems on the organization and environment for which they were developed. The study discussed here aims at just that. The organizational environment for this management information system is a large regional blood program serving a population of about two million people.

We shall describe the development and implementation of a management information system for a regional blood service's (RBS) inventory and distribution control [10,14] and blood collection scheduling [3,11]. For several years this information system has been in operation at the Buffalo American Red Cross Regional Blood Center (the Center), the headquarters of an RBS which serves over two million people and supplies over forty hospitals in Western New York State and Northwestern Pennsylvania. Costs and benefits of the system have been analyzed and reported [13]. The objective of this paper is to discuss the nature of the impact upon management of both the existing and proposed features of the management information system. The development of the inventory and distribution system modules is discussed first. Next the operation of the blood service and some major attendant problems are explored. The third section discusses recruitment and collections scheduling systems. Lastly, the impact of these systems on the blood service is reported and analyzed.

## 2. Modular development and testing

The overall management information system has been developed in modules which were implemented one at a time, and often on only part of the RBS. The various modules that provide inventory and distribution information are listed at the left in Table 1, with impacted management levels shown as columns. The management levels used were proposed by Anthony [1], and time horizons are adjusted to reflect pecularity of blood service delivery caused primarily by the limited useful life of blood and blood components.

The first module, inventory reporting gives blood center management a daily listing of blood inventory at each major hospital blood bank. These hospital blood banks serve as the "storage warehouses" of the system. The operational impact (in the form of locating blood for emergencies) has been great; use of the information for operational control has been limited.

The inventory reporting module not only provides information on the aggregate number of units by blood type in each hospital blood bank and in the blood center blood bank but also on each uniquely-identified blood unit in each hospital blood bank. The unique identification of each unit of blood allows the information system to determine exactly on which day each available unit in a hospital's blood bank outdates.

Inventory reports are furnished to all participating hospital blood banks and are used extensively for their respective internal inventory controls. Providing the inventory reports has proven a tangible and valuable reward for participation, as well as a painless means by which the hospital blood banks verify the data.

A second module provides short term forecasts of the total inventory (Center and hospital blood banks) for each type of blood [7]. This has enabled RBS management to engage in intensive recruiting to ward off shortages, and to lower collections activity during periods of forecasted oversupply.

The forecasting 1 odule consists of four sub-modules; These are 1) blood transfusions forecast module; 2) blood expiration forecast module; 3) blood collections forecast module; and 4) blood inventory projection module. The last module also serves as the executive module which controls the other three submodules.

The blood transfusions forecast module uses an exponential smoothing model with a cyclical component and a ten day cycle. The ten day cycle was selected to facilitate making forecasts ten days or two weeks into the future. Ten days are equivalent to two weeks because each week was considered to consist of five work days. Transfusions which occurred over the weekend were included in the Monday data.

The blood expiration forecast module consists of a positive exponential function. The dependent variable of the exponential function provides the expirations forecast and the independent variables are collections of previous periods. The exponential function has been developed from historical expirations and collections data.

The blood collections forecast module is not an analytic model. Blood collection forecasts are made by committee. The committee forecasts are entered into the forecasting model to be incorporated with the outputs of the other modules.

The blood inventory projection module is the executive module which integrates the output of the other three modules and generates the inventory level forecast for each day, ten days into the future. This is accomplished by the following approach. For day one $t = 1$ ), the ending inventory is found by the formula: Ending inventory forecast $(t) = \text{actual beginning inventory}(t) - \text{transfusions forecast}(t) - \text{expirations forecast}(t) + \text{collections forecast}(t)$ . In subsequent days the same formula is used except that actual beginning inventory $(t)$ is replaced by ending inventory forecast $(t - 1)$ .

A classical operations research application was used to develop the third module, which allocates blood among the hospital blood banks. A computer based decision heuristic was developed to distribute blood according to forecast needs. Prior to implementation, this module was shown to substantially reduce shipping costs. The decision heuristic was designed to replace a largely arbitrary and time consuming negotiation process between the Center and hospital blood banks.

Following the implementation of the blood allocation module, blood center management decided to send out more blood to hospital blood banks, resulting in a relatively lower blood center blood bank inventory and increased hospital blood bank inventories. It appears that the above management decision was as much the result of the information provided by the inventory reporting and forecasting modules as the result of the blood allocation module. With hospital blood bank inventory information at their finger tips, management was willing to “live with” much smaller blood center inventories.

A variety of RBS performance measures are provided by a fourth module. Since uniform measures of performance have not been established for blood services, it was necessary to develop a trial set of performance measures. The measures that were tested include unit-days of stock per unit transfused, unit-days of crossmatch per unit transfused, proportion of red cells to whole blood used in transfusion, average age of transfusions, and rate of blood wasted due to outdating and other causes. Jennings suggests others [8,9].

The measures allow for supervisory control in the few cases where authority relationships exist. More commonly self and peer evaluation are supported by observing how changes in one measure cause changes in other measures. As experience is obtained with the measures, priorities for the objectives can be determined by upper level management (e.g., board of directors), peer groups and public officials.

To evaluate in more detail the impact of the management information system on blood service, it is necessary to provide some background on the national blood service industry.

## 3. The blood service industry

The need for a national system of blood supply organizations is a fairly recent one. Before the Second World War, individual hospitals were generally able to supply the blood needs associated with the level of medical care that existed then. Many volunteer and commercial organizations responded to the blood needs that have been growing in the last four decades. However, a number of problems have surfaced as national blood collections have grown to over nine million units of whole blood each year. It will aid in understanding these problems, and the ability of an information system to alleviate them, if we view the blood service from both a functional and an institutional perspective.

The ten functions that must be performed for the successful delivery of blood service are shown in Figure 1. Excluding civic minded groups such as employers and churches, over 5,000 institutions participate in supplying blood in the U.S.A., including regional or community blood centers, hospitals, and commercial pharmaceutical houses [4].

The blood service industry consists of two sectors, blood banking and pharmaceutical houses. The combination of nonprofit regional and community blood centers and hospital blood banks is known as the blood banking sector. The blood banking sector collects primarily whole blood from volunteer donors and produces whole blood and components for transfusion. Whole blood components require collection in multiple integral packs and can be efficiently produced in small batches (one to four donor units) using only mechanical processes. Common components include antihemophiliac (AHR) cryoprecitate (cryo), packed fresh and frozen plasma and the cellular products, red cells (fresh and frozen), platelets and leukocytes.

The profit making, pharmaceutical sector collects primarily plasma from paid donors by means of plasmapharesis, a process in which the cellular products of blood drawn from the donor are separated and returned to the individual. Additional plasma for fractionation is obtained from the blood banking sector resulting from surplus blood supplies in the manufacture of blood components, from outdated whole blood, and from placentas.

The pharmaceutical sector uses a process called fractionation which requires both chemical and mechanical means of production and large batches (1000 or more donor units). Common fractionation products (fractions) include serum albumin, fibrinogen, plasma protein fraction, Rho-gram, AHF concentrate and the gamma globulins. Some fractions can be substituted for some components, although their cost is usually higher.

Having briefly compared and contrasted the two industrial sectors and indicated their interrelationship (primarily through plasma), we shall next focus on the blood banking sector; each of the primary functions is now discussed and related to the information system development process.

## 4. Primary functions of a regional blood service

The first function, donor recruitment, is performed by a variety of organizations using methods that run from cash payments to humanitarian appeals. The RBS may recruit directly by using telephone, mail, mass media and face-to-face approaches. Alternatively, church, civic, social, and employment groups or hospitals may be recruited to conduct the recruitment of donors. Incentives for both individuals and groups include altruism, assurance plans, avoidance of fees for units transfused to patients, and direct payments. It is estimated that only 3 percent of the American population respond to these methods and appeals in any single year.

The remaining initial functions, which comprise a production type process, are performed by either the blood center or the hospital blood bank. Bleeding of the donor occurs at either mobile or fixed collection facilities. The usual donation process includes donor registration, recording of medical history, temperature taking, determination of blood pressure, hemoglobin counts, bleeding, and serving of refreshments. After collection, a unit of blood is tested at either mobile or central laboratories to determine ABO and Rh types, the presence of rare antibodies, and whether it carries venereal disease or hepatitis. From the laboratory, a unit is either sent to inventory for distribution, or separated into plasma and red cells. The plasma can then be utilized for component production.

The final actions taken with a unit of blood involve a specific patient, and the patient's physician takes control, identifying needs and selecting the appropriate blood product. However, this decision is all to frequently limited by the available inventory. Sampling and typing the patient's blood, crossmatching, and transfusion are performed by hospitals. Crossmatching is a final check which involves testing the compatibility of patient and donor blood by mixing a sample of each and checking for reaction. Transfusion may range from infusion of whole blood or packed red cells to injection of a blood product by syringe.

5. Management problems of the national blood service complex.

Several management problems with the national blood delivery system have become evident. First, a substantial proportion of collected blood is not used before the expiration of the legal life of the red cells, presently 21 days. The authors estimate this proportion currently at about 15%; it was twice as high about 7 years ago [4,7]. The legal life of blood is set by the Federal Drug Administration for blood that is transported across state lines. For interstate use the legal life is determined by state government agencies, usually health deapartments. Second, in large urban areas a fair amount of blood is collected from paid donors. Despite recent improvements, there is no way to identify all hepatitis carriers. Paid donors have an incentive to conceal their past; studies confirm that paid donors are more likely to carry hepatitis than volunteer donors. Third, there are recurrent shortage periods in which scheduled surgery must be delayed for lack of blood. Third and last, blood components are underutilized relative to whole blood.

It has been argued in the National Blood Policy [5] that these problems have their roots in the way that the blood service is organized and controlled. There is not a distinct national blood service but a loose federation of more or less independent organizations. Most regional and community blood service centers are affiliated with the American Red Cross, The American Association of Blood Banks, or the Council of Community Blood Centers; but these national associations can exert only minimal control over members. Furthermore, the blood center has but little control over associated hospitals, because hospitals are not always totally supplied by a regional blood center, and, therefore, some operate their own bleeding facility. In either case, the hospital is not subject to full regional control; persuasion and subtle pressures must be used. Knowledge provided by an information system can be a powerful tool in this kind of control.

Control tools alone are not enough. The objectives and motives of blood administration are not clearly operational. Five objectives that are in partial conflict are:

1. Keep sufficient blood on hand to satisfy needs (supply).

2. Provide high quality blood, fresh and disease free (quality).

3. Maximize use of packed red cells (effectiveness).

4. Minimize outdating losses (efficiency).

5. Minimize cost (efficiency).

Most of the attention is now focused on outdating, quality (use of voluntary rather than paid donors) [5], and the use of packed red cells (probably because these objectives are most easily measured).

## 6. Recruitment and collection information

Our management-information system was initially concerned only with blood inventory reporting, blood inventory level forecasting and blood distribution. Experience with the above system suggested that the addition of new information modules would increase overall system efficiency. Now we shall describe modules that aid in the recruitment of voluntary donors and planning collections.

Six major computer-based modules that provide donor recruitment and collection information are shown in Table 2. The ways in which modules can have impact upon the various management functions are shown in the columns of the table, following the same approach used in the last section.

The donor records module keeps recruiting information on all donors. Much of this information had previously been kept manually, but the ability of the computer to select donors makes it advantageous to place additional information in the file. Additional information includes geographic locations of homes and greater detail about past donations and group affiliations. Some of the impact is on clerical operations, but monitoring the file allows planners to detect long-term changes in the donor pool in time for remedial action, and the machine readable file provides a basis for the donor group control module.

The payoff from computerized donor records comes from using the selective recruiting module that retrieves donor names, addresses, and phone numbers for recruiting. For example, all donors close to a specific bloodmobile visit could be retrieved for a mailing, along with donors having A—(A negative) blood from a larger area, and any donors who previously credited the civic club sponsoring the blood-mobile. In this way the manager can alleviate imbalances indicated by the shortterm forecasting module.

The donor group control module performs clerical tasks associated with civic clubs, church groups, employers and similar groups that offer members blood assurance in exchange for meeting a quota of blood donations from the group. This module also helps in controlling groups, and monitoring the effectiveness of methods used by the groups.

A similar module provides bloodmobile history and forecasting information about groups that sponsor and recruit for bloodmobile visits. Such groups differ from donor groups in that assurance is not necessarily offered. Information alone may not be sufficient for control because organizational independence limits the ability of an RBS to affect the performance of a sponsoring group. Even without authoritative control, the historical information can have great impact for its value in forecasting collections of future bloodmobile visits.

The bloodmobile scheduling module accepts collection forecasts as inputs, and projects inventory levels (shortages), outdating and age at infusion throughout the planning period. The objective of this module is to alleviate the predictable imbalances between supply and demand, which lead to either shortage or waste because of the short life of blood. Shortages are felt regularly during the summer and end of year periods, with surplus collections and consequent waste occurring in the spring and fall. The effort required to develop and evaluate schedules has been so great that the first schedule considered, usually similar to last year's, may be accepted as "satisfactory."

The bloodmobile scheduling module makes it easy for a blood manager to experiment with a variety of schedules, and determine the implication of each upon the performance measures before finalizing a schedule.

The bloodmobile scheduling module is quite cost effective [3,1]. Unlike some of the other modules, it assists rather than replaces the decision maker. For reasons outlined elsewhere [2], replacement of the human scheduler is not advisable even when behavioral considerations are omitted. Of perhaps greater importance, the module is willingly used by the manager both to aid thinking, and to persuade other organizations that sponsor bloodmobile visits that a change in scheduled data would be worthwhile. In several other centers, donors are individually scheduled by that center in order to supply the exact quantities needed by type. Such an approach is costly because it requires direct contact between the RBS staff and all donors. The bloodmobile scheduling module achieves aggregate balance between supply and demand with far less communication activity, and does not require the donor to adapt to the convenience of the RBS.

The scheduling module can also be operated in a simulation mode to predict performance measures for commonly advocated policy changes. In one study [12], the relative effectiveness of future policy changes were evaluated in terms of their impact on inventory level, outdating, and average age of blood at transfusion. Policies studied were: use of frozen red cells, extending the legal life of fresh whole blood and red cells, improved inventory control and improved bloodmobile scheduling. Results showed that extending the legal life had the “best” overall increase in efficiency, but this policy is not under the control of the regional blood manager. Of the remaining policies, improved bloodmobile scheduling appears best.

## 7. The impact of the information system

The interactions between large numbers of volunteers, charitable multipurpose organizations, and a production type process under a set of medical constraints create a unique environment for an automated information system. The repetitive nature of the production type processes would at first glance, indicate significant potential for cost reduction and increased effectiveness by using an automated information system. However, heavy use of volunteers reduces the perceived need for cost control. The medical constraints tend to value quality over efficiency, allowing cost to be someone else's concern. Furthermore, there appears to be an aversion to quantitative performance measurement. Charitable organizations (such as the American Red Cross) have many purposes in addition to delivery of high quality blood service, and consequently they may tend to focus more on the cost side of the costbenefit relationship rather than on the benefits to society.

Automation of the first major stage of the blood inventory system (inventory reporting) resulted in a reduction of 6 percent in blood outdating, as well as a reduction in deferred surgical procedures. In addition, administrative work was simplified at the regional center and also at each of the participating hospitals.

The short-term blood forecasting module reduced outdating by an additional 2.5 percent, substantially reduced regional blood shortages, and reduced the need to call additional donors by telephone or mass-media announcements [5].

The heuristic blood allocation module was not intended to reduce outdating. Rather, the allocation module attempted to distribute blood more equitably among hospitals in the region. The effect would have been to prevent hospital blood shortages from occurring. Implementation was only partially achieved since the regional blood administrator chose not to exercise full control over individual blood units.

As previously indicated, the donor recruitment and donor group modules have been designed but not implemented. Implementation testing of the blood-mobile scheduling module has taken place in two regions, and according to users the molecule has been helpful in reducing inventory imbalances.

The performance measurement system was implemented and tested for a trial period of six months. Implementation involved the support of the regional scientific committee which made visits to selected hospitals whose performance was substandard in comparison to other similar hospitals. During the six month trial period, outdated decreased from 21.8 percent to 14.8 percent. In addition to reducing outdating, the performance measure module can be credited with improving two quality objectives: first, component usage (particularly the use of packed red cells) increased dramatically; second, the amount of fresh blood transfused increased. Furthermore, comparison of similar hospitals in the performance measure reports led to reductions of relative inventory levels; this provided for lower blood wastage and improved availability of blood units for emergency situations.

The performance measure module showed substantial improvement during the test period and some measures relating to the quality of blood continued to show improvement. However, after the test period was completed, outdating increased somewhat as hospitals were no longer under the watchful eye of the scientific committee. As Freidson [6] notes "only the requirement of a formal, periodic, outside professional review can provide the counterlever to the tendency for the development of self-sustaining parochial standards in varied practice settings."
