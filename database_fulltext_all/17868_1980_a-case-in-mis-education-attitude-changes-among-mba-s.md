# A Case in MIS Education: Attitude Changes among MBA's

Lawrence F. Young \*

Faculty of Industrial Engineering and Management, Technion, Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa, Israel

Universities usually require MBA students to take one or more courses in MIS. Little or no formal evaluation of the effectiveness or results of such courses is usually undertaken. Many teachers of MIS continue to question and try out new ways to present material.

In a recent course offering at the University of Connecticut, the author measured student attitudes before and after a first course in MIS. The results indicate that course content influenced students' job preferences (generally away from activities seen as mainly technical and toward staff activities) and widened their perception of computers as devices for potentially increasing organizational flexibility, managerial effectiveness and problem solving capability, rather than as mere "number-crunchers".

The apparent influence of the teachers own attitudes in shaping students' attitudes, even when this was not a conscious goal, argues for selecting MIS teachers from among experienced and successful practitioners.

Key Words: MIS education, MBA education, attitudinal change, teacher influence, job preferences, managerial attitudes.

## 1. Introduction

Graduate business students are required to study computer-based management information systems (MIS) mainly to gain familiarity with a set of concepts and technical terminology, and to prepare them to deal with related administrative issues they are likely to meet as managers [1,2]. It is hoped that such exposure will increase an MBA student's confidence and effectiveness in using and controlling computer systems to support managerial decision making. How effective such courses actually are, and how educational effectiveness can be improved, are largely open questions in this relatively new area of business education. It would be valuable to undertake follow-up studies of on-the-job performance of MBA's who received different types and amounts of computer-MIS education.

Lacking direct evidence, MIS education should be considered experimental while also aspiring to be practical. If courses and teaching methods in this and other areas are to comprise useful experiments, they should be documented and their results measured in ways that go beyond standard academic procedures.

Toward this end, we have attempted to measure and report changes in various attitudes and beliefs of a group of MBA students immediately before starting and after completing a basic introductory course in MIS. Assuming attitudes reflect probable actions as managers, attitudinal changes should provide an indication of some of the effects of a current mode of formal MIS education.

## 2. The Sample and Course Content

This case study reports on 60 MBA students who completed an MIS course taught by the author as a visiting professor during the 1978–79 academic year at the School of Business Administration of the University of Connecticut in Storrs. The students were in three different course sections but received essentially the same material and completed the same or similar course work assignments. A general profile of student characteristics is given in Table 1. While the sample and course are not necessarily representative of all MBA students and all MIS courses, neither do they appear to be atypical of full-time students.

The course utilized lecture material based on Davis' text on MIS [3] supplemented by examples and elaboration based on the author's own 20 years of experience (as programmer, analyst-designer, manager, and consultant). Lecture material, of course, was meant to present and explain factual and definitional topics and managerial procedures rather than to "sell" conclusions on controversial issues or personal attitudes. It is recognized, however, that no clear unchanging dividing line can be drawn between fact and opinion in MIS or in other management topics.

The following outlines the course topics:

\- Definition and Summary of Historical Evolution of MIS;

\- Systems Concepts;

• Information Concepts (definitions, value of information, quality of information);

\- Computer Basics (hardware components, cpu operations and functions, systems and applications software structure: and functions);

## 3. Attitudes Measured and Results

The attitudinal variables that were measured are grouped under two main headings:

(1) Interest in Various Types of Jobs and Computer-Related Activities;

(2) Attitudes About Using Computers.

The items and results under these two headings are reported in Tables 2 and 3 respectively. The results shown were derived from identical self-administered questionnaires which the students filled out prior to the first lecture meeting and again at the conclusion of the course (prior to the final exam and computation of final course grades). The students before and after responses were identified by a number so that each student's attitude change could be measured, but their names did not appear on the questionnaires. A five-point scale was used, compacted to dichotomous positive-negative response categories for the tabulation results shown there. A two-tailed statistical significance test for paired sample responses was made using SPSS - the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences; these results are given in Tables 2 and 3. (This test was made on the full five-point scale responses and not on the diochotomized data.)

With respect to interest in certain types of jobs, as might be expected a majority of these young relatively inexperienced graduate business students were interested in all types of jobs, whether in large or small firms, manufacturing or service companies, etc., except for technical or engineering jobs and jobs which mainly required working alone on complex problems. They overwhelmingly opt for interaction with people, a job with decision-making authority, and a "management type of job". The only changes of 10% or more in these job preferences over the span of the MIS course were positive shifts in favoring staff (advisory) jobs and jobs in manufacturing concerns (both shifts are statistically significant). Since the course presented some examples of applications in manufacturing and also referred to a manufacturing company in the mini-computer feasibility project, it appears that this exposure whetted the interest of some additional students in manufacturing enterprises. The MIS specialist was also described in the course as an advisor to managers, and students were asked to imagine themselves in the role of consultant to the president for purposes of their mini-computer evaluation project. This role playing and course exposure would appear to be a likely cause for the significant increase in interest in staff jobs.

Mixed positive and negative shifts can be seen with respect to interest in more specific activities. The big loser was interest in computer programming, with a proportional drop of nearly half of the number initially interested in this activity. An interest drop of similar proportions can be seen for the activity of preparing or transmitting input data. While the course did not require students to actually perform these activities, it did describe what was involved and gave examples. It appears that a number of students who initially had little or no knowledge of these activities had first responded with some lukewarm interest (possibly because of some general computer mystique). When they then discovered the nature of these activities, they found a more realistic basis for their attitude and saw them as not being interesting.

The next three shifts of magnitudes of 10% or higher were in the positive direction of interest. These, in order of magnitude, were: operating a personal mini-computer, solving problems by interacting with a computer; and specifying required information content and its manner of display. A large majority of the students were already interested in these activities prior to the course, but the course work apparently attracted even greater interest.

For the above-mentioned activities and the others listed in Table 2, the course experience appears to have augmented already existing proclivities one would expect from those interested in management, rather than a technical career.

Less consistent effects can be seen with regard to various attitudes about using computers, as shown in Table 3. Many of these items deal with more subtle issues, and matters more difficult for the inexperienced person to link with personal interests. For example, the greatest shift is seen with respect to agreement with the statement that "local, connected computers in a network provide greater organizational flexibility". In the absence of specific experience or data, logic could argue this issue either way, and about half of the students initially disagree while half agree. The course experience, however, pushed over four-fifths of the students to agree with this proposition. This conclusion seems to stem from illustrations on applications of such a network rather than any direct conclusion being presented about organizational flexibility.

It may also be of interest, especially to those involved in offering computer services as well as those who rent or sell in-house computers, that a large majority both before and after the course reject both extreme positions that either in-house or out-of-house computer services are nearly always preferable (items ranked 9 and 20). Thus, the new about-to-be-managers seem to agree with experienced computer users that only a particular situation dictates what is best (and, by implication, that a mix of in- and out-of-house may be suitable).

Other results, in summary, show that most students believed both before and after the MIS course that:

\- managers are not replaceable by computers;

\- that managers should not leave the choice of information they receive to computer specialists;

\- a manager-computer interaction aids in problem solving;

\- not each and every manager should have his/her own mini-computer; and

\- knowing something about computers is a great career advantage.

## 4. Conclusions

It seems clear that the choice of examples and student assignments have a significant effect on interests in types of jobs and activities. This apparent influence of education on widening students' job knowledge and choice should be an explicit goal of education.

The additudinal changes found after taking a basic introductory course in MIS indicate that, aside from the opportunity to gain more specific knowledge, such a course can provide the following benefits:

\- aiding the business student in selecting a career path;

\- confirming correct predispositions and correcting incorrect perceptions about the nature of computer activities and their utility to managers; and

• providing a broader basis upon which to analyze and judge less clear-cut managerial issues and organizational effects.

It also seems clearer, in retrospect, that a teacher's own attitudes are often conveyed to students by choice of examples, manner of presentation and other means, even when this process is subconscious and unintentional. The educational effect of communicating the interpretations and conclusions of a veteran to neophytes seems to be highly desirable. This is especially needed in areas that will require judgment and wisdom by practitioners who will affect the public welfare and the private good of their organizations, as well as their own careers. This communication of interpretations and attitudes reinforces the argument that MIS teachers should have been practitioners prior to becoming teachers.
