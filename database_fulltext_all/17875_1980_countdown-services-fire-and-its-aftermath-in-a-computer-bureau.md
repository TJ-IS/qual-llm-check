# Countdown Services: Fire and its Aftermath in a Computer Bureau

Neil M. Duffy

Professor of Information Management, School of Business Leadership, University of South Africa, PO Box 392, 0001 Pretoria, South Africa

Destruction of a computer centre by a physical hazard such as fire is often talked about but not often acted upon sufficiently. The computer at Countdown Services was destroyed by fire in April 1977. Despite sound security measures before the fire, considerable difficulties were experienced with file recovery and ongoing processing due to the unavailability of a compatible computer system in the city. Recovery and processing had to be undertaken over a period of time in three different cities with accompanying staff morale, operating standards and communication problems.

The case raises insurance, hardware replacement, backup, user attitude, morale and other considerations. It also caused Countdown Services to plan even more stringent physical security measures. The experience of this organization can serve as a useful trigger for a review of security and control measures in any computer installation.

Keywords: Physical security, fire hazard, physical installation planning, computer system backup, file recovery, user attitudes, staff morale.

## 0. Introduction

This case was prepared as a basis for discussion and for the practical lessons it contains, rather than to illustrate handling of an administrative situation. The details are exact and occurred as stated.

## 1. Fire!

At 7.10 pm on April 26th, 1977, the fire alarm sounded in the computer room and the fire station. Both the smoke and fire detection devices had been triggered. Curtains and ceiling in offices adjoining the computer room were burning and giving off dense clouds of smoke.

Navine, the operations supervisor, ran through but was stopped by the dense smoke at the door of the machine room. He grabbed the nearest fire extinguisher but was unable to get through the smoke into the machine room. In desperation he threw the extinguisher into the room in the hope that it would discharge.

![](/api/attachments/3FXGMNP4/fulltext/images/85b3a5bf276b634abf5a4ba0144f337f86063ab99e551b1c410832c29259ce77.jpg)

As far as he could determine the fire had started outside the computer room and moved over it through the ceiling. Navine knew that the building was an old one with no sprinklers. What really scared him was the thought of the garage on the first and ground floors below. The motor cars, petrol, and paint would turn the whole building into an uncontrollable inferno.

At that stage the Fire Department rang to confirm the fire; Navine hoped they would break all speed records getting there. He closed down the computer and started allocating tasks to the personnel working the shift. In accordance with the fire drill they began moving out the specially designated tapes and disks and phoning the senior managers of Countdown Services.

Within 3 minutes of the confirmatory phone call the Fire Department arrived. They came up the stairs, cut through the wrought-iron gates and proceeded to use axes and water hoses freely.

Twelve minutes after receiving the phone call the operations manager arrived. He was in time to resist an attempt to evict all non-firemen from the computer room and to see most of the important tapes and disks loaded safely into the trunk of his car. Fortunately the online system had been shut down at 5 pm and the data base dump had been completed a short while before at 6.30 pm. The magnetic tapes containing the dump were grabbed from their designated place together with other tapes and disks – some of which were already wet.

Water from the fire hoses was at this stage pouring from the air-conditioning ducting in the ceiling on the CPU, disk drives (containing the disks just dumped) and tape units.

The thick smoke was extremely acid due to the chipboard ceiling, fibreglass insulation, PVC electric cable insulation and the varnished office partitions outside the computer room. Although the fire alarm had switched off the air-conditioning in the computer room the air-conditioning for the front offices was still running. It was sucking in smoke from the fire area and pumping it into the offices.

There were approximately 2000 magnetic tapes on the premises. The operations staff worked desperately to remove them. The smoke was so thick that they were crawling on their hands and knees to avoid the worst of it. Despite the efforts of the firemen to keep them away, the operations staff saved all of these tapes.

At 10.15 pm the Fire Department announced that the fire was out but left 2 firemen to keep watch. Within an hour and a quarter the fire broke out in the ceiling again and raged until 1 am before it was finally extinguished. The asbestos roof exploded sending jagged missiles into the sky—a hazard for the personnel attempting to direct the fire hoses.

The evacuation continued until 1.30 am. Everything possible was removed despite the smoke, the smell and the absence of lights. Eventually the operations staff moved to offices in a nearby building leaving one of their number behind as nightwatch.

In the meantime magnetic tapes were being stacked in number sequence in the boardroom, Burroughs computer users round the country with equivalent, or larger systems were being phoned, and the directors of the Company were being contacted – the Financial Director was in Cape Town at the time.

## 2. Background

Countdown Services (Pty) Limited is a wholly-owned subsidiary of McCarthy Group Limited. The McCarthy Group is the largest retail motor organization in South Africa and comprises various companies each marketing particular vehicle franchises for specified areas in the Republic. The group has extensive property interests which, in the main, are leased to certain of its trading and administrative divisions. It also has subsidiary companies involved in the operation of a computer bureau (Countdown Services), the hire of commercial vehicles, motor insurance broking and the financing of vehicles hire purchase and leasing transactions.

The group thus derives its income from motor trading activities comprising mainly the marketing and leasing of new and used passenger and commercial vehicles, the sale of parts, accessories and service, from the financing of these transactions and from commission received on motor insurance. ## 3. The Aftermath

Kevin Stent, DP Manager of Countdown Services, sat back reflectively. "Of course we had computer security before the fire. Besides the detectors there were the normal fire extinguishers and hoses. (See Fig. 4 for floor layout). Copies of both our supplier and application software were kept in a fireproof safe in another building. Source documents were returned to the originating companies straight after the data had been captured. One month's captured data was kept in-house. Copies of vital program coding were kept in a remote location. Copies of all files for one period back were kept in-house while copies of the previous generation of files were kept remotely with the supplier and application software backup. File copies were taken daily or monthly depending on their processing cycles."

"As far as stationery is concerned we kept enough continuous stationery for one salaries and wages run on other premises. Nothing else is vital as there is time to order new stationery."

"Fire procedures had been made available to all staff members so they were aware of what to do to fight fires and to evacuate the premises. In fact we had had a dry run about two weeks before the fire but unfortunately this was stepped through around a table and not actually carried out."

"After the fire, of course, we had plenty to think about. There was the whole problem of moving out of the computer centre. Virtually all the computer units had been damaged by fire or corrosion and the

Your are probably aware of the fact that on Tuesday evening, 26th April, there was a serious fire which destroyed the computer room and much of the office accommodation at 150 Smith Street. We had a fire alarm system linked directly to the fire brigade and this enabled the firemen to be on the scene and fighting the blaze within minutes. Despite this fact the fire coupled with the inevitable accompanying water damage, was such that the computer room and the equipment contained therein was destroyed. Losses were suffered in adjacent areas where terminals, desks, etc. were also unfortunately burned out.

Representatives of our insurance brokers, Messrs. C.T. Bowring, were soon at the scene of the fire and we have worked closely with them since that time. We have their assurance that the insurance which we hold to meet such eventualities appears totally satisfactory and they believe that those losses which we do suffer will be settled in terms of our insurance cover.

In terms of our security arrangements which are intended to safeguard us from just such an eventuality, copies of programs and master tapes are maintained at separate premises and these back-up tapes are intact and available to us. We are still involved in establishing the position as regards our tapes and data which were stored at the site of the fire but the present indications are most reassuring. We have established that we are able to reconstruct our computer records up until 5,00 p.m. on Monday, 25th April. Further checks are currently being made and it appears probable that this time will be extended to 5,00 p.m. on Tuesday on the night of the fire. Furthermore, we are able to establish the situation as regards that data which will need to be reprocessed in order to recover the ground lost between 5,00 p.m. on Tuesday evening and 7,07 p.m., which was the time of the fire. The fact that we have had a disaster of this magnitude and yet are in the happy position of having saved our computer programs and all important records, in particular those relating to debtors and stocks, etc., does I think reflect very creditably upon the Countdown team. It is quite clear that thanks to the competent manner in which they have prepared for and managed this problem, we have averted what could have been a disastrous situation.

We have been inundated with offers of assistance and our two suppliers, namely Burroughs and ICL are both distinguishing themselves in this regard. At this point in time I should perhaps make clear that our two computers are stored in separate premises and it was the newer Burroughs configuration which was destroyed. Our old ICL computer was not involved as it is resident elsewhere. The Countdown Services staff are responding with enthusiasm to the challenges which have been forced upon us and they are certainly setting a splendid example in terms of loyalty to the company. Their dedication will certainly be needed, and appreciated, in the difficult times which must inevitably follow.

In this short term we shall be making use of computer time through the courtesy of the following Burroughs installations, Nedbank and Trust Bank in Johannesburg, we shall be relying heavily on those two resources, Ford in Port Elizabeth, and Motor Assemblies and Tongaat in Durban. Unfortunately the Durban installations are of a relatively small size and it is for this reason that most of our work will need to be routed to Johannesburg. The logistics of operating in this manner and the longer lines of communication will undoubtedly be disruptive. This fact coupled with the development work which was lost together with the inevitable disruption and other inconveniences as a result of the fire, will undoubtedly cause a delay in new program development. So far as existing systems are concerned, turnaround times are likely to be a little slower. Our chairman, Mr. Brian McCarthy, has already asked for all members of the Group to offer as little criticism as possible in the testing time immediately ahead, and I too would like to solicit your support and understanding in this regard.

With regard to the on-going computer usage, present indications are that the on-line system to the payroll offices, in respect of the wages system, and the on-line hire purchase system, will be restored and up and going early next week. There should therefore be no disruption or problem in respect of wages this coming week.

Hire purchase statements and open debtors statements had already been despatched in respect of the month of April. Some of the open debtors statements had been held back by the auditors who were conducting an audit test thereon and were planning to post direct to debtors with their normal audit sticker thereon. These statements were still on hand at the time of the fire but were intact, except for some minor water marks. They are being sent out to customers under cover of an explanatory letter and this completes the posting of open debtors statements for April. The vehicle open statements for the month of April will be delayed by a few days, but apart from this delay in posting, no other inconvenience is anticipated.

It is anticipated that the final financial accounts for the month of March will be run this coming weekend and distributed on Monday, together with the initial budget run.

As the computers which we shall be using do not have identical configurations to the machine which was destroyed, it is necessary for us to make some minor program modifications before we can continue with the processing of our daily work. This task is currently being tackled and early in the new week we should be able to set about catching up on the backlog. As soon as this has been done we shall be able to close off the April months data, process the early May data and initiate the credit control procedures in respect of debtors accounts.

We believe that a replacement computer, of slightly reduced power will be available to us within two months. If Burroughs are able to accomplish this as an interim measure it will obviously further improve the situation. The permanent replacement equipment will probably be installed in December. If Burroughs are able to meet that installation date this will be extremely gratifying is real time for this sort of equipment is normally of the order of nine to twelve months.

## COUNTDOWN FIRE CLAIM

In terms of our insurance policy, Countdown is entitled to make a claim in respect of increased cost of working as a result of the fire. Included in this claim should be a charge in respect of time spent by employees on tasks which would not otherwise have been undertaken had it not been for the fire. This would include the time spent in relocating the Countdown offices at Field House and McCarthy Leyland, time spent in flying to the various computer sites which we are utilising, time spent in copying magnetic tapes and restoring data, time spent on recreating the computer tape library, etc. From the examples mentioned, it can be seen that where we would not otherwise have had to undertake the task, this is an item which is claimable in terms of the insurance policy. It should be noted, however, that where a job is run on one of the Johannesburg or Port Elizabeth computers, then the actual running time of the job would not be claimable, unless the job itself takes longer to complete due to the fact that it is being processed on a machine of lesser computing power. In this instance, only the difference in time taken to complete the job would be claimable under our insurance policy.

From the foregoing, it is obvious that a certain amount of judgement will have to be applied in all instances. However, to minimise the loss for the company, it is imperative that full details of all such additional costs be collated regularly and submitted to me so as that I can initiate the necessary claim procedure. Should you be in any doubt as to whether an expense is claimable or not, you should nevertheless document the details thereof and send these to me. In consultation with our insurance brokers, I will then be able to determine whether or not the item is an allowable expense.

To facilitate the collation of all such information, I enclose herewith a specimen form which should be completed daily and remitted to me on a weekly basis. As this element of our insurance claim is typically not initiated by a voucher of one sort or another, it is imperative that you give it your close attention on a day to day basis. Unless you ensure that you and your staff attend to it in this manner, it will not be possible for me to accumulate the necessary information with which to make a claim on our insurance company. "As far as morale is concerned there was a fear among operating staff that their jobs might be at risk. It required explicit reassurances from management to allay these fears. What we didn't realise then was that morale would become a major factor as the recovery exercise progressed."

"Processing away from home is not something I would recommend if one has any choice. The disk packs damaged by the fire had to be replaced and the new packs initialized and loaded before we could commence the file recovery exercise. Programs had to be recompiled to prevent their files being scattered over packs that did not belong to us. There were the frustrations, both to our operators and to the staff of the companies who so readily assisted us, of synchronizing machine time over week-ends and in the graveyard shifts. They, after all, had their own processing to do. We never seemed to be able to get enough time on any one machine and different working standards caused some grief. Lack of machine time and interrupted machine time inevitably led to a drop in our operating standards -- for example dumps were not always done. Then simply communicating with one another was extremely difficult at times since we had operations teams in Durban, Port Elizabeth and

Johannesburg. Tapes were sent to the wrong cities, and, because our experienced staff could not be in three places at once, difficulties were experienced with run balancing and quality control."

"The impact of all this, together with periods of up to three weeks away from home and irregular sleeping and eating, caused the morale of our operating staff to plummet. They could see no end to it."

"In the meantime a makeshift configuration was put together on our other premises. This was 30% slower than our previous Burroughs System. We also compiled our fire claim (Fig. 6)."

"Eventually we received a B4700 on 16th May 1977 and this was operating on 23rd May. We also received a B3700 on 3rd June. As a result we were able to have our last run in Johannesburg on the 10th of June and move our staff down on the 14th. While our tapes and disks weren't compatible and we were working in a very restricted area, at least we had come home."

"We subsequently suffered some tape failures and eventually replaced them completely. The smaller building in which we were then housed was subject to power surges and cuts. With operations in Field Street and systems in South Street, with the attendant parking problems, we encountered further communication problems and morale hit rock bottom."

"You know I would advise every computer use to take a completely new look at his computer security arrangements. You cannot afford not to. We certainly are."

Graham Damp, Group Accountant, collected his thoughts more than a year after the fire.

"The lessons that stick with me start off with insurance. You need to check the insurance implications of working on different machines and how this affects your recoupment from the insurance company. Plan for the worst.

"Then one should look very carefully at the whole backup scene. It is more difficult in practice than you can imagine. Be sceptical about the claims of your suppliers.

"An interesting thing is the attitude of one's users. Their memories are short. After about a month they have forgotten the fire and tend to be intolerant of inconveniences. I guess its understandable.

"One positive effect is that security consciousness permeates the whole organization now. We are currently considering:

– having the entire computer room surrounded from floor to ceiling by a 4 hour fireproof wall,

\- halon gas protection within the area,

\- fire detection linked to the Fire Department as before,

\- physical access to the computer room controlled by magnetic cards, closed circuit TV at the main entrance, an internal release mechanism and a panic bar in the reception area,

\- a mechanism on the front door that will trigger an alarm if the door remains open for more than 30 seconds,

\- a single access only to the computer room,

\- the complete absence of any signs identifying the computer centre,

\- a bomb scare alarm at the telephonist, and

\- bullet-proof glass in the computer room.

"And, no, I don't think we are over-reacting in the current climate when one bears in mind the Remington Rand study on people who have had fires where they found that 27% went out of business, 46% had a lower level of profitability and only 27% were unaffected."
