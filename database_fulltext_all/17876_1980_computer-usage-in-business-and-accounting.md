# Computer Usage in Business and Accounting

Michael J. Cerullo

Professor of Accounting, College of Commerce and Industry, The University of Wyoming, Laramie, Wyoming 82071, USA

Studies over the years have indicated that most users of in-house computers and off-premise Electronic Data Processing (EDP) service bureaus are not taking full advantage of their potential, resulting in automated jobs which provide suboptimum benefits. Proper use should enable companies to develop sophisticated information systems whose output helps management to make more effective decisions. The purpose of this paper is to point out why computer systems are ineffective and how companies can develop computer applications to provide the major advantages.

## 1. Computer users

The larger an organization, the more likely that it uses an in-house computer. In terms of numbers of computers, Figure 1 shows only 50 computer systems in use in 1953 but 155,000 by 1975. This figure is expected to increase to over 400,000 by 1980, with a potential market in excess of 1,000,000.

## 2. User Satisfaction

Many managers feel that their computer operations are characterized by uncontrolled costs and uncontrolled personnel—in short, that their computer operations are not amenable to normal business practices and operations [2]. Most studies conducted over the years indicate that only a small percentage of companies are satisfied with their computer systems. Most feel there is room for much improvement, for users of in-house computers and off-premise data processing service bureaus. A recent study of Peat, Marwick, Mitchell, and Company showed that fewer than one-third of the respondents were satisfied with the services they were receiving from their in-house computer center [2].

Studies completed by the author revealed that most service bureau clients were not using service bureaus effectively. Most service bureau clients surveyed are receiving routine services which do not save them money or give indirect benefits and savings. Such clients are not satisfied with current services, yet they are not planning to expand into more sophisticated, higher pay-off applications [3].

To maximize profit potential, companies should develop and implement computer-based Management Information Systems (MIS) designed to improve staff work and management decision making.

## 3. Computer Applications

Fig. 3 presents applications of computers in the 1950's and 1960's. Of those shown, payroll, general bookkeeping, account receivable, accounts payable, customer order processing and billing, sales analysis and control, inventory control, and mailing list operations were the most frequently automated. It has been estimated that these eight applications accounted for about 80–85 percent of total usage. The remaining applications were designed to provide companies with quality information for making decisions. In the 1950's and 1960's, the computer was used as a "super bookkeeping machine".

In the author's experience, the current most frequently automated in-house business applications are basically the same seven as the 1950's and 1960's. Thus, many (perhaps most) companies have not extended their computer usage. This statement also holds true for off-premise users. The current business applications are shown in Fig. 4.

In summary, most computer users are underutilizing their profit potential. However, smaller companies, if they properly automate routine jobs, should measurably benefit from their computer systems.

## 4. Benefits of Automation

There are basically two benefits, direct and indirect savings. Currently, computer users who automate routine accounting and bookkeeping jobs should receive measurable direct cost savings such as:

\- Increased accuracy and speed in processing data.

\- Reduced inventory carrying expenses

\- Reduced clerical labor expense

\- Reduced equipment expense

\- Reduced manufacturing overhead expense

\- Reduced supplies expense

\- Reduced nonclerical labor expense

While it is true that these benefits alone can pay for a computer system, the small number of users satisfied with their computers may indicate that these direct benefits are not being received in full. In fact, a survey of service bureau clients disclosed that some respondents who use a service bureau had actually incurred increased clerical costs. Only 13% of the survey respondents reportedly were saving money; 28% were not; and 59% did not know if they were saving money [3].

Companies which determine that it is feasible should consider automating jobs which provide them with indirect benefits and savings. Such benefits include:

\- Shortened time to process data

\- Increased processing capacity

\- Improved customer service

\- Improved forecasting

\- Improved strategic and other types of planning

\- Improved management and operational control

\- Improved information (e.g., new, more accurate and/or comprehensive)

\- Reduced error rates

\- Improved purchasing functions that allow buyers to more carefully negotiate contracts

\- Improved production function that reduces lead time between order receipt and delivery, provides improved materials availability and better controls of work flow.

\- Improved transportation scheduling and routing so that faster delivery time would be achieved.

\- Other indirect benefits which depend on management use of computer systems.

In most companies, the indirect benefits are not being considered in a serious and meaningful way. Nevertheless, these benefits and savings are the principle reason for using computers.

Once problems are identified, a company should attempt to overcome as many as possible, so that they will not hinder the high-payoff applications. For example, top management involvement was ranked as the first problem to be overcome. A company should see that top management is involved from the start, and in the planning and systems design phase. In order to encourage their participation, education programs should be conducted to foster knowledge and appreciation for the potential of computer systems.

Secondly, if a company uses a computer requiring a complex data base management system (DBMS), it should hire a qualified person to manage, control, and operate the DBMS. This person, referred to as the Data Base Administrator (DBA), will ensure that the DBMS is properly utilized to store and retrieve information, create files, update and restructure existing files and produce reports. The DBA will assure that the cost of operating the DBMS is kept to a minimum. Most companies using small computers will not require the services of a DBA since the DBMS packages employed are not complex.

Next (3), companies should undertake long term presystems planning, which should be commensurate with the size of the computer facility. In the smaller microcomputer or minicomputer operations, the plan may consist of a few pages of thoughts and projections. Some items to include in the computer plan are:

\- Future jobs to be automated

\- Implementation planning

\- Projections of computer department budgets

• Post-implementation audit procedures

\- Development of backup and recovery procedures

\- Location of computer site

\- Organization location of computer

Recruiting qualified personnel (4) should become less a problem because many colleges and universities now offer computer science and MIS curriculas designed to produce computer programmers, computer technicians, and MIS analysts.

The fifth problem can be overcome by developing an adequate strategy for extending the MIS to other parts of the company. For instance, the company should learn from experience by introducing successful approaches into new areas, identifying interfaces between existing and proposed systems, minimizing behavior problems by developing a strategy to overcome dysfunctional behavior and so forth. If feasible (6) companies should undertake cost benefit analysis studies of each proposed computer application and automate only those jobs whose direct and indirect benefits are greater than costs.

The expanded college and university curriculums in business systems, MIS, and computer science should lessen the problems of integrating the computer with the rest of the company (7) and planning what jobs to automate (10). Higher-level computer applications require the use of custom-written computer programs, and few packages are available for these applications (8). However, as companies recruit qualified computer personnel, this problem should be minimized.

In order to minimize employee (9) and management (13) resistance toward the MIS all personnel should be informed periodically about its status, brochures should be prepared describing the new system, the impact that the MIS is likely to have on jobs should be discussed, and intracompany training programs should devote time to explaining the new system applications. As more qualified computer personnel become available, they should begin to develop informative and sophisticated instructional materials (11).

Another problem is that companies may feel that many possible computer applications are too time consuming to automate (12). As companies progress from low-level to high-level applications, this time factor will increase; MIS development is evolutionary rather than revolutionary, therefore, companies should be prepared to spend many years developing increasingly sophisticated computer applications – which can ultimately result in MIS, if well integrated with one another.

As managers become involved in computer planning and systems design, they should begin to accept information processing (14) as a major corporate resource, to be managed along with capital, labor, and materials. It has been demonstrated that appreciation of computers and involvement with MIS are coproduced. Managers who are appreciative become involved and accept the computer. Another problem is that the role of systems analysis/designers is not clearly defined. (15). Companies should clearly define the roles of computer personnel so that they do not overstep their functions.

Finally, due to large scale integration, the development of chip circuitry, and other technological advances, the present generation of computer hardware will require less maintenance than earlier generations.

## 6. Testing for Computer Applications Feasibility

Whenever a company decides to automate a particular job, four feasibility tests should be met:

\- Technical;

• Economic or cost benefit;

• Operational or human relations;

\- Schedule.

A company usually can determine the technical feasibility of automating business applications. If an EDP system can be developed from today's existing hardware and software technology within limits of company resources, then it is technically feasible. On the other hand, if management desired a highly complex computer system installed, then technical feasibility may become more difficult to determine. The company might encounter problems with the program for the new computer system. Programers normally either want to copy ideas that are familiar or start from scratch \*. Neither extreme really fits the need. Computer programmers should study successful methods of similar systems. Then, the programmers with that knowledge should be encouraged to copy only what is good but create only where necessary.

Also, if the proposed system includes a newly or not fully developed hardware component, the successful implementation of the system may prove to be infeasible. But if all hardware components are proven, the chances of technical failure will be minimized, even though the new system may not reflect the latest technology by the time it is actually operational [4].

Economic feasibility or benefit-cost analysis cannot be determined as quickly and objectively. The company must estimate the recurring operating costs, start-up costs, and the rate for discounting cash flow. Then, the cost savings should be computed. Both direct and indirect benefits should be included. Finally, capital budgeting techniques should be applied to determine if the net savings are greater than the net present value of costs.

The next consideration is to determine if the new system will perform its functions within the existing organization structure. The designer must indeed recognize human problems from the beginning of the system development process. A system will never be operationally successful if users ignore, resist, sabotage, or otherwise show aggression toward it. In order to minimize operational feasibility, companies must be familiar with the types of dysfunctional behavior likely to be exhibited by various classes of personnel and develop a strategy to overcome such behavior.

The final feasibility test is concerned with estimating the time to analyze, design, and implement the proposed application. In recent years, Program

Evaluation Review Technique (PERT) has proven to be a valuable tool in estimating the time or schedule feasibility of proposed systems.

## 7. Developing a Level of Computerized Applications

As the problems are overcome and each proposed computer system application meets the aforementioned feasibility tests, more and more companies who own or lease in-house computers or use outside EDP service bureaus receive both direct and indirect benefits by automating increasingly higher levels of jobs. Since many of these increasing levels are accounting related, accountants should assume an important role in this development. Management should ensure that internal accountants are included in the design team.

There are basically three such levels of computer applications as shown in Fig. 7, which shows levels of applications from low-complexity applications designed to reduce general and administrative expenses to high level decision making aids. Examples of low level jobs are presented in Fig. 8. These applications normally provide directly measurable benefits. Normally, such applications are the first jobs to be accomplished. Some companies never advance beyond this level. Small companies can often successfully use micro or minicomputers or off-premise service bureaus to provide low level automated applications: the “data processing” needs. Management “information” needs in smaller companies can be met by developing manual management information systems (on an informal basis).

## 8.1. Cost of Goods

The following examples will illustrate some computer applications in the area of cost of goods manufactured and sold. An automated inventory system should result in lower carrying charges, insurance premiums and material handling costs as well as a possible reduction in warehouse space rental. It would provide for an automatic reordering of items, resulting in an increased inventory turnover and smaller capital investment. The increased turnover would increase cash flow and improve the gross profit margin.

An automated purchasing system should result in an improved purchasing function. The biggest savings in this area can be realized by minimizing the time spent on routine purchase orders. Most of the routine orders can be eliminated by designing an automated purchase system that will initiate a replenishment request, select a vendor and generate a purchase order. With this type of system the purchasing agent can be freed for handling emergencies and doing a thorough job on those orders that account for most of the company's purchasing dollar.

In companies with a more sophisticated approach, the computer can handle the entire purchasing function on routine orders without human intervention. A company can tie its purchasing function directly to its inventory control system. When an inventory item reaches a predetermined minimum level, a purchase order is automatically printed out and mailed to a preselected vendor. Such an automated system also provides a report to the purchasing department if the order is not delivered within a reasonable time. In addition, more detailed analyses of vendors are easily obtainable. The automated purchase system can produce a report evaluating the vendor's delivery record, or how much the company is spending with a particular subcontractor. This type of information, coupled with the elimination of routine work, can be important to buyers in negotiating contracts, and thereby cutting costs.

## 8.2. Typical Level 2 User

The following is typical of a "sophisticated" level 2 user [5]. The company, a precision sheet metal fabricator that manufactures metal boxes for office and communications equipment, grosses about \$ 4.5 million yearly. It indicates that it has been able to do 50% more business with the same number of employees and half the inventory.

Since installing the computer, the company has progressed beyond the routine level 1 computerized accounting applications. To illustrate:

1. The small-size computer system prepares a job cost analysis that serves as the basis for making bids.

2. If the contract is awarded to the firm, then the same information (still stored) is used to generate material lists needed for the job.

3. By using a special customized computer program, the loading of the plant is kept as high as possible. This means that none of the 200 employees are idle and waiting for work; e.g., as soon as a punch press operator completes one run, the next job together with all applicable tooling and materials — is at hand. Similarly, correct equipment and supplies are always available.

4. The computer system plays a critical role in helping management decide if they should accept rush reorder jobs -- and how quickly these requests can be met. This is done by simulating the loading of the plant as if a new job had been accepted. If the simulation indicates that the job can be handled without delaying delivery on current runs, then the order is accepted. If not, the customer is told the delay.

5. Flexibility of plant scheduling is enhanced when the opposite occurs - a customer asks for a delay in delivery or even cancels an order. Within 24 hours, the computer calculates a new plant schedule.

6. A 50% inventory reduction was achieved by coding all purchased materials and parts. Management realized for the first time how much material was common to housings made for different customers. Based on this fact, the entire approach to inventory was changed. Instead of buying parts and materials for each job, a system of floating allocation evolved. Before ordering an item, the computer first checks current inventory to determine the quantity on hand. Based on immediate future needs of work in process, the system determines the additional quantity to order and yet leave enough on hand.

This approach to inventory control is particularly valuable when suppliers are unable to deliver or deliver late. The computer gives advanced warning of shortages. Or, it can indicate a substitute item in case a material or part runs out.

7. The computer is used in purchasing. Sometimes a supplier offers to sell material at discount. Based on a computer analysis of past and current jobs, management can make an estimate on the advantage in acquiring the material and make a valid decision.

Often, as a result of tight supplies, it becomes apparent that the company will run out of materials before a job can be completed. The computer can indicate various alternatives, such as a screw of the same length but made of a more costly material or one made of a less critical material (only if the parts are appropriately coded).

This trend is not expected to significantly decline through the eighties, which means that there should be a tremendous increase in the number of level two applications.

A computerized marketing information system should provide marketing personnel with decision making information related to pricing, advertising, product promotion, profitability, sales force effort and sales forecasting. With respect to sales forecasts, the computer can be used to forecast the sales volume of each item. Briefly, this can be accomplished as follows:

\- The computer model analyzes the trend of sales for each item.

\- It compensates for seasonality of demand as well as for unusual circumstances such as special promotional efforts.

\- It calculates and applies a factor to compensate for the extent of error in past forecasts.

\- It then prints out the new sales forecast.

The above steps are basically the ones that a good product manager would follow in making a sales forecast. However, the computer speed can permit forecasts to be made more frequently and with less effort; also top managers can ask "what/if" questions and get an immediate response.

A properly designed human resource or personnel information system should provide managers with information about the total investment in human assets, manpower planning information, career profiling information, and any systems modifications necessary to achieve organizational goals.

## 10. Summary

A few years ago, only large companies could afford to purchase or lease in-house computers. Today, smaller organizations are able to afford inexpensive mini-computers, which enable these companies to compete with larger companies more successfully. By the year 1985, the actual number of installed computers is expected to increase to more than 500,000 units, exclusive of minicomputer systems. However, studies have indicated that only a small percentage of in-house and off-premise service bureau users are satisfied with the performance of their computer. In fact, most computer systems are not fully utilized.

Surveys of computer users show that most companies use computers to automate routine jobs (accounts receivable and payable, billing and invoicing, sales analysis, inventory level, preparation of financial statements, payroll, and preparation of mailing lists). These applications provide companies with direct benefits and savings, such as reduced clerical labor expense. No indirect savings (such as improved customer service or improved forecasting) are provided by these routine jobs.

Very few companies have expanded their computer usage into more sophisticated applications, because there are presently many problems which are hindering the development of higher-pay-off jobs. Some of these problems are: lack of top management involvement in the planning and systems design phase, cost and complexity of using data base management systems, inadequate presystem planning, recruiting qualified computer personnel, inadequate strategy for extending the MIS to other parts of the company, etc. Companies should develop a strategy to overcome or minimize these problems.

Companies should consider the technical, economic, operational and schedule feasibilities whenever they are considering an application for automation. These four feasibility tests must be met before any job is computerized.

Companies will receive both direct and indirect benefits from automation as they automate increasingly higher levels of applications. There are three computer application levels: level one - applications designed to reduce expenses; level two - applications designed to reduce items affecting cost of goods manufactured and sold; and level three - applications designed to improve management information. Practically all companies have automated level one jobs. Some companies never progress beyond this level. Smaller companies do not need to expand their computer applications into levels two and three; they can develop informal, non-computer based MIS.

However, the majority of companies must extend their automation to levels two and three to take full advantage of the computer capacity to provide the major benefits of computerization – the indirect, intangible, or intrinsic benefits. For many years in the future, companies will be expanding their jobs to encompass levels two and three jobs. Since many of these higher level jobs are accounting related, accountants will assume a key staff role in their development.

Companies should also be familiar with the major factors which affect the development of MIS. Once these factors have been identified, the reasons for their importance should be analyzed. Companies can use these factors to help them predict what factors are critical to the success of a given systems effort.
