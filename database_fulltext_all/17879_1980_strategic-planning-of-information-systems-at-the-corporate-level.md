# Strategic Planning of Information Systems at the Corporate Level

Ronald H. Kay \*, Norbert Szyperski, Klaus Höring and Gangolf Bartz

BIFOA, Institute for Organization and Automation, University of Cologne, Universitätstrasse 45, Köln 41, D-5000 Germany

Strategic issues related to information systems are subject to conflicting trends. One is the more rapid change in environmental factors; the other is the growing complexity of requests that reduce the rate of response. This study examines the basis for concern about current issues. The factors are reviewed in the light of recently published material. Three organizational structures for information system planning are compared in terms of their ability to meet different organizational requirements. Techniques which have been used successfully in the definition of strategic issues and analysis of environmental factors are presented. The object is to aid the integration of information system strategies into the overall strategy of an enterprise.

Keywords: Information system planning, strategic planning, corporate planning, organizational structures, strategic issues and analysis.

## 1. Motivation and objectives of study

Corporate management and those responsible for Information Systems appear apprehensive about their ability to manage information and communications functions today. This comes at a time when operational dependencies, operating expense, capital investment, and staffing of the information system functions has reached an all time high.

Apprehension about the management of information systems is not new, but the size of the problem and the ability to implement solutions appear to be diverging. Canning [4] reviewed this problem to find, if anything, growing concern. Tricker [11] identified the three issues most likely to have the greatest impact on information system developments in the next decade as:

\- internal organizational problems;

● involvement in the organization by third party interests:

● the impact of external factors.

A conference at the MIT Sloan School [8] examined the new information systems environment and its implications for management. The 29 participants included twelve corporate executives with primary responsibility for information systems; their principal concern focused on management philosophy.

During the Business Data Processing Symposium in Germany, devoted to the subject of "Trends in the Development of System Analysis", Szyperski [10] provided a framework for the consideration of strategic planning of information systems as part of corporate planning. It was this problem and the suggested approach which provided the motivation for this study. It aims to facilitate a discussion between corporate management and executives responsible for information systems. The objective is a common understanding of current concerns and a review of the state of the art and related experience which may alleviate these concerns. This study does not attempt to provide a new "framework for strategic planning." Rather it is aimed at extracting (from experience) those methods, techniques, and practices which appear most applicable to the solution of the problems. The limitations of traditional "strategic planning" were recognized by Szyperski [9]. For a definition of the scope of our effort, the concept of Strategic Issue Analysis elaborated by Ansoff [1] seems

most appropriate:

"To respond to significant discontinuities outside and inside the enterprise. To respond, not according to the enterprise organizational jurisdiction, but according to the logic of the issue. To respond not on a fixed calendar basis but whenever urgency and priority dictate."

To limit ourselves in this fashion is not to play down the role of traditional strategic planning, but to recognize the existence of such excellent sources as the work of Vancil [12].

## 2. Basis for Current Management Concern

The rapid evolution of technology has produced many options to be evaluated in the selection of systems and component, but the effective application of these has been difficult to integrate into future needs. The trend of decreased price to performance ratio should provide new opportunities and remove constraints rather than serve as an impediment.

## 2.1. Interactive Systems

Online and interactive systems have found a level of acceptance which was not cost effective until recently. These systems have created new awareness on the part of the user. Variation in response time at the terminal gives a user the basis for a subjective yet quantitative evaluation of the system. Various system aids allow the user to inquire into current resource allocation, such as service priorities of other users. Thus change in system operations is subject to scrutiny by a multiplicity of users, often before the operations manager has evaluated the effect of a change. Remote access (or virtual machines) have given the terminal user a feeling of independence: no longer scheduling work for the turn-around of a batch system.

Conversely, any deterioration of service level or constraint of available resources is perceived as a threat to independence in terms of work habits; since work habits are the users' control of their effectiveness, their tolerance toward impediments are predictably low.

This new awareness reaches top management as an escalation of issues that result from conflicting interests of disparate users in separate business units. Resolution of such conflicts often involves basic strategic and organizational issues which are beyond the responsibility of information system management: yet a high level of information system competence is required to solve the problems.

## 2.2. Decentralization

Another derivative of the reduced cost/performance ratio is the growing feasibility of stand-alone systems. The centralized system derived its justification from economies of scale and concentration of programming skills. It also allowed control. Since this has been the prevalent strategy, much effort was devoted to concentrating applications into a single facility. This strategy facilitated a number of very important objectives: integrated data base and communications, standardization of operational protocoll, and concentration and prioritization of application development. On the other hand, the complexity and need for shared facilities made resource allocation the principal objective of system design. In this process, the needs of the “majority of users” took precedence over the concern for the individual user. Thus, the users’ motivation to obtain a stand-alone system; it is not necessarily based on its superiority but upon the desired independence.

Another factor is the increasing formalization of the approval process for new applications or resources. If evaluation of needs is confined to the strategic objectives of the requesting unit, decisions become simpler. The growing awareness that the financial commitment to information systems is becoming an increasingly large portion of the total budget, on the other hand, has produced efforts to consolidate requests and tighten up the approval process. To the users this appears to be an impediment to efforts to increase their effectiveness, particularly when they see their needs readily satisfied by a small stand-alone system. Information Systems management has been absorbed with the implementation of systems based upon accessibility of the appropriate information at all levels of the enterprise. User autonomy hardly seems to advance such objectives. Yet the users' new awareness has created a demand for responsiveness and flexibility which is difficult to satisfy for all users at the same time. An analysis of the managerial and economic issues of distributed computing, such as Emery's [5], illustrates the multifaceted aspects of this topic. The objectives of centralization or decentralization can be considered from different viewpoints:

\- long term corporate strategy. If it calls for overall centralization or decentralization, the information system should support it.

\- facilitating an alternative corporate strategy not realizable without new information system organization (e.g., banking, mailorder retailing).

\- improving the organization of the information system function.

These viewpoints are not mutually exclusive but may be appropriate at different times. Decentralization or distribution of some information systems functions can be motivated by a variety of objectives. To quote Ziegler [14]: "The key incentives that motivate a business firm to assess distributed systems usually fall into one or more of the following categories: subjective, economic, technical, organizational, and a desire to reduce the operational dependencies on a single resource. Subjectively, management must assess distributed systems because of industry pressure and for protection against a lost business opportunity. Economic considerations are those of potential savings in communication costs, the sharing of costly resources, a higher responsiveness to new business applications and higher user productivity. Technical considerations stem from the opportunity to reduce the response time for specific applications, to improve availability to the end user and to reduce the complexity associated with trade-offs required for a single node-system supporting all applications. Organizationally, distribution with communication links provides a vehicle to relate data processing to organizational structures and organizational information flow. By separating and distributing business applications, the impact to the business by a single outage can be reduced."

Decentralization has been the most important issue drawing the attention of corporate management to their need to become involved in the development of an integrated strategy for the entire enterprise.

## 2.2 New Demands for Information

As suggested by Nolan [6], many enterprises are becoming aware of the growing value of the data they process, store, and use relative to the value of the boxes used to store and process the data. This realization gives rise to an entirely new viewpoint, the management of data resources, rather than data processing. In some enterprises, data is seen as raw material acquired at a cost; this raw material must be efficiently processed into a value added product and distributed to satisfy the demand of the consumer. This view places Information Systems management in the position of assuming responsibility for understanding the value of their product to specific groups of consumers and thus creating new markets. Additional demands have been created by legislation directed at security and privacy of data: it is difficult to facilitate access to all (personal) data by the concerned individual, yet restrict access to others, especially when this is an a posteriori task.

In some enterprises new and imaginative uses of already existing data may provide greater leverage than improved efficiency of the information system. Corporate management would like to be in a position of choosing among alternatives with an understanding of the short and long term implications and trade-offs.

## 2.3. Communication and Network Facilities

New communication and network facilities raise issues which go far beyond the realm of information systems management as it has been known in the past. For example, if the consumer has access to a public network, can a retail enterprise use this network to reach its customers? Are the requirements for an intra-company network compatible with those of a public network?

The growing interdependence of information and communication systems suggests the need for a coordinated approach at the corporate level.

An examination of the basis for these current concerns cannot fail but validate their reality. Moreover, the multiplicity and diversity of issues suggests the need for effective methods of dealing with them.

There is evidence that some efforts to deal with such problems have failed dramatically, but they are hardly attractive case studies for publication. One U.S. bank which undertook a project in distributed data processing related their experience at the end of five years as follows:

\- The application which motivated this development is running successfully, meeting all operational requirements which were initially defined.

\- The hardware economies, as defined at the price level five years ago, were realized.

\- Development costs (i.e., software) not only exceeded estimates by a significant factor, but more than wiped out any savings in hardware.

\- Hardware available today is much less costly than the hardware of five years ago.

\- What made the solution attractive five years ago was its stand-alone nature. Today we cannot afford to implement one application at a time; time and cost mitigate against it.

\- Management is asking whether we are in the banking business or in the system development business. Currently available vendor software directed at our problem, if anything, is better since it was developed for today's hardware. It is less costly since the development costs are amortized over multiple users.

The organization which undertook this development is recognized as a leader in the field. Yet it seems that corporate and information systems management at the time of project initiation did not have the perspective to recognize the implication of a “successful” completion of the project. Indeed, both the definition of the original objectives as well as the criteria for success appeared inappropriate by the time of completion.

## 3. Definition of Issues

## 3.1. The Process

Ansoff [2] assumes the existence of three groups of individuals who respectively detect, prioritize, and resolve strategic issues. We first examine the process by which issues are defined (see fig. 1) in order to motivate the need for groups or organizational constructs that deal with strategic issues.

Clearly strategic issues may be raised anywhere. There is no problem if the issue can be resolved where it was raised. Not so with issues which require input from multiple units or which require expert knowledge outside the scope of the organization. Such issues must come to a point of coordination (a "clearing house") which can size the issue in terms of its importance and determine what part of the organization can best resolve it. This clearing house must also be responsible for the adequacy of the evaluation and move positively evaluated issues to a point of decision. Without such a coordinating function, new issues outside the range of established organizational activity tend to be ignored, because many organizations do not look kindly upon an organizational unit that assumes responsibility for issues outside its own sphere of activity.

Some organizational environments are hostile to new ideas; this leads to an avoidance of the problem of how to deal with new issues Corporate executives who sense such a situation may well consider specific assignment of responsibility for new ideas. A variety of methods have been developed for stimulating new ideas: suggestion programs, periodic task force directed at investigation of new opportunities outside the traditional sphere of the organization, and a separate budget for the pursuit of new opportunities.

The organizational response to the need for defining strategic issues should produce specific work products as a result of the definition process. Warfield [13] suggests 12 such work products:

1. A well-conceived title for the problem or issue.

2. A descriptive scenario, explaining the nature of the problem, and how it came to be a problem.

3. An understanding of what disciplines or professions are relevant to an attack on the problem.

4. An assessment of scope.

5. A determination of the sectors of the organization involved.

6. An identification of the actors in the problem-solving situation.

7. An identification of need.

8. An identification of alterables (those elements subject to change).

9. An identification of major constraints.

10. Some partitioning of the problem into relevant elements.

11. Some isolation of the subjective elements of the problem.

12. Description of interactions among relevant elements.

## 3.2. Initial Sizing of Issues

One of the most difficult tasks in a rapidly changing environment is prioritization. The established planning process is often unable to respond rapidly to discontinuous change. Most experienced executives have a mental checklist which serves as a basis for initial sizing of an issue. But those assigned to the solution of the issue do not share the information used by the delegating executive. Hence, the lack of basis for common understanding of the issue and the desired course of action. While it is unreasonable to burden the executive with the responsibility for assuring common understanding, help in clarifying the definition of such problems is essential.

A formalization of the “mental checklist” as illustrated in Fig. 3 should suggest the rationale for problem definition. The initiating executive should indicate concurrence with the priority assigned and the anticipated course of action before the issue is delegated beyond the coordination point. While it is not always practical to pin down an executive, the very fact that some questions are left unanswered (question marks, Fig. 3) help focus attention upon possible future issues.

## 3.3. Issues of Current Interest

What information systems related strategic issues are likely to require top management attention in the future? Beyond the issues of decentralization of information systems and the new demands for information discussed in Section 2, we see as the most important:

● development of I/S competence

\- prioritization of application development

\- software development.

Each issue deserves detailed consideration and is in fact receiving attention in the current literature. In our study, emphasis has been placed on organizational implications.

## 3.3.1. Development of I/S Competence

A balanced approach to the matter of decentralization requires a combination of in-depth information systems competence and the seasoned judgment of an experienced corporate executive. Unless or until executives can be drawn from the ranks of information systems personnel, most of today's corporate executives must acquire in-depth information systems competence. In banking and insurance, extended involvement with the information systems function is often part of the career development path of people with executive potential. Manufacturing, distribution and service organizations are becoming aware of this need also.

## 3.3.2. Prioritization of Application Development

In largely decentralized organizations of independent strategic business units, application development prioritization may be more difficult than in an enterprise organized along functional lines. In either case, common or interdependent applications require judgment and decisions which encompass nearly every unit and function. Some organizational construct and top management involvement must exist to deal with the most far reaching application development projects. Peisl [7] advocates that a corporate executive manage large development projects that affect several units or functions.

## 3.3.3. Software Development

Software development is the most labor intensive part of the information system. What kind of software competence is required of the users and desired in the short and long term? What should be the policy toward ready-made versus custom software? How is policy influenced by the growing competence of software vendors? Who is best qualified to set policy for the entire organization – the user or the I/S department?

## 4. Organizational Structures

## 4.1. Top Management Involvement

Information systems strategy must be influenced, reviewed and approved by corporate management. The top information executive should chair a steering committee which reviews and approves all I/S related issues that are to be brought before top management. He should not be looked upon as spokesman for the I/S function. The members of the steering committee may be the executives of the major functions and/or divisions of the enterprise whose operations are affected by the I/S issue under discussion. The committee should be able to:

\- direct both the corporate and I/S planning staff to undertake studies;

\- appoint task forces to address issues;

\- allocate resources to facilitate these studies.

It is essential for top management to define the responsibility of the steering committee. If the committee must review every project and system selection, the review will either be perfunctory or absorb all available time. If the review is deemed necessary, it should be delegated to a subcommittee that returns only controversial or major issues. The steering committee should establish policy, set objectives, and set time tables.

Many organizations use the ad hoc committee or task force approach to resolve an issue for steering committee review and top management approval. The task force approach obviates large staff operations which are often removed from operational considerations. Experience suggests that such task forces provide excellent training and serve to acquaint all levels of management with information system issues.

Our recommendations for an I/S executive at the corporate level and an I/S steering committee are based upon analysis of several organizational structures for the planning and management of I/S related issues. ## 4.3. Comparison of Organizational Structures

The division of responsibility and the established procedures have a significant effect upon the quality of response to strategic issues, such as:

● the detection of weak signals;

\- the quality of the information which provides the basis for decisions;

● the acceptance of proposals and recommendations;

● the resolution of conflict (see also Figure 8).

It is difficult to make a comparative evaluation of our three structures without reference to a specific organizational context. The listing of advantages and disadvantages in Figure 8 draws attention to some of the issues that should be considered in an evaluation. The pros and cons are of varying significance (in fact, an advantage in one context could be a disadvantage in another).

To limit consideration to three structure is justified only for clarity and emphasis. Obviously numerous variations are possible. A systematic approach to the selection of the optimum structure is one of the important issues which deserve more attention.

## 5. Impact Analysis

In order to evaluate the impact of environmental factors, we saw the need for an instrument which makes it possible to analyze strategic issues in terms of our ability to:

1. predict the manifestation and effect of environmental factors relative to that issue; and

2. react with a discretionary response to environmental factors.

Ball and Lorange [3] have suggested such an instrument as adjunct or aid to strategic planning. In adapting their techniques, we have generated two lists of possible environmental factors: those which relate to the enterprise in general (Fig. 9), and those which relate more specifically to I/S related issues (Fig. 10). The lists are not meant to be exhaustive, but to suggest some factors of concern here. Additional factors relevant to particular organizations should be included in such prioritized and periodically updated lists.

The analysis is also valuable in assessing the level of risk and in directing early attention to important but ill defined issues.

A. Decentralization of Information System

There is a trend toward more decentralized capabilities due to:

1. high communication costs (particularly in Europe);

2. decrease in complexity by isolation of function;

3. desire for autonomy by users;

4. difficulty and delays in centralized approval; and

5. security.

## B. Organizational Response

There is a growing awareness of the need for organizational response to new problems and opportunities. Some reasons are:

1. top management involvement in the integration of organizational and informational needs;

2. lack of I/S experience in top management;

3. lack of business experience in I/S management;

4. growing concern of institutions outside the enterprise with the role of information systems.

## C. Cost of Software Development

Cost are rising due to:

1. increasing personnel costs;

2. increasing complexity of systems to be develop-

ment and maintained;

3. desirability to adapt to most advanced hardware offerings for function and cost effectiveness.

Affordability of increasing systems capability is the only countervailing force.

## D. Growing Competence of Software Vendors

The improved products:

1. permit operation with minimum critical mass programming staff;

2. allow load balancing of programming effort;

3. require in-house competence for specification, testing, and quality control of vendor products.

## E. Communication Capabilities

These are expected to show:

1. no technological barriers for indefinite future;

2. cost as a function of market and national policy;

3. new market opportunity or service offerings.

4. required investment in pilot projects;

## F. Integrated Data Base Systems

These have shown:

1. growing pains, but effective reality in many organizations;

2. emergence of various size systems;

3. challenge to make more effective use of existing information and to define informational needs better;

4. distributed systems not yet generally available, but coming (within the next 5 years).

## G. Text Processing

This presents:

1. a real opportunity to increase productivity of office operations;

2. rapidly evolving capability toward integrated office;

3. a basis for future electronic mail;

4. ultimate integration with data processing.

But there are currently many divergent approaches to the problem.

## H. Hardware

1. Cost/performance improvement is expected to continue. Some hardware economies will be traded-off for ease of software development and maintenance.

2. Minicomputer cost advantage only if supplied without operating system software with its implied maintenance and continued software enhancement.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

During the course of this study, the authors were able to discuss some of the ideas with a group of German corporate and information system executives, who represented many industries including steel, chemicals, automobiles, auto parts, banking, and insurance. Along with a generally positive reaction to the study, they raised a number of additional issues that deserve attention:

1. Consideration of the social problems. Most prominent is the issue of changing work content and skill requirements relative to the current work force.

2. There is a problem of technical competence. At the corporate level, there is a lack of I/S related experience, at the I/S management and staff level there is often inadequate understanding of the business.

3. There is a division of opinions about a common basis for discussion across industries. Some felt the questions to be the same but the answers different.

4. There is disagreement about the uniqueness of information systems related issues vis a vis other strategic issues such as investment policy, plant automation, etc.

5. The personalities of the executives have a significant influence upon the structure of the organization and the decision making process. There is disagreement about the desirability of changing this influence (in either direction).

6. Strategies for enhancing communication between software developers and user groups are lacking. How much I/S and business specific knowledge is required of the user and application developer?

7. Methods for establishing priorities among application developments are generally considered inadequate.

8. There is disagreement about the relative influence of the user when considering software developed in-house or outside.
