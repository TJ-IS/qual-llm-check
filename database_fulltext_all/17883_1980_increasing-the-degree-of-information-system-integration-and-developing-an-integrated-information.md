# Increasing the Degree of Information System Integration and Developing an Integrated Information System

Michal Solotruk and Milutín Kristofič
Computing Research Centre, 885 31 Bratislava, Dúbravská č.
3. Czechoslovakia

The structure of an information system is defined, and the structural elements are considered as factors in providing measures of the degree of integration of an information system. Absorption, exclusion, reorganization and transfer are processes considered in the role of increasing the degree of integration. Finally measures for the effectiveness of integration of two or more systems are discussed.

Keywords: Information system, structure, relation, integrated information system, integration of information systems, increasing the degree of integration, integration processes.

An information system may be investigated at various levels of detail. One of the levels determines the components of the information system. A class of elements of the same type is considered to be a component. For example we can define the following components: technique, data (their groupings), people (the human factor), activities, and methods (directives, programs, rules, procedures). Our approach is based on the assumption that the integration of information systems is realized by creating linkages between elements; a structured approach is needed to define the elements with their properties, and then to define the linkages.

On a lower level, we may consider every component to have a basic structure which contains homogenous elements. However, this basic structure could still be further split into other more detailed structures.

We consider the following to be the basic structures of an information system:

\- technique,

· data.

\- people.

\- activities.

\- methods.

We thus consider the information system to be a complex of suitably interfaced basic structures that provide activities from data collection to presentation.

A property is generally defined as: an aspect of the object that shows its differences from or similarities to other objects. A property manifests itself by correlation between objects. Every property is relative. Every individual thing has a infinite number of properties, the unity of which expresses its quality.

The property of an element allows the identification of the element or shows the difference from other elements. Because of the properties, relationships exist between them; some are realized by linkages. An element may have several properties. One of the possible partitioning of properties is through identifiers (keys) which characterize and identify the element.

A relation is the connection between two or more elements through their properties. The change or destruction of a relation occurs through the change or destruction of a property.

An integrated information system is an information system created by integration of two or more already existing, independently functioning information systems.

The integration of information systems is a goal oriented process of setting up linkages between elements of two or more information systems.

Integration linkages are connections which are set up in the process of integration and which ensure interaction between elements of information systems. Because every element of the information system has several properties, there may be several integration linkages between two elements.

Interaction is the mutual influence exerted between elements of a system or between elements of the system and its environment.

The degree of integration of the information system is a number expressing the degree of mutual dependence of its elements.

Increasing the degree of integration of an information system occurs when the interdependence of its elements is deliberately increased without producing a new information system. Quite often some sort of integration occurs by internal subsystem interlinking. In our view, this is not the integration of information systems, i.e. the setting up of an integrated information system. If we did not differentiate the process of integration of information systems and the process of increasing the degree of integration of an information system, any improvement of the existing information system which increased the mutual dependence of its elements would be considered to be integration; it would then be practically impossible to differentiate between improvements to an information system and its initial implementation.

## 2. Increasing the Degree of Information System Integration

The process of increasing the degree of information system integration will occur on removing disfunctional linkages or by adding or excluding elements of the system. In this, it should be added that (in certain cases) a high degree of information system integration is not necessarily good.

Increasing the degree of information system integration may occur because of the following processes:

\- absorption;

\- exclusion;

\- reorganization; or

\- transfer.

Absorption is a process of receiving new elements from the environment into the structures of the information system. This can occur through error correction (by original system author mistakes; e.g., by not including some data into the system), or through the natural development of the information system (e.g., change of user requirements through changes in management).

The prerequisite for absorption of some element from the environment into the information system is that it have such properties that allow it to enter the system and produce linkages. Thus, by absorption the degree of information system integration is increased.

Exclusion is the reverse of absorption. The degree of integration may be increased by excluding elements that provide obstacles to the natural development of the information system, because they have properties which cause disfunctional linkages or prevent creation of functional linkages. An example is the exclusion of data which is hardly used but can slow the processing speed. Exclusion can achieve a higher degree of integration by strengthening or increasing the number of linkages between the elements.

Reorganization does not cause changes to the number of elements, but it changes the arrangement of elements in the structure (i.e., the linkages between the elements). The linkages may change in their number and strength (intensity). Linkages which are superfluos, inefficient, or act in a disfunctional way are removed, while new linkages are generated (those with a favourable effect on the functioning of the system).

Transfer is a special case. It includes absorption and exclusion and acts simultaneously as a factor changing the degree of integration of two systems; i.e., absorption and exclusion are specific cases of transfer. For transfer, we follow the changes in both information systems, trying not to decrease the degree of integration in either system and to increase the degree of integration in one, at least, of the participating systems. For example, if two systems were constructed according to their separate requirements or the administrative partitioning of the organization, then some time there may be numerous demands on simultaneous and combined data processing of both systems. In order to make data more accessible, it may be necessary to transfer data from one system to another.

Another problem is that of element duplication or cross-system transfer (where they are transferred from the original system). Elements may be reproducible by an information system (e.g., elements of data, methods, and activities), or they may not be reproducible (e.g., technology and people). We presume that transfer will mainly consist of reproducible elements (e.g., the transfer of data).

Programs are also frequently transferred. There is also the possibility of transferring specific activities; e.g., data checking, or establishing the protocol associated with errors. It may be possible to transfer such activities into another information system (though each activity should be preserved in the original system). The case for transferring non-reproducible elements (i.e., people and technology) will seldom be made, though bilateral transfer (exchange) may sometimes be advantageous. The transfer of some elements will sometimes cause a chain reaction causing further transfer, or exclusion, or absorption, or reorganization.

In a wider context we may consider the types of processes as not only increasing the degree of information system integration but also as helping to improve the functioning of the information system. Further, these processes can be and quite often must be mutually combined; e.g., after exclusion of some unsuitable element it must be replaced by some other. This element may have properties allowing it to form further linkages in the system.

As already stated, the process of integration take place between at least two information systems. In transfer, the process refers to two information systems; thus there is potential for developing an integrated information system, and transfer represent a sort of transition which increases the degree of integration to the point of total integration into one information system.

## 3. Integration of information systems

Before discussing the various types of integration processes, we must briefly state the aims and substance of the process of integration.

Demand for information systems integration occurs because of qualitative demands on information that are not fulfilled in the currently functioning information systems. Filling these demands becomes the main aim of the integrated information system, but the integrated information system should meet other requirements; e.g., increased speed of execution, decreased costs, higher quality information, etc. Inefficient integration is bad. The new integrated information system should have qualitatively new properties and new aims.

Because we are trying to achieve an integrated information system, there must be new common structures of data resulting from integration of the participating information systems. Should this not occur, there would be a paradoxical situation where the integrated information system effectively contains two or more different information systems; the only integrating fact would be that they operate on a common computer (i.e., common technical structure).

information systems integration does not take place by chance: it is a process. The process occurs through several stages in which the systems are making contact through the structure of people. First, there is mutual (or maybe unilateral) study and analysis of the information systems, involving efforts to identify identical or complementary properties of elements. The next stage generally involves transferring (unilaterally or bilaterally) some elements.

The transfer of elements (especially of data) may advance from irregular to permanent exchange. When this exchange of elements is formalized, interlinking normally occurs.

A significant feature of interlinking is that integration takes place without substantial interference with the structures of the participating systems and without substantial limitation of their independence. As an example, in the sphere of socioeconomic information systems, organizational changes may be envisaged only in the register of organizations; the operational data (except for any time series) is not effected.

Intersection (or overlapping) takes place when the structure of each of the participating systems has one or more elements whose properties are identical (or sufficiently similar) or one of the participating systems has elements which can be used in everyone of the systems. Integration by intersection then eliminates duplicates.

Data, methods and activities may be eliminated by intersection if they are duplicated. However, techniques and people must be treated differently!

Intersection may set up a common data base. The common data base does not contain duplicate data. Thus intersection is really a higher form of interlinking.

Unification is the process of producing a common structure for two or more information systems; independence of the original structures disappear. Economic reasons are often the stimuli; they lead to unification of organizational units which implies unification of their information systems. However, unification may result from natural development. Within the sphere of statistics there are often reasons for unification; e.g. of the system of sector statistics of industry with the system of statistics of industry and transport. But for unification, the functions and aims of the information systems must be compatible.

By unification, a system is created which is bigger than those systems which were integrated. The size might cause it to be clumsy, costly, and inflexible. Thus it is necessary to study the efficiency of the integration.

The important parameters of an integrated information system may be:

-- its functional capacity; i.e., the number of functions which the integrated information system can fulfil, including their quality and content.

\- its range; i.e., the number of systems which were integrated and their size (number of elements).

— its integrity; i.e., the degree of mutual dependence of the individual information systems.

its complexity; i.e., the number of structures which were integrated.

\- its "compactness"; i.e., the number of elements of integration, integration linkages, and their intensity.

These parameters are, of course, both quantitative and qualitative.

## 4. The Aims of Information Systems Integration

The objectives of integration depend on the view; either

-- defining integration as a process, where the result is given without means of evaluation; or

-- defining the result as increasing some quality.

There must be some qualitative evaluation of the result, because integration has little meaning if it does not lead to some advantage. Thus efficiency and appropriateness of integration is usually included in the definition. But from a theoretical standpoint, it may be advantageous to analyze integration that does not bring about any evident change in quality, though it is unlikely that integration would be worse from all criteria for evaluation.

Integration should be evaluated according to the results really achieved compared to those envisaged in the project plan. When evaluating the functions of any system, two approaches are possible: either consider only the output from the system, or else evaluate the relation of output to input; the first can be termed the aspect of effect, the second as the aspect of efficiency.

It is not possible to set à priori which aspect is better, however, efficiency seems to be generally superior. But there may be situations where the system has to fulfil its task regardless of the means. The problem of evaluating the effectiveness of integration may be solved by not trying to evaluate all aspects. It is extremely difficult to find criterion for real socioeconomic systems (and indeed even of technical ones). In the best case, such a criterion is constructed artificially; the setting of the shape of functions may be disputed.

Real systems provide further proofs that it is difficult to measure the effectiveness of the system and especially to quantify it. It is theoretically alluring to start from the profit value of the system output, but the value of information is generally subjective to users. Partial criteria for evaluation of information systems are sometimes applicable: there are three critical:

\- Content; here we consider the quantity of data which the system transforms and presents (including some factor for the size of the data base), as well as the quality of stored data, its accuracy, reliability, completeness, etc.; all can be factored into the metric.

\- Time; here we consider response of the system, both internal (transformation) and external (between the external request and information presentation).

– Economic; here we consider costs of system operation. (The problem of determining economic efficiency of information systems is dealt with in paper [7]).

It is worth noting that these three criteria are “linearly independent”, because they are independent measures of the information system. With a given quality needed by the system, improvements in one of the criteria normally results in worsening of another; if however, improvement of one criterion occurs without making others worse, the new (integrated) system may be considered to have a higher quality. Generally it may be stated that an information system fulfils its functions if it provides the right information to the right person, at the right time, in a cost-effective way [4]. But this implies some proportioning of the criteria.

Information systems may also be evaluated according to how they are able to accommodate change; i.e., by their flexibility, adaptability, etc. The significance of this criterion depends on the relation between the natural development of the information system and the change of external conditions. Change is inevitable. Software becomes outdated, hardware obsolete. If the frequency of innovation is approximately the same as the frequency of external changes, it is not necessary to deal with the flexibility of the information system. If it is not, flexibility is an important criterion. This is probably the present situation in Czechoslovakia.

Flexibility is important because the development of an information system does not occur at equal rates in all components. The forms and rate of development are depending on the part of the system; e.g., the input of a statistical information systems is not often changed, whereas the output may be changed throughout the whole period of the system existence. Thus the original hardware and software must be increased to comply with user needs.

The information system should always (to a certain extent) be adaptable; adaptability can and should be built into the system, but lack of adaptability will lead to worsening of the system quality. These criteria may be used both for formulating the aims and for measuring the effectiveness of the integration; e.g., the criterion of content is the stated goal of most data base efforts. The same is true of the criteria of time, because data that were first stored in several information systems and had to be transformed and merged for further use, generally results in a slow (and probably uneconomical) method.

We feel that information system designers and implementers neglect the three criteria – often considering only one (which is generally content); this may lead to poor or unacceptable systems (e.g. time of response may limit the integration by the demand that the new system functions as fast as the old). The economic criterion also limits integration by demanding that the costs of operation of the integrated system be less than or equal to the total original cost.

Therefore it is easy to evaluate integration when the aim is to increase one quality without worsening others. But other criteria are much more difficult to evaluate. The range of problems in integration is very wide and exacting. We have assumed that the solution is found by considering the efficiency of individual information systems. We feel that the problem is greater than this. Only future research, implementation, and verification can confirm the truth of our approach. Research so far indicates that it is necessary to deal with questions of elements and those properties which enable relations and linkages to be set up between them.
