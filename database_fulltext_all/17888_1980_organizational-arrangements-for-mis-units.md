# Organizational Arrangements for MIS Units

Phillip Ein-Dor \*

Senior Lecturer, Faculty of Management, Tel-Aviv University and Adjunct Professor, Naval Postgraduate School.

and

Eli Segev

Senior Lecturer, Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University and Visiting Associate Professor, Graduate School of Management, UCLA

Decisions on organizational arrangements for MIS units comprise location in the organization, rank of responsible executive, organization of MIS implementors, degree of centralization, the user interface, the study group, the steering committee, the data base administrator, the corporate computer staff, and external assistance. Based on a wide-ranging survey of papers in the field of MIS, the authors have formulated guidelines which derive from the impact of organizational decisions on the success or failure or MIS and MIS projects.

Keywords: Centralization/Decentralization; Corporate Computer Staff; Data Base Administrator; Executive Responsible; External Assistance; Management Information Systems; MIS Implementors; MIS Units; Organizational Arrangements; Organizational Location; Steering Committee; Study Group; User Interface.

## 1. Introduction

A number of organizational decisions need to be made with respect to the MIS unit. These decisions concern the location of the information processing system in the organization, the rank of the responsible executive, the organization of MIS implementors and the degree of centralization in MIS efforts, the user interface with MIS, and the existence of five groups related to the MIS effort: the study group, the steering committee, the data base administrator (DBA), the corporate computer staff, and external assistance to the internal MIS staff.

## 2. Location in the Organization

The information system should be an autonomous unit rather than being located within another functional area.

In many cases, the data processing unit is initially located in the department in which it is first applied and later becomes autonomous when the range of applications is broadened [23]. This may explain the finding that, in most companies, the computer is assigned to the finance-accounting department [22].

Perhaps the most pragmatic approach to the question of locating information units is that the location should be determined by the power structure in the specific organization; when the center of power shifts, the information function should shift with it [51].

There are, however, several more doctrinaire approaches to this issue. One authority distinguishes between accounting and finance and strongly advocates locating the information system in finance, because of its close relationship to management; and not in accounting, because of its inhibiting effects of the development of systems for management [27]. This is the solution at Inland Steel, where the information system organization is part of the finance function [66]. A different argument leading to somewhat similar conclusions is that, because managers are interested in profits, computer should be located in profit centers rather than in cost centers such as accounting [20].

Finally, another study argues that the MIS department should be autonomous, reporting to a senior level of management [23]. Evidence indicates that MIS are in fact becoming recognized as an independent area with considerable organizational autonomy [19].

## 3. Rank of Responsible Executive

The executive responsible for the MIS unit should be no more than two levels below the chief officer.

The question here is, to whom does the MIS chief report? In other words, who in the organization holds ultimate responsibility for information systems? The answer to this question determines the organizational status of the information function, and in practice, it is closely related to the organizational location of the MIS unit.

While many writers hint rather being specific on this issue, there is only a narrow range suggested by those who are explicit:

Chief Executive Officer. Some authors recommend that decisions on computers be kept at the highest level and that the corporate manager or chief executive become directly involved in the configuration and use of MIS [21,23,25,40,42,52,62]. In the Pillsbury Company, responsibility for information systems was explicitly assigned, by corporate policy, to the General Manager at the plant level and to the Executive Officer at the corporate level [27].

Vice President. Other authors suggest that a respected vice president can act as a bridge between senior management and MIS staff [21,23,26]. In some organizations there are titles such as Vice President for Information Systems [46]. One researcher refers to the administrative vice president as the level to which the computer and systems group should report [19].

Other. One survey found that in successful police department information systems, the chief or his top assistant were deeply involved in the operation [8]. Several studies indicate that the computer system effort can succeed if the computer executive is within two levels of the chief executive [11,22,40].

## 4. Degree of Centralization

The structure of the system development function should parallel overall corporate organization structure.

One should distinguish between centralization of the computer resource ("hardware"), and centralization of the systems resource ("peopleware") which is discussed here. One study distinguishes five possible combinations of hardware and system resource centralization and decentralization, namely: 1) centralized hardware with decentralized system development and programming, 2) centralized hardware and programming with decentralized systems development, 3) decentralized hardware with centralized development and programming, 4) centralized hardware, systems and programming with directly linked satellite installations, and 5) centralized hardware, systems and programming with autonomous satellite installations [44].

It has been stated that the computer resource should be centralized. It is the organization of the systems resource which is the subject for contention between centralization and decentralization [49]. The following are advantages and disadvantages of centralization and decentralization of the system function:

1. Centralization permits uniform reporting systems and company-wide consolidation of operating results [44].

2. Centralization can improve coordination of the information system as a functional entity and helps retain well-qualified and well-motivated professionals because of the greater leeway in providing status, promotion, and state-of-the-art technology in larger organizations [44,49].

3. Centralization may provide greater flexibility for the organization by shielding the information from organizational changes, but at the cost of greater system complexity, higher communication outlays, and a loss of sight of users' needs through concentration on overall organizational objectives [29,44,49].

4. Too much centralization may lead to bureaucratization and reduce the responsiveness of the systems organization, a problem compounded by isolating systems from functional management, while decentralization heightens familiarity with local problems and promotes rapid response to local needs [44,49]

5. Decentralization can sometimes provide better opportunities for management development [49].

6. Decentralization mitigates the "scipc goat function" – the tendency to blame the computer for all problems [49].

7. Decentralization provides greater autonomy [29] and places profit and loss responsibility with the local manager who is in the best position to evaluate benefits [44].

While calls have been made for more centralization of information system planning [40,44] and others claim such centralization is impossible [12], the general attitude towards this issue seems to be highly pragmatic. One author notes that from the systems point of view, centralization is invariably called for, but political issues may militate in favor of decentralization [49]. The argument, therefore, is that there is an optimal degree of centralization in any organizational context, and the article offers indices of over- and under-centralization. This approach is supported by the finding that companies generally organize information systems in conformance with their traditional plans of organization. Those that do not, experience abnormal difficulties [22].

The tendency to configure information systems in accordance with general corporate philosophy has led to the development of a “hybrid” organization with shared control. This is currently the most popular form as evidenced by a survey which showed the following distribution of organizations by type of control of information systems: highly centralized, 32.5%; hybrid, 46.5%; and highly decentralized, 20.0% [33].

In passing, one may note that it has been recommended that hardware systems should also be configured so as to conform to the structure of the organization [3].

## 5. Organization of MIS Implementors

Team organization of project implementors enhances the likelihood of MIS project success.

The two major forms of organization are functional (with each individual assigned a clearly defined function, e.g., system analysis, programming), and project team (in which the team is totally responsible for all phases of development and implementation and with no clearcut functional definitions) [42,60]. Both types of organizations are found in companies with successful computer programs [23,42]. Regardless of the form that is used, there should be an open, adaptive organization in the development stage, and a closed, stable, mechanistic organization in the production stage [64].

Although system success was not found to be affected by the organization of the MIS implementors, team organization is conducive to MIS project success. In 1972, IBM introduced the concept of the chief programmer/librarian team in conjunction with structured programming, top down development, and the development support library. The productivity ratio of such teams over more conventional forms of organizations has been estimated as five to one [9]. There is evidence that an unsecessful case under functional organization can be transformed by adopting the project team approach, and there is empirical evidence of a distinct tendency toward team organization within project groups [35,60]. It has been suggested that the three member team is the right size for effective communication between team members which promotes successful system development [48], but in many cases, the nature of the problem may dictate larger teams [5,53].

## 6. The User Interface

Systems analysts should be assigned to the functional areas served by the system.

In decentralized systems with users at remote sites, it has been found necessary to assign system trained people at the remote site to maintain the local subsystem and to identify and solve the local problem [44]. In general, it is felt that systems analysts will become integrated in the functioning of user's area and will serve as a communications link between users and the computer resource [23]. Another alternative which has been suggested to alleviate the remote users' feeling that they are being ignored in the production stage, is to communicate to them the service levels provided by the system for each application [36].

## 7. The Study Group

Study groups should be composed of qualified operating management, supported by technical personnel.

Operating management should play a strong role in project selection and planning. Groups composed of appropriately qualified operating managers as well as technical experts are likely to be a better source of ideas for profitable changes in operations than are computer professionals alone [6,34,42].

## 8. The Steering Committee

Steering committees should be composed of high level corporate officers.

As organizations become aware of the importance of information systems, they tend to establish steering committees to guide the MIS effort [23,46]. The steering committee is composed of senior executives [13,46,47,66]; and its functions are:

1. determining information system policies, objectives, and priorities [7,13,40,47],

2. identifying potentially profitably projects [13,31], and

3. projecting manpower, hardware and software requirements and budgeting [1,13,40].

It has been suggested that steering committee involvement should be limited to budgetary aspects and that the committee should not be tied up with technical issues.

The steering committee is both a way of showing management support and a method for attaining user involvement [37].

## 9. Data Base Administration

The data base administrator should occupy a high level staff position independent of data processing. This position should be occupied by a person with both technical and political skills.

In 1972, Kriebel stated that the whole issue of MIS would reduce to that of data base management [32]. Whether this is completely accurate or not, the importance of data bases has certainly increased since then, and with them the significance and understanding of the role of the data base administrator (DBA). The evolution in the perception of the DBA's role parallels the increasing acceptance of the view that information is a major organizational resource; thus, it is now not uncommon to read of information compared to money, and this leads naturally to the comparison of the DBA with the comptroller, the one responsible for financial resources and the other for information resources [67]. From the perspective of this paper, the functions, capabilities and organizational location of the DBA are of interest.

There is, as yet, considerable confusion over the DBA's area of competence [63]. A technically oriented description of the data administration function comprises data base description, control of data access, information enhancement, and systems support, protection and tuning [50]. A more general description charges the DBA with developing standards and procedures for creating, processing and upgrading all corporate data [39]. These very different perceptions of the DBA's role also give rise to different views on the appropriate organizational arrangement.

The suggestions for appropriate organizational positions run the gamut from somewhere inside the data processing function, through an independent position reporting to the first or second level of data processing management to a high level staff support function at the VP level and independent of departments and divisions. A compromise solution suggests a matrix organization with local data base administrators reporting both to local operating management and to a staff level DBA at headquarters [50,57,58,67].

Just as the perception of DBA functions colors views on organizational location, so does it affect capability requirements. At one extreme it is seen as a purely technical position with primarily technical qualifications required. Those who see it as an organization-wide function also stress the need for political skills in order to permit effective promotion of data base activities [50,67]. There seems to be little doubt that the importance of data bases will continue to grow, and with them the stature and centrality of the DBA function will also increase.

## 10. Corporate Computer Staff

Companies should have appropriately qualified corporate computer staffs.

There is conflicting opinion as to whether a corporate staff is necessary to guide the systems effort. It has been stated that centralizing the control of a company's information system in a staff group creates insoluble problems and is therefore infeasible [12,28].

In practice, however, it has been found that especially among decentralized companies, corporate staffs can lead to a higher level of success in the MIS project (22).

The functions of the corporate staff have been defined as:

1. providing overall strategy as a framework for long term systems planning [28],

2. monitoring programs [22], and

3. appraising results [22].

Those capabilities which have been found to distinguish successful staff groups are superior technical knowledge and effectiveness in working with non-non-technical personnel [22].

## 11. External Assistance

Larger firms have fewer benefits from the use of consultants and service firms.

Faced with an information system project, organizations may recruit external assistance in the form of consultants or computer service firms.

Feeling are mixed on the use of consultants. Some regard them as a useful extension to existing staff, with local managerial resources being used to implement and monitor the consultant's work [55]. Others suggest that outside consultants may be more successful than internal EDP experts because they normally have direct access to top management [52]. A survey of computer users concluded that the use of outside consulting is limited, that it is more extensive among small users than large and that the use of computer vendors as consultants is more widespread among users with administrative applications only [54].

The use of computer service firms is attractive because of the high cost of in-house computers and staff, a scarcity of good software talent, and the inability of management to communicate its problems to computer people [2]. On the other hand, the service bureau approach is limited by the small range of applications provided, and by the inadequacy of packaged systems designs and programs [59].

Several case reports attribute success in part to the decision to implement systems in-house [6,34].

## Conclusions

The organizational arrangements for MIS units play a significant role in the success both of MIS and of MIS development projects. While appropriate arrangements cannot of themselves guarantee success, poor solutions to organizational questions contribute considerably to failure. Furthermore, all the relevant issues should be dealt with correctly in order to enhance the likelihood of success; ignoring any one of them may cause difficulties. The combination of the answers to all the organizational questions represents the organizations's approach to these issues. Thus, consistency is important, otherwise intrinsically good solutions to each of the problems may cancel out.

With respect to some of the organizational arrangements, universal best solutions may be indicated. This is true, for example, of the existence and rank of the steering committee or of the rank of the MIS chief. In other cases, the best solution is contingent on the particular organization; examples are the degree of centralization and the employment of external assistance.
