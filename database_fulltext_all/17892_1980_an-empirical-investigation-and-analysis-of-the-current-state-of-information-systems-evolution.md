# An Empirical Investigation and Analysis of the Current State of Information Systems Evolution

Donald W. Kroeber

School of Business Administration, James Madison University, Harrisonburg, Virginia, USA

Hugh J. Watson

College of Business Administration, The University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia, USA

Ralph H. Sprague, Jr.

College of Business Administration, The University of Hawaii, Honolulu, Hawaii, USA

Information systems evolve in terms of computer hardware and software technology and system performance. These two dimensions can be conceptualized in terms of a matrix, which can then be used to investigate and analyze the current state of information systems development. This article reports the results of a study of over 150 large organizations which operate computer-based information systems.

## 1. Introduction

The information systems field seems to be at a transition point. After an initial period of “oversell” in which management information systems promised to satisfy all the information needs of management, an inevitable period of disenchantment and pessimism followed. More recently, a mood of cautious optimism seems to accompany information system potential. This new mood, based on the maturing capabilities of data base management and data communication systems, carries the hope of renewed growth in information systems capability, especially for meeting the decision-making needs of top management. Technology advancements, however, have not always been immediately followed by increased systems performance. Therefore, it is critical at this time for managers, data processing practitioners, and researchers alike to assess the status of information systems development, the nature of coming changes, and the actions which must be taken to support appropriate development in the future.

In this article, we present a conceptual model of information systems based on the interaction of two major dimensions — technology and performance. We then report the results of an empirical study conducted to ascertain the current state of information system development among large organizations across the United States.

## 2. Two Dimensions of Information Systems Development

There is a wealth of literature on the information systems industry and where it is going [5,10,13,28, 35,43,44]. Many of these projections are heavily, if not entirely, based on technology forecasting. However, this approach has the tendency to characterize a particular information system as “advanced” if it uses the latest hardware technology.

We have found it more appropriate to describe an information system using two dimensions: (1) the computer hardware and software technology; and (2)

the system performance capabilities. The generations of computer hardware and software are useful for describing the technology dimension. Generations typically are defined in terms of the speed and capacity of the central processor, capacity and type of files, input and output devices, and types of systems software. The characteristics of technology generations are summarized in Table 1.

Less widely discussed and documented are evolutionary generations of systems performance, which utilize the increased technological capabilities [2,9,23,44]. The characteristics of these performance generations usually include the size and complexity of the application programs, whether or not the programs are integrated and linked to other programs, and whether the primary function of the system is to serve transactional processing, summary reporting, or decision support. We have chosen to designate the performance levels as basic data processing, integrated data processing, management information systems, and decision support systems. The attributes of these performance levels are summarized in Table 2. $^{1}$

The systems performance dimension has been recognized in some of the literature cited earlier, but frequently it is co-mingled with the technology dimension. As early as 1969, Aron discussed the evolution from simple data processing to MIS [5]. Thiereuf discussed three hardware generations and then divided the systems performance dimension into custodial accounting, responsibility reporting, integrated data processing, integrated MIS, and finally real-time MIS [37]. The first three categories are defined primarily by the types of reports produced by the system, while the last two categories are a mixture of systems performance and the development of real-time data communications technology. Also heavily oriented to the use of technology (in the form of CRTs), the work by Scott Morton generated considerable interest in systems designed specifically to support decisions [29,31]. Withington seems to feel that all information systems evolution is technology driven, so he combines both dimensions in his five generations of computers [44]. Strassman indicates that the evolutionary stages of information systems growth are dependent heavily upon profitability and cost-benefit issues that derive from the S curve of EDP costs [35]. However, once again the technology and performance components are intermingled so that it is difficult to perceive the role of each in moving information systems forward.

## 3. Information Systems Development Matrix

When the four technological generations are considered as the rows of a matrix and the different performance levels as the columns, an Information Systems Development Matrix results; see Figure 1. The matrix provides a convenient framework for conceptualizing, analyzing, and investigating the relationships between information system technology and performance. Notice that the matrix in Figure 1 has three areas indicated by "X's," "Y's," and "Z's." The cells marked by "Y's" might be referred to as "performance intensive" in that the information systems in such a position are delivering more in performance than might be expected given the available hardware and software. The "Z" cells are "technology intensive" in that relatively low level information systems performance is being provided given the available technology. The "X" cells reflect a balance between performance and technology.

## 4. The Study

Using the Information Systems Development Matrix as the conceptual framework, an empirical study was conducted to investigate the current state of information systems development. The study, described in the following paragraphs, consisted of four major steps: (1) the definition of the relevant population; (2) the selection of a representative sample; (3) the development of scaling and measurement techniques; and (4) the collection and analysis of data.

Advancement is commonly measured by the heighest level of achievement attained. For example, civilization is said to be in the “Atomic Age,” although the vast majority of the world’s population is only dimly aware of atomic energy. Similarly, the state of information systems is inferred from the most innovative and advanced systems in use. Traditionally, innovation in information systems development has come from large business and those government organizations that can afford the associated risk [44]. The population for this study was therefore defined as large business and government users of computer-based information systems. $^{2}$

Fig. 1. Information Systems Development Matrix.

Two frames were used in the selection of the sample, Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives for private sector organizations and the General Services Administration's Inventory of Automatic Data Processing Equipment in the United States Government for the public sector. The ratio of government to nongovernment organizations in the sample was based upon the ratio of organizations in the two frames – approximately one to five. The nongovernment sample was further stratified by industrial categories of engineering and construction, finance, manufacturing, retail/wholesale, and services. $^{3}$ The stratified sample was drawn randomly from the frames with a total sample size of 300 being used in the study.

The data collection instrument employed in the study was a questionnaire designed to yield information on the technology and performance dimensions of the information systems in the sample organizations. In general, questions were based upon the characteristics of technology (central processor, input/output media and devices, etc.) and performance (applications, decision-making capabilities, etc.). Responses to the questions were keyed to the specific characteristics of the four generations of technology and the four levels of performance. For example, a question of decision-support capabilities had response choices of “none” (basic data processing), “summary reports” (basic data processing), “operations decisions” (integrated data processing), and so on to “interactive managerial access to management science models” (decision support system performance).

The technique of keying responses to technological generations and performance levels is convenient for classifying a given characteristic, but it is less effective for determining the position of an entire system on either dimension. For example, when a system possesses a third generation CPU but only first or second generation peripheral devices, it is not clear what position it occupies in the technology dimension. To resolve this problem, a panel of seven experts was asked to: (1) weigh the five characteristics established for each dimension; and (2) within those weights, to establish relative weights for each possible response. The weights thus assigned to the characteristics of technology and performance are shown in Table 3. To illustrate the technique of response weighting, the decision-support example cited above is continued in Table 4.

In order to position respondents in the matrix cells, it was necessary to establish placement criteria for each cell. The criteria were established by specifying minimum response requirements to qualify for each level on the technology and performance dimensions. For example, if an information system was to qualify as MIS performance, it had to have capabilities to support production control, sales forecasting and capital budgeting applications, possess a common data base with interactive access by programmers, include management science models and allow managers to define report formats, serve the information needs of lower and middle management, and include interfaces between applications and system components. Since weights were determined for each response, it was possible to calculate cutoff scores for each technology and performance level.

Based upon the cutoff scores for each technology and performance level, the responding organizations were placed in the matrix as shown in Figure 2.

Not surprisingly, there were no systems using first or second generation technology. Maintenance costs alone have made these technologies uneconomical for several years. Perhaps the most striking feature of Figure 2 is the concentration of systems in cell (3,3), third generation technology and MIS performance. Indeed this fact alone leads one to conclude that the current state of information systems progress in large organizations is (3,3). When the six systems in (4,4) are added, the number of balanced or "X" systems is over 65 per cent of the sample.

The matrix also shows 41 systems (27 per cent) occupying "Z" or technology-intensive cells and only 12 systems (8 per cent) in performance-intensive "Y" positions. This distribution of systems confirms the contention that, among unbalanced systems, there is more likely to be an excess of technology than performance. It should be noted, however, that a technology intensive system is not necessarily wasteful or uneconomical. Industries which are characterized by extremely heavy transaction volume may find advanced technology necessary just for “brute force” speed.

In addition to contributing to the representativeness of the sample, the strata provided an interesting opportunity for analyzing the relationship of industry type with matrix position. For example, government and manufacturing made up slightly over one-half of the sample, but accounted for three-fourths of the performance-intensive systems. There are no performance-intensive systems among financial institutions, and retail/wholesale firms have more than twice as many technology-intensive systems as balanced systems. The earlier comment about transaction-heavy industries may help explain these results for retail/wholesale and finance. These and other matrix location data are shown for all categories of organizations in Table 5.

An examination of the technology and performance scores that led to the number of "X," "Y," and "Z" systems shown in Table 5 reveals some interesting combinations. Engineering firms are below average in both technology and performance, although not significantly so. Financial institutions have higher than average technology ratings and significantly lower performance ratings. Manufacturing firms have lower than average technology, but combine it with significantly higher performance to produce the high proportion of "Y" systems. Retail/wholesale firms are lower in both dimensions, but are unbalanced toward the "Z" area of the matrix because their average performance level is integrated data processing while their technology averages third generation. Retail/wholesale is the only category whose average ratings do not fall in the (3,3) cell. Service industries fall closest to the overall results both in numerical ratings and proportions of "X," "Y," and "Z" systems, Government is the only category with significant higher technology ratings, which cause several third performance level systems to fall into "Z" cells, but it also has above average performance, which produces four "Y" systems.

## 5. Implications for Management

The usual criterion for business operations is return on investment. Is there greater return on investment in "Y" systems, "X" systems or perhaps even "Z" systems? The benefits of information systems are at best difficult to measure and no attempt was made in this study to collect either cost or benefit data. However, data processing managers were asked for their opinion of benefits versus costs and for their opinion of top management's appraisal of benefits versus costs. The results of these two questions are tabulated, by matrix location, in Tables 6 and 7.

Although two-thirds of all DP managers consider the benefits of their systems to be much greater than costs, this opinion is significantly more prevalent among "Y" than "Z" system managers. Not surprisingly, DP managers feel that top management holds a somewhat lower opinion of system benefits. Furthermore, they apparently do not think that system performance influences top management's opinion as much as it influences their own. This latter statement is based upon the smaller differences between "X," "Y," and "Z" systems shown in Table 7 as opposed to those shown in Table 6. In particular, there is little difference between "X" and "Y" systems evidenced in the managers' perception of top management's opinion.

For those managers who wish to raise systems performance (and over 70 per cent of the survey participants plan to do so this year) the analysis of survey data revealed some areas in greater need of improvement than others. Table 8 shows performance characteristic scores as a percentage of the maximum possible score. The lowest percentage is in the area of decision-support capabilities. The six point average for decision support suggests only a slight capability beyond automatic operational decisions. As a minimum, the integration of management science models and the addition of interactive managerial access to the data base would raise the average decision-support score to approximately 12, or a respectable 60 per cent of maximum. The introduction of interactive access would also tend to increase the interface between the information system and other managerial processes – the performance characteristic with the second lowest score.

## 6. Conclusion

The Information System Development Matrix has been a useful framework for the authors in investigating and analyzing the relationship between information systems technology and performance. Based upon our study, it appears that most organizations are achieving a balance between technology and performance, with MIS performance (as operationally defined in this study) combined with third generation technology being the most common state. There are, however, interesting differences among industry categories. Manufacturing and governmental organizations are the leaders in balanced and performance-intensive systems, while retail/wholesale organizations tend to be predominantly technology-intensive. Data processing managers feel rather strongly that the benefits being derived from their information systems exceed the investment. They also feel that management shares their general feeling, but to a lesser extent. The strongest favorable feelings of benefits versus investment is found in "Y" organizations, perhaps suggesting the need for more performance than technology advances. The greatest area for improving appears to be in enhanced decision-support capabilities.
