# Information Systems Curricula: Business versus Academic Viewpoints

Gary R. Reeves

Associate Professor of Management Science, College of Business Administration, University of South Carolina, Columbia, South Carolina 29208, USA

and

Robert S. Bussom

Associate Professor of Administrative Science, College of Business and Administration, Southern Illinois University at Carbondale, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

This paper compares the results of a survey of managers of information systems activities in Fortune 500 organizations and a survey of information systems faculty of accredited business schools with respect to their opinions on the desired composition of undergraduate programs in information systems offered by schools of business. Information is presented on the respondent's opinions about the relative importance of: 1) educational output objectives that graduates of such programs might be expected to possess, and 2) courses and topics within courses designed to achieve these objectives. Similarities and differences between business and academic responses are analyzed.

Keywords: Education, university courses and programs.

## 1. Introduction

An important problem for faculty in schools and colleges of business is the development and continued refinement of educational programs in information systems. This is especially important because of the rapidly evolving nature of the information systems field and the increasing demand for graduates of such programs. Most of the work that has been done on the development of educational programs in information systems has been accomplished, as would be expected, largely by academics. ## 2. Methodology

Questionnaires were mailed to the chief executive officers of the 500 largest industrial corporations and of the 50 largest non-industrial corporations in each of the areas of banking, finance, insurance, retailing, transportation and utilities, respectively, in the United States as defined by Fortune Magazine. A similar questionnaire was sent to the deans of 200 accredited business schools. Recipients of the questionnaires were requested to direct them to the person in their organization with the most knowledge and responsibility in the information systems area.

Both forms of the questionnaire used the C $^{3}$ EM report [1] as a model and consisted of three major sections. The first dealt with the importance of each of thirty-three output objectives identified by the C³EM for graduates of undergraduate educational programs in information systems. Respondents were asked to indicate their opinion on the importance of each of these output objectives on the five point scale of Table 1. The second section dealt with the emphasis that specific courses and topic areas within courses should receive in the major portion of an undergraduate information systems curriculum designed to achieve the objectives of the first section. Following the C³EM recommendations, seven courses were outlined. Respondents were asked to indicate their opinion on the emphasis that the indicated courses and topic areas within courses should receive in the overall curriculum on the scale of Table 2. The third section consisted of questions about characteristics of respondents and their organizations.

## 3.2. Similarities

Businessmen and academics appear to have similar rankings and mean scores for objectives they consider most important. These objectives relate to communications abilities and systems expertise, categories I.A. and I.C. respectively. Objectives related to written and oral communication head the list of both groups ranking first, second, and third. Prospective employers, as well as academics, continually emphasize the importance of interpersonal communications for success in white collar positions. The present survey reinforces the position that information systems educational programs may do well to emphasize student achievement in the communications area. Systems related abilities ranked second, third, and fifth for the business group and second, third and fourth for academics. These business responses tend to confirm a position often held by academicians that the systems viewpoint is a very valuable, practical approach as well as an interesting academic structure.

Information gathering (E.3. and 4.) and specific problem solving skills (E.5 and 6.) were valued similarly by business and academic respondents. Both groups ranked the society objective (I.F.I.) thirty-second, the next to the lowest position. Since the society objective is neither related to task accomplishment nor job performance, this result might be expected.

Groupings of objectives, other than the C $^{3}$ EM's, are possible and might provide additional insight into the commonalities between the two groups. Consider the grouping of objectives into the three areas of communication, problem solving, and technology. Objectives involving written or oral communication are ranked 1, 2, 3, 7 and 16 in the business group and 1, 2, 3, 11 and 6 respectively in the AACSB sample. Problem solving objectives dealing with information gathering, problem definition, and development and evaluation of alternative courses of action ranked 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 18, 19, 20, 21, 28 and 29 in the business group and 5, 4, 9, 10, 17, 24, 16, 26, 20, 15, 14, 22 and 29 in the AACSB sample. As these rankings indicate, the groups generally agree that communication is the most highly valued ability with objectives related to communication appearing in the top half of the rankings. Problem solving is next in importance with most of these objectives ranking in the middle of the list. In general, technology was least emphasized, with the majority of the technological objectives appearing in the bottom half of the rankings.

A more analytical approach was taken to investigating similarities between the responses of the two groups by factor analyzing the combined response scores. A factor analysis identified five factors that explained 48% of the variation in the combined sample. The first or strongest factor is highly positively correlated (0.5 or above) with objective I.E.2. and objectives II.1 through II.6. Since these objectives concern knowledge about organizational patterns, functional areas, roles, goals, and individual group behavior, this factor can be most aptly named organizational technology. The second factor resulting from the factor analysis correlates quite strongly with objectives dealing with logical structures (III.1 and III.2.). Experience is the next factor to be identified and correlates highly with objectives I.G.1. through I.G.3. The fourth factor is related to objective I.C.2., objectives I.D.5. through I.D.7., objective I.E.5., and objective I.E.7. These objectives relate to development or evaluation of alternative courses of action. Consequently, the factor is named alternatives and evaluation. The fifth factor is highly correlated with objectives I.D.6. through I.D.8., abilities related to computing.

The factor analysis provides a classification scheme for objectives that represents the commonality of responses in the combined data set, both between the two sample groups and among individuals in combined groups. The differences between this resultant classification scheme and the structure imposed à priori on the list of the 33 output objectives by the C³EM is in Table 7. As can be seen, there is some agreement between the C³EM and factor analysis classifications. Both contain classes on computing and on experience. The other three classifications resulting from the factor analysis — organizational technology, logical structures and alternatives and evaluations – were not considered to be major groupings in the C³EM structure. Of course, many of the objectives listed by the C³EM did not correlate highly (0.5 or above) with any of the five factors, thus indicating that there was not common agreement in the overall sample concerning the importance of those output objectives and abilities that were excluded.

An additional analysis of the relative rankings of the output objectives by the two groups was made using a Spearman rank correlation. The correlation between the business sample and the academic sample was 0.65 with significance at the 0.001 level. While correlations at this level are considered relevant in behavioral research, only 42% of the variation between the two groups is accounted for.

## 3.3. Differences

The academic mean importance score is higher than the corresponding business mean importance score for 32 of the 33 objectives. This indicates that, as a group, academics tended to rank the objectives higher than businessmen. Differences between the two groups are statistically significant at the 0.05 level or better for 21 of the 33 objectives. The only objective for which the business mean importance score is higher than the academic mean importance score is the experience objective (I.G.2.). This difference is also statistically significant.

Academics view the objective involving operations research models (I.B.) as substantially more important than businessmen. The business group ranked the O.R. objective thirty-first; academics ranked it nineteenth.

Computer related abilities, the objectives in category I.D., are generally ranked higher by the academic group. The academic mean is significantly higher than the business mean for five out of the eight objectives in this group (I.D. 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7), although there is no significant difference for the programming

objectives (I.D. 2 and 3).

Five of the seven organizational objectives are viewed as significantly more important by the academic group, with the exceptions being in information gathering objectives (I.E.3. and 4). Academic means are significantly higher than the corresponding business means for all six objectives in the organizational only track, category II, while the means are not significantly different for any objectives in the technological only track, category III. In the organizational only and technological only areas, academics' responses seem to be consistent with the intent of the curriculum posed by the C³EM; i.e., graduates of business school programs in information systems should emphasize management and knowledge of organizations and not information systems technology. However, the business responses do not support this position. The business group ranked the organizational concentration objectives (category II) 17, 24, 28, 29, 30 and 33. They ranked the technological concentration objectives (category III) as 11, 12, and 18. These results are interesting. Even though respondents were instructed to think in terms of educational programs offered by schools of business, they appear to view technological output objectives beyond the common core as more important than organizational ones. This is certainly divergent from the academic viewpoint.

The business group ranked experience objectives, category I.G., and objectives involving logical structures and specifications, category III, higher than the academic group. However, academicians ranked knowledge of hardware and software (I.D.1) and the ability to update knowledge of technology (I.D.4) as substantially more important than the business group.

Discriminant analysis was used to identify objectives that were perceived as more important by the two separate groups. As Table 8 indicates, the discriminant analysis shows that the academic group felt that objectives I.B.; C.2; D.1, 2, 4, 5, and 7; E.2, and G.3 were more important than did the business group. These objectives have to do with models, writing, computers, programming, updating knowledge of technology, alternatives, economic analysis, information systems and organizations, and oral communication. The business group on the other hand felt that objectives I.D.8, E.4, E.7, G.2, and III.1 were more important than did the academic group. These objectives are related to computer specifications, information gathering, information system specifications, logical structures, and project team and programming experience. It is interesting to note that the objective concerning general knowledge of sources of updating knowledge of technology (I.D.4) is the most discriminating between the two groups. This means that the score on the objective is most likely to indicate whether the respondent is a member of the business group or a member of the academic group.

It appears from the discriminant analysis that the business group is more interested in those abilities involved with doing and with practical application: developing specifications, information gathering etc. Conversely, the academic group thinks that objectives related to modeling and technical knowledge are the kind of objectives that are more important. This result was, of course, expected. Differences in orientation and values between academics and practitioners is a well accepted, but, as far as we can determine, ill supported position. The usual position taken by businessmen is that academics are too theoretical and do not emphasize enough of the practical abilities that enable graduates to perform successfully in the business world. In contrast, businessmen have been thought by academicians to be narrow minded in their demand for practical training and unappreciative of the value that theory and deeper understanding of organizations and management will eventually have upon the long run performance of graduates. In this paper, we have not resolved this apparent conflict. However, the results of the survey, particularly the outcome of the discriminant analysis, does provide evidence substantiating the position that these assumed differences between businessmen and academicians do exist.

## 3.4. Core Courses

As indicated at the beginning of the paper, a second and much smaller section of the questionnaire asked for reactions to seven core information systems courses proposed by the C $^{3}$ EM. Reactions were solicited using the scale of Table 2. The C $^{3}$ EM's proposed core included the courses shown in Table 9. This summary of responses from the survey concerning the importance of the proposed courses shows, both by mean score and by rank, that there are few differences between the business group and the academic group. Business and academic responses show a statistically significant difference for courses 1 and 4 only. This evidence indicates that there is a fairly high degree of agreement between the business group and the academic group regarding the importance of the seven core courses.

Although the comparisons of the business and academic groups' responses to the importance of the 33 output objectives proposed by the C $^{3}$ EM identified a number of differences between the groups, the data provide little if any indication of disagreement about the value of the seven core courses. In general, courses related to systems (courses 3, 6 and 7) are most highly ranked and should probably receive increased emphasis in information system curricula. The course on programming (course 4) scored somewhat lower, and from the respondents perspective is adequately treated. However, the respondents felt that courses on modeling, behavior, and computerware (courses 1, 2, and 5) are over-emphasized in the C $^{3}$ EM's proposal and should be consolidated.

## 4. Conclusion

In this paper, data have been summarized and analyzed concerning business and academic responses to a proposed information systems curriculum model. Due to the evolving nature of the information systems field and to the high level of demand for graduates of educational programs in information systems, it is important to continue to monitor and refine curricula in this area. It is important also to consider the inputs of business practitioners concerning curricula since they are the employers of the outputs of the educational process.

The data provide substantiation for some often stated but infrequently tested hypotheses concerning differences in business and academic opinions on matters of curriculum. They also provide evidence that the two groups share many common opinions. As such, the present survey and its results should provide useful input to academic decision makers in examining existing programs. It is hoped that the inputs of business practitioners in the markets served by the academic institutions will also be solicited. Further details on the survey and the results reported here are available from the authors.
