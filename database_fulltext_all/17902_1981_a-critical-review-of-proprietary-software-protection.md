# A Critical Review of Proprietary Software Protection

Charles R. Franz

Management Science Department, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

Susan J. Wilkins

Management Department, University of Nebraska, Lincoln, NE 68588, USA

and

Jonathan C. Bower

Adams, Arnold and Herring, Columbia, South Carolina, USA

The purpose of this paper is to delineate primarily the present state of U.S. patents, copyright and trade secrets to the area of computer software. These three areas were chosen because the protection afforded by them is the greatest and because the application of these laws is pretty much unique as applied to software. A brief mention will be given to the law of contracts (contractual agreements), but an analysis of this area is not required because the application of the law of contracts to software is not unique. The paper ends with a brief look at the Doctrine of Unfair Competition, ethical considerations and finally presenting various proposals that have been made to help rectify the problem of proprietary software protection.

Keywords: Computer program protection, proprietary software protection, computer software copyright infringement, law of intellectual property, copying computer programs, software patenting. Computer Software Protection Act, machine readable copyright notice, computer program copyright registration, computer software trade secrets protection, computer software contractual agreement.

## 1. Introduction

This paper addresses the various types of legal protection available today in the USA for computer software. Patents, copyrights, trade secret laws, contractual agreements, and the Doctrine of Unfair Competition have been used by owners of computer programs to attempt to prevent unauthorized appropriation. A great deal has been written regarding the advantages and disadvantages of each of the protection methods. Generally arguments are presented which claim that there is one best device suitable for everyone wishing to protect programs. Of course, there is substantial disagreement over which method is best. The fallacy in many of the arguments, though, is caused by failure to realize that people have significantly different objectives in protecting software. In the final analysis, it could very well be that all legal weapons might be used, depending on the specifics of the case at hand.

The primary reason legal protection is inadequate, and will continue to be, is the difficulty involved in detecting infringement of legal rights. If the owner of a computer program desired to restrict its use, he or she must employ care, common sense, and built-in hardware and software mechanisms whenever possible. Hence, the form of legal protection which is most appropriate, although still flawed, requires that the owner take special care to avoid accidental, unauthorized use of the software.

## 2. Protecting Proprietary Software

A complex problem that so far has not been satisfactory answered in today's software industry is the legal protection of software developer's proprietary interests against unauthorized use by other users and vendors. The need for some type of legal protection can best be demonstrated by a quick look at the emergence of the software industry.

With the advent of the general purpose computer containing in-house programming capabilities, the demand for software packages began. At first, the hardware manufacturer filled this demand. The customer-user of these computers next turned to consultants for advice to supplement that of the suppliers. This sudden increase in software demand let to the rapid emergence of the computer software industry. Initially, this industry was so busy filling the demand, that they spent little effort protecting their proprietary interests. Instead the industry turned to secrecy as a means of protection. The secrecy was accomplished by not publicizing advances in technology and through contracts with customers prohibiting use other than that specified. This secrecy led to the unnecessary duplication of many programs due to the similar needs of customers. In essence, different manufacturers were "reinventing the wheel" [26].

The need for some form of legal protection became apparent due to the large investment of time, money and effort required in developing software and the ease with which it could be duplicated. Since one of the main motivational factors for developing software packages is the expectation of marketing them at a profit, this expectation would be diminished without some form of proprietary legal protection. In the USA, there are two possible methods of legal protection available for proprietary interests: the law of intellectual property, which is mainly governed by statute and includes patents, copyrights and trademark laws, and common law, including the law of trade secrets, unfair competition, and the law of contracts.

Some of the arguments that have been advanced for offering legal protection to proprietary interests in software include:

1. Encourage program owners to make programs generally known to the public.

2. Encourage investment.

3. When programs cannot be protected by statute, they must be protected by secrecy. And when not freely disclosed, they are not as likely to be improved by use. Secrecy does not follow the US Constitutional plan for intellectual property which calls for "the promotion of the progress of Science and the useful Arts". [Art. 1 section 8, clause 8, U.S. Constitution].

4. Avoid duplication.

5. Prevent unauthorized use.

Argument: advanced against legal protection include:

1. It delays the development of computer technology because of the constant threat of infringement actions, and this would result in minimal growth in the field.

2. Protecting interests is very costly [4].

While the general concensus is that some form of legal protection for proprietary interest in computer software is needed, given the present state of the law, there is no generally followed scheme in protecting these rights.

Further complicating the issue of which type of software protection to use is the knowledge that infringement can be extremely difficult to detect. This is true if one has a patent, a copyright, a contract, or a trade secret.

By its very nature, computer software is hidden from view when it is operational. The program is magnetically, or electronically stored inside a complex computer system. A human being is only able to see the effect the program has on input to the program and output from the program: the program itself is "invisible." Copies of the program can be made in seconds for a few dollars, reproduced on magnetic tape, and sent anywhere in the world for use on a compatible computer system [3].

In addition, programs are never "used up." Once the copy is installed on a computer system, the program can be executed millions of times without replacement. Therefore, the owner of the program need not be contacted by the user after installation has taken place (except when error is detected, or planned changes), and a considerable amount of surreptitious transferral of programs can occur without the owner's knowledge.

Some professionals in the computer industry believe that because of the difficulty involved in detecting infringement, the best protective devices are those built right into the software or the hardware on which the program operates [19]. It is common practice for vendors of proprietary software packages to insert a small section of machine code at the beginning of the program which checks the date and the model number of the computer to insure that it is running at an authorized time and place. If not, the program self-destructs.

Hardware protection of computer programs has been too expensive so far, but industry consultant Philip H. Dorn said, "Use of execute-only, read-only, PROM, EPROM, or ROM would appear to be a very near-term development," for such protection [19]. However, even if software or hardware solutions were available for some types of software protection, they could not provide protection in such cases as unauthorized program copying, or violation of the secrecy of competitive software. Therefore, the legal issues must be considered, not only in today's environment, but also well into the foreseeable future.

The question of providing protection for proprietary software may be best summed up by two questions:

1. Who is allowed to make copies of the program, and for what purposes?

2. How well protected are the ideas and concepts inherent in the program?

## 3. Patents

The granting of a patent on a software package extends to the grantee the exclusive right to use the package for 17 years without infringement by others. The attractiveness of the patent as legal protection for software stems from the protection of the idea or concept on which the program is promised [24]. However, the cost of obtaining a patent, the unlikelihood of receiving one, and if granted, the remote possibility of successfully prosecuting an infringement action make this form of legal protection for proprietary interests in software impractical in most cases. Usually the success in obtaining a patent, however, has made this type of protection available only to the very persistent and the very lucky.

The federal law permitting patents (35 U.S.C.A) and copyrights (17 U.S.C.A.) is contained in the United States constitutional directive which states:

"The Congress shall have the power to . . . promote the progress of science and useful arts, by securing for limited times to authors and inventors the exclusive right to their respective writings and discoveries." (U.S. Const. Art. I, sect. B).

In particular, the Patent Act covers the invention or discovery of "any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof." (35 U.S.C.A., sect. 101). In addition, the invention must be novel, and non-obvious "to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the subject matter pertains." (35 U.S.C.A., sect. 103).

Since a patent is a statutory grant of an exclusive right, the application for a patent must fit within the above statutory definition set forth for patentability. The first step to securing a patent is to determine novelty. In the computer software field, due to the lack of publication of new technological advances, it is often hard to determine if a new "invention" is novel. "A person shall be entitled to a patent unless, (a) the invention was known or used by others ..." (35 U.S.C.A., sect. 102). This leads to problems in searching to see if the invention has already been made. Due to the fact that the desired result can also be achieved in hardware, both software and hardware patents must be searched. In fact, checking prior art may not be limited to the hardware and software fields. In MICR-Shield Co. v. First National Bank (ref. [16]), the patentee of a method for dissolving magnetic encoding ink argued that the only prior art that need be examined was that of the data processing and banking industries. The court held, however, that reference to the prior art of chemical solvents was also appropriate and subsequently found the patent to be invalid. This case also helps point out the fact that even after a patent is issued it still can be attacked due to the notion of lack of novelty. In fact, the validity of a patent is usually attacked after issuance because patent applications are always kept secret until issuance.

4. United States Supreme Court

The U.S. Patent Office, and the Patent Office Board of Appeals have consistently denied applications for patents for computer programs. The Court of Customs and Patent Appeals (CCPA) has just as consistently awarded patents for computer programs as can be seen In re Prater and Wei (ref. [22]), In re Musgrave (ref. [17]), In re Bernhart (ref. [2]), and In re Chatfield (ref. [3]). The U.S. Supreme Court has heard three cases related to the patenting of software, and has overturned the decision of the CCPA each time. The three cases are Gottschalk v. Benson, (ref. [11]), Dann v. Johnston, (ref. [7]), and Parker v. Flook, (ref. [19]). The net result of these procedures has been increased confusion, and an inequitable application of patent law, since those people who carried patent claims only as far as the CCPA did obtain patents on software.

Early policy at the Patent Office dictated that patents would not be granted for software (ref. [20]). This policy was based on the mental steps doctrine which had been established in a non-computer case in 1951, In re Abrams (ref. [1]). The mental steps doctrine consisted of the following test:

1. If all the steps in a method or process claim are mental, then it is not patentable;

2. If the novelty in a process claim rests on a purely mental step, then it is not patentable;

3. If mental steps are present, but are only incidental to the more important physical steps in a process claim, then it is patentable.

Even though applications for patents for computer programs have been made as either machine or process claims, or a combination of both, which application is more suitable for software has never been resolved. Process claims might appear to be more desirable, because a patent for a process provides a broader area of monopoly rights than does a machine patent (ref. [12]). On the other hand, process claims have received the strongest rejections from the Supreme Court. In addition, the mental steps doctrine specifically applied to process claims, and even though the CCPA systematically denied that process claims for software violated the mental steps doctrine, the Patent Office continued to use the doctrine to deny patents for computer programs.

The main problem with obtaining a patent today lies in the inability to describe a software package in terms that fit the statutory language permitting issuance of a patent. These statutes essentially provide protection for embodiments of inventor's concepts. The general approach of the Patent Office today appears to be that computer programs are not patentable because they are not new methods or apparatus, but rather mathematical processes or formulae (mental processes). This approach stems in part from The Report of the President's Commission on the Patent System. This Commission was established by executive order 11215 on April 8, 1965. Its purpose was to investigate the patent system. The Report of its findings was released on December 8, 1966. It provided:

IV. The classes of patentable subject matter shall continue as present, except: ... (3) A series of instructions which controls or conditions the operation of data processing machine, generally referred to as a "program" shall not be considered patentable regardless of whether the program is claimed as: (a) an article, (b) a process described in terms of the operations performed by a machine pursuant to the program, or (c) one or more machine configurations established by a program.

Although this report has no weight as law, the persuasiveness of it can be seen in the field. The position adopted by the President's Commission, coupled with the rejection by the Supreme Court in the only three cases it decided in the field of patentability of computer software, lead one to believe that there is little prospect for obtaining patent protection for software.

In the cast of Gottschalk v. Benson, (ref. [11]), the first patent application for software to reach the Supreme Court, the patent was denied on the basis that it would have given the inventor a monopoly on a mathematical formula, or algorithm. Mathematical formulas had been ruled unpatentable by the Supreme Court as early as 1939, McKay Radio and Telegraph Company v. Radio Corporation of America (ref. [15]).

In this case, the application for patent was on a method for converting binary-coded-decimal numerals into pure binary numerals for use with general purpose digital computers of any type. The Supreme Court held that the computer program, a mathematical formula without substantial practical application except in connection with digital computers, was not a patentable process. In so holding, the court said that the "process" claim is so abstract and sweeping as to cover both known and unknown uses. It would in effect preempt the mathematical formula and in practical effect be a patent on the algorithm itself. The clue to patentability is the transformation and reduction of articles to a different state or thing. (409 U.S. 68). Here they held that the process was a phenomena of nature and although just discovered, mental processes are not patentable as they are basic tools of scientific and technological work. The Court treated the application as a process claim, rather than as a machine claim, primarily because the patent could have covered "any use of the claimed method in a general purpose digital computer of any type (ref. [11])." However, the decision also stated, "We do not hold that no process patent could ever qualify if it did not meet the requirements of our prior precedents. It is said that the decision precludes a patent for any program servicing a computer. We do not so hold (ref. [11], p. 67)." Therefore, and as subsequent activity in the Patent Office, the CCPA, and the Supreme Court proved, the issue was not at all settled.

The second case was that of Dann v. Johnston (ref. [7]), considered by the Supreme Court in 1976. Because Benson had been rejected as a process claim, Johnston applied for his patent an a machine or apparatus. This turned out to be irrelevant, since his application was denied on the basis that his invention was obvious "to one reasonably skilled in the applicable art," at the time the invention was made (ref. [7], p. 228). Johnston's apparatus was "a machine system for automatic record keeping of bank checks and deposits (ref. [7], p. 219–20)." This application was held unpatentable by the Supreme Court primarily because Johnston's system was similar to other banking and record keeping systems, and was therefore obvious. In order to avoid deciding on the general concern of patentability for software, the Court declared the issue of obviousness to override other considerations, and refused to address them.

The conditions for patentable non-obvious subject matter are set forth as.

A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102 of this title, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negatived by the manner in which the invention was made, (35 U.S.C.A., sect. 103).

The Court reasoned that the mere existence of differences between prior art and the invention does not establish that it is non-obvious (425 U.S. at 227). In fact the court said, "Obviousness" does not turn on whether invention is equivalent to some element in prior art, but rather whether difference between prior art and subject matter in question is sufficient to render claimed subject matter unobvious to one skilled in applicable arts. This test of obviousness tends to eliminate many software programs from patent consideration, because these programs mainly consist of an investment in time and money. In considering whether to patent a program, consideration must be given to those possessing ordinary skill in the field and their ability to reach similar results using the state of the art.

The third and most recent case is Parker v. Flook (ref. [19]). This case involved a “method for updating alarm limits.” The method contained three steps described as follows:

1. The initial step is to measure the present value of the variable in the process (e.g., the temperature).

2. The intermediate step uses an algorithm to calculate an updated alarm-limit value over which human attention or action is required.

3. The actual alarm limit is adjusted to the updated value.

The Court held it was not patentable because, the identification of postsolution applications of the formula did not make the method eligible for patent protection, and the application simply provided a new and presumably better method for calculating alarm limit values, in which the only novel feature was the mathematical formula or algorithm. The case turned on the construction of the following:

Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title (35 U.S.C.A., sect. 101).

Here, Justice Stevens in speaking for the court, says it is unpatentable “not because it contains a mathematical algorithm as one component, but because once that algorithm is assumed to be within the prior art, the application, considered as a whole, contains no patentable invention.”

Again the decision to overturn the ruling of the CCPA was based on the process claim only demonstrating novelty in a mathematical formula or algorithm. The Benson case was used directly as a precedent in Flook. However, Justice Stevens, in his assenting opinion, sent a message to Congress stating:

"Difficult questions of policy concerning the kinds of programs that may be appropriate for patent protection and the form and duration of such protection can be answered by Congress on the basis of current empirical data not equally available to this tribunal.

It is our duty to construe the patent statutes as they now read, in light of our prior precedents, and we must proceed cautiously when we are asked to extend patent rights into areas wholly unforeseen by Congress." (ref. [19], p. 2528-29).

In other words, it patents are to be allowed for software, then the Supreme Court wants a clear, statutory directive from Congress providing the authority to do so.

But the Flook opinion was not unanimous, unlike Benson and Johnson. Justice Stewart wrote the dissenting opinion in which he said that this case was far different from that of Benson. Here, he argued, only one step of several involved a mathematical formula, and the patent should not be disallowed on the basis of that one step (ref. [19], p. 2530). The particular application involved a series of steps which would be used to control certain “alarm limits.” Alarm limits are numbers against which temperatures and pressures are compared during the refining of petroleum. The alarm limits would be stored in a computer system which operated the refinery. When the limits were reached, the computer would adjust valves and temperatures. The process for which the patent was sought was a technique for adjusting the alarm limits depending upon whether the plant was an equilibrium or in a start-up phase.

Justice Stewart also argues the Benson precedent only applied when the algorithm was "not limited to any particular art or technology, to any particular apparatus or machinery, or to any particular end use (ref. [19], p. 2530)." In the Flook case there existed a rather specific technology, apparatus, and end use to which the patent would apply.

Rather than speculating that Flook, in its dissenting opinion, points the way of the future, it would be more reasonable, perhaps, to conclude that the dissenting opinion reflects the ambiguity involved in patenting software.

A very interesting side issue was brought to light in the Flook opinion. Justice Stevens, in the assenting statements, felt it important to remark,

"The Commissioner of Patens and Trademarks files a petition for a writ of certiorari, urging that the decision of the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals will have a debilitating effect on the rapidly expanding computer "software" industry, and will require him to process thousands of additional patent applications." (ref. [19], 2524).

The only reason the Supreme Court agreed to hear the case, apparently, was because patenting of computer programs would overload the Patent Office with work! It is not clear how much this threat affected the majority opinion, but the temptation to believe that it had some effect cannot be ignored.

Taking these three cases in conjunction with the policy of the Patent Office on the patentability of computer programs leads to the conclusion that a program will be nonpatentable unless it:

1. Specifies or is limited to a particular end use of technology, and any algorithms used therein are also so limited; and

2. does not involve a mere idea or purely mental steps; and

3. does not have as the final inventive step the solution of merely one non-incidental algorithm or mathematical formula in the independent claims, or otherwise "preempt" the algorithm or formula; and

4. does not encompass a law of nature, printed matter, or a method of doing business; and

5. relates to a programmed general purpose computer, a special purpose computer, or a method of controlling or operating a physical process or a computer system.

Due also to the threat of having a patent declared invalid, infringement actions have been scarce. In deciding whether to apply for a patent on a computer program the different factors mentioned above should be carefully weighed against the cost of obtaining and the possibility of having to defend its validity. In most cases, the seeking of patent protection will not be the most efficient way of protection.

## 4. Copyrights

The Copyright Office began to register computer programs as "books" in May of 1964 under the 1909

Copyright Act [20]. However, the area of copyrights for computer programs is in a state of flux today. Even the Copyright Office is still not certain what protection is afforded programs when they are copyrighted, or whether justification for registration is sufficient. With the 1964 announcement that registration would be allowed came the following two questions:

"Is a program the writing of an author and thus copyrightable?" and,

"Can a reproduction of the program in a form actually used to operate or be 'read' by a machine be considered an acceptable 'copy' for copyright registration?" (ref. [6]).

Since the Copyright Office had a policy of accepting doubtful items for registration, software was copyrighted as long as:

1. Original authorship could be established.

2. Publication had taken place.

In the case of computer programs, this means that copies of the program had been distributed, or made available to the public, along with a notice of ownership.

Congress passed a new Copyright Act in 1976 which went into effect on January 1, 1978 (17 U.S.C.A., sect. 101 and P.L. 94-553). The new act does not specifically address the copyrightability of computer programs because Congress was waiting for the report of the National Commission on New Technological Uses of Copyrighted Works (CONTU). When the final report of CONTU was issued (July 31, 1978) it recommended three relatively minor changes to the Copyright Act of 1976:

"(1) to make it explicit that computer programs, to the extent that they embody an author's original creation, are proper subject matter of copyright; (2) to apply to all computer uses of copyrighted programs by the deletion of the present section 117; and (3) to ensure that rightful possessors of copies of computer programs may use or adapt these copies for their use." (ref. [10]).

Accompanying the CONTU report was a thoughtful dissenting opinion by Commissioner John Hersey which argued that software protection could best be achieved by enacting special legislation rather than including such works within the ambit of copyright protection. (See [23.30] for a discussion of the final (ONTU report, ref. [10].)

A new Computer Software Protection Act has been introduced in Congress by Robert W. Kastenpoy of Wisconsin (ref. [4]) which incorporates many of Commissioner Hersey's ideas (ref. [21]). Essentially a hybrid model for software protection, the proposed act offers copyright protection (of expression through a program) and patent protection (for the mechanical embodiment of the instructions themselves). As of March 1981, the Act still remained at the introductory level in Congress.

Since the new Copyright Act of 1976 does not contain any statutory refusal to grant copyrights for programs, the Copyright Office continues to operate under the old policy. Due to the newness of the 1976 Act, however, judicial and statutory interpretation of the new copyright Act is minimal. Therefore, in order to determine the present status of the law in this area, it will be necessary to examine the wording of the 1976 Act along with the legislative history leading to its enactment.

Under the Copyright Act, the author or other copyright holder is granted the exclusive control of the right to reproduce the "form of expression." To receive this "exclusive control," the elements of a computer program must fit within the words of the statute. This is so because under the 1976 Act (sect. 301) the statute effectively preempts such rights or equivalent rights granted under the common law or similar state statutes. These state statutes can give added protection, but they can not detract from that granted by the Federal Government.

Computer Programs are not explicitly mentioned in the definition of what is proper subject matter for a copyright. However, the following is provided:

Copyright protection subsists ... in original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium of expression, now known or later developed, from which they can be perceived, reproduced or otherwise communicated, either directly or with the aid of a machine or device. Works include the following categories: (1) literary works: ... " (17 U.S.C.A., sect. 102(a)).

The term "literary works" refers to works expressed in "words, numbers, or other verbal or numerical symbols or indicia" (17 U.S.C.A., sect. 101). Through implication, computer programs have been held to fall under the term "literary works" as proper subject matter for a copyright.

Just because a computer program is considered proper subject matter though, does not mean that all aspects of a program are copyrightable. Remembering that copyrights only protect expressions (i.e., manuals), the following limits what parts are in fact copyrightable:

In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work (17 U.S.C.A., sect. 102(b)).

In enacting this section, Congress stated that the intent of section 102 (b), as applied to computer programs, was to make clear that the expression adopted by the programmer is the copyrightable element in a computer program, and that the actual processes or methods embodied in the program are not within the scope of the copyright law. (Notes of Committee on the Judiciary, House Report No. 94-1476.)

Thus, even though the expression of a program is copyrighted, the underlying concepts and processes are not protected. The result of this is that an action for infringement will not exist when another reaches comparable results without the use of the copyrighted material. Also, once the basic idea becomes known, others will be free to use the underlying ideas. In fact, having a specific set of instructions copyrighted may not be enough to prevent use by others. Continental Casualty Company v. Beardsley (ref. [5]), held the position that specific instructions may sometimes be "so basic, essential and integrated with the underlying idea that use of the same language would not constitute an infringement."

Once a computer program (or part of) is deemed copyrightable, it must be registered. They are registered as nondramatic literary works! (Class TX, 43 Fed. Reg. 966, January 5, 1978). A copy for deposit must accompany the registration application. The deposit of the work is provided for by sections 407 and 408 of the 1976 Copyright Act. Final deposit rules under the new act require that if the program is in machine readable form, the copyright holder must deposit one copy of the identifying portions of the program in a form visually perceptible without the aid of a computer. The identifying portions are defined to mean the first and last 25 pages or equivalent unit that contains the copyright notice (43 Fed Reg 763, January 4, 1978).

Reasonable notice of the claim of copyright is also required by the Act section 401. It provides for this notice to be placed on all publicly distributed copies from which the work can be visually perceived, either directly or with the aid of a machine or device (17 U.S.C.A., sect. 401). This notice requirement can present problems to program producers when distributing program copies to users because of the inclusion in the programs of components such as disks. Under the proposed rules for placing copyright notices on works in machine readable form that cannot be visually perceived, (42, Fed Reg 64377, December 23, 1977.) the Copyright Office gives four examples of acceptable methods:

1. A notice embodied in the machine readable copies so that when a visually perceptible printout is made, the notice appears either with or near the title, or at the end of the work.

2. A notice displayed at the user's terminal when signing on.

3. A notice that is continuously displayed on the terminal.

4. A permanently legible notice reproduced on a gummed or other label that is securely attached to the copies, or to a box, reel, cartridge, cassette or other container used an the permanent container for the copies.

A number of advantages and disadvantages exist with copyright registration. Among the advantages are: original authorship and publication restrictions to registration; inexpensive and relatively quick-to-obtain copyrights; effective registration for the life of the owner of the copyright plus fifty years; collection for damages on infringement without proving actual damage (P.L. 94-553).

The disadvantages, however, are substantial. They include: the owner of the copyright has only protected the expression of the idea and not the idea underlying the copyrighted material (Mazer v. Stein ref. [14]); someone independently writing the same program is free to use his or her program without recourse from the first author [8]; publication deliberately places the program in the public domain, enabling a person with determination the opportunity to copy it [3]; copyrights are more suitable for large programs than small ones because of the difficulty in copying a large one. In addition, the question has been raised whether protection of the version of the program in a high-level language such as COBOL or FORTRAN also protects the version of the program in machine language [3].

As of March 1981, only one copyright infringement suit had been seen in the courts since the enactment of the 1976 Act (Data Cash Systems, Inc. v. JS&A Group, Inc., ref. [8]). JS&A Group copied plaintiff's CompuChess program from a Read Only Memory (ROM), which was part of the computer circuitry. But the District Court ruled that no copyright infringement had taken place since a program on a ROM is not observable to the human eye. In the subsequent appeal (Data Cash Systems, Inc. v. JS&A Group, Inc., ref. [9]), the results of the District Court were upheld, but on the grounds that plaintiff's copyright had been forfeited. Although plaintiff distributed the source language version of the CompuChess program after January 1, 1978, more than 2500 CompuChess units (including the ROM) had been sold to the public prior to that date. Furthermore, no copyright notice had been placed in the ROM for machine readable display, since plaintiff assumed (erroneously) that the contents of the ROM were not susceptible of being copied. Hence, the court ruled that the concept of limited publication (restricting the ROM both as to persons and purpose) was not applicable, and plaintiff's request for injuctive relief against JS&A Group was denied.

Even if Congress passes a revision to the Copyright Act specifically allowing copyrights on computer programs, certain of the questions raised above will undoubtedly have to be answered through litigation. A 1977 report of the National Bureau of Standards includes:

"the protection against reproduction afforded by copyright may be of little or no significance with respect to programs designed specifically for a particular user. Such protection may be quite valuable, however, for a program that would have a market of many users and could be reproduced cheaply in the absence of copyright" [20].

Of course, this contradicts those who believe that a program can be copied cheaply, even with a copyright, and that it will probably happen.

## 4.1. Trade Secrets

Trade secret law is the most popular form of protection for computer software [2,10,19]. It is also the only commonly used type of protection under which sanctions have been imposed ([25], and Ward v. Superior Court, ref. [26]).

Being a combination of state statutory and common law, trade secrets vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction [8]. The most generally accepted definition of a trade secret is:

A trade secret may consist of any formula, pattern, device, or compilation of information which is used in one's business, and which gives him an opportunity to obtain an advantage over competitors who do not know or use it. It may be ... a pattern for a machine. (4 Rest. Torts 757, comment b, 1939).

Therefore, software, being a formula, device or compilation of information providing a competitive advantage to a business, can clearly be covered under trade secret law. A rare area of agreement exists on that fact Whether trade secret protection is adequate, however, remains an issue.

The law of trade secrets exists in harmony with the Patent Laws of the USA because if a program is patented, trade secret protection is not preempted, Kewanee Oil Company v. Bicron Corporation, ref. [13]). It has been developed to protect companies from unauthorized users and unauthorized vendors. In essence, trade secrets protection prevents someone from being able to "reap where they have not sown," International News v. Associated Press, ref. [12]).

The courts have awarded computer software trade secret protection to companies if they properly can answer two questions: Is it a trade secret? Was obtaining it fair or unfair? Tests that have been used to determine whether a program qualifies as a trade secret are:

1. Is the program really secret?

2. Is the program really valuable?

3. Was the program developed and owned by the company?

4. Was it difficult to develop the program?

5. Has the program been copied?

6. Is it fair to protect the program [29]?

Which of these tests will be used and how much weight is given each in deciding whether a program is a trade secret will depend on the law of a particular state. This is so because each state has a different common law. Some states have more conservative approaches, while others are more liberal in this area. But, even under the more restrictive views, programs should qualify for protection as processes or mechanisms.

In protecting a program as a trade secret, it must be remembered that the protection must be continuous. Trade secrets do not protect against discovery by fair and honest means, such as by independent invention, accidental disclosure, or by so-called reverse engineering (i.e., by starting with the known product and working backward to derive the process which aided in its development or manufacture – National Tube Company v. Eastern Tube Company, ref. [18]).

Thus, the most efficient way to afford protection appears to be through a comprehensive prevention plan. One such prevention plan could consist of the following:

1. Proper classification procedures.

2. A comprehensive labeling requirement

3. A rigorous contract policy for all trade secrets materials.

4. Careful monitoring of use of the product.

5. Litigation of necessary.

A policy such as this would tend to prevent the leaking of secrets as well as stop inadvertant disclosure. Another advantage would be that if litigation were required to recover damages for unauthorized use, evidence of good internal controls (to protect programs) could be supplied. This would help to show that the unauthorized user obtained the program unfairly.

Aspects of trade secret law not covered here include restrictive agreements with employees and contracts with customers restricting use. The very large number of possibilities of using these types of measures takes it beyond the scope of this paper. The present state of the law of trade secrets appears to be one in which programs can be protected. A company desiring to protect their programs through this avenue needs to consider an enforceable policy as to prevention and continuously abide by it.

The benefits of trade secret protectiodn are: violation of trade secrets is a criminal offense and therefore a greater deterrent than patent protection; the owner of the software is protected forever as long as no one else discovers or inadvertently publishes the secret (ref. [15]); disclosure of the secret to a customer can be safe if the contractual agreement between the parties describes the liability of the licensee [7].

The disadvantages of trade secrets are: if anyone independently discovers the secret, or if accidental disclosure occurs, then the secret is no longer protected; it has been claimed that judicial hostility exists against the owner of a monopoly not protected by patent [8], society is not provided the advantage of knowing the secret at any predetermined time; the trade secret obligation to employees and customers is transferred through contractual agreements which may not exist under some circumstances. It is not at all unusual for proprietary software salespeople to allow a customer to "try out" a package for some length of time without benefit of a license to use the program [14].

Trade secret protection is not perfect. Often employees are hired by other firms strictly on the basis of prior experience. A fine line exists between legitimately using such experience in the new job position and a violation of trade secret law. In one case where an employee quit and went to work for a competing firm, seven years passed before compensation was granted for trade secrets he took with him when he left. In its decision, the Superior Court of California found, "documentation need not be taken in order to establish liability, since appropriation by memory alone" is sufficient. The defendant, Pinckney Whitfield, was ordered to pay his former employer, Cybertek, \$50,000. Whitfield's new employer, Tracor Computing Corp., was enjoined from marketing a software package similar in nature to the one Whitfield had developed at Cybertek unless a royalty agreement with Cybertek was entered into by both parties. Tracor was found guilty, along with Whitfield, because the company had continued production of their version of the package after having been notified by Cybertek that Whitfield, while employed by Cybertek, had signed an agreement of a disclosure [25].

Senator Ribicoff introduced the Federal Computer Systems Protection Act in June 1977. Congress adjourned without passing the bill, and, although Ribicoff indicated an intention to reintroduce the legislation in 1979, as of March 1981 the bill had not been reintroduced. The act would make it a federal crime with penalties of up to 15 years in prison and a fine of \$50,000, to access, alter, damage, or destroy computer software used by the federal government, banking institutions and in interstate commerce. The bill would also cover such damage to data and computer hardware [17]. It was felt that this legislation would serve to tighten the protection provided by trade secret law [21]. However, unless theft occurred while actually accessing a computer system, the bill would appear not to cover the same situations.

The privacy afforded in using trade secret law has been a major factor in the wide-scale adoption of this type of protection for software. Any firm contemplating relying on it, though, must remain sensitive to the need for careful contractual agreements between both employees, and customers, and anyone else with potential access to the computer programs needing protection.

## 5. Alternative Legal Protection

Patents, copyrights, and trade secrets have been examined as the most popular approaches to protecting proprietary software. Yet, as has been discussed, each of these approaches has its problems. Three other alternatives are available for attempting software protection, but these are also very subjective in nature. They are: contractual agreements, Doctrine of Unfair Competition, and ethics.

## 5.1. Contractual Agreements

Contractual agreements are always available to perform functions left undone through other means of proprietary software protection. They can be used primarily to extend responsibility for secrecy of computer programs to employees and to customers, although it is not difficult to imagine other situations in which contracts would be appropriate.

As pointed out earlier, employees are in key locations to become familiar with program design. They do not have to carry away listings, card decks, or voluminous notes from the shop to provide a competitor with rather complete details of proprietary software. In fact, in certain cases, employees are encouraged to give potential customers actual copies of program to use while the customer is deciding whether or not to sign a license for the software over a longer term [14]. It can be argued that such arrangements work to the advantage of the software house, since borderline customers could be swayed to adopt a package they have taken the trouble to install.

But the software vendor must recognize the risk involved in such indiscretions. A number of recommendations have been made regarding the clauses to be included in such a contractual agreement. These are:

1. the contract should bind the customer to maintain the confidentiality of any information disclosed during preliminary negotiations.

2. The vendor should describe his or her own rights regarding trade secrets, and should define the liability of the customer to protect against disclosure of the secrets.

3. The "buyer's use" of the software should be spelled out in detail. This should include the types of copies which can be made, and for what purposes, the number of computer systems on which the programs are allowed to operate, and the extent to which the customers may allow their customers to use the software. This is important if the vendor's customer is a service bureau.

4. The parties covered by the contract should be described, and the sanction allowed against the customer, in case the employees of the customer violate the agreement, should be spelled out.

5. Perhaps the employees of the buyer of the software should also sign the agreement. All the problems inherent with the vendor's own employees also are present with the employees of the customer.

6. If the buyer of the package makes improvements to the software, the ownership of the improvements should be established.

7. The length of time that the customer should maintain secrecy after the termination of the contract should be described. This is especially important if the vendor cannot be certain that all manuals, tapes and copies of the program are returned at the end of the contract period [3].

The risk for a vendor is not limited to customer knowledge of software. Vendor employees are in a prime position to carry detailed knowledge of vendor software in their heads and convey it to competitors or other external recipients. In drawing up agreements with employees, vendors should:

1. At least state the types of physical materials the employee is permitted to remove from the premises when he or she resigns.

2. Attempt to define the types of information disclosable to a competitor for a finite length of time after the resignation [3].

But, in the final analysis, the primary source of security is that only a few people know the trade secrets. This requires a strong management policy, including tight physical security, rules against customers knowing details of software without a license agreement, and software design procedures which prevent any single employee from knowing all the specifications of any program [14].

Brandon [7] argues from the point of view of the customer when he says that the buyer should insist on a "hold harmless" clause in a license contract which prevents liability on the part of the customer in case the vendor does not hold title to the software.

## 5.2. Doctrine of Unfair Competition

The Doctrine of Unfair Competition in common law is closely related to trade secret law. "It may encompass any form of activity which the courts view as taking unfair advantage of one's competitors," but it would pertain to protection of computer programs by providing a civil recourse on the breach of a confidential relationship, or appropriation of a trade secret [3]. For example, in the case of Ward v. Superior Court, (ref. [26]), Ward took a copy of a program with him when he quit working for one employer. When Ward went to work for another firm he brought along the program, in an "intangible form," which he subsequently caused to be printed out in hard copy form. Ward was charged and found guilty of trade secret violation under the California Penal Code, and Ward's new employer was found liable under unfair competition in tort, in a separate action.

## 5.3. Ethics

A variety of notions regarding ethics and the proprietorship of software have found their way into the media. Apparently a number of computer programmers feel that any program they write belongs to them, even if they are in someone's employ during the construction of the program [19]. This idea is resoundly discounted by firms paying the salaries of programmers. But the not-so-clear middle ground regarding exactly what a programmer is entitled to take along when he or she quits has generated controversy.

The following series of questions have been proposed for the programmer to ask himself or herself when leaving a firm:

1. Did the new company hire you specifically because it knows exactly what you are doing? Is it in direct competition with your present or former employer for a particular job? It is more interested in specific work you are doing rather than your general experience or education?

If the answer is yes, the possibility of unethical conduct may exist.

2. Even if you did not sign a conflict of interest statement, you may be legally free to do what you wish; ethically, however, you may not be if you determine that bringing your program with you could damage your former employer's opportunity for business, expansion or growth.

3. If you are doing anything secretly, then you may be in the area of unethical or illegal behavior.

4. Would you approve of an employee doing what you plan to do if you owned or represented the company or organization for which you work or formerly worked? [9]

If one could be certain that all employees were ethical, as implied in the above questions, then the whole issue of software protection would be far less important than it is. As depressing as it may sound, in order to protect a large investment in computer software, it may be prudent to assume that people are not ethical.

## 6. Possible Alternatives

In the attempt to protect proprietary interests in computer software, many alternatives to remedy the problem have been presented. In choosing among these alternatives, it must be remembered that along with protecting investments in a software program, there is a desire to increase the level of technology through publication of advances and the sharing of ideas. Some of the alternatives forwarded include:

1. Maintain Status Quo - This would continue with the case by case approach as it is today. Eventually, clearer standards would be developed.

2. Registration System - This would be done before a cognizant government body set up for this purpose.

3. Short term patents.

4. Partial disclosure patents - this would provide for the disclosure of the conceptual design and the object of the program with sufficient detail and practical know how about the program to ascertain that its operability would be rataiained.

## 7. Conclusion

A common theme connecting patents, copyrights and trade secrets for protecting proprietary software is the potential opportunity for the Supreme Court and/or Congress to clarify the Status of computer programs. Regarding the Dann v. Johnston case, the Supreme Court had an opportunity to clarify once and for all whether computer programs were patentable, but did not do so. Furthermore, the case of Parker v. Flook resulted in the Supreme Court waiting for a clear, statutory directive from Congress providing the authority to patent software. Thus, regarding patents, the final determination of the most appropriate policy rests with Congress. It is unlikely that the Supreme Court will hear another patent software case until the Patent Act has been changed, and then, presumably no need will exist to carry software patent appeals to the high court.

Many computer programs have been copyrighted, but all things considered, copyrights are probably the least effective method available for protection of software. The greatest advantage they have, one can speculate, is that they intimidate users of proprietary computer packages from indiscriminately copying them, not through an actual threat, but because of an appearance of a threat. Evidence for this hypothesis lies in the fact that a great many vendors of software display a notice of copyright without actually registering the program. In addition to user intimidation, the length of a copyrighted program (life of the author and fifty years, 17 U.S.C.A. 302) has been argued as excessively exceeding the useful life of a program. Other complications arise as to what is required when a program is upgraded and modified. These questions have not been sufficiently answered to provide a basis for a conclusion as to their present state. Although Congress passed a new Copyright Act in 1976 and enacted it on January 1, 1978, no statutory refusal to copyright computer software was included. Thus, the Supreme Court and Congress have not added any further clarification to the issue of protecting proprietary software.

Protecting software through trade secrets is possible when it meets certain criteria discussed previously, but whether trade secret protection is adequate may still be argued. Since trade secret law is a combination of state statutory and common law, no Federal laws exist to provide any consistent rulings. The Supreme Court and Congress have not yet been able to provide any precedent on trade secret law regarding proprietary software.

The use of a case by case approach to determine the applicability of protection under patents, copyrighting, or trade secrets makes the attempt of supplying the present status of the law an extremely difficult tank. The standards are subject to change at any time. It should be remembered that each of the three fields discussed are independent of each other, i.e., a program which is not patentable may be copyrightable. In fact, the part of a software package that constitutes the process may be patentable while the manuals that describe the operating instructions are not. However, these manuals might be protectable by the copyright laws. In determining the type of protection desired for a particular program, a company should first consider the novelty of their program package. Then they should try and fit the definition of their package or any part of it into the statutory requirements. If they are believed to fit, it will next be necessary to balance the amount of protection and the need for protecting this package against the cost of obtaining the protection and the possible costs of litigating.

Computer programs are not completely protectable under law as it exists. Copyrights, contractual agreements, trade secret laws, the doctrine of Unfair Competition, and even patents can be used to some degree to attempt to keep unauthorized use of programs to a minimum. But infringement can be so difficult to detect, that, unless someone begins to market a very similar program, even with ironclad legal protection, a person could not be certain the software was safe.
