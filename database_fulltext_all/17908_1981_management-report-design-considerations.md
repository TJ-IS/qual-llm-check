# Management Report Design Considerations

Michael G. Assad \*

Antohony's classical management levels are used as a base for defining information required for management in organisations and the transaction processing required to support organisations' business processes. Operational control and management control are considered, gap analysis via mechanistic and analytic forecasts are built-in, and the various dimensions of information at various levels of management are covered. Variance reports which tie into gap analysis and an exception matrix concept are also put forward in the paper. The effect of delaying reports to project managers is illustrated and the article concludes with two specific analysis techniques. The objective of this article is to document the major factors involved in producing more useful and effective reports for managers; technical considerations of report design are not considered.

Keywords: Management, Reporting, Information, Report-Design, Information Analysis, Variance Reporting.

## 1. Background

The considerations that are put forward in this paper draw heavily upon the framework for planning and control which was developed by Anthony [1].

Anthony identified 3 major levels of management activity. These are:

\- Strategic Planning (the formulation of organizational objectives, and the setting of direction and policies in an organization);

\- Management Planning and Control (the formulation of detailed plans and the acquisition of resources to achieve those plans), and

\- Operating Control (the fine scheduling of activities and performance reporting).

Tricker [2], Davis [3] and Duffy & Assad [4] have illustrated a further level of organizational activity which fits below the management levels of Anthony. This level is referred to as the “transaction processing” level, i.e. the processing concerning the events,

Consider also the concepts of feedback and feedforward. These are illustrated respectively in Figures 4 and 5.

Figure 4 indicates a management process with an input and an output. The management information system (MIS) monitors the outputs produced by the process as well as the process itself and reports to management; management compares the outputs with the objectives (or standards) which were set. This comparison can trigger some action (especially if the outputs are below the standards set) and the action will influence inputs to the process or the process itself. Feedback is thus post facto, and is therefore reactive.

Feedforward on the other hand is proactive. The processes that a manager will be monitoring are the total set of processes in the organizational domain, and the measurement of the processes within the organization will be called Organizational Analysis (i.e., analysis of the internal status of the organization). The MIS will also present information about the environment via an Environmental Analysis.

A manager will compare the information being presented with objectives or targets which have been set and thereby take necessary action. The action could affect the inputs to the processes, the processes and their characteristics, the organization as a whole (including its structure or even objectives), or the environment (to improve the organization's future success potentialities).

Feedforward will thus tend to look to the future and predict future events that are likely to occur if the existing momentum is maintained. The predicted result of existing events can then trigger future-orientated action (geared towards overcoming the differences which exist between targets and the results).

Such forecasts, however, are usually influenced by environmental changes which could be positive, negative, or both. Figure 6 sets out the two types of forecasts pictorially – Mechanistic (purely time series projections of past historic data), and Analytic (modification of the mechanistic forecast by applying "guesstimated" or known environmental changes). The gap between the target (objective to be achieved) and the analytic forecast is the challenge presented to a manager.

The sequence of events is as follows:

1. Report to manager shows Mechanistic forecast.

2. Manager uses entrepreneurial ability to identify an Analytical forecast, perhaps with the aid of a computer and/or model.

3. Manager develops action plans to close gap between the target and the Analytical forecast(s).

## 2. Dimensions of Information

To illustrate the various dimensions of information, consider two types of management decision at different levels of Anthony's hierarchy. Assume the decisions are at:

(a) operational level (e.g., the daily sequencing of a machine shop), and

(b) strategic level (e.g., the investment in a new factory in a new market area).

Obviously decisions would be made by managers at different levels in an organization. Their information would be somewhat different in nature. The source of their information would vary, in case (a) the information would be primarily internal to the organization, whereas in case (b) it would be primarily external to the organization (i.e. related to competitors, economics, environmental/industrial trends, and market place needs). The summary level of the information would be low in case (a) the information is not summarized here, but given in detail so that a schedule or loading can be produced) in case (b), the information would be highly summarized (showing market trends, competitive movements, costs and financial returns, breakeven points, etc. so that the decision can be easily made).

The required Accuracy of the information at the various levels will also vary; in case (a) it must be highly accurate, whereas in case (b) the information could be quite inaccurate.

The information in case (a) would also tend to be be repetitive (i.e. presented in the same form at a fixed frequency); Case (b) would not, as this sort of decision is not made repeatedly. As covered under feedforward case (b) information would be futuristic, because the nature of that sort of decision is futuristic; the time orientation for case (a) would, however, be current (or just passed) -- with very little future-orientation.

The information in case (a) would be fresh and up-to-date whereas case (b) would build in old data to evaluate trends. Case (b) data would have a low frequency, (not produced daily, but probably monthly)

whereas case (a) would have a high relative frequency (probably daily or weekly).

Finally, the information in case (a) would tend to be formal, and usually on paper; the information in case (b) would tend to be mainly informal and verbal, coming from outside the organization.

Therefore, one can expect information for different levels (or for different decision types) to be different too. An entrepreneur would typically expect to receive about 80–85% of the information informally, whereas a foreman would expect to find 60–70% of the decisionmaking information from inside the organization and presented formally [5].

It is important, therefore, to ensure that information being presented at a level has characteristics matched to that level (i.e. the information profile of a management function should be matched by the information being reported to that management function). Any mismatch will tend to influence the manager's activities away from delegation (and relevant zones) towards operation (and irrelevant zones).

## 3. Variance Reports

The major type of report to trigger control action is a variance report, shown in Figure 8. Figure 8(A) is the simplest form of a variance report, showing a standard (to be achieved), the actual achieved, the variance (or difference), and the variance, expressed as a percentage. Figure 8(B) shows a more complex form of the variance report, which allows a manager to compare this year's performance with last year's performance.

What should be more relevant, however, is the forecasting of future events rather than only the analysis of past events. A variance report should be produced which shows:

-- target to be achieved

\- actual achieved

\- variance (in units and/or percentage)

\- Mechanical forecast of what is likely to be achieved

— variance between mechanistic forecast and target to achieved (in units and/or percentage).

Figure 9 illustrates such an extended variance report with extended variance triggers.

Such a layout will not include an analytic forecast or variance between the analytic forecast and target to be achieved. This is evaluated using "what-if?" analyses, usually via a modelling process (often computer-assisted) to evaluate the gap requiring attention.

Fig. 8. Variance Reports.

From the above comments, it can be appreciated that correct presentation of information to managers can influence performance and have an obvious effect on the situation of Figure 3 tending to improve it towards that of Figure 2.

Another useful tool, related to variance reporting, is the exception matrix (shown in Figure 10). Here, reports and the managers receiving them are matricized with the content of each matrix cell indicating the appropriate break points (or triggers) for the receipt of each report. In Figure 10, manager 1 will receive report 1 on two conditions (monthly, or for those items which have a variance greater than 10%); the same report will be given to manager 2 weekly or for those items with a variance of 5% or greater. Manager 6 will receive report 1 daily or for items with a zero per cent variance (that is all items).

Two things can make an exception matrix more useful:

(a) the variance expressed as a percentage, and also

(or alternatively) as a value (or units);

(b) the triggers not only as frequency or variance but incorporating a delay and/or a trend change.

## 3.1. Delays

Delay is illustrated as follows: Two managers (a superior and a subordinate) may be receiving the same report on the same day. This will not allow the subordinate to take action on the report before the superior queries the situation. Delaying the-superior's report can allow the subordinate time to identify the problem, evaluate alternatives, and decide on an action plan. The management action has already been executed, encouraging action at the right level, and improving delegation.

## 3.2. Trends

If a forecast (mechanistic) shows an 'expected trend in historic data, then no reporting is needed. However, if there is a change, then this could be notified to the appropriate manager. Trend change, like variance, could be in units or percentage. It must however be big enough to filter out noise, and a 50% change is suggested as a starting point. A further embellishment can be applied to the variance. Assume that a manager is at operating or middle level. This manager's target is usually based on past performance. Figure 11 illustrates this.

Assume results are plotted over time, and the average is calculated. Assume also that the result varies from standard, then when should a higher level of management be notified? The trigger could be one standard deviation from the mean (i.e. as long as the results being achieved are between +1 and -1 standard deviation from the mean, then no further action is taken). But once out of range, a higher level manager is notified. Variances greater than two standard deviations can go to even higher levels.

Such an approach leads to two interesting extensions. Firstly as in Figure 12(A), a manager can be performance rated on a scale from 0 to 5. Such a rating will tend to encourage managers to perform higher than standard (the past historical average). This can cause the average to improve over time as shown in Figure 12(B). Further, narrowing of the spread of results can be expected.

This however does not ensure that the right things get done but does help because things are carried out correctly.

So far, however, we have given no guidelines on the frequency of reports to managers. Research (6) on a project management model plotted the over- (and under-) swings caused by management action, where over-expenditure (or under-expenditure) on a project was reported at different times after the event (see Figure 13). Figure 14 illustrates some practical, proposed timings for various information dimensions and management levels.

Although a perfect answer to management report frequencies is not given, one very important point is illustrated, i.e. that early reporting of an event can cause over correction, an undesirable fluctuation or ripple effect. It seems necessary to alter the report frequency until a good balancing of the factors of management comfort and delegation is achieved.

## 4. Other Report Forms

Reports to management do not, obviously, have to be in written or tabulated form. Common alternatives include verbal reports, highlight reports, pictorial reports (pie charts, bar charts, histograms), and computer visual display. These have one purpose: the illustration of obvious exceptions. Such reporting may have negative psychological implications [7]; they often make managers feel uncomfortable, though their decisions improve. Colour can obviously be used as an extra dimension to assist in the contrasting of important and relevant information. Highlight reports are a common exception report; a format is shown in Figure 15. Thus subjective ("gut feel") communication can be included. Wherever possible, a highlight report should be kept simple and not take more than 1/2 hour to complete. Taking too long is demotivating, and seen to be wasteful "administration."

Modern-day software allows a manager to structure an ad hoc enquiry. Such an enquiry tends to interrogate only internal types of data or information. An executive could structure the enquiry to his data base and display the results. An enquiry can also be standardized and stored for later use, and this allows an executive to store an existing ad joe enquiry for the future.

Informal reporting is also important. But it must be managed intelligently if it is to be useful. This requires some pre-knowledge on what information would be helpful.

Informal reporting is very important to management as Mintzberg's research [8] has shown. A large percentage of a manager's information used for decision-making comes from inside the organization, verbally via the executive's own management team. At strategic levels a further large portion of information comes from informal contacts outside the organization [5].

## 5. Information Analysis - How to Decide What is Needed

An executive needs assistance in the establishing of what information is needed. The Decision Analysis of Duffy & Assad [4] is put forward as a guideline for analyzing a decision and identifying what information is required for it; assistance in analysis can be obtained from a business (or information) analyst. Practice in using decision analysis will eventually enable a manger to internalize the process without assistance.

Decision Analysis is illustrated in Figure 16; it starts off with the organization's (or business unit's) key objective or mission, identifies the business unit's key areas or critical success factors (i.e., the important areas where performance is essential), identifies specific objectives, then identifies the key decisions to achieve those objectives, and finally establishes which key information is needed. Once the information is identified, a mechanism can be defined to manufacture that information (clerically and/or on a computer) at the correct frequencies.

```sql
SPECIFY PROBLEM
BUSINESS UNIT MISSION
KEY PERFORMANCE AREA (OR CRITICAL SUCCESS FACTOR)
ANALYSIS
KEY OBJECTIVES FOR KEY AREAS
KEY DECISIONS
INFORMATION NEEDS
SYSTEM LIFE CYCLE (TO MANUFACTURE INFORMATION/REPORT)
```  
Fig. 16. Decision Analysis (Source: ref. [4]).

If, however, a manager already has a set of reports which are close to what is needed, an Output Analysis [see 4] can be very useful in defining the information requirement. Such an analysis is shown in Figure 17. It follows three major phases:

– study the present report/information;

\- analyze it;

\- identify changes.

(a) Studying of the report entails questions as shown.

(b) The analysis of the report can then be made against a person's functional position within Antony's hierarchy. Any mismatch between these information dimensions see Figure 18) will lead to an area where the report can be improved.

(c) Changes can then be made (sometimes quite minor), such as:

\- frequency of reporting;

\- exception level;

\- inclusion of standards of performance;

\- improving report layout (physical appearance and readability); and

\- Sending it to the right people.

```txt
- SPECIFY PROBLEM
- ASK QUESTIONS
  - WHAT IS THIS REPORT?
  - WHAT IS ITS CONTENT?
  - DEGREE OF SUMMARIZATION
  - DEGREE OF EXCEPTION
    REPORTING
  - WHO GETS IT?
  - HOW OFTEN?
    WHY DO THEY GET IT?
- ANALYSE ANSWERS
  - REFERENCE TO ANTHONY'S HIERARCHY
    AND JOB/FUNCTION
- FORMULATE CONCLUSIONS
  - IS IT DATA OR INFORMATION?
  - IS THE REPORT MISDIRECTED?
  - IS THERE AN INFORMATION/DATA
    VACUUM IN A FUNCTION?
  - ARE TRIGGERS CATERED FOR?
- SYSTEM LIFE CYCLE
(TO MANUFACTURE INFORMATION/REPORTS)
```

## 6. Conclusion

This paper documents the major factors involved in producing more useful reports. Technical aspects are not considered, nor are the informal information links and networks inside and outside the organization. The techniques have, however, been tested in real life and found to be extremely useful to managers.

Application of the techniques can be expected to make information more effective and decision-making more directed. A goal-directed and objective-orientated management process can be developed by improving the quality, content, and nature of the information being presented.
