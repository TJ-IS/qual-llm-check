# Corporate Management's Role in System Development

E. Nikulainen

Oy Widenius, Sederholm & Someri AB, Annankatu 27, SF-00100 Helsinki 10, Finland

Assimilation of computer technology by organizations is an organizational learning process that has unique characteristics. Organizations are in different stages of development. These stages provide the foundation for developing adequate situation management emphasis.

This paper is based on experience gained in the author auditing and consultancy practice. It discusses some underlying factors that nowadays in typical organizations seem to drive the assimilation process. The consequences that arise with regard to appropriate management emphasis are highlighted.

Keywords: Management control, organizational learning, systems development.

Mr. Nikulainen was educated at Helsinki School of Economics and Business Administration, Finland and worked for seven years prior to 1977 as a systems analyst and a head of Information Systems Department. ## 1. Introduction

The management control, internal control, and quality control of the Electronic Data Processing function do not seem to be at the same level as more traditional functions like accounting, production, etc. Moreover, creativity and adaptability towards data processing appear to be missing in many organizations. Corporate management often has to face these two separate problems.

In good organizations, control should be of high standards. The EDP function is a tool for better control and productivity within the organization, and this should work according to the needs of organization, guarded by the control procedures. Management should also create an environment in which the internal productivity of the entire EDP function will be high.

If a company's EDP professionals stick to current methods, relative productivity will decrease. To improve this situation, a creative climate must exist. The situation in an organization may often look orderly: management and internal control procedures have been established and adaptability is evident. However, a closer look reveals that the activity of the EDP Steering Committee is intermittent and improvised in character. This is often complicated by reports from the EDP department full of mainframe (vendor) oriented words and abbreviations.

Unfortunately, adaptability and creativity among data processing personnel often manifest themselves in hardware and programming-level oriented experiments. These ventures do not usually raise productivity of programming. On the contrary, they tend to be expensive, reduce the reliability and maintenance of the systems, and hamper quality control of applica-

Under these circumstances the control is inefficient:

1. Systems are often created in areas where the users' need is not greatest. In this situation it seems to be that the manager who is most interested in EDP gets the best service, because of weaknesses in the priority procedures.

2. Completion of systems is delayed, and considerably more funds are spent on development than budgeted, because proper project control and design standards do not exist.

3. Making changes in running systems often exceeds any cost estimate. Many systems are rebuilt every few years because of their inflexibility. It is usually difficult to justify economically a system for administrative routines if its lifespan turns out to be less than seven years. Return-on-investment in production and process-control oriented systems is usually much higher.

## 2. Emphasis on the Management of Systems Development

Formal, on-going management and control procedures are better kept to a minimum. Problem oriented measures should be substituted depending on the stage of development of corporate data processing. In practice, it is important to identify this stage of development. A large multi-site company may have divisions in very different stages of development. It is also useful for executives to gain such a perspective.

Knowledge of the stages [1] provides the foundation for developing appropriate management emphasis. Introduction and application of new technology of any kind is difficult to manage; data processing has, apparently, a certain ethereal quality. Mechanization in the factory is much more concrete than the automation of functions connected with administration and production control (i.e., data processing).

A tendency to deal with concrete matters and to ignore the more immaterial aspects is illustrated by the observation that decisions on hardware acquisition are often made by the corporate management, whereas decisions on application software investment are usually made by middle management. Corporate management does not often see the development of application software as an investment, like the acquisition of hardware. Yet in most cases, the direct cost impact of the former nowadays exceeds that of the latter even without the hidden effects: the commitment of the organization to routines largely determined by application software is usually more important, longer lasting, and more difficult to change. Often hundreds of people adjust themselves to these routines, and it may be difficult to bring about change without harmful side-effects. Application software development ought to be seen more clearly as an investment.

But I have seen corporate management selecting a mainframe, comparing the speed of the basic cycle of the processing units of several computer. Yet, in real life the speed of the computer is mainly determined by the architecture of basic and application software. Often, existing management procedures that have stood the test of the time are not applied in the control of data processing function. Furthermore, decisions on data processing investment are frequently made in isolation, without being compared to other investment proposals, though automation might give a better return. In addition, the methods of determining profitability in data processing are often different from those applied to other functions; thus alternative profitability comparisons cannot be made.

There are many managers who seem to regard the EDP function and especially systems development as almost unmanageable. But the DP function can be directed and controlled by professional managers through the use of established methods. In many companies, corporate management has a laissez-faire, technically oriented attitude towards systems development rather than trying to manage and control it. There was a time when the engineering function was considered too technical to be managed effectively. But as the expanding economy made effective management mandatory, management skills were introduced into the engineering process.

There are analogies between engineering and programming functions, as well as product and systems design. The computer, as a unit of production equipment, parallels units of factory equipment. In the eighties, the emphasis should be shifted from management of computers towards manage ment of data resources. New planning and control systems should be data-oriented. At least, corporate management should compare DP investment with key areas that contribute to the company's profitability. For example, if the company's business is manufacturing, and half of the DP investment goes to supporting accounting, corporate management should rethink matters. Crises in management and organization appear to be more common in data processing than in other parts of the organization. But management-by-crisis hampers organizational learning; the organization repeats the same mistakes. Some of the specific reasons are:

1. The objectives are vague. There are no proper ways of giving measurable standards for later decision-making and evaluation. If the objectives are not clear and well-documented, there are different opinions about them which tend to disrupt effective work in systems development, because objectives are discussed without the members of the project group even recognizing it. Perhaps decisions have not been real. A decision is unreal if: (1) No alternatives have been considered; (2) Alternatives have all been predictably had: all will lead to crisis; or (3) Decision-makers did not realize the positive effects of some alternatives.

2. Risks have been ignored or underestimated.

3. Attempts have been made to establish such vast and complex systems that the methods have been inadequate for their construction.

4. The system has been designed for short-term efficiency without consideration of long-term effect. Methods development has stopped, and external communication has been minimized.

Corporate management should shift its emphasis in directing the systems development from management-by-crisis to management-by-improving-organizational-learning. The first and most important role of corporate management is the setting of priorities and objectives for systems development. (Figure 1).

The corporate management role in systems development is not to make every single decision, but if DP management makes decisions without an agreed strategy and plan, they are apt to be wrong or the decision will not be adequately understood by users.

The environment of many DP departments is turbulent: This requires open systems [2], but corporate management generally manages the DP department as a closed system (see Fig. 2). A turbulent environment requires that the DP department's organizational emphasis is on problem solving, but often corporate management requires only short-term performance. In the eighties, it is ineffective to have a single-goal-maximizer-type of DP department, because the tasks will not be repetitive in nature: DP will be more and more involved in business's multi-goal and varied-task areas.

Often it is wise to arrange two kinds of internal environment within an organization. In a control environment, EDP function budgeting, project management, personnel performance reviews, and charge out or cost accounting systems are used to ensure that EDP activities are effective and efficient. In a slack environment, formal controls are absent, giving the opportunity of using DP in new ways. Organizational slack should be directed towards business-oriented (not only technical-oriented) experiments.

When management permits organizational slack in the systems development activities, it commits more resources to DP than are strictly necessary for getting the job done. An imbalance of high control and slow slack can impede the use of information technology. Conversely, an imbalance of low control and high slack in mature data processing can led to explosive DP budgetincreases and inefficient systems. The third role of the corporate management in systems development is to strengthen the control environment around and within the data processing function in order to allow more future slack.

And fourth, the old slogan "If you can't measure it, you can't manage it" will be needed in the Eighties, too. Corporate management's task is to be sure that lower-level objectives are measurable. Organizational learning can made more effective by post mortems. Corporate management participation in planning systems investments should be as intensive as in corresponding plans in other sectors: top executives often deal too much with proposals provided on an ad hoc basis without broad guidelines.

Systems investment proposal makers should answer the question: What is being automated and what will be the effects? Often, it is not really automation, but some sort of mechanization that is in question: data systems are frequently used merely as calculators and duplicators; management participation is then not imperative, because the risks in the proposal and the commitment of the organization are fairly readily measurable. The situation is different when the purpose of the system is to extend automation to complex areas or to decision making. What matters here most is the maturity and skills of the user and data processing compared to the level of objectives. Past experience shows that it is here that the risks are greatest.

Data systems proposals often involve hidden effects; e.g., considerable hardware extensions are not always evident from the proposal. The soundness of the proposal depends on the standards used or the tradition and experience gained.

If several corresponding projects using similar technology have been successfully accomplished, the undertaking will probably also be successful. If there is reason to doubt the soundness of the proposal, it might be advisable to ask outside experts to consult on it. Its considerable gaps may then be disclosed, especially in arguments for software investment. The proposed system should coincide with the strategic view of the entire organizations. In this respect, notions vary from one organization to another: some make fixed, long-term plans, whereas other aim at flexibility, prepare for changes, plan different or alternative courses. In the latter case, especially, it is difficult to conform the systems structure to this strategy. So, before the decision making and during design management might test how the system changes can be effected, e.g., by asking for cost estimates which may provide a better estimate of the value of the investment.

When the systems environment is considered relatively stable, the lifetime cost of the system can be readily evaluated. The assumptions behind these evaluations must be recognized and recorded -- this itself promotes learning. Owing to the development of hardware, one must make decision on centralization versus distribution on connection with data processing investment decisions. The guiding principle should be that the centralization and distribution of data processing activities ought to follow the general organizational patterns. Technical distribution is another matter: a centralized system may be distributed at the technical for reasons of expediency.

From the corporate management standpoint, the centralization and distribution issue concerns primarily the data base policy of the organization. The distribution possibility provided by hardware does not yet solve anything. On the contrary, past experience shows that trying to solve the problems of data processing through uncoordinated acquisition of department computers may lead to even greater difficulties. Complexity grows increasingly, because a situation results where software and hardware are not compatible, and the same data recorded in several different data bases. With present methods it is almost impossible to verify the integrity of the data.

On the other hand, coordinated hardware distribution is (in most situations) more advantageous than a centralized system if the data bases works on a partitioning basis instead of being duplicated [3].

The objective of corporate management in project control is to ensure that the investment stays within the plans. This requires standard reports that show the real progress of the project. Standard reporting demands that adequate methods are employed in development. Planting standards in an organization, especially in a data processing unit is no easy task. A set of standards is usually a source of embarrassment. If there exist no project control standards, they must be adopted without delay. These must include instructions with frequent check points at different levels where the progress and substance of the system are compared with the planned objective. The objective of corporate management in arranging the audit after the adoption of the system is in the quality control of an investment. An additional objective might be an evaluation to probate lifespan. If the maintenance costs have increased considerably or the reliability of the system has been reduced, it is advisable to consider the total replacement of the system.

## 3. Conclusions

The corporate manager's activity is often described and thought of in terms of a mechanical regulation process: a deviation is discovered through control, and corrective action will adjust the matter. Here two essential features are ignored. First, it is the task of the management to express what is wanted: to establish the guiding standards of control. Second, neither control data nor executive impulses are absolute and exact; human inaccuracy and personal influences are involved. Even the accuracy coming from EDP is ostensible. A figure may not be up to date, it may refer to something else than imagined, or it may contain unknown effects. The participation of the corporate management in the system development increases its awareness of the nature of the data produced by the system. More important, the corporate management should gain some insight into what the future may hold and then try to develop appropriate management strategies that will accomplish corporate purposes. In doing this, it is useful to evaluate where the information system stands, which problems were a result of weak management, and which problems arose from natural growth.
