# Interaction between Modern Society and EDP

R.B.J. van Eldik

Rabobank Nederland, Executive Board, Postbus 8098.
3503 SE Utrecht, the Netherlands

Although it is tempting to consider the development of EDP just as one of the technological developments of the past, there are differences that ask for a new approach to the problems created. The development of EDP is placed in a historic prospective. Both the economic and sociological differences of the 80's, compared to the past decades, when technology evolved, are outlined. The coming EDP developments will be influenced by social constraints. It will be insufficient to let EDP develop autonomously and to try to minimize the negative side effects. One must anticipate forthcoming developments and try to device a framework in which these developments can be used without negative side effects for society and the individual. On the other hand, one has the challenging task to steer the EDP developments such that they will meet the needs of the individual and society, and will increase the feeling of well-being, the prosperity and the feeling of self fulfillment of the individuals in that society. When we are able to solve the problems in our own society, we might be able to find ways of sharing our achievements with countries with lower prosperity.

Keywords: EDP, technology, future developments, social constraints, anticipatory management.

## 1. Introduction

At a conference on Managing and Controlling EDP in the 80's one must discuss the interaction between EDP and modern society. It is evident that there is such an inter-relationship, although many of us have the feeling that this is just a one-way relation with problems such as employment, quality of labour, privacy, freedom of information, and communication. It soon becomes evident that the inter-relationship between society and EDP is complex, and that an analytical description in terms of cause and effect is very difficult.

## 2. Historical Perspective

In early days, technology was hardly of any significance. The period of the Greeks and Romans was a period of craftsmen. They considered nature to be an intelligent organism which functioned autonomously according to a rational plan. Cicero argued that no art could ever compete with the skill of nature, and although many useful items had been invented, they were all subordinated to the natural order of life.

Right or wrong, a break-through came in the Middle Ages. In many areas we can observe a series of technical innovations (influenced by the expansion of trade): rather simple ones like the stirrup, but also far-reaching ones such as the application of the wheel to the plough, the water- and wind-mills, the compass, and so on.

Between the 10th and 17th centuries the growth of technological self-confidence enabled the "great adventures" to be undertaken, and with the development of trade we see technology becoming more and more engaged in the economic process. The linking of technology and science came about, and the acknowledgement of the economic possibilities of this combination was shown in the foundation of institutions such as the Royal Society in 1662 and the Académie des Sciences in 1666.

During the era of the industrial revolution, technology was practically applied in mass-production processes. The output increased considerably and dramatic changes in production methods, social relationships, and life style took place. Factories evolved and due to technological and economic reasons division of labour occurred in them whilst the workers were degraded to "wage slaves". Urbanisation and the reduction to poverty of the greater part of society led to antagonism between the classes. Often, "machine breakers" unloaded their frustrations by destroying machines. It was clear that technology no longer contributed to better self-fulfillment for the majority of the people. Suspicions grew, social and economic discrepancies led to uproars and revolutions and even to global wars.

From at least two angles technological development was stimulated, and more and more accepted. On the one hand, technical engineers developed a social technology and tried to get the human factor in industry under control. On the other hand it was realised more and more that mass-production was production for the masses and that it made sense to increase the purchasing power of those masses.

An enormous number of unfulfilled wishes, initiated and increased by growing technological possibilities brought about a material welfare unknown previously. Although there was a growing suspicion that social behaviour was not keeping pace with technological progress, the latter was accepted as long as material progress lasted.

Technology now regulates the network of our society. Beneficial illustrations are the mass-communication and transport network. On the other hand, there are detrimental aspects such as power black-outs in cities as New York, difficulties with nuclear energy, pollution, and so on. But in spite of the increasing possibilities, more and more individuals seem to feel at a loss. In many respects these individuals feel degraded to a number, living in an increasingly impersonal world. Although technological achievements have diminished the world's vulnerability to natural disasters, they have on the other hand considerably increased the overall vulnerability of society.

## 3. Comparison of the Development of Technology and EDP

It is tempting to apply a comparison of the process of emancipation of technology to that of EDP development. After a hesitant start, EDP seems to be a powerful problem solver. But standing on the doorstep of the "Information Age" of the 80's, we can ascertain a number of essential differences between the emancipation of previous technology and the coming emancipation of EDP.

First of all there is a difference with regard to the economic environment. We have reached a stage of global inter-dependence. High quality products are being produced in various powerful economic blocks of the world, whereas (in areas with a high purchasing-power) more and more signs of saturation are evident. Whatever the economic or socio-cultural system of a given country may be, practically all countries aspire to the Western prosperity model, whether it be in competition or in cooperation with others (with different tendencies in some Middle Eastern countries).

Secondly, social factors play a much more important role. In highly developed countries, especially in Europe, many factors determine the social constraints within which the economic process has to develop. Examples of such factors are: quality of work, low mobility of the working population, penetration of democratic principles in the decision making process, the reduction of the total number of productive years, relative aging of the population, higher level of education, longer education, and the increasing supply of labour due to the emancipation of women. These social factors impose constraints on the development and application of EDP. However justified and understandable, they will most likely have to be adjusted if we take into account global inter-relationships.

Thirdly, there is the difference in the speed of penetration. We all know that there is a big difference in the useful life of certain technological solutions. There were 50 computer systems in 1953 – now there are more than 300,000. Whereas it took more than 100 years for the invention of photography to evolve into a mass-product, 50 years for the telephone, 35 years for radio, and 12 years for television, the “incubation time” for the integrated circuit was only 3 years. And this invention will probably have the greatest impact on society.

In the coming decade the trend in improvement of the price to performance ratio will hardly be bounded by inherent physical limitations. Interesting developments are about to be available in the areas of speech input and output, hand-writing recognition, bulk memory, communication bandwidth (due to fibers and satellites), world-wide communication infrastructure, and relatively natural language with which the user can address the computer.

This leads to the fourth difference: the areas affected by EDP. Mass proliferation of EDP products took place first in areas of routine work, where its application could improve efficiency. This is the current stage. The new supply of “liberated” labour could – in a growth economy – meet the increasing business volume or an alternative demand. EDP can penetrate all sectors of society at all professional levels, and this development will create resistance where the saved labour does not find alternative applications. Thus EDP penetrates into sectors of society previously unaffected by technology. Information is becoming one of the most vital factors in business, in government, and in private life. Apart from the advantages (for instance the provision for better management), there are doubts about intrusion on our privacy. This calls, however, for coordinated action and legal security.

Summarising, we see that technical improvement allows change, but forces countries to keep pace with others (because of international competition and regional saturation). But the social environment in some highly developed countries does not allow for rapid change – partly because of suspicious ill-feeling, and partly because of other priorities. We shall have to recognize that EDP is an excellent servant, but the worst of all masters, and then act accordingly.

## 4. Quo Vadis?

Considering now the society entering the 80's, we can observe a number of characteristics:

1. In many parts of the world a very high standard of living has been achieved, even with tendencies to saturation. In other parts of the world, however, there is extreme poverty.

2. In our Western society, we see the presence of a self-assured labour force, well-organized trade unions, and a wish for workers' participation in the decision making process.

3. A nostalgic desire for improvement of the quality of life entails a preference for well-being instead of prosperity. In many instances, there is lack of appreciation for the work of bigger industries. The impossibility of identifying oneself with the company or the job, the feeling that things happen in spite of oneself, and mistrust of the intensions of those who take decisions occur in the working communities.

4. Tendencies to minimise income differences are considered to be a fundamental factor of social solidarity. However, this frustrates motivation for heavy, difficult, or managerial work.

5. Lower economic growth can be observed, with high unemployment rates and discrepancies in the labour market.

6. Women's lib and women's emancipation have brought many women back into the working process. This has certainly increased the difficulties of the labour market. Needless to say, this should not inhibit woman's fundamental right to work.

7. High social security levels have created an atmosphere of euphoria in which misuse of the provisions does not seem infrequent, and this has certainly contributed to inflexibility and higher immobility of the labour force.

8. Growing uncertainty about the future tends to lead to an attitude of "keep what you have and defend acquired position". In politics and industry there are tendencies to polarization. Only a minority of people is willing to question whether a change in attitude could perhaps lead to a more humane society.

9. A world-wide communication network, and easy and relatively cheap means of world-wide transportation enable people to experience other cultures.

10. Finally, there is an increasing penetration of EDP equipment into the daily life of the citizen, both the working environment and leisure time.

This is just a very incomplete picture of a very complex society, but I would like to no e a number of elements which are highly inter-related. There is a strong inter-relationship between productivity, price level, international competition, and employment. Other examples of intervoven facets are:

1. Social security, wage level, and competitive position.

2. EDP development, productivity, and wage level.

3. EDP development, employment, resistance, and ill-feeling.

4. Uncertainty, need for information and participation, and self-fulfillment.

5. EDP, need for job-enrichment, and need for re-training.

6. Speed of technological change, need for re-training, and mobility of labour.

7. EDP supplier push, user resistance, and need for participation in development.

8. Need for information, communication network, databases, possible loss of privacy, and need for legislation.

Again, without claiming completeness, it should be clear that social and economic factors are interwoven. It is most probable that the economics of the 80's will not be as favourable as we have seen in the past. Necessary changes will take place in a potentially negative climate. Moreover, in several countries the social climate is a limiting factor in the execution of these changes. These circumstances will demand high managerial skills, quiet determination, and (above all) joint action by those who bear responsibility in our society.

There are a number of ways in which we can approach the complex problems of managing change by EDP. In the first place we could carry on as before, reacting to EDP applications which develop almost autonomously. In this case, we could just absorb the techniques and try to mitigate the unpleasant side-effects. This approach has created, and will create, ill-feeling and mistrust. People will feel manipulated by a development beyond their control and on which they have no influence. The huge centralized computer systems which are now used in the administration of companies often produce cryptic outputs. And though the device is most effective many older employees find that they cannot adapt, and the number of psychosomatic complaints has considerably increased. There is a growing discomfort because we become highly dependent on the functioning of others. The user-unfriendly forms and the enormous paper loads (which do not keep pace with the need for information) have all contributed to discomfort and ill-feeling.

However, in spite of these negative side effects, we cannot neglect the fact that many of today's "blessings" would not have been possible without EDP applications. And although first reactions were certainly not always positive, we recognize the contributions these developments have made to our society.

We can approach our future problems through anticipation. One can pose the questions: "which new possibilities are created by EDP?", "how much is EDP stimulated by new wishes?" and "which new possibilities should we use?". An anticipatory attitude from the user is necessary, but the supplier also bears a responsibility to better anticipate the requirements of the people who have to use the equipment and systems.

Technological developments will help us perform better in the future. The increasing penetration of mini and micro processors, the improved price to performance ratio, the improving communications technique, as shown for instance by data networks — these will all affect directly the work place of the individual. The improvement of the interface between human beings and equipment by means, for instance, of the spoken word, may make EDP technology more and more user-friendly. Anticipation means preparation for change, and thus anticipation is a vital element of the “management of change” (which allows us to steer social and technological development).

It is vital that the social partners and the government come to an understanding and speak one language. Misunderstanding leads to confusion and social unrest, loss of time, and tempo. Hence, it should be possible to:

\- make clear the economic and social constraints and inter-dependencies and subordinate EDP developments to them;

\- plan for the future;

\- increase the capacity for change in society. This means that resistance to change should diminish considerably, either by creating understanding or by adapting the technological application.

All good things in life must be earned by hard labout, and in an open (international) economy, we do not get anything for nothing. Hence, it should be clear that economic considerations are ultimately the dominant factor in applying technology. EDP applications do not stop at the frontier; the country that innovates better and quicker, and performs best, will have the highest opportunity in future. Only on a sound economic basis can we afford our highly appreciated social benefits. So it is of vital importance that we understand and subsequently avoid the social constraints which could hamper the application of EDP and its innovations.

There is a number of things we should do company-wise, nationally, and/or internationally:

1. Nobody should be threatened by EDP development; open communication on future developments and possible changes for the individual are necessary.

2. Application of new techniques should be guided by people who are aware of the reeds of the people involved. EDP applications should contribute to a greater degree of self-fulfillment for the individual. The people concerned should be involved in the development process.

3. Working conditions must be created at the lowest levels in the organizations, so that EDP applications lead to higher job-satisfaction and creativity for the individual.

4. Training and re-training programs must be developed; time and money spent for training during the productive life-time is more useful than earlier retirement.

5. EDP knowledge should be built up in users, so that they can perform on equal terms with EDP suppliers. Users should not allow themselves to be put under pressure. It is time that we ask whether certain EDP applications are really important to our competitive position, and whether these applications really contribute to happiness or well-being.

6. Pension provisions should be such that they do not inhibit change of company or job.

7. The flexibility of the organisation should be increased so that it looks for ways for job-enrichment: minimizing routine work and avoiding specialisation. Policy making, decision making, and routine tasks should be combined as low in the organization as possible.

8. All schools train for creativity and change, and adapt better and quicker to new developments. There should be periodic re-training of teachers. Our educational institutions should give more attention to the significance and possibilities of EDP technology.

9. Government should see that the interests and privacy of the citizens are safeguarded.

10. Social partners should agree on partial restoration of the price mechanism in the labour market – allowing more wage differentiation and stimulating change.

11. Users should combine forces to make suppliers more user-friendly; computers should be brought out of the sphere of mystery.

12. International standardisation is needed in the field of EDP. Enormous amounts have been invested in the labour organization around EDP, but standards are also a social necessity: people should grow accustomed to standard interfaces.

13. The international competitive positions are strongly determined by the relative standards of living, wages, and social security levels. It is important to mitigate destabilizing international differences.

As long as differences exist, we must compete with communities that are applying the highest level of technology with the highest possible speed, and yet with a minimum of social constraints. It is necessary to ensure that technology and social standards develop in the same direction, that they enforce each other, and that social constraints disappear. If we succeed in subordinating EDP to individuals, allowing full scope to their gifts, then EDP innovation could be an enormous driving force again.

Industrial activity and innovation should be supported by all concerned. Government and bankers should look for ways and means of providing the capital. Science and industry should work together in applied research projects.

Within economical boundaries, EDP development could make an important contribution to the realisation and increase of prosperity and well-being. We have entered a period of frictions; but frictions are temporary, and can certainly be mastered. Perhaps in the end, we will be able to earn our material standard of living with less physical effort, giving far more leisure time and enabling us to improve the quality of life. If in the future we should be able to find ways and means of sharing the advantages of higher productivity with countries with lower prosperity, then we could contribute to the elimination of international discrepancies.

(summary of a speech delivered on November 19, 1979 at Amsterdam-IFIP-Conference.
