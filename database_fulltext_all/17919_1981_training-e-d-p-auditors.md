# Training E.D.P. Auditors

Nuri Bar-Hava

Chairman, Board of Directors, Bank "Yahav" 14 Hilel St., Jerusalem, Israel

Formal education of Auditors does not include E.D.P. Auditing. Therefore special training schemes should be provided. Theoretically, E.D.P. Auditors can come from two origins: either as Auditors or as E.D.P. $^{1}$ specialists (In Israel E.D.P. people usually do not move into E.D.P. Auditing).

Generally, the training should have three parts: (1) General auditing, (2) E.D.P. knowledge, and (3) Auditing E.D.P. systems.

For Auditors the training can concentrate on the two last parts. This is a 325 hour, one-year course, designed in combination by: the Israel Institute of Productivity (Government), the Institute of Auditors and C.P.A., and the Institute of Internal Auditors. The course is composed of three sections: (1) E.D.P. techniques – appreciation, (2) How to audit E.D.P. systems, and (3) Supervised field work.

The largest difficulty for Auditors is to overcome the "fear barrier" toward the computer. Two tools may help: Exercises on the Institute's computer and some supervised field work, done by groups of students in computer installations.

E.D.P. auditing training in the future should go four ways:

1) Introduction of E.D.P. auditing into Universities.

2) Multistage training.

3) Promoting the profession

4) Cultivating an adequate climate for auditors with cooperation of management and E.D.P. professionals.

Keywords: Auditors, C.P.A., Computerized audit package, Supervized field work, E.D.P. auditing manual, "Fear barrier", computer exercises.

## 1. Introduction

After graduating, the general auditor does not generally know how to audit E.D.P. systems, because universities do not include E.D.P. audit material in their curricula. Meanwhile, clerical systems (especially financial and accounting systems) become more and more computerized. Yet with the spread of service bureaux and the introduction of mini computers and distributed processing, even small companies use E.D.P. Thus almost every auditor will, at some time, have to audit an E.D.P. system for some client. This will be difficult because E.D.P. is complicated, and one must learn before working with it. Therefore special teaching should be provided to train E.D.P. auditors.

Theoretically trainees to E.D.P. auditing come from two sources:

1. Auditors: either C.P.A's or internal audit personnel.

2. E.D.P. specialists with an interest in E.D.P. auditing.

Thus, the actual training program should be designed to match with these sources.

Experience in Israel has shown up to now, that E.D.P. people do not normally move into auditing. Thus we tailor our courses to the first source: the auditors. And apparently other countries have the same experience. In some large countries the large auditing firms provide E.D.P. auditing courses for their personnel and clients. In Israel, even the biggest auditing firms do not provide such training. So we combine efforts of the three major parties, involved:

\* The Israel Institute of Productivity – Israel center for Information Systems (government);

\* The Institute of C.P.A.'s & Auditors;

\* The Institute of Internal Auditors.

Each of the parties has representatives in the steering committee of the course. This committee is responsible for the development and updating of the course.

Who are our students? The profile of the typical student is:

Age: late 20's or early 30's;

Experience: several years in auditing;

Position: junior;

Sex: male;

Knowledge of E.D.P.: Nil.

The advanced age is partly explained by the fact that our youth spend 3 years in the army.

Who are the trainers? We have both E.D.P. experts and experienced E.D.P. auditors.

## 2. The Course

Generally speaking, the curriculum has three components:

\* The theory and practice of general auditing;

\* E.D.P. systems, hardware and software;

\* Auditing E.D.P. systems.

Only a comprehensive knowledge of these can make a good E.D.P. auditor. If the trainees are auditors, the training can concentrate on the last two components.

Our current course is the result of our experience over the past three years. It consists of 325 contact hours and takes a full academic year. The students are taught twice weekly, for one whole day and one afternoon. The course is held at the Center for

Some groups have even managed to write and run special small audit retrieval programs with very interesting and effective results. One group, for example, audited a cigarette firm. The test data showed that their system accepted negative prices, and did not even reject 100% discount. In another system, the group found that the computer gives a daily print-out of the log book, but only showing results from the main system. After the week of field work the group prepares a detailed audit report. Some weeks later, the groups participate in a full day seminar where every group presents its report for evaluation and criticism.

## 3. Conclusions

1) Again, the main obstacle to be overcome is the "fear barrier" toward the computer. Two tools can help, besides teaching:

1) Access to the computer;

2) Supervized field work.

2) The backbone of the training is a good, up to date, E.D.P. auditing manual. This especially helps the students in their actual supervised field audit.

3) Followup is important for three reasons:

a) It helps in improving the course. This technique has been very effective.

b) It acts in updating the staff on recent developments. We have only just started to plan this.

c) It further promotes the E.D.P. auditor profession.

Recently some of our graduates formed the E.D.P. auditors association in Israel; this cooperates with the institute in promoting the profession.

4) This course has become one of the most prestigious in the Institute of Productivity. It is expensive and attracts auditors from banks, firms and institutions. In the latest course we have an impressive number of students from our central bank.

5) In many cases, management of the firms that allowed us to perform a field audit adopted the major recommendations of our students.

## 4. Final Comments

a) There should be more E.D.P. auditing technology in the regular curriculum of auditors at universities.

b) There is need for a multistage training curriculum which updates the auditor in moving towards advanced E.D.P. systems. This scheme should also account for the ever-changing technology.

c) It is important to develop and promote the profession of E.D.P. auditors.

d) We must cultivate a good climate in E.D.P. to help auditors fulfill their task. To achieve this we need full cooperation from management and E.D.P. exercise, only thus we can build more secure and to reliable systems.
