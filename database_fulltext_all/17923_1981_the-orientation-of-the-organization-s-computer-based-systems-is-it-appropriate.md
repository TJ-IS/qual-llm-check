# The ‘Orientation’ of the Organization’s Computer-Based Systems: Is It Appropriate?

Tony Moynihan

Irish Management Institute, Sandyford Road, Dublin 14, Ireland

The distinction between computer-based systems which are “future” as opposed to being “past” orientated and systems which are “outward” as opposed to being “inward” orientated is introduced. It is shown that the orientation which is appropriate in any instance is determined by the degree of complexity of the organization’s task environment and the rate at which this environment is changing. It is argued that, when assessing the effectiveness of computer-based systems, it makes sense to ask the question: “Is the orientation of our systems appropriate to the nature of our task environment?”

Keywords: Orientation of systems, assessing effectiveness, contingency theory, environmental complexity, environmental stability.

## 1. Introduction

In everyday conversation about organisations, we often use short phrases to convey the overall tone or culture of an organisation. For example, we might label an organisation as being ‘aggressive’ as opposed to being ‘passive’. Or we might say that an organisation is ‘people orientated’ as opposed to being ‘results orientated’.

How valid is it to project characteristics such as these onto entire organisations? Empirical research has shown that it is valid to use at least a small number of such labels when describing organisations. This paper focusses on two in particular: The extent to which an organisation has a ‘future’ as opposed to a ‘past’ orientation and the extent to which an organisation has an ‘outward’ as opposed to an ‘inward’ orientation. These two scales were selected because they seemed particularly useful when assessing the effectiveness of computer-based system within an organisation.

## 2. "Future" versus "Past" Orientation

A ‘future’ orientated organisation is generally understood to be one in which planning and goal-setting are undertaken in a committed and methodical way, in which new ideas are welcomed and supported, and in which there is little or no concern with past events. In contrast, the ‘past’ oriented organisation is one in which there is little or no commitment to planning and goal-setting, in which there is little or no effort to elicit and support new ideas, and in which there is a pre-occupation with past events (see, for example, [4]). Clearly, most organisations fall somewhere between these two extremes.

## 3. "Inward" versus "Outward" Orientation

Empirical research has shown that it makes sense to speak of the degree to which an organisation is 'inward' as opposed to being 'outward' oriented. (See, for example, [1]). An 'outward' oriented organisation is one in which people have a preoccupation with things happening outside the organisation. Having a preoccupation, for example, with customers' needs and with how these are changing, with emerging legislation and social trends and with developments in technology. In contrast, an 'inward' oriented organisation is one in which people are preoccupied with internal issues, to the exclusion of events and trends in the environment. Obviously, an organisation would quickly get into trouble if it adopted either of these extreme forms of behaviour; an organisation which does not keep an 'eye on the ball' internally will soon run into great difficulty with costs, and so forth; on the other hand, if an organisation is obsessed with internal matters, threats and opportunities in the environment will not be identified, and the future of the organisation will be at risk.

## 4. Level of Sophistication of Management Practices

We now introduce a third framework. In 1975, The Irish Management Institute completed a survey of management practices in manufacturing firms in Ireland (See Ref. [3]). The research included detailed examination of the extent of use of various management techniques in a random sample of 163 companies. One finding that emerged from the survey was the existence of a high, positive, average correlation between the levels of sophistication of management practice in different parts of the organisation. For example, a company which tackled its marketing planning in an effective way was more likely to have a well designed costing system than a company that did not adopt a professional approach to marketing planning. In other words, a firm which made effective use of management techniques in any one area of the organisation was likely to do so across the entire organisation. This finding suggests that one can validly create a single measure of a firm's overall level of 'sophistication', at least in respect to the degree to which the firm effectively uses established management techniques.

We were interested in answering the question "are there additional factors existing in our data?": are there groups of management techniques such that if any one management technique is found to be used effectively in the firm, the other management techniques in the same group are highly likely to be used effectively? This was so; the process of analysis yielded seven such groups of management techniques. We gave a name to each group which seemed to express the underlying connection between the management techniques making up the group. The names were: Financial Management; Marketing Planning; Sales Control; Production Management; The Management of Training and Staff Development; Personnel Management and Planning Practices Generally Across The Organisation.

So, when describing the level of sophistication of management practice in a company, it makes sense to speak of the 'overall' level of sophistication of use of management techniques across the whole organisation. But it also makes sense to take a separate sighting of the effectiveness of management practice under each of the seven headings, e.g., an organisation can, simultaneously, be very sophisticated in its approach to marketing planning and yet have a very unprofessional approach to the management of production and operations.

## 5. Examples of the Use of These Frameworks

We have introduced three frameworks which may be used in describing organisations. Clearly there is some overlap between these three frameworks. For example, one would expect a company which is future orientated to also be externally orientated. Again, almost be definition, one would expect an organisation which is future orientated to be one in which planning is taken very seriously.

These three frameworks were originally developed and used to describe entire organisations; they are equally useful in any assessment of the effectiveness of computer-based systems.

One example of this involves a medium-sized company in the tobacco industry. Since introducing computer technology into the organisation, the emphasis has been on developing systems to help with marketing planning and sales control. The first computerbased system they developed was for sales order processing and despatch control. This was followed by the creation of a system to maintain a 'market research' data base which trapped the details of every sales transaction. This data base is used continuously to monitor changes in the pattern of sales through the 25,000 retail outlets they service. They have put effort into creating sales forecasting routines and have built a number of marketing and financial models which are used for planning. They have also put emphasis on developing systems to help with the financial management of the organisation. So one would describe their computer-based systems as having a very outward and future orientation with a big emphasis on systems to help with marketing and selling. In more recent years, the emphasis in systems development has moved into production and operations management, but this has not been done at the expense of the ongoing development of the former systems.

A second example is a company making and distributing a perishable food product. Here the emphasis is all on the development of systems for financial and production management. Little or no effort has been made in developing systems in the marketing planning and sales control areas. It would be fair to describe the orientation of their computer systems as being very inward and financial and production orientated.

The final example is a company making car accessories. It is a manufacturing subsidiary with little or no marketing. All the emphasis in systems development is in the area of financial management. Little or no resources are being put into the development of systems to help with production management. An examination of the company's systems shows them to be very 'past' orientated with a strong emphasis on financial management.

## 6. Which Orientation is Appropriate?

In the above examples, the orientation of computer-based systems varies greatly. It is natural to ask the question "do these orientations make sense?" Or, more generally, "under what set of circumstances does it make sense for a company's systems to have a particular orientation?"

There is empirical research which can provide some guidance in answering these questions. Godi-walla and his Associates [2] believe that there is a systematic relationship between the nature of the organisational environment and the management issues which preoccupy the chief executive and the top management team. To test this link, they undertook a survey of chief executives of industrial corporations in the United States and Canada. gathering data on the chief executives' perceptions of aspects of the environment relevant to the firm. They also gathered data on the aspects of the organisation's performance which the chief executive felt to be most critical to accomplishing the firm's objectives. When the data had been gathered, each firm was classified into one of four different cells depending on the degree of complexity of the firm's environment and the rate at which the firm's environment was changing. The classification scheme used is shown in Fig. 1.

The chief executives of the firms which operated in the simple, stable environment (Cell 1) experienced the least difficulty in accomplishing the firm's objectives. The stable and predictable environment provided the chief executive with certainty and confidence in managing the business and in formulating strategy. Chief executives of firms operating in complex, dynamic environments (Cell 4) experienced the greatest difficulty in managing their firms towards their goals, had the least understanding about the precise nature of their firms' environments, and had the greatest problems in planning for the future. Chief executives of firms in Cells 2 and 3 fell somewhere between those of firms in Cells 1 and 4.

In describing the orientation of the firms in Cell 1, Godiwalla concluded ... "Cell 1 firms, which were characterised by the least experienced difficulty in accomplishing the corporate objectives and goals, emphasised production ... the simple, stable environment provided little externally generated challenges and, therefore, it permitted the firms to concentrate upon the management of the internal affairs of the firm. The production group of strategies focussed upon the optimisation of the firms' factors of production and operations." In sharp contrast to the Cell 1 firms, the firms in Cell 4 were both internally and externally orientated. Chief executives in these firms placed emphasis on issues related to marketing and financial management and strategy. Chief executives of firms in Cells 2 and 3 had views which lay between those of the chief executives in Cells 1 and 4. They emphasised marketing and financial issues, followed by production issues.

In summary, they concluded that “The nature of the environment, of course, influences the top management’s orientation. For example, the more erratic, unpredictable, dynamic and complex the environment the greater will be the endeavour of the top management to be concerned with developing a faster adaptive and organic organisation with an external orientation for keeping in tune with the external changes affecting the firm. On the other hand, the more stable, predictable and simple the external environment, the greater would be the inward orientation of the various levels of management because the minimal external pressures permit the executives to be internally orientated.”

Godiwalla and his colleagues were interested in the general orientation of the managers in firms within different environments; they were not directly concerned with the orientation of computer-based systems in these organisations. Nevertheless, as computer-based systems are now an integral part of the management process, we believe their findings have great relevance. For example, the findings suggest that in firms operating in a stable, simple environment the emphasis should be on systems which have an 'inward' orientation and which are designed to support management in the production and operations area. On the other hand, in organisations operating in a very unstable, complex environment, the emphasis should be on systems designed to support marketing planning, sales control and financial management. The findings further suggest that in organisations operating in environments between these two extremes, there should be a roughly equal emphasis on systems in the Marketing, Production and Operations and Financial Management areas.

Let us now return to the three examples and see how these ideas can help.

In the tobacco company, the early emphasis was on the development of systems in marketing planning and sales control, followed by financial management. The company was operating in a very fast changing and complex environment. Social attitudes towards cigarette smoking were undergoing great change. Legislation controlling advertising and promotion of tobacco products was being introduced; e.g., the Government introduced a requirement that a health warning appear on each packet of cigarettes. Consumption patterns of cigarettes were changing; the proportion of women who smoked was on the increase while the proportion of men was on the decrease. Whilst the company had achieved leadership in the market, its competitors were working hard to regain market share by launching new brands. In the circumstances, the emphasis in the company on developing systems to help with marketing planning and sales control made sense.

In the second company, a leader in the food industry, the emphasis was very much on systems to help with financial and production management. This company was also experiencing a rapidly changing complex environment. The company was facing intense competition from imported brands, which it could not meet on price because of the considerably lower cost of raw materials in the exporting countries. The company's channels of distribution were also changing very quickly. Small sole traders were disappearing and an ever increasing proportion of the firm's sales were being achieved through supermarkets and chain stores. The convenience food market was rapidly expanding and this was providing both threats and opportunities to the company. In the circumstances, the preoccupation in the organisation with developing systems for financial and production management was probably misplaced. The Marketing Director of the company put it thus: "I really feel the tail is wagging the dog. We have a large computer department here but they don't seem to be doing much for me. For example, my monthly sales analysis is nearly three inches thick. We have to go through this ourselves and summarise it. I know it's probably my fault for not saying exactly what I want but that's far easier to say than to do. When I ask the computer people for a special sales analysis, I get fobbed off and not treated very seriously. I accept that some of the reports I have asked them to produce would create a lot of work for them. But mostly, my requests are pretty reasonable and I know the information I need is on file.

I would like to try out some of the marketing models and sales forecasting methods that I have read about but they don't seem to be interested in helping me, probably because they haven't got anybody who is comfortable with these ideas. I'm seriously thinking of 'doing my own thing' and buying a small computer of my own".

The third company makes accessories for cars. It engages in no marketing itself and manufactures against a plan provided by corporate headquarters. The environment within which the subsidiary operates is fairly simple and stable. So, one would expect the emphasis in systems development to be on manufacturing systems and cost control. In short, one would expect the emphasis to be on systems concerned with 'fine tuning' the local operation. In fact, the emphasis is very 'past orientated' and is almost entirely on systems to help with financial management.

## Conclusion

This paper introduced the notion of the 'orientation' of computer-based systems and tried to show that the orientation which is most appropriate in any particular organisation is determined by the environment within which the organisation operates. So, in assessing the effectiveness of computer-based systems, it makes sense to ask the question: "Is the orientation of our systems appropriate to the nature of our environment?"
