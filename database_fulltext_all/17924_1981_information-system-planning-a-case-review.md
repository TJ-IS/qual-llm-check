# Information System Planning: A Case Review

S. Gill \*

Tel-Aviv University, The Louis Calder Computation Centre, Ramat-Aviv, Tel-Aviv, Israel

Information System Planning (ISP) is a structured approach developed by IRM to assist organizations in establishing a plan to satisfy their short and long term information requirements. The ISP methodology was implemented at Tel Aviv University. A comprehensive plan for the development of a Management Information System (MIS) was derived. This paper presents a review of the process by which the plan was obtained, a discussion of the methodology, and its ramifications.

Keywords: Information System Planning, DSS, ISP, Management Information System, MIS, Information Requirements Analysis, University.

## 1. Introduction

The inadequacy of information systems at Tel-Aviv University had reached a point at which top management ordered a stop to all current development activities until a review was undertaken of all the information system requirements of the university. The lack of timely and accurate data for the strategic planning level, as well as poor service to all the functional units, intensified the determination of top management to seek new tools and methods for the development of Management Information Systems (MIS) for the university. The general consensus was that the advantages derived from efficient systems would justify the expences incurred in their development. At the global level, benefits would be delivered from improved utilization of university resources. Similarly, at the operational level, improved services for education and research would impact the image and the role of the university in the local and federal community.

At this stage International Business Machines Corp. (IBM) proposed the conduct of an Information System Planning (ISP) [2] study under the guidance of IBM representatives, but under the leadership of the Managing Director of the University.

An ISP team headed by the Managing Director was organized. Two IBM representatives were included as ex-officio members of the team; their task was to insure the integrity of the application of the ISP methodology. A control room was allocated and a time schedule set for the study. It was planned that the study should span a period of two and a half months, with the team conducting meetings four days a week. Team members undertook the ISP task in addition to their normal duties, and consequently were under great pressures for the duration of the study. The team members were largely recruited from the administration of the university. Representation of the academic community on the team was spars.

This paper presents a review of the ISP methodology, a description of the experience at Tel-Aviv University, and an evaluation of the approach, its power and deficiency, for use by public non-profit organizations.

## 2. A Review of the ISP Methodology

The Information System Planning (ISP) approach which has been developed by IBM differs from many other structured methods which have been proposed for the planning and development of Management Information Systems (MIS) [5]. The method incorporates, among other things, the understanding of the organization from the viewpoint of top management, the identification of the resources of the organization, and the derivation of the processes that encompass strategic planning, management control, and operational control, associated with each resource. The generation of the overall MIS plan is an evolutionary process consisting of several major activities:

\- Documenting the organization objectives: A statement of the objectives of the organization as seen by top management is solicited. Consequently, the benefits which can be derived from the introduction of management information systems may be measured with respect to the goals of the organization. An essential part of any MIS plan is to provide top management with the tools to evaluate it; even though the data which is presented may be biased, it represents a commitment to achieve the desired results.

\- Defining organization resources: An MIS plan should address the needs of all the levels of management within the organization. The information requirements of the three distinct but concurrent planning and control levels (strategic planning, management control, and operational control) [1] may be determined through the resources they manage. Each resource is managed through planning and control decisions of the three levels. Resource management cuts across organizational boundaries and provides an excellent framework for process analysis. The identification and definition of the resources of an organization is an important cornerstone of the ISP methodology. It should be noted, however, that specific resources to be managed may vary from one organization to the next.

\- Defining organization processes: Processes are defined as groups of logically related decisions and activities required to manage the resources of the organization. Processes are identified and studied regardless of the organization unit responsible for them.

\- Defining data classes: A data class is a category of logically related information. The definition of data classes assists in identifying the data to be managed. Data classes are identified through the previously defined business processes: specifically, what data is created and/or used by each process.

● Analyzing organization/process relationships: Pursuant to the identification and definition of the organization processes, they are related to the organizational structure. An organization/process matrix is developed in which all the processes and the organization entities are arranged. The matrix is completed by indicating the degree to which each organizational entity is involved in each process.

● Analyzing process/data class relationships: Data classes are placed on a matrix opposite processes, and the letters C and U are entered to indicate for each process which data classes are created and which data classes are used, respectively.

\- Defining the information architecture: The understanding of the organization processes and the data required to support them, provides a framework for the analysis of the architecture of the data to be managed to support the processes. Data classes can be logically grouped into data bases. Information systems are the vehicles by which data are inserted into and extracted from the data bases to support the organization processes. Fundamentally, all the data bases and the information systems comprise the MIS. However, information subsystems may be identified. These are a group of processes which are logically related in the use and creation of data classes.

The ISP method is an iterative process by which the classification of processes, data classes, organization structure, and information architecture are refined at each stage in order to attain a consistent view of the organization from the information and decision making point of view. The study is conducted by a team of executive level employees of the organization, working under the leadership of a top level executive sponsor. The team operates in a highly structured environment, with a fixed time table, and ample resources (a control room, wall charts, etc.). The study is conducted according to a pre-arranged work plan (which is carefully documented in a control book) and includes a schedule of interviews, a description of the organization, processes, and data classes

An integral part of the approach are the executive interviews. These interviews provide the team with the necessary information for systems analysis and planning. The interviews contribute to the validation of the analysis and a review of the definitions of processes and data classes which are compiled by the team members, as well as creating a rapport between team members and the executives of the organization. The interviews help ensure completeness of the processes associated with each resource and of the data classes, spot redundancies, and clarify responsibilities within the organization. During the interviews, problems are solicited. Subsequently, problems are divided into two categories, those that have to do with information system support and those that do not. The assessment of the ramifications of information problems is a major factor in determining the order in which the MIS plan is implemented.

The final phase of the ISP project is to review the information systems function within the organization, the current information systems, and the available technology. Successful implementation of the plan is dependent on an accurate evaluation of the usability of the current systems. Slight improvements in the application of current systems may allow the organization to defer the creation of new systems until more critical subsystems have been developed. The adoption of a uniform set of standards and tools, both for providing information for top management and for data manipulation, is inherent to the approach. Finally, an action plan is proposed which outlines recommended follow-on projects, resources, schedules, and interactions of the projects.

A written report and a presentation of ISP are prepared in order to obtain the commitment of top management to implementing the recommendations of the study.

## 3. Implementation of ISP

The ISP method was implemented at Tel-Aviv University in order to develop an overall plan for information systems for the university. The first task was to obtain a precise and concise definition of the goals of the university: the acquisition and distribution of knowledge. Measures of performance were defined for the dual roles of a university: education and research. Ambiguity in the definition of the measures complicated the task of determining the benefits which could be derived from improved information systems. Given the university goals, the ISP team could identify the resources and the services of the university.

Six main resources were identified:

1. Students;

2. Human Resources;

3. Finance;

4. Facilities:

5. Documented Knowledge; and

6. Public Relations.

Similarly, the two main services of the university were delineated: Education and Research.

The process of identifying resources was iterative; as an example, it was only at an advanced stage of the study that the team acknowledged documented knowledge as a resource. For each resource and service, the processes associated with decision making at the strategic, tactical, and operational level were identified and defined. The strategic and planning processes for all the resources were grouped together under three separate headings: Policy, Long-term Planning, and Annual Planning. Formally, the division of the planning activity into two separate categories represented a departure from the methodology. A fourth heading was generated for the delineation of the measurement and control processes associated with each of the policy and planning activities. A convention was adopted for each of the operational level processes, by which measurement and control were imbedded in the process. The operational level processes were retained under the headings of the different resources and services. Over eighty processes were identified.

Concurrently, the organization members involved with the processes were identified. A three member scale of involvement was adopted: partial involvement, heavy involvement, and responsible/decision-maker. The classification of involvement in each process by the various functionaries was verified during the interviews.

The matrix depicting the relationships between the organization and the processes set the stage for the interviews. Over fifty organization members were interviewed ranging from the president of the university through deans, faculty administrators, department chairmen, and administrative personnel. The interviews were duly recorded and summarized for subsequent analysis.

Upon completion of the interviews, the process of identifying data classes and their definition was initiated. During the duration of this activity, the identification of processes, their definitions, and the relevant organization structure were continuously updated and refined. Less than a hundred data classes were identified. They were grouped into three categories: planning, measurement and control, and operational. Under planning, various plans and standards were identified as playing a central role in the strategic planning process of the university. In addition to the formal plans, the ISP team acknowledged the existence of informal plans as depicted by various resolutions and regulations appearing in the minutes of various committee meetings. Three data classes were identified under the heading of measurement and control: performance vs. planning, statistics, and measures. Of all the different groups of data classes, those associated with the measurement and control activity are the least refined. The operational data classes were grouped under the resource umbrella, in a repetition of the procedure followed in the case of the grouping of processes. A relationship matrix between data classes and processes was derived. For each process, a determination was made as to which data classes were being used (U) and which were created (C).

The relationship matrix provided the framework for the information system architecture. Given that the whole system is viewed as one MIS, it was deemed necessary to identify sub-systems for the development and the implementation of the plan. A sub-system is a group of processes with a logical connection between them, i.e. partners in the use and creation of data classes.

The network, that was thus defined, formed the basis for the analysis of the problems which had been summarized during the interviews. The problems not associated with information systems were noted and referred to top management for their action. All the other problems were categorized according to their sources and their ramifications.

The analysis of the problems, coupled with the analysis of the current information systems, formed the basis for determining the priorities in the development of information subsystems. An action plan was developed as required by the methodology, its content reflecting the opinions expressed during the interviews. The total effort was documented in a predetermined format and comprised the information system master plan which was presented to the Executive Committee of the University. The plan was adopted with some minor modifications. In particular, the order in which the development effort was presented was modified to accommodate political forces within the university.

## 4. Evaluation of the MIS Plan

The MIS plan and its accompanying action plan has become a frame work and a term of reference by which the development efforts of the university are evaluated. Obviously, the strength of the plan lies in its approach: viewing the university as a whole and applying the systems approach to the study of it.

Unfortunately, herein also lies the weakness of the plan: for a structured top-down approach to the analysis of a system to be effective, it must be accomplished in a limited time span. Time constraints prohibited in-depth consideration and evaluation of issues and problems. The analysis and planning of information systems with the ISP approach allows two levels of MIS definition. Consequently, the product of the study, the MIS plan, may appear to be shallow, and does not reflect the amount of effort put into the formation of the final product. Moreover, the quest for unity in the presentation obliterates the details and the refinements achieved in the analysis.

Overall, in the development of the MIS plan, the contention between two contending forces remained unresolved: on the one hand, the desire for wholeness (presenting the system as a whole and escaping the folly of too much detail), and on the other hand, omission of important details in the consideration of the whole. Therein lies the dilemma: One cannot understand the whole without some knowledge of the parts. So from where should a good study embark?

From the whole, or from the parts, or perhaps from both (intermittently)? This issue was not resolved in the study conducted at Tel-Aviv University and the result, the MIS plan, reflects this ambiguity.

## 5. A criticism of the ISP Approach

A structured approach to systems analysis may result in a distorted point of view of the world, i.e., Weltanschauungen. Most of the "real world" organizations have very little structure, and are in a sense "wicked" (they are not amenable to formal methods of analysis). However, organizations tend to have more structure, both formal and informal, at the bottom of the hierarchy. Consequently, a structured approach may tend to concentrate on the structured end of the organization. Overall, the tendency to focus on the processes at the operational level and the sub-information systems thus derived will distort the role of the approach as it was presented to top management, namely, as a tool which would allow the definition of information system requirements from the viewpoint of top management (as well as at the operational level). Inherently, the system failed to provide the necessary analysis for the requirements of top management.

## Cliches such as:

Most of the information requirements of top management can be met if, at the operational level, the data exists for their own needs.

\- What top management needs is a Decision Support System (DSS) that is nurtured from data extracted and deformed from the operational level.

General truths cannot assist in the correct choice of tools for DSS that would best suit the needs of the organization. With little additional effort, it may have been possible to generate the information requirements for the strategic planning process within the university.

Structured methods for the analysis of the information requirements of an organization, although very popular, tend to oversimplify the problems in order to generate a consistent plan. Structured interviews, in particular, constrain the discussion to the format of the report, thus contributing to the distortion of the image of the required system. A free form analysis, on the other hand, depends on the maturity of both the organization and the people conducting the study. The methods for the analysis and the planning of information systems should be chosen to match the abilities of the organization, thus enhancing the probability of success.

Finally, a constant danger accompanies each and every team analysis effort: over time, the team has a tendency to generate an informal language in use among its members, and they thus create a barrier between themselves and the rest of the organization. The lack of objective criticism during the process may cause dissonance between the conclusions reached by the team in its deliberations, and the expectations of the organization. In particular, policy recommendations of the team should be carefully reviewed by top management prior to their presentation to the organization at large to avoid embarrassment all around. Such review will not detract from the quality of the product and will enhance the likelihood of implementation.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

The ISP approach, while far from being a panacea, has resulted in a precise and concise definition of the information system requirements of the university. While the scope of its applicability may be limited to the operational control of the organization, its contribution to the understanding of the decision making processes and the information requirements of the functional units justifies the amount of effort expanded in the development of the MIS plan.

Unfortunately, the “hero syndrome” [3] still prevails: tools which are best suited for specific tasks, are being “sold” as a “cure for all maladies”. Specifically, the MIS plan did not generate a detailed definition of the information requirements of the strategic planning level of the organization. In this instance, the plan set forth general truths that are impossible to verify and are not particular to the university. Consequently, top management will not find in the MIS plan an information system that will satisfy its needs (as had been originally promised). Overselling the virtues of their products remains a vice among computer professionals. Notwithstanding this caveat, the management of Tel-Aviv University is very pleased with the product of the ISP process.
