# Computers, Management and Organization: Reflections on a Pilot Study

E. Bartezzaghi \*\*, C. Ciborra \*, A. De Maio \*\*, P. Maggiolini + and P. Romano \*\*

\* Dipartimento di Organizzazione Aziendale, Università della Calabria, Cosenza (Italy)

\*\*Istituto RSO, Milano and Istituto di Elettrotecnica ed Elettronica, Politecnico di Milano (Italy)

\* Centro Studi sull'Ingeneria dei Sistemi di Elaborazione delle Informazioni, CNR, Politecnico di Milano and Dipartimento di Organizzazione Aziendale, Università della Calabria, Cosenza (Italy)

$^{++}$ Istituto di Elettrotecnica ed Elettronica, Politecnico di Milano, Piazza L. da Vinci 32, I-20133 Milano, Italy.

Please send all correspondence to this address.

Results are presented of a pilot study dealing with the interaction between computers, management, and organization structures: a study carried out to explore the computerization phenomenon in a small sample of manufacturing firms. A short survey of the literature introduces the perspectives and objectives of the field study. Two main research outcomes are presented. Firstly, a theoretical scheme of analysis, based on the control system of administrative processes, is considered as a means of evaluating computer impact on the organization. Secondly, a set of specific hypotheses is introduced in order to analyse the empirical data.

The following interactions between computers and organizational structures have been examined (while considering the firm as an open socio-technical system): technology and environment, organizational structure, automatized applications, and the computerization process.

Keywords: Impact of Edp on management and organization, information and decision processes, computerization process, power relationships.

This work was subdivided as follows: A. De Maio and P. Maggiolini sections 1, 2 and 6, E. Bartezzaghi and C. Ciborra sections 3 and 4; C. Ciborra and P. Romano section 5 and the Appendix.

## 1. Introduction

In 1975 a research project was started by our group at the Politechnic of Milan. The research attempted to investigate the interaction between the organization and its computer-based information systems. Specifically, the main objectives were:

\- To describe the state of business and administrative computerization in the manufacturing industry;

\- To identify and verify significant relations among characteristic variables in the computerization process.

Paolo Romano (Chemical engineering degree at the Politecnico of Milan) is researcher at the Politecnico. His main fields of interest are now in theoretical studies in organization (including qualitative mathematical models applied to organizational behaviour) organizational methods of analysis and on managerial aspects of computerization.

For the survey, both structured questionnaires and open interviews were used. Three industries in the Milan area were examined: food, chemical, and (partially) electro-mechanical. Personnel totals of the (more than 20) firms interviewed range from less than 50 to more than 4000; the mean number of employees is about 900. The average state of computerization is approximately that of the “second stage” level of R.L. Nolan’s model [20].

The problems investigated are clearly multi-dimensional and cannot be given a definitive treatment using our limited empirical material collected.

To be sure, data gathered in our pilot study regard the systematic study of about twenty organizational settings and cannot be extended beyond that reality. But those data were sufficient to unfold a rich "Gedankenexperiment" consisting of analytical frameworks, a coherent set of general and specific hypotheses and their interpretation in front of our (limited) empirical evidence.

We think that the presentation in full of such experiment to the reader may be more useful than an episodic and loose discussion of twenty single stories of computerization in a sample of firms.

## 2. The Analytical Model

## 2.1. Three Points of View

The basic hypothesis of our research is that a strong interaction exists between organizational systems and the computer. This interaction is assumed to be dynamic and in both directions: from computer to organization and vice versa.

Much literature today focuses only on the dynamic process of organizational change (effect) while assuming that it depends upon computers introduction, implementation, and usage (cause). To clarify our opposition to this view, it is useful to summarize the results of various studies that examined the organizational impact of EDP technology. The contribution of representative authors can be placed into three groups:

The first group introduces the hypothesis that the computer is a more efficient mean of pursuing predetermined objectives; i.e., management strategy is fixed, and the computer is one of the means of implementing strategy. In this context some authors [1-3, 32-34], consider the correlation between technological evolution and the development of EDP application to be explained by Fig. 1.

The second class [4-6,8,9,12,35-39] considers technology as a rigidly influential factor. The structure of the machine (hardware and software) heavily conditions the organization according to the model of Fig. 2.

The third class [6,7,17,19] points out and describe the interaction between the computer and the organization. Here, the process assumes that the computer is chosen and used according to objectives, goals, and constraints of the antecedent organizational structure; then it is assumed that the computer conditions both the formal and informal organization structure. Finally the technological subsystem and the social system modify each other producing organizational coherence. However, the new organizational pattern is not necessarily more efficient than the preceding one.

Our model deals with the decision process of a generic organizational unit with respect both to the information network and the formal and informal organization structure. We adopted this decision model because it seems the most suited to study macrostructural changes within organizations (see [40]).

## 2.2. Conceptual Framework

We consider an organization is an “open socio-technical system” [26] in which:

(i) Organizational subsystems (divisions, department, etc.) are identified by: a set of homogeneous characteristics (e.g., their task, technology, professional skills and output); loose coupling; delegation of responsibility for decisions and actions; and a set of control and coordination rules.

(ii) Each organizational subsystem can be interpreted as a “control system” on a definite set of performed activities or processes (Fig. 3). In this control system two aspects must be emphasized:

a) the decision maker's "autonomous standards" can act in conformance with or in opposition to organizational goals. In any event, they interfere with the objectives/constraints structure defined by the organization (or more generally, by higher hierarchical levels).

b) the existing feed-back from the output to organizational and social subsystems can bias the decision maker's behaviour (through negative and positive sanctions).

(iii) One specific process can be interpreted as a set of output, input, and information technologies.

(iv) The decision rules for each subsystem take account of both explicit and implicit constraints and objectives. These can refer to the organization as a whole, to a specific subsystem, or to a decision maker. The decision maker then operates on the basis of:

\- a feasible and consistent set of objectives and constraints;

• a knowledge of process laws;

![](/api/attachments/8WSFN7A6/fulltext/images/cc756e4b5078893f7c4ef574cb1975047a506cf2756c80660e041bfd3254c1d5.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Process Control System.

\- a knowledge and/or forecast of relevant variables affecting the process.

(v) The utility of and/or necessity for delegating decisions and actions depends specifically on the degree of turbulence of the environment, the knowledge of process laws, the response time of the control system, and the extent to which rules and techniques are known and can be transmitted.

(vi) The less the rules and techniques are known and transmitted and/or the more turbulent the environment, the more important the "process memory" becomes. Its task is to accumulate theoretical and empirical knowledge of the process under control (Fig. 4). From a conceptual point of view, it is then possible to divide the decision process into two phases: the first (memory) sets the rules (references, procedures, norms, standards) to the second (operator). But the operator maintains a certain level of discretion or autonomy. More precisely there is decisional autonomy in (a) rule interpretation; and (b) action implementation. Due to this, it is important to understand the operator's autonomous standards.

(vii) The place where the process memory is located is of importance in determining where the "real power" lies. The decision maker who has "real power" can control the results, while others cannot. This implies different "visibility" relations between decisions and results.

(viii) Organizational changes represent changes in the real power location.

(ix) Decision makers can achieve their goals, only if they have a high quality process memory.

It is now possible to interpret the impact of computerization on organizational changes. Data processing modifies both the quantity and quality of information (and simultaneously the process knowledge) to a different extent for different decision makers. Computerization can change formal and real control rules and outcomes for a specific subsystem (e.g. by improving techniques and reducing costs), and can substantially change the real power distribution.

Fig. 4. Process Control System with 'Memory' and 'Operator'.

In most cases information system design assumes that the computer can play the role of a rationalization tool with respect to the decision process and structure. The hypotheses are:

a) an organizational separation of memory from operator is obtainable and desirable;

b) all autonomous standards are "noise";

c) autonomous standards at top hierarchical levels do not exist, or they coincide with organizational goals and objectives;

d) it is possible to overcome the lack of knowledge by increased data flow.

Finally a particular “process” must be considered:

the EDP specialist can be viewed as a decision maker with respect to the data processing subsystem. EDP's autonomous standards, or more specifically, the EDP culture and conceptual frame of reference, must then be considered. If our hypotheses are correct, the more the automation is diffused, the more the EDP unit increases, not only via the increasing number of EDP specialists but also due to the diffusion into the organization of the conceptual reference, approach, and culture of "informatics".

## 2.3. A Master Hypothesis

Different decision makers interact in the computerization process; they are either internal or external to the organization. The goal of internal decision makers is to redistribute internal power in a favourable manner and achieve higher effectiveness and efficiency with respect to organizational goals. The EDP

Group assumes particular importance once the computerization process has started; they therefore have a strong cultural influence on the design. Outside the organization, the computer manufactures attempts profit maximization (if possible, in both short and long term). To achieve this goal, the manufactures' technology may change, regardless of the users' situation and needs. Thus computer manufactureres attempt to control and sell their solution by dominating the cultural role in the EDP field. Meanwhile, the users normally have neither the scientific knowledge nor the professional skills to verify and evaluate the technical assumptions. Furthermore, it is significant that no effective-methodology has yet been established for the economic and efficiency evaluation of computer systems [25].

These remarks lead to the master hypothesis:

The computerization process must consider the objectives and constraints derived from the goals of the organization. At the same time, organizational change is initiated so that computerization is affected by the internal and external decision makers' goals. The stability and integration of the new structure requires subsequent expansion of the EDP role (measured by hardware size and sophistication, number and quality of computerized procedures, organizational level of the EDP unit, etc.). This self-reinforcing process can be accelerated, delayed, or interrupted depending on specific contingencies $^{1}$ .

## 3. Research Methodology

Early in our project, we examined the state of similar research in Italy and noticed that it lacked data relating to the Italian situation (for an exception, see [8,9]). We therefore carried out a number of open interviews with dp specialists, dp managers, consultants etc. to discover answers to the following:

\- What are the decision processes in the acquisition and use of major hardware?;

-- What are the successes and failures in dp applications, and what are the causes?;

\- What are the education problems of the dp specialist and users?

The interviews and a review of the literature were the input for the design of the questionnaire for the field survey. The questionnaire was divided into three sections to be administered to middle managers, top managers and F DP managers respectively.

Because of the complexity of the questionnaire and the many facets of the phenomenon to be investigated we decided to use it as a support for structured interviews in a sample of manufacturing companies in the Milan area (as of spring 1975). Fig. 5 shows data for the three industries (food, chemical and electromechanical) from which the initial sample was drawn.

The response rate was 60% for food, 40% for chemical and 20% for electro-mechanical industry.

Collected questionnaires were coded to allow use of existing statistical packages (SPSS [23]) and 245 variables were defined. Automatic data analysis included:

– description statistics for all variables (absolute and relative frequencies);

\- relationships between two or more variables (cross tabulation);

\- correlation analysis;

\- correspondence analysis [22].

It -is important to notice a few methodological caveats, before examining the results.

Though the initial aim of the empirical research was to test hypotheses using data of a representative sample of companies in all industries of the Milan area, time and resource constraints curbed that ambition: the discussion of the data gathered to follow in the next sections regards the non-random sample of the 24 companies. We had to give up hypothesis testing strategy. What the data are really about are 24 systematic case studies, and the domain of validity of our conclusions is limited to those case studies.

However, we present the results of the empirical survey under the unusual report format of hypothesis and their subsequent discussion (and confirmation limited to the companies examined), because we think that it is useful to present to the reader, as an outcome of the research, the entire set of detailed hypotheses we developed. He or she may be willing to include them in a future, more comprehensive research endeavour on the same subject (which is still badly needed today). We also think that the hypotheses and limited testing may trigger reflections and further discussion by practitioners, managers and specialists regarding computerization issues, even if our conclusions cannot be labelled as scientifically "true" or "false".

## 4. Hypotheses and Main Results

Our basic hypothesis is that a strong interaction between computer and organizational system exists. Certain groups of elements identify specific aspects of this interaction. These include: technology and procedures; environment, organization, and the process of computer introduction and application.

We therefore grouped items into four sets. However these sets only model reality by attempting to impose an order on the great number of interrelated elements.

1. Technology and automated procedures. The technology of data-processing was assumed to be an independent macro-variable dependent on the specific hardware and software. Here we endeavoured to build an index of the technological complexity of the system in order to observe its relationship to other elements (such as automatic processes, organizational structure of the EDP department, etc.) and to evaluate the impact of technological change. The number and range of the automated procedures in each firm was included here.

2. Environment. The organization is inextricably woven into and conditioned by the environment. There are different “regions”: economic, technological and social. In our survey we treated environ-

ment, in two different ways. Firstly, we limited our empirical study to a definite industrial area (the Milan district), which is relatively homogeneous in facilities, density of branches of computer manufacturers, technical and scientific milieu, etc. Secondly, we included some variables which distinguish properties market relationship of the firm such as size.

3. Organization (structure and management) The effect of socio-organizational factors on the use of technology can be assessed by isolating the characteristic elements. Our first subgroup relates to the formal organization: its pattern; location of EDP Department; and existence of a specific Organization and Procedures Service. The other subgroup takes into account informal factors, such as: managerial policy; ability of the EDP specialist; relationships between specialists and users; the role modification of employees, etc.

4. The computerization Process. It is possible to split the automation process (application of EDP technology) into a number of decision stages: choice to shift to automation, choice of the computer mode, planning the application, creation of the EDP Department, etc. The process can be considered as the "history". Relevant factors are: the explicit objectives of the automation process; intervening actors (as individuals or in coalitions); time modes and degree of user participation and the role of computer vendors and consultants.

## 4.1. The Hypotheses

The following hypotheses were then derived from the master hypothesis:

1. A tendency to underuse the data processing system may exist. Oversizing of the data processing system should be allowed;

2. Increase in system size should not necessarily correspond to high usage level;

3. A relationship exists between the range of automated procedures and the specific industry of the firm; this means that automation follows some efficiency standards. However, the criteria which characterize the selling policies of the mainframe vendors (proposed applications depend on the industry) may introduce rigidity and cause the design to overlook the company specific problems, (causing a gap between critical administrative need and the automatic solutions).

4. There is a correlation between the complexity of the system, EDP expenditures, and size of the company (possibly due to the sales policy of computer manufacturers);

5. There is a strong intervention of external actors in the automation process;

6. The degree of utilization of the EDP system is conditioned by organizational factors (rigidity of the organizational power structure, conflicting interests, etc.).

7. The degree of utilization is linked to user participation during project and implementation phases.

## 4.2. Main Results

Specific hypotheses are now presented, together with the main findings. The statistical techniques used have been carefully adapted to the limited sample of firms available (refer to Section 3 for methodological caveats).

## 4.2.1. Technology and Automated Procedures

The data on macrovariable technology deals with the physical configuration of the system (model, CPU size, peripheral units, etc.), software for data transmission and application packages, automated processes using the computers, technical characteristics of the more important procedures (processed data, operating modes, memory, number and size of files, number of programs, processing frequency, response time, etc.).

The rent (only in two instances was the system bought) and size of the central unit (CPU) were used to measure the degree of complexity of the system.

An index of complexity is defined, considering the various technical aspects of the procedures. The index of automation takes into consideration the number of automated procedures and the 'type' of procedure. Procedures have been classified by considering their "structure", (i.e. the kind of processing, the input/output characteristics, the degree of freedom in interpreting the results, and the sensitivity to interference).

The index given relates to an a priori valuation of the automation level. It is necessary to define an index of use of automation, which is not a technological indicator but which takes account of results at an organizational level. The new index is based on the following elements:

\- computer use according to top management; aid in solving (critical) strategic problems; aid in critical operational areas; use of the results by management etc;

\- relevant changes introduced by the use of the computer in the more important activities (amount of data, quality, speed and reliability of the processing, etc. as evaluated by management);

\- comprehensive evaluation made by the responsible user: advantages and disadvantages of automation, costs, time, reliability, completeness, rigidity of the procedures, etc.; existence of cost evaluation methods, etc.

The hypotheses discussed in this section are:

Hypothesis 1. Correspondence should not exist between either the degree of technical complexity of the system and the level of technical complexity of the procedures on the level of automation and actual use of procedures.

This leads to four specific sub-hypotheses:

(i) when increasing the technical complexity of the system, the level of technical complexity of the procedures should also increase.

(ii) The degree of technical complexity does not correspond to the level of automation.

(iii) Higher technical complexity does not correlate to the level of use.

(iv) The level of automation does not correlate to the level of use.

Sub-hypothesis (i) was confirmed in our sample, while sub-hypothesis (ii) has not. In fact, a connection seems to exist between the technical complexity of the EDP system and the degree of automation, both as regards the number of procedures and the range. Sub-hypothesis (iii) seems to be valid in the sample.

We found that in cases of low technical complexity, there is a tendency towards high usage, while in systems with medium complexity, the level of use tends to be low; this trend is even more pronounced for high complexity. Sub-hypothesis (iv) too finds empirical support; although the level of high use does correspond to a high level of automation, the opposite was not found.

The lack of a correlation between the level of automation and the degree of technical complexity on level of effective use reinforces our assumption of a tendency towards sub-use of the EDP system. This leads to the confirmation of:

Hypothesis 2. A high level of effective use does not necessarily correspond to a greater effort in the development of the EDP system.

The "structure" of the automated procedures was

then examined to investigate:

Hypothesis 3. There is no 'best way' to design a procedure.

Key features of an organization influence the way its procedures are implemented. The most frequent procedure type is "bi-polar". It links the EDP service to a specific organizational unit sending and receiving data. In the "series" procedure, the flow of data goes from an operative unit (wages, warehouse) through EDP to the relevant management department (administration, sales, purchasing, etc.). This is also frequent.

In such cases, the EDP system seems aid in control along pre-existing hierarchical lines, the information flow coming up from low management to functional management.

In contrast, “integrated” procedures (where data from various units are collected into a single output for top marvement) are few; this structure can be found in companies with a high level of automation, with substantial user participation.

Regarding the hypothesis of a connection between types of procedure and organizational methods of implementation this seems to exist. On the one hand, we have procedures with different technical characteristics, being applied by different organizational areas, to solve diverse management problems, but having the same structure in the same company, while on the other hand, we have different procedures solving to the same management problem through different structures in different companies.

The fact which thus results is the existence of different organizational ways of using the computer independent from (or more important than) the technical requirements of each procedure and of EDP technology.

Thus we have organizational choice in decisions on:

\- input/output of data;

\- allocation of responsibilities and controls in managing this and the procedure;

\- the intervention of EDP specialists and users during the implementation stages.

Considering now the structure of the procedures and their implementation, and linking them to automation level and level of actual use of automation, the following conclusions result:

\- there is no procedure which directly links top management with the EDP service: top management does not use EDP directly;

\- more "participation" on the part of the user tends to result in a high usage of automated procedures and a lack of conflict with EDP;

\- the realization of complex procedures (with inter-functional integration) seems to depend on the participation of the user in the various phases of implementation and management;

\- the structure of the procedures (elementary flow of information and methods of control of input/output data) seems to be similar in each company, varying with different applications. The same procedures have different structures in different companies; the automation process seems to play a determining role in this.

From this, we note that the actual use of automation and the structural characteristics of the automation processes are correlated “internal” organizational factors.

## 4.4.2.Environment

Two hypotheses were examined:

Hypothesis 1. The industry (a variable which defines the production and marketing characteristics of the company) has an influence on the range of automated procedures.

Data of Fig. 6 shows shows that:

\- in the food industry there is greater use of statistical and accounting procedures;

\- in the electro-mechanical industry accounting and control procedures are more frequent;

\- in the chemical-pharmaceutical sector, there is more use of decision models;

\- use of statistics procedures increase in going from the chemical to the electro-mechanical to the food industry; the reverse is true for complex decision models.

-- in the food industry, high percentages are observed for procedures in administrative, sales, and stock control areas;

\- in the chemical industry, production purchasing, cost control and budget procedures are more frequent. Production, cost control, research and development application are prevalent in the electro-mechanical sector.

Investigation of the relationship between critical areas of company administration and automated procedures has not given significant results at the strategic level. However, there seems to be some correspondence (by industry) between procedures and areas most frequently labelled “critical” at operational level; these are: (a) sales control and finance for the food industry; (b) sales, inventory and production control for the chemical-pharmaceutical sector; and (c) quality control in the electro-mechanical sector.

Hypothesis 2. The technical complexity and the type of automated procedures depend on the size of the company.

The data seems to confirm the first part on technical complexity, while no clearcut indications were obtained on the second. But specifically, relating the size of the CPU (kbytes) and the size of the company, a high correlation coefficient (0.81) is obtained; this also occurs between the rent of the EDP system and the turnover (0.67). These correlations refer to 15 cases.

## 4.4.3. Impact on the Organization

Here, the following questions were investigated:

\- Is EDP a decision variable in the management of the organization?

\- What are the consequences of computer usage on employees?

In two firms, the EDP Department changed its location from a line to a staff function; in two other cases the reverse change took place: these apparently contradict the trend of Nolan's model [20].

Another organizational aspect of EDP activity concerns the existence and role of a Management Services group. In small firms this group does not exist separately and its functions are accomplished by the EDP Department: the computer takes there the task of "management and organizational science" [30]. In medium and large firms the relationship between these is strengthened by the introduction of the computer.

Finally middle managers were interviewed in order to clarify their attitude towards the computer. A uniform feeling emerges: "subordinate acceptance" of "technological innovation and of EDP experts. Although the idea of automation comes mainly from outside the unit, users co-operate during the design and implementation phases.

EDP personnel consider their relationship with the user in a very technology-oriented fashion.

The answers regarding the existence of conflicts resulting from EDP application vary from one manager to another. Often, when top managers admit the existence of conflicts, middle managers of the same company say that "everything is all right". Considering these contradictory answers, conflicts seem to be present in at least twelve firms. They apparently occur because of extensive organizational modifications caused by EDP. In three firms, conflicts were so widespread that computerization decelerated and remained at a very low level.

Conflicts are often associated with “too intensive” intervention of the EDP Department in the design and implementation stages. The lowest level of conflicts is found where users and specialists have the opportunity to cooperate fully during the design stage.

## 5. The Computerization Process

The company's new information system involves a social change process: computerization. The study of this process is motivated by various hypotheses:

1. The history of how the computer is integrated into the firm is an important factor that may explain the current form of the information system;

2. The effectiveness and efficiency of the new system seem to be largely determined by the decisions and actions carried out by the various actors during the introduction and application phases;

3. The role of the EDP specialists may be better focussed by analysing their modes of participation: (The same argument applies for the link between computer vendors and EDP personnel).

4. Changes in the EDP service location are useful indicators of the power changes.

## 5.1. The Data Gathered and the First Hypothesis

In analysing the data, we hoped to describe the global characteristics of computerization in the firms, keeping in mind the fact that the information referred mainly to the formal aspects of organizational processes.

We based our effort on the identification of an interesting set of actors, whose behaviour is studied during a series of decision phases, during the introduction of the computer into the firm. Computerization may be considered as a set of activities directed to change of the work procedures and control mechanism in specific organizational sub-system. The result of such a change is the implementation of a new computer-based organization model and a new power distribution.

Two relevant "decision maker" classes exist:

1) external (vendors, manufacturers and consultants)

2) internal (top management, middle management, and EDP specialists).

One subdivision of the computerization process is logical-chronological based, as shown in fig. 10. In the initial phase, the stimuli to create a computerized information system are perceived and the initial objectives are formulated. The computer acquisition/installation process follows. Then the application phase begins. The evaluation of the results with respect to the initial objectives might result in the formulation of new objectives and to subsequent modifications, and so on.

## 5.2. Results

5.2.1. The EDP Department and the Role of External Actors

The results of the study provide a picture of the normal process of introduction of a computer into the firm. The pattern which emerges is characterized by the split in the behaviour of external and internal actors. There is significant (i.e. heavy) intervention of decision makers external to the firm. The EDP department behaves in a highly differentiated way from the other units of the firm. EDP specialists seem to constitute an homogeneous group directed by external forces (the suppliers). In fact, there is a strong affinity with the suppliers in the “technical” phases of training, installation, etc. Such high differentiation of the EDP unit and low integration into the firm (with a high rate of conflict between specialist and user) suggests low integration of computer technology in the company.

The role of external consultants is in providing know-how on the managerial and organizational processes. They provide a coordination of knowledge on the processes of the firm which exist with a high degree of dispersion throughout the user departments. There is a similarity in the behaviour of consultants and EDP specialists; both provide a service tied to expertise.

If the major objective of the manufacturer is summarized as short and medium term profit maximization, their interest is evident in maintaining open and capture of the educational training centre in the EDP is a major goal. Due to the efficiency of such a channel the EDP specialist acts as a internal but heterodirected consultant, as far as the technological and organizational solutions her or she proposes are concerned. Through the consulting and educational channel, and the consulting expertise of the EDP men, technological solutions pass from outside to inside the firm; often the solutions are independent of the problems and the real user requirements.

The crucial role of the “educational chain” which relates the internal and external environments (manufactureres, EDP personnel training, creation of EDP service) points out the importance of studying the influence of outside actors on the computerization process.

## 5.2.2. The EDP Department and the Internal Actors

As far as the internal environment of the firm is concerned, the analysis of collected data indicates that the organizational location of the EDP service (and the change in its location) is the result of organizational dynamics and decisional behaviour of the intervening actors. Four typical situations can be postulated (see Fig. 1 $^{1}$ ); some have been reported in the sociological and management literature, others have hardly been mentioned.

1. The existence of a coalition between top management and the EDP department; EDP is under the direct control of top management from the beginning. In firms of this type, top management appears to be in a position to fund and define a project and the relevant course of action affecting control of the technology;

2. A lasting agreement between one line department (in most cases the administrative one) and EDP: this is mentioned by several writers as "provincialism" of the EDP; i.e., computerization as a phenomenon of local interest within the firm.

These two situations are characterized by stable behaviour (little or no conflict), while the other two are “unstable”:

3. Poor coordination between the formal organization chart dependence of the EDP department on some line department and the real control due to heavy participation by top management. This is an unstable organizational structure which ultimately brings about change to the formal structure when the EDP department is placed under the direct control of top management (see [20]).

4. Poor lines of organization between the formal dependence of the EDP department on the top management and effective control and participation in the computerization process by a line department. This situation generates a condition through which the control of the EDP department is transferred to the line department participated most in the introduction of computer technology. This type of change has not been considered by most studies, especially in cases of parallel expansion of the technological system.

We can now compare our results with the set of hypotheses. With respect to the initial goal of the research (i.e. to relate computer application with organizational change), some results are surprising:

contingent factors, such as the type of task and the relative product/market matrix, the turbulence of the environment, the size of the firm, the technology of the production process, etc., do not seem to fully explain the characteristics of the information systems within the firm, except for the correlation between the size of the firm and the size of the EDP system and in the areas of application compared to the operational efficiency of the firm.

\- factors bound solely to the technology of the system do not appear to allow for better explanation. Even though certain systems are more complex than others and there are different levels of automation of procedures, the traditional “three phases” of the EDP technology (“electronic clerk”, integrated system and MIS [12]) do not seem to discriminate either the various organizational stages of the process of computerization, or the various aspects of the information systems.

For example, there are firms (small and big in size) in which top management intervenes from the beginning into the computerization process by centralization of control of the EDP resources (be they considerable or modest); in the “three phases” model this is only expected to occur in highly complex and integrated procedures (MIS).

## Some results show a possible explanation

\- The structure of the information system (type, number and structure of the computerized procedures) varies from firm to firm, independent of the type of application or hardware and software.

The structure of the information system is strongly related to the phases of planning, implementation, and operation of the procedures and to the form of user participation or the involvement of EDP specialists. Consequently, it seems appropriate to analyze the organizational changes which precede and follow the introduction of the innovation. (These must be examined in the light of the relations political) that are established between the various actors around the application, considering the structure of the technology or the role of other factors (which are constraints on the collective action).

If this approach is empirically sound, its adoption has important consequences on the interpretation of the "impact" of the information systems on the firm. For example, the classical issue of centralization v. decentralization of information and decision flows appears to be irrelevant. Correct evaluation of the required "degree of decentralization" of EDP systems should include the analysis of the behaviour of the decision makers, of their strategies and 'games' governing the organizational change.

## 6. Conclusion

It is difficult to establish definitive statements, but some interesting conclusions may be drawn. First, our data seems to confirm the importance of the “interaction” concept: in order to evaluate computer impact it is important to examine the “path of computer application”, otherwise one may attribute positive or negative effects to technology, through they may instead stem from the dynamics of implementation and usage. And we believe that the investigation of computer impact on the decision-making process can be carried out successfully only through intensive case studies.

Second, insight into socio-organizational problems related to computerization in Italian firms has been gained.

Usually design and implementation processes are characterized by a conceptual frame based on technological determinism and the goal of reaching a higher rationality state.

The main results are that in most cases, we can consider the automation process as a self-realizing prophecy. The role of EDP personnel, both internal and external, in the organization is such that enlargement of automation causes an increase of EDP power and of the “informatic” frame of reference. Consequently, every failure can be seen as a corporate opposition to increasing organizational rationality.

The problems and failures of MIS are now so huge that it is impossible to ignore them. One important area seems to be the need for new methodologies for information system analysis and design; these may overcome some of the observed deficiencies such a process may lead to the normative application of studies regarding the problem of interaction between organization and computer-based information systems.

The term “computer introduction” stands for the decision-making process that supports the earlier stages of computerization: the acquisition, implementation and planning of data-processing facilities and the creation of the EDP group.

Here the computer introduction process is analyzed using data from eighteen firms.

A generic decision-making process can be formally defined as a triple $(D, S, P)$ , where:

D = the set of decision makers (computer manufacturers, consultants, top management, EDP department, middle management);

S = the set of decision stages: computer acquisition, applications selection, system implementation, development plan, training of EDP personnel, organization of EDP department.

$P \subset D \times F$ (the Cartesian product) is the following binary relation: For each $(d, f_{i}) \subset D \times F$ , $(d, f_{i}) \in P$ if the decision maker $d$ has taken part in the decision phase $f$ . Because $D$ and $F$ are finite sets, the characteristic function $\mu_{\mathbf{p}} \to D \times F$ is defined by the correspondence

$$
\mu_ {\mathbf {p}} \colon (d, f) \rightarrow \left\{\begin{array}{l l}1&\text { if } (d, f) \in P\\0&\text { if } (d, f) \in P\end{array}\right.
$$

can be represented by a logical table T, i.e., one with truth values as entries.

In order to identify the $P(i)$ , in each firm, senior managers were asked to mark the elements of the $5 \times 6$ table $T_{p(x)}$ , according to the history of computerization in their respective firm and to the definition of $P$ . Table $\overline{T}_{\mathbf{p}}$ of fig. A1 was then obtained by averaging the results of eighteen firms.

Table $\bar{T}_{p}$ shows the number of firms in which top managers interviewed indicated that a decision maker had intervened in a given stage. Data in the fourth row suggest that the degree of intervention of the EDP department is relatively high and uniformly distributed throughout all the stages; the highest values occur for:

\- computer manufacturers/training of EDP personnel

\- EPD department/organization of EDP department

\- EDP department and computer manufacturers/system implementation

\- top management/computer acquisition.

It appears that the most important decision effort was made in the stages relative to machine and personnel acquisition. Computer introduction in the firms appears not only to be a process of “technology internalization”, [26] but also an incorporation of a group of specialists to manage and exploit the data-processing technology. Moreover, specialists were trained outside the firm by computer manufacturers.

## A2. The application of correspondence analysis

Two decision makers are said to have "similar" or "proximate" behaviour if they intervene mainly in the same stages, i.e. if the two corresponding columns of $T_{\mathfrak{p}}$ are to some extent proportional. The proximities among decision stages can be analogously defined. The statistical method of correspondence analysis /22/ uncovers the "proximities" existing within the sets $D, S$ and between $D$ and $S$ by representing the elements of $D$ and $S$ as "clouds" of points in Euclidean space. The visualization is obtained by intersecting the "clouds" of points with planes, which optimize the explained variance of the initial data.

For table $\bar{T}_{p}$ the results obtained are presented in fig. A2. The first two pairs of axes explain 93 percent of the total variance of the data: consequently the resultant structure can be represented in the usual Euclidean plane with a fair degree of accuracy.

Along the horizontal axis, which gives the abscissae of the set of points representing the decision makers and stages, one finds (from left to right) computer vendors, EDP department, consultants, lower and higher management: the first axis may be interpreted as a gradation from external to internal decision makers. With regard to the decision stages, the same axis discriminates between technology-oriented responsibilities and (mainly) non-technical management responsibilities. In conclusion there is a division of roles between two groups of decision makers and stages: management is responsible mainly for drafting the outline master-plan for applications whilst computer vendors train the personnel who will later implement the system. The EDP department has an interesting position: being almost equidistant from all the important "political" stages, and it lies on the half plane "dominated"

A3. A specifying variable: the functional dependence of the EDP department

The EDP department location is a variable which might specify the general pattern which emerged in fig. A2 assuming that the functional dependence of the EDP unit is an organizational result of the strategies and coalitions stated by decision makers throughout the computer introduction process.

The eighteen firms were divided into two subgroups according to the location of the EDP manager. In the first group (seven firms) the EDP director reports directly to top management, in the second (eleven firms) the EDP group is located in a functional area (mainly the accounting department). The data structures obtained applying correspondence analysis are shown for the two subgroups in fig. A.3. and fig. A.4. The two diagrams show the same basic pattern: the division of roles between the external and internal decision makers and the neutral role of the EDP department in the process. Middle management seems to be excluded from computerization; when the EDP unit is situated in a functional area, top management controls the process through the development plan. Consultants play an interesting role: they seem to be involved in computerization in order to provide top management with the organizational "know how" that cannot be provided directly by users.

Consultants become less important when middle managers can make their information needs explicit and apply their knowledge directly.
