# The Potential of Commercial Intelligence in the Ocean Shipping Industry

David Ronen

University of Missouri-St. Louis, School of Business Administration, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

This article discusses the potential for legal commercial intelligence in the ocean shipping industry through the analysis of the information in the industry; it also describes an information system for processing such industry data. The system collects and verifies data from many sources and supplies information for the decision-maker in a comprehensible format, based on facts and not on guesses. Typical applications of the system are discussed and their impact is presented for several cases.

Keywords: Ocean Shipping, Commercial Intelligence, Shipping Management, Maritime Intelligence.

## 1. Introduction

Most military intelligence consists of the collection and analysis of information from sources available to the public, and the subsequent arranging of the vast amount of data in a comprehensible manner. The same approach can be applied to business, where large quantities of data are being published by various agencies and businesses. The intelligent collection and compilation of such information may result in a clear market picture and in early identification of business opportunities. This article analyzes the characteristics of business information in the ocean shipping industry and outlines a Maritime Intelligence System which may be used for market data collection and compilation.

In the international business arena, the acquisition of market information is more complicated than in a national arena due to the spatial separation of business activities and the cultural differences between the various locations. The ocean shipping industry is somewhat different from other industries in this context. On one hand, information supplied by various companies is harder to verify but, on the other hand, many other sources publish a huge amount of data which can be collected, cross-verified and processed in order to achieve a better picture of the market.

Providing the decision-maker with analyzed data and intelligence reports can be achieved by two fundamentally different methods:

(i) Specific research, and

(ii) The systematic recording and immediate processing of current information.

A specific research project is structured to answer a specific question; it is characterized by the lack of an alternative technique and is more prevalent than the latter method. Such an approach is justified by a lower investment in time and money. The shortcoming of this approach is that it produces results with a relatively short duration of validity, because of the volatility of the information which entered into the conclusions.

However, given an unstable shipping market (where fluctuations of rates and diversity of employment require frequent repetition of the same analysis), a method of systematic recording and immediate processing usually warrants the expense and effort expended in developing such a system. Furthermore, the scope and variability of the questions make it impossible to prepare specific reports that answer any question at short notice.

The problem of timely, available, and accessible input-data for decision-making has been proposed by Grayson [1] as a major reason for the gulf between management science and decision-makers. Zannetos [2] claimed that a major reason for secrecy in the ocean shipping industry is the inability of those who possess the data to use it for managerial decisions. This is probably because of the many data sources involved and the lack of data selectivity, comparisons, and summaries at the required levels.

The systematic data recording and processing approach has been applied in the system outlined in this article in an attempt to overcome such problems. The use of computer for data recording, selection, comparisons, summaries, and analysis enables the timely supply of data to the decision-maker in a comprehensible format. Its aim is to define and establish a data base readily accessible to the decision-maker in a way that key decisions will be based on known and proven facts. The system presents a practical method for the decision-maker in making quantitative assessment of problems, such as trends in ship supply, the commitments of operators, and the effect of competitive service in trade routes.

## 2. The Nature of the Ocean Shipping Industry

The decisive role of information in the ocean shipping industry is dictated by the nature of the industry; its major characteristics are:

(i) Spatial distribution. The ocean shipping industry exists all over the world. This has two effects: it complicates the flow of information and its verification and, it enables owners to take advantages of different tax laws, subsidies, and operating regulations (especially manning standards). This often necessitates a complicated ownership structure and the use of flags of convenience.

(ii) Capital intensive industry. A modern ship represents an investment of the order of several tens of millions of dollars and a daily cost of thousands to tens of thousands of dollars. A ship is manned by about two dozen people. Thus, management decisions have high financial risk, and any reduction of risk provides substantial benefits.

(iii) Selective information flow. The basic unit is the ship, controlled by a Captain who reports directly to the owners. Since the ship is usually disconnected from other units (either inside or outside its organization) very little verification of information can be performed, and the information flow is controlled by the Captain and the owners.

(iv) Relatively free market. Usually there are no restrictions on entry and no regulation of rates. Recently there have been moves to restrict third party participation (i.e., a flag that belongs neither to the origin country nor to the destination country) to 20% of the trade.

(v) Diversified industry. There are many different types and sizes of ships (probably less than 20% of the ocean going vessels have sisterships), many owners, many trade routes, and many commodities.

(vi) Long lead times. The building of a new ship takes at least two years (from the decision to build); even changing the trade route of a vessel takes several weeks.

(vii) Cyclical industry. The demand for shipping space depends on the status of world and local economies, exchange rates, and weather conditions; very often only residual quantities are shipped. For commodities that are often locally produced, like grain, coal, and iron ore, only the quantity that is not produced is imported.

These characteristics of the industry explain the importance of timely decision making information. A management that can forecast, identify, and take advantage of a temporary situation can show higher levels of performance (i.e. profit). It should be noted that such situations are temporary because of lack of barriers to entry and long lead times.

## 3. The Maritime Intelligence System

In order to supply management with a clear and reliable picture, it was decided to base the Maritime Intelligence System (MIS) on facts and figures and not to use guesses, crude estimates, or analytical models.

The data base is organized by ships. Every ship is assigned a unique identification number (ID), which includes a control digit, and all the data concerning a ship is accumulated under its ID. Three types of data are collected about every ship:

1. Physical characteristics, such as: ship type; size - deadweight, capacity, length, breadth, draft; propulsion - type of engines, type of fuels, fuel consumptions, engines' sizes; builder; classification society; speed; number of holds; loading gear, and other features.

2. Control history: owners, flags, names, employment status, prices paid and offered, date of every change, charter information - type of charter, charterers, rates, options, and other charter data.

3. Ship movements: name of port, date of arrival, date of departure, commodity carried, nature of call, special events.

The physical characteristics of a ship hardly ever change whereas control and movements data is accumulated over time. In addition to the above mentioned data, a set of tables is needed for the various codes that are used in the system.

The data is collected from many sources, such as newspapers, trade journals, shipping registers and directories, newsletters, listings and reports published by insurance companies, research organizations, information services and ship brokers. The sources vary in their publication frequency from daily to annually. Since the data is not available in a machine readable format, it is fed into the system manually. Conflicts between contradictory data are settled according to the experience of the data bank manager and the reliability that he assigns to the various sources (part of the data is verified through several sources). The indispensability of automation is a consequence of the extensive amount of data that enters the system. The computer programs which update the data base include many logical checks on the data, and will not introduce illogical data into the data base. Since the MIS system processes external market information it has very little interface with other information systems of the company. The only interface is the set of tables used for the various codes. These tables are used by all the information systems in the company.

The user, or the system manager, is expected to be acquainted with the ocean shipping industry, its structure, components, trades, procedures and data sources. The system aids by detecting trends and changes before they are reported in the trade publications.

Two types of reports are generated. Shipping market control reports are produced regularly; they include exceptions reports intended to identify irregular events, and regular market surveillance reports. The second and more interesting type of reports is produced on request; the user specifies the needs and the reports are produced by a set of highly flexible programs. The user must specify characteristics in which he is interested (selection characteristics), the characteristics according to which the data will be sorted (sorting characteristics), and the data in which he is interested. Every special report consists of two parts: a detailed report and a compact summary (the summary is usually on a single page).

The annual operating cost of the system depends mainly on the number of ships included and may be up to half a million dollars (usually there is no advantage in including only specific ships in the system. The full value is realized when all vessels of given type, like oil carriers, dry bulk carriers, reefers, gas carriers, or container ships, are included). The benefits, direct and indirect, were several times the costs of the system.

## 4. Typical Examples of Application

The Maritime Intelligence System has a wide range of applications, from short-term (operational) to long-term (strategic). The following demonstrate its capabilities:

(i) Commodities flow data. Major bulk commodities flow can be calculated by origin and distination ports, countries, or areas, with very short time-lags (2–3 weeks). This was found very useful to answer the question: "Is there actually a decrease in the world supply of oil?" during the Arab oil embargo in 1973–74.

(ii) Spot market operation. During periods of excessive demand for shipping space, the rates in the voyage charter market for oil tankers fluctuate heavily dependent on the daily tonnage supply. An operator who knows the exact tonnage supply (in house and competitors) available in a certain loading zone within a two week period can close a deal at the highest possible rate. This rate difference can be of the order of one million dollars per 50 days voyage, for a medium size tanker.

(iii) Competition analysis. Valuable inputs available for management decisions concerning competitors' fleet operations include: competitors' fleet composition (owned, chartered); tonnage commitments; specifications of vessels. As an example, on one contract negotiation, by knowing the flag of the competitor's vessels, those specifically available were identified; it was found that those did not meet the specifications required by the shipper. It is also possible to provide: the performance of a competitor's fleet in specific trade routes (volume, sea days ratio speed); ownership structure (for example, Onassis had hundreds of ships each sailing under a different company).

(iv) Port operations. The average stay can be easily calculated for every port, country, or area, as well as for every type and size of vessel (it was found that the average stay in port for tankers was more than twice the industry norm!).

(v) New building decisions. The order book of every shipyard and its vessel size and type specialization are easily extracted and used for new building decisions and negotiations.

(vi) Other applications. Tonnage scrapping rates, lay-up figures, prices of vessels, charter market analysis, verification of published data, detection of changes in the industry, all these can be calculated and followed.

The system has been used in guiding daily operations as well as for feasibility studies and capital investment decisions. For example, the present excessive supply of oil carriage capacity was forecasted to appear (without the quadrupling of oil prices) in 1975, through the analysis of shipyards' order books and demand growth rates. That forecast resulted in selling of tanker building contracts, thus avoiding losses of tens of millions of dollars (that oversupply actually appeared in 1974).

## 5. Post Mortem

The MIS had been in use for several years in a large shipping company. The management of the company had speculative inclinations, and the system was "sold" to the management because it promised large profits for spot market operations. (These profits were realized in several instances.) Other capabilities were not fully utilized by the management because the system's flexibility made it almost impossible for the managers to spend their time in utilizing the options. Thus they required an intermediary who was well acquainted with the system. It seems also that secrecy concerning business deals in their formative stages prevented usage of the system even when it was appropriate.

Use of the system was substantially reduced when the spot market collapsed and it terminated when the company faced severe financial troubles. These financial problems resulted from operating without working capital, which proved very lucrative in a booming market but disastrous in a tight market. The cost of the MIS system was just a drop in the bucket. Thus the factors that facilitated the development of the MIS system were those that terminated it.

For quite a few years the mere existence of the MIS system was kept secret due to its uniqueness. Presently one commercial system exists which have some of the capabilities of the MIS system. That commercial system is a spin-off from a data base which was developed for different purposes, and therefore is not designed for shipping management support. The type of data and the scope of analysis of that system are very limited, and the results are less reliable.

## 6. Summary

This article demonstrates the potential of commercial intelligence in the ocean shipping industry through a proper data collection and processing system. It provides the means whereby the decision-maker possesses readily usable information for examining business ventures and opportunities in the light of good data. Although no new or innovative use of data processing technology is involved, and the underlying concept is applied regularly to military intelligence, a practical business application was presented here.

It should be remembered, however, that meaningful use of the analyzed information presented by such a system is preconditioned by the conjunction of experience and knowledge of the particular market. By receiving accurate information, the user can elevate the process of decision making to higher levels, and improve the position of the organization.
