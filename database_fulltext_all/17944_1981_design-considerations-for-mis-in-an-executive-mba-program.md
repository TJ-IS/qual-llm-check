# Design Considerations for MIS in an Executive MBA Program

Jeffrey A. Hoffer

Associate Professor of Operations and Systems Management, School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, USA

Executive education courses are typically decision making and policy oriented. This paper suggests and empirically evaluates such an introductory MIS course suitable for an Executive MBA program (EMBA). The analysis reported here should be helpful to any educator teaching or preparing to teach MIS to executives. The evaluation indicates that executives seek both a hands-on educational experience with selected MIS technologies as well as a conceptual framework for managing and using MIS. Although not all MIS topics are deemed relevant, executives develop a strong need to insure a proper organizational direction for MIS activities. They are most concerned with understanding and controlling the evolution and rapid changes of MIS and look for ways that MIS can help them cope with change. For this reason, a successful course pedagogy includes joint case analysis with functional or technique courses in the overall curriculum.

Keywords: MIS education, Executive MBA education.

## 1. Introduction

Executive level management education has been an important “product” in graduate schools of business. Executive development programs are well established continuing education opportunities for “life-long learning”. But none of these deliver the prestige and visibility of the MBA degree. Consequently, the Executive MBA (EMBA) is available at approximately 30 business schools in the United States to satisfy the demand for formal accredited management education.

Proposals for MBA Management Information Systems (MIS) education have recognized the MBA orientation [2,7,9]; sources [10] and [14] have encouraged addressing a broader range of issues. Input-Processing-Storage-Output; computer equipment; programming; structured, top-down design; order entry; accounts receivable; and the like are topics in most introductory MIS textbooks (e.g., [4,8,15,18]). Whereas an MBA program deals primarily with operational and tactical business problems and objective methods for problem solving, the EMBA is more policy and strategy oriented.

This paper addresses the purpose, content and conduct of an introductory MIS course as part of an EMBA curriculum; extension of this to non-MIS major MBA MIS education may be possible, but is not directly addressed here (see [10]). In particular, the EMBA program at Case Western Reserve University's (CWRU) Weatherhead School of Management will be used as an in-depth (although limited sample size) experience base to evaluate such a course.

## 2. EMBA MIS Environment

Two major variables exogenous to any (MIS) course and its vast set of potential topics must be understood in order to design a good (MIS) course; these externals are: student Profile and total degree program curriculum/purpose/conduct.

An EMBA student is a "self-made" manager, usually has ten or more years of managerial experience, and often does not have any formal college training. Students may include Presidents of \$3M-\$30M firms, may come from both the private and public sectors, and usually will have had a career in only one functional business area. To illustrate, Table 1 lists the titles of those who were in the 1979–80 first year EMBA class at the Weatherhead School. (This single sample is the basis for all subsequent evaluations).

Many students may never have been exposed to a computer-based MIS; others have directly experienced the pains of MIS Growth Stages 2, 3 and 4 [16]; some will have found a way to acquire their own computer hardware and MIS staff. Thus, the students are quite heterogeneous along many dimensions, well established in managerial patterns, already successful at intuitive management, and diversely experienced in MIS. They have achieved positions which, in general, are not operational; rather, they are often an integral part of policy formulation in their organizations.

## 3. Objectives of EMBA MIS Course

Given the context for an EMBA MIS course, what then should be its purpose? Should certain skills be taught? Are there myths about MIS that should be dispelled? What role will the student play in MIS development, design, use, and control?

At the Weatherhead School five general objectives were chosen to establish a course framework and classroom atmosphere. These objectives are meant to be reasonable MIS interpretations of the general program objectives outlined in the previous section. Table 3 lists these objectives with a review of a student (post-course) evaluation of the achievement of objectives. Only the goal of “understanding MIS technologies” received a low achievement rating by anyone; all other objectives were considered to have been achieved by the majority of the executives.

An MIS course designed around our objectives was seen by the student as strongly improving their opinions of MIS and its positive contribution to organizational success. ## 4. Topics Covered in Course

The topics addressed in a EMBA MIS course depend upon the overall curriculum purpose, student backgrounds and exposure to MIS, and MIS course objectives; in particular, consider the following four EMBA characteristics:

(1) The EMBA is top-management, planning and policy oriented (this suggests that students will be conditioned to look for tools to support policy/plans formulation and evaluation);

(2) The EMBA curriculum at CWRU has a strong technology management emphasis in several courses (this suggests that courses in each area of management should familiarize students with the latest procedural, socio-technical and equipment technologies that will redirect management in the future);

(3) The EMBA students, because of a planning orientation, are interested in change management (this suggests that an evolutionary, staged growth framework for the MIS course could be an effective method to relate topics); and

(4) The EMBA students have very heterogeneous experiences with MIS (this suggests some remedial, knowledge leveling is necessary to at least equalize the confidence level of students).

These four EMBA environmental conditions were analyzed and suggested to us certain general topical sections for the MIS course. Course time, capabilities of instructors and university resources also helped to choose topics. The ones listed below are believed to highlight a majority of MIS policy issues and to place these into a conceptual framework that all students can understand. Systems development methodologies (for non-DSS applications), information theory, general systems theory, MIS career paths and other topics were rejected as being too abstract or more appropriate for operational or MIS managers. Applications of systems theory did arise several times during analysis of cases or issues.

The five topical sections actually chosen, along with some appropriate subtopics and objectives, are:

## (1) MIS Stages of Growth

The objectives of this section were to understand the descriptive nature of this model and the trends in MIS applications, personnel, user-MIS relations, user systems development roles and control that is suggests. Students were asked to evaluate their own organizational position along such growth patterns. This topic was covered first in the course; subsequent topics were all introduced briefly under this topic so that students could relate the subsequent topics into a wider perspective.

A discussion of this topic also points out the experience differences among students and suggests specific points to be addressed in later topic areas. This topic thus provides an integrating framework for the whole course (see environmental conditions (3) above).

## (2) Data Processing Systems Operations

Here the objectives were to understand transaction, reporting and inquiry processing cycles, typical computer hardware and software components, and concepts of computer languages. This topic is needed to achieve a leveling of MIS experience (see environmental condition (4) above).

## (3) MIS Advanced Technology

The objectives of this section were to:

(A) understand the concept of a database and the capabilities of database query languages, the concept of information resources and know some source of external, intelligence information.

(B) understand the functional components of the office of the future, a strategy for implementing one, and the organizational effects of the automated office.

(C) understand the issues in MIS centralization/decentralization, minicomputers and computer networks and draw on the experience of several firms which have dealt (at a policy level) with distributed processing.

These topics address the program and personal factors found in conditions (2)-program technology orientation and (3)-student change management orientation.

## (4) Decision Support Systems

Here the objectives were to understand different process models of decisionmaking, phases of decision-making and the information requirements of each, the requisite technologies that are merged to form a DSS, examples of DSS, development and implementtation patterns for a DSS, and design of student's personal DSS. These objectives deal directly with the need for planning and policy tools, embodied in condition (1) above.

## (5) Corporate Modelling and Planning Languages

This last topic has as its objectives to understand different classes of budgeting, planning, and tradeoff analysis languages, actual use of a pre-programmed corporate model (alternatives evaluation and model reformulation), and model building and use scenario. This topic also addresses the need for planning tools and ways to cope with organizational change, which were deemed to be critical student and curriculum factors.

Table 5 presents the student/executive evaluation of the relevancy of these topics and the degree to which the students wish to be involved in evaluating, directing, designing, or utilizing these topical areas in their own organization. Each topic was not equally received by the students as being relevant to their education and career, but this did not diminish their wish to participate in setting the associated organizational direction. The student evaluation speaks strongly for the necessity of an integrating mechanism, such as MIS Stages of Growth, for executive students.

Associated with this numerical summary is the following comment made by one student:

I believe this course teaches as much about management style and decisionmaking as it does about computers (MIS/DSS). And that is good! (President, Automobile Dealership)

Lecture style, homeworks, exams and readings all contribute to molding a student's perception of the relevancy of each topic. The first three of these variables will be discussed under pedagogy. Students evaluated the various readings used in the course; detailed summaries are available, but are not necessary for purposes of this paper. Not too surprisingly, the highest rated materials were all manager reader oriented (as opposed to, say, MIS specialist) and were found in such publications as Harvard Business Review (not EDP Analyzer). Our conclusion from this finding is that the MIS field must write more managerial reviews of MIS topics (especially technologies) to better explain our field to the general managers who control our destiny.

## 5. Pedagogy

The pedagogical style of a course is realized in lectures, homeworks and examinations. Our observations in this important area are:

(1) Executive students are very motivated by and skilled at relating material to their own job experience. For this reason, lectures can be designed to introduce ideas by "dynamic case writing;" i.e., by asking if any student has seen some particular MIS issue in his/her organization and use this history to create and then emphasize the major points of the issue. The faculty member must some prepared, however, with a list of points to be covered, tradeoffs to be highlighted, management consequences, etc..

Student experience also permits personalization of homeworks. For example, the final homework given to all students in the EMBA MIS class was:

Describe (no more than 5 pages) your personal Decision Support System (DSS). You will be graded on your ability to identify your activities which are appropriate for a DSS. Your description should include: your activities which the DSS will support (use an appropriate general model for your activities that fits into a DSS framework), database contents, sources of data, functions of any models, whether your DSS is stand-alone or integrated into ongoing MIS operations, and how the DSS might evolve.

Fifteen out of sixteen students (or 93.75%) who participated in the course evaluation rated this a very creative assignment, one student classified it as busy work. Further, nine out of sixteen (or 56.25%) evaluated this as very useful, five called it generally useful, and two ranked it marginally useful. The one person who rated this assignment both busy work and marginally useful, criticized the homework because he was not forced to develop his DSS and use it!

(2) Executive students are also highly motivated to integrate material learned in several courses. Therefore we:

(a) demonstrated a corporate model for a case study (Browning Lumber [5]) which had been manually analyzed in a previous session of a finance course—this prompted the students to request that an elective course be designed to be structured around this same sequence of manual and then automated analysis, and

(b) introduced the BRANDAID marketing strategy DSS [13] and then, jointly with the instructor teaching market strategy, had the students invent (on paper) a DSS for a market strategy case study called Concern Kitchens [3].

Both of these experiences reinforced the relevancy and usefulness of a computer-based tool for semi-structured decisionmaking and satisfied the student desire to integrate material across courses.

(3) Executive students, in addition, are highly motivated by “hands-on” exposure to MIS technologies. The power of both word processing and corporate modelling systems was demonstrated in class. The utility of these tools was quickly seen and students wanted to apply them and gain first-hand experience with them. For example, one student commented that the course could have included

more hands-on exposure. It would have been more useful if in Browning Lumber we would have worked in groups of 6-8 and had a print out of the existing situations — then 30 minutes to decide which 4 or 5 critical “numbers” we want to change and then time at the machine to actually relate those changes to the “bottom line”. (Typical of comments from 62% of the students.)

The above approach to teaching led to a course pedagogical rating by students of 4.15 on a 1-5 scale, with 5 being highest, using the standard course evaluation form in the Weatherhead School. This is above the “traditional” dividing line of 4.0 which distinguishes very acceptable from adequate evaluations. Further, this rating was in the middle of the range of scores (3.8-4.25) which were achieved in the EMBA courses taught in the same semester as the MIS course.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

This paper has outlined and evaluated a required first course in MIS for an Executive MBA program. The success of this course suggests that this is a viable course as outlined and, more importantly, that MIS is a valuable component of an overall EMBA curriculum.

The experience with an EMBA MIS course indicates that its success relies heavily on carefully preparing material for consumption by students who

(1) are aggressive and highly involved and who seek to understand every detail (and become frustrated when they do not);

(2) come from diverse organizations and MIS experiences and require remedial MIS and EDP training; and (3) want first-hand exposure to tools that have immediate and personal application in business planning.

Further, an “MIS Stages of Growth” framework helped to relate all topics; it brought to mind issues to be addressed as each topic was introduced. Decisionmaking (as opposed to how a business operates) is the orientation of most EMBA students. Consequently, understanding Decision Support Systems can be an effective course objective; this naturally evolves from addressing those MIS technologies and theories that give direction to top-management’s handling and use of information.

The experience of teaching EMBA students suggests the following course modifications:

(1) More computer-based planning models should be used in conjunction with other functional area courses (e.g., finance and marketing) so that the achievement of a rich analysis, via information system support, can be experienced first hand.

(2) A different approach to automated office and distributed processing/minicomputer topics since these were rated noticeably lower with less perceived relevancy than other topics.

(3) An evaluation of alternative integrating frameworks to that reported here; educators must determine to what degree other concepts (e.g., Anthony's [1] levels of management, Simon's [19] phases of decisionmaking, or general systems theory) are also suitable.

(4) More systematic utilization of “dynamic case writing”; e.g., by preparing brief discussion papers for subsequent offerings of the course.

Teaching MIS to senior executives over the period of a complete semester permits greater reflection by the students, and a careful evaluation by them of the relevancy of topics presented. Their evaluations also give insight (not always new, but certainly reinforcing ing) into an executive's view of MIS:

(1) Although not all aspects of MIS are deemed personally relevant, a course such as that described here instills in an executive that MIS is one major way business policies are carried out and monitored. Possibly for this reason, executives want to take part in directing and evaluating various MIS activities, even though these may not be immediately relevant to their business activities. Executives are concerned with change management and seek ways to cope with the rapid changes in MIS. An evolutionary pattern (or set of complementary patterns) is sought to explain and manage these changes.

(2) MIS technologies (especially their capabilities and features) are not easily understood by general executives. They do, however, grasp the impact that systems using these technologies have on their organizations. The impact seems to be most apparent for information system applications that affect their own decisionmaking. Information system professionals still need ways to explain the impact of high volume data/information handling technologies to those senior managers who often must approve technology acquisition.

(3) The value an executive places on MIS is derived from its support of decisionmaking. MIS activities must be consistent with, yet supplement, an executive's decisionmaking process. MIS activities that support integration, consolidation and extrapolation of data across functional management areas seem to be exciting to executives. Hands on exposure to such services, although actual business usage may be only through staff assistants, can be a very successful strategy for introducing decisionmaking support service concepts.
