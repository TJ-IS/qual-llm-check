# Information Integration within the Church of England

Malcolm King

Lecturer in Quantitative Methods, Department of Management Studies, University of Technology, Loughborough, Leicestershire, England, LE11 3TU

The Church of England collects and processes many separate items of information from its constituent parishes but does not combine them into a system to provide management information. However, it is technically feasible to integrate the data to provide substantial benefits to management, particularly the diocesan administration; this could reduce administrative costs and would be inexpensive, but it would require important organisational changes.

A survey demonstrated support for the technical aspects combined with fears that it might erode the autonomy of some groups, and so the scheme was shelved. This demonstrates how political pressure in a diffuse organisation can stifle schemes to improve the flow of management information.

Keywords: Church, Church Management, Parish, Ecclesiastical Data, Congregation, Religious Denomination, Clergy, Information Integration, Diocesan Management, Church Commissioners, Clerical Data.

Malcolm King is a Lecturer in Quantitative Methods in the Department of Management Studies at the University of Technology, Loughborough. In the period 1964–1970 he studied Mathematics at Oxford University and for this work received the University's BA (1967), Diploma: Advanced Mathematics (1968), D. Phil. and MA in 1972. From 1970 onwards Dr. King has researched into and taught the application of mathe matical methods to management problems at Lancaster University, Stirling University and Loughborough University. This has increasingly involved the study of management's information needs and since 1971 he has undertaken a series of projects for the Church of England including six months study leave spent with the Church Commissioners for England in 1977.

## 1. Introduction

For many years the Church of England has collected and recorded information. Indeed, parish registers are a source of much valuable historical information and until recently a separate ecclesiastical census was published. At present substantial data is collected from individual parishes; in fact, many clergymen complain about the time spent filling in forms and completing questionnaires. However, little data is used to assist in the Church's management; much is used for basic functions such as paying stipends and levying a "quota" tax on parishes; the data is then lost. Consequently virtually the same questions are posed several times each year, often with just sufficient variation to necessitate considerable work.

As well as creating problems at the parish level this also leads to difficulties for the central bodies, who process the data, and to intermediate groups who wish to use it. In response to growing awareness of these problems a study was carried out to investigate the possibility of integrating the data available and providing more information to assist management. The study was undertaken with the help and encouragement of the central bodies. The results reported here cover the main features of that study, without describing the details. This work is presented as a case study of an attempt to solve some of the problems of information and management in an unusual organisation with a peculiarly difficult structure. The views expressed are the author's own opinions and do not have any other authority.

## 2. Structure of the Church of England

In order to appreciate the problems and benefits of integrating data within the Church of England, it is necessary to know some aspects of its organisational and management structure.

The Church of England has been for 400 years the established (Protestant) church in England. Estimates of the numbers of active members range from 2 to 3 million people although some surveys [2] show that as many as 30 million people (about two thirds of English families) claim allegiance to it. For most of these people the basic unit is the parish: which is an area of land with a church and served by a clergyman.

England is divided into about 14,000 ecclesiastical parishes, with 17,000 Anglican churches and 8,000 incumbents. An incumbent is a clergyman of the Church of England, usually called the vicar or rector of a parish, who ministers to the people living in the parish. There are also almost 3,000 other clergymen serving as assistants to incumbents or having limited responsibility for a parish.

The parishes are grouped into 43 dioceses, each with a bishop and some full-time administrative staff. The diocese is an important unit of management. The Bishop exercises control over the clergymen and various boards oversee planning and finance at the local level. At the national level there are two important administrative bodies, the Church Commissioners and the Central Board of Finance. National church policy is guided by the General Synod which is a composite debating forum composed of delegates from clergy and laity.

The largest national office is that of the Church Commissioners which deals with the management of the Church of England's assets and the processing of stipends. However there are some senses in which this office is more responsible to Parliament and the Crown than to the General Synod of the Church of England [3]. In contrast, the Central Board of Finance is relatively small, but serves as the financial executive body of the Church and the financial authority of the General Synod.

The interrelationships between the dioceses and the central bodies are complex, reflecting their overlapping responsibilities. For instance, although the clergy are paid by the Church Commissioners, they are responsible to the Diocesan Bishops. Further the dioceses can influence the deployment and level of payment of their clergy, within limits set centrally. However it is doubtful if these limits could be imposed if the dioceses did not accept them voluntarily.

Because of their concern with clergy stipends, the Church Commissioners have no complete computerised record of parishes or churches. The Central Board of Finance collects data and provides information about local finances and membership, so that its emphasis is on churches. On the other hand the diocesan emphasis is mainly on the management of parishes.

These differences in emphasis are found in almost every area of management. They lead to a continuing tension between the national organisations and the dioceses, which occasionally amount to open conflict. Furthermore, attempts to integrate the work of the two central bodies were opposed by some who feared a take-over of the Central Board of Finance by the Church Commissioners.

There has always been a strong desire for autonomy at all levels in the Church of England. Individual parishes and priests would prefer to continue without change if the resources were available. Dioceses wish to make their own independent plans for resources and the central bodies would like to encourage common policies in administering resources. Thus any move to greater integration or control will be resisted. If an information system is seen as threatening autonomy, it will be opposed.

## 3. A Parish Information System

At the outset of the study we considered collecting a large data base which included information about every ecclesiastical parish in England; this was soon dismissed as infeasible. Even if the technical resources were available, there was no means of collecting the raw data. Previous attempts to collect such data directly [1] had shown a response rate of less than 50% of reliable information.

In fact a substantial amount of data was routinely collected, and so the first major step was to see if this could be processed to provide a useful, if limited, data base.

The most readily accessible data maintained by the Church Commissioners concerned clergymen. It was geared to the payment of stipends but it could be manipulated into a parish based system. Where one clergyman served more than one parish some notional division of his income (say on the basis of population) between the parishes would be necessary.

However to obtain a realistic financial picture of each parish it is necessary to incorporate a summary of parish annual accounts. One such summary, the central return of membership and finance, is collected by the Central Board of Finance; other summaries are collected by the dioceses. Unfortunately the central return is not made every year and diocesan summaries have different forms.

The obvious proposal for creating an integrated system involved issuing and collecting the central return of membership and finance every year in a way compatible with the Church Commissioners' computing resources. This required some substantial administrative changes and the whole feasibility of the proposed system depended on making these changes.

The central returns were issued and collected by a department of the Central Board of Finance with little other function. The work ran on a two-year cycle and was carried out entirely manually by eight clerks; they maintained a parochial register, posted the forms, scrutinised the returns and then averaged the statistics. Such a system could not be adapted to an annual cycle without unacceptable increases in staff and costs.

The alternative was to consider automation. It was shown that the annual processing of these returns could be efficiently performed using the computing facilities available if the returns were of high quality, so that they did not require much central scrutiny before being converted into computer readable form. Clearly, this would produce a substantially different work load for the department concerned.

In particular, the need for high quality returns implied transferring the work of initial scrutiny of the returns to the diocesan level or even more conveniently to the deanery level. A deanery is a grouping of about twenty parishes. Deaneries have no full-time officials but might be able to find a volunteer capable of taking on an information liaison role. Volunteers are well equipped to ensure that all returns are legible, consistent, and forwarded on time.

If these returns were processed annually, they would provide a comprehensive summary of parish finances and supply basic figures of membership and attendance. Together with the clergy data the information on these returns would provide a useful picture of the status of the Church in each parish but would provide little information about external features. Basic data on the size and nature of the parish population has become increasingly difficult to obtain as civil boundaries have become less consistent with ecclesiastical boundaries. Demographic data about parishes can only be obtained from sources outside the Church. Whilst there is some advantage in parish officials doing local research, the only way to ensure reliable information for the whole country is to collect it nationally.

After consultations with the Office of Population and Census and Surveys, it was clear that the basic data required by the parish information system could be acquired by purchasing a simple machine readable abstract of the national census data and then matching census districts with ecclesiastical parishes, though the matching process would not be easy. However, an approximate method would involve each deanery volunteer in identifying two or three hundred grid references with parishes.

Although other alternatives were considered, the scheme just outlined was the simplest approach to an integrated system which could be proposed as feasible at that time. To evaluate the proposed system it was necessary to look at the benefits, the organisational consequences and the financial costs as they affected each part of the organisation.

## 4. Benefits

The work of the Church Commissioners' office would gain little from the suggested information integration as most of its information requirements had already been efficiently organised. In direct contrast there would be substantial benefits for the Central Board of Finance, if the full advantages of the system were sens bly exploited.

The department collecting statistics would be relieved of much of its manual drudgery, and so would be able to provide a better service with fewer staff. The department could expand its services to include servicing working groups, processing figures for dioceses and answering enquiries from parishes. Such a role would be welcomed by critics who regarded the group as an expensive data collecting machine, of little use to those facing management decisions.

An analysis of the work at the diocesan level showed that the most important benefits would be achieved by those dioceses which willingly collaborated with the system. Assuming the financial data were collected annually and processed quickly, dioceses could rely on the system for their financial information about parishes thereby eliminating several separate forms issued and processed by dioceses. Furthermore the crucial decision making bodies would have much needed up-to-date and comprehensive information on each parish.

At the lowest levels several deaneries had expended considerable effort in large scale surveys, collecting data which the parish information system could supply more effectively. The system would allow deaneries to concentrate on matters other than data collection. Parishes would benefit by receiving less requests for form filling from their deanery or diocese.

In a more subtle way, parishes and deaneries might benefit from the way the information was integrated. The data could be presented to make explicit the values and factors critical to parishes; eg: a simple standardised financial statement for each parish could reveal their true financial position, perhaps for the first time.

An overall benefit of the integrated system would be an improvement in the number and quality of returns (the response in one diocese using the figures for its own calculations was about 95%, whereas elsewhere it was as low as 50%). Some dioceses noted that central returns were not completed as carefully as returns used locally: there was strong evidence that parishes complete forms more carefully and quickly when they see advantage from their reply. This is the classical case of data collection, and as the system would rely on one form providing the data for several decisions it seemed likely that this form would achieve a high response rate of good quality returns.

## 5. Organisational Consequences

At an early stage it was clear that the organisational consequences of the system were critical to its acceptance at different levels. The parishes would see substantial benefits through a reduction in workload and deaneries would receive added significance by taking on the work of liaison and preliminary scrutiny of forms. However an obvious corollary of the diocesan savings would be some reduction or reorganisation of diocesan staffing.

Such changes combined with their dependence on the central bodies for annual figures used by the diocese, would have a significant effect on diocesan administration. Some would undoubtedly see it as an erosion of autonomy, whereas others would welcome it as a move to greater efficiency and integration. The actual impact would vary from diocese to diocese and depend on the state of the relationship between each individual diocese and the central bodies. Provided those handling the information at the centre were committed to supply an information service for dioceses, it was considered unlikely that the operation of the information system would itself lead to any greater central control.

The proposed integrated system implied significant changes to the two central bodies. The department responsible for the manual data processing would need complete reorganisation. Further, if the Church Commissioners' data was to be integrated with that of the Central Board of Finance, then a new relationship between the two bodies would be required in order to establish a sensible overall management for the system.

One solution would be for the Church Commissioners to take complete responsibility for the system. But this would remove one of the Central Board of Finance's major functions and produce a situation where many members of General Synod would consider it was less responsive to the dioceses and to the General Synod. At the other extreme the responsibility of the system could rest with the Central Board of Finance, operating on the Church Commissioners' facilities. This would lead to increasing tensions and conflicts between the two bodies.

It seemed impossible to devise a completely satisfactory organisational arrangement until there was greater integration between the two central bodies: but that is a much larger issue, possibly involving the relationship between the Church of England and the State. Even if a workable compromise could be devised for the management of the system there would still need to be substantial changes in the group responsible for the membership and finance returns. The clerical work would be lost to the computer and devolved to dioceses and deaneries. Instead the group would need to concentrate on supporting an integrated information service to all sections of the Church of England. Fortunately, the age structure of the staff made such a change easy provided that suitably skilled replacements could be recruited.

## 6. Financial Costs

The costs of installing and running the system would have almost all fallen on the centre. In assessing these it was assumed that existing computing facilities had sufficient capacity, except possibly the need for one extra key-to-disc machine. Indeed, the current equipment could easily handle the estimated load with little or no extra running costs. It was further assumed, the software would be developed internally.

There would be a change in staffing but this could be achieved within the existing budget. Effectively eight clerical staff would be replaced by four administrative and programming staff. However there would be an extra cost initially while the system was being designed and installed. This overall cost was estimated to be about five years salary and support for a senior programmer/systems analyst.

The other major cost would arise from incorporating the census data, purchased in computer readable form. Assuming the matching was done by the deanery volunteers the total cost would be equivalent to the annual salary of a clerk and would only be incurred once every ten years. Thus, if each of the 43 dioceses had spent as little as six working days every ten years collecting population figures then purchasing the census information nationally would be cost effective.

At this stage it was clear that an integrated parish information system was technically feasible and viable in cost terms, that it could provide substantial benefits, particularly in diocesan management, but that it would require a system of volunteers at the deanery level and a difficult internal reorganisation at the national level.

## 7. Internal Opinion Survey

An important part of the study was to determine the likely reactions to the information system and devise an acceptable presentation of the proposed scheme, since it would be impossible to implement the scheme if it was opposed by certain groups. Rather than survey each relevant group by questionnaire, a series of visits and discussions was arranged with a cross-section of people. Those interviewed had intimate knowledge of eight out of forty-three dioceses and all held some diocesan appointment including bishops, archdeacons and diocesan secretaries.

All those interviewed agreed that it would be possible to establish a series of volunteer deanery assistants, and most regarded this as having several beneficial side-effects. In fact some dioceses already had such an arrangement. However this was the only point on which there was agreement.

Most respondents thought there would be willing co-operation by parishes if changes were introduced sensitively. However almost all respondents believed it would be disastrous to ask parishes for any extra information and that even the move to annual central returns would be counter productive unless it was coupled with the elimination of some other form or questionnaire. On investigation it was found that two of the diocesan secretaries expressing this point of view were already making excessively complex information demands of all parishes. Furthermore some respondents who had been actively involved in individual parish problems believed that more parish information could and should be collected.

Although the majority of respondents were keen to see standardised integrated information made available to deaneries and parishes, there was less enthusiasm for using the system at the diocesan level. The lay administrators tended to say “we are managing well by ourselves and don’t want any more interference from the centre”. Against this the clergy were more inclined to believe that diocesan administration would benefit from some outside help and even the administrators often went on to confirm unwittingly that they were in some difficulties over information flows.

Similarly, although the diocesan offices spent a considerable amount of time and effort in collecting and processing financial figures each year, the administrators were not anxious to have the figures they required provided centrally, virtually free of charge. They argued that replies on central returns were not accurate and that the central authorities were unreliable in providing processed figures. This attitude was obviously based on experience and it was difficult to convince them that the integrated information system could be designed to be at least as efficient as their systems.

Even the evidence of two dioceses which had found centrally processed figures useful was dismissed. Indeed the impression emerged that two or three of the administrators would refuse any help, however much it saved money or improved decision making, if it might threaten their autonomy. This impression was indirectly confirmed by other respondents, who said they would accept this loss of autonomy if the benefits were sufficiently well proven.

It was clear from this survey that many people would genuinely welcome the benefits of an integrated parish information system, but that they were opposed to the proposed system because of its 'political' implications. To minimise this opposition it was proposed that the system should be introduced in stages and that the first steps should be taken experimentally while the final administrative structure was still being considered.

## 8. Conclusions

Despite the limited nature of the proposed scheme, the central authorities decided not to proceed any further with this or any other plans for an integrated parish information system on the grounds that some dioceses were opposed to it. Subsequent to this decision, the Central Board of Finance independently decided to proceed with a partial computerisation of the membership and financial analysis. Similarly a few dioceses decided to invest in small computers as a way to improve efficiency in handling their financial data.

The implication of these decisions is that the Church of England will continue collecting much data from every parish but will still be unable to consider the results as a consistent whole or use them effectively in its decision making processes.

With increasing problems of allocating men and money, the lack of properly co-ordinated data is a hindrance to sensible and efficient management and there is little doubt that if the Church of England was organised on a simpler and more hierarchical basis the information flows within the church would be more integrated. Thus one price of diocesan autonomy and the split of functions is a system with poor internal information processes and less efficient diocesan administration.
