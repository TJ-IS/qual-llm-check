# The Use, Operation, and Control of The Small Business Computer

Ralph M. Stair, Jr.

The Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL 32306-1042, USA

William F. Crittenden

Northeastern University, Boston, MA 02115, USA

Vicky L. Crittenden

Harvard University, Cambridge, MA 02138, USA

For the small business, the hope and promise of computerization is no longer a dream. It is reality. The overall purpose of this article is to explore the use, operation, and control of the small business computer. The article also reports the results of a survey indicating the perceptions of directors of Small Business Institutes (SBI) on the use, operation, and control of the small business computer. Routine processing, information, and decision support systems are explored. The impact of computer training and education and important staffing levels are discussed. Levels of satisfaction with the ability of computer systems to provide information and better products and services are also revealed. Satisfaction with existing hardware, personnel, and computer programs is also reported.

Keywords: EDP growth, Computer related training and education, Computer personnel, Computer usage, Disaster prevention and recovery, Computer impact.

Nolan describes six stages of EDP growth [14]. These growth stages are:

-Initiation,

-Contagion,

-Control,

-Integration,

-Data administration,

-Maturity.

These stages describe the phases of growth for the typical organization, from the introduction of computer technology to its optimal and steady state use. Initiation is characterized by the early use of computer technology by a small number of its users in the organization. Contagion refers to experimentation with the new technology and the rapid and uncontrolled expenditures associated with it. During control, efforts are made to place boundaries on the use of the technology and establish cost effective guidelines in their use and acquisition. The integration stage is oriented towards developing applications that are tied together to share common data resources through data base systems. Data administration is the refinement, control, and use of the formal data base system and related equipment, software, and procedures. Expensive and sophisticated data base management systems are fully utilized during this stage. Finally, maturity treats IS as strategic resources. The acceptance of joint user and data processing accountability is implemented. Figure 1 shows these stages.

The S-patterned curve in Figure 1 reveals a reasonable amount of time in each stage. Two important factors, level of data processing expenditures and time at each stage, are likely to be different for the small business. Figure 2 more accurately reflects the growth stages for most small businesses and shows that the time spent in the first stages is compacted, and that expenditures as a percentage of sales, profits, or assets start off relatively high and remain high. The consequences of this growth pattern are important. First, the small business does not have the time to make mistakes or to experiment. Secondly, data processing expenditures can be large and mistakes costly. Finally, a large part of the business is affected by the introduction of the new technology. Thus, problems have a greater chance of jeopardizing the operation and survival of the small business. All these factors make the use, operation, and control of computer technology a critical success factor in a small business.

## 2. The Methodology

Many colleges and universities have a formal Small Business Institute located within the college or school of business. These institutes provide a number of important services to small businesses that are located nearby. The SBI director, faculty, staff members of the institute, and students may provide consulting services to these small businesses; many SBI directors are involved with small businesses in other capacities. Thus, most SBI directors are in a good position to know about the use, operation, and control of small business computer systems. In order to get a better understanding of computing in the small business, a survey of Small Business Institute (SBI) directors was conducted.

An initial survey was developed and pilot tested on several directors. Feedback from this pilot study was used in refining the final survey that was sent to SBI directors across the country.

The purpose of the final survey was to gather information from SBI directors regarding small business computer systems. Specifically, the SBI directors were asked to give their perceptions about the use, operation, and control of the small business computer. The survey was sent to 300 SBI directors throughout the country. There were 72 returned questionnaires that were usable, representing a response rate of 26%.

## 3. Use of the Small Business Computer

A number of important factors influence small business computer usage. These include the effects of the degree of computer related training and education for managers and employees and the number and type of computer personnel $[2,10,15]$ .

Most small business owners or managers have not had formal computer education or training. Our survey of SBI directors revealed that only about 40% of small business owners or managers using computers had participated in one or more computer related courses or seminars. The survey also revealed that only 27% of all small business owners or managers subscribe to or currently read at least one computer related journal. Because most small business managers or owners purchase their software instead of developing it, education, training, and a knowledge of computer systems and usage is very important. In too many cases, the small business manager or owner spends money on equipment and software without really satisfying the needs or goals of the business.

## Computer Personnel

A potential problem with computer use occurs in the personnel area. While large corporations often employ hundreds or thousands of people who design and operate the computer system, the small business typically has only a few key computer people: in some cases, this is the small business owner or manager, while in other cases, one or two people are hired to perform computer-related tasks. The SBI survey revealed that the typical small business has approximately 1.3 people dedicated to the computer system.

## Computer Usage

Currently, most computers used by small businesses are generating routine bookkeeping type of output. The survey showed that about 67% of the applications performed by small businesses are routine. These routine applications include general ledger, accounts payable, accounts receivable, payroll, inventory control, and other related applications. Only 21% of the applications are related to management information and only 12% to decision support. These applications are oriented towards supplying the small business owner or manager with better and more timely information as well as actually supporting the decision making process $[5,19]$ . It is expected that the small business manager will place a greater emphasis on using the computer to provide timely information and support executive decision making in the future.

## 4. Operation of the Small Business Computer

After the original purchase, many small businesses place their computer systems into operation without much thought or planning [2]. In some cases, there are no difficulties. In other cases, poor planning and ineffective operational procedures spell disaster. Problems of invasion of privacy, crime and fraud, key personnel leaving the company, and natural disasters can literally cripple a small business.

The results of the survey reveal the magnitude of this problem. According to the directors, only about 3.5% of small businesses have formal procedures and written documents pertaining to computer crime and fraud. In a study conducted at the University of Minnesota, the impact of a major data processing failure or disaster was documented and studied $[1]$ . About one day after a data processing failure or disaster, 84% of the day-to-day business activities could continue. After about five days, this went down to only 28% of all the business activities, while after about 10 days, only 9%.

At a minimum, data processing disaster prevention and recovery requires careful planning and strict implementation. Detailed procedures to both prevent and recover from such disasters should be in place before the disaster strikes. Furthermore, these procedures should be well documented, so they can be implemented in a rational fashion.

## 5. Control of the Small Business Computer

Reviewing and evaluating the performance of the computer should be an ongoing task. There are two basic ways to initiate system review: time driven reviews and event driven reviews. With the former, the computer system or a part of it is reviewed on a periodic basis. For most small businesses, the review period should be on the order of six months to a year. If the small business operates in a dynamic marketplace with competition changing rapidly, the periodic review may be shortened to a few months. With the event driven review process, a particular event, either internal or external, triggers the review. Internal events include: a computer system producing too many errors, bottlenecks in important operations, lack of adequate storage or speed of the processor, or a desire to find a better way to utilize the computer system. External events include: competition, a dynamic marketplace, and changes in tax laws. Such changes may necessitate modifying the computer system or at least reviewing the system for possible improvements. Future technological breakthroughs and price reductions may also cause the small business owner or manager to look at a new system.

## 7. The Impact of Computer Education and Training

Computer education and training can have a positive impact on the use, operation, and control of the small business computer. Computer courses, computer aided instruction (CAI), and subscriptions to journals are all important sources. CAI is the most frequently used. The survey reveals that 54.1% of small business owners use CAI. Computer courses and computer journals are used by 40% and 27% of small business owners.

In addition to being the most frequently used, CAI has had the greatest impact on information and computer system satisfaction. ## 8. Conclusions

Although many small businesses have been using computers for some time, there is generally inadequate planning when it comes to the operation and use of the computer system. While most small businesses use the computer to provide routine bookkeeping reports, use of the small business computer can be expanded to produce management information and support decision making.

In terms of the actual operation and control of the computer system, small business can learn from the experiences of larger corporations. Developing logs and manuals, providing automatic software backup, guarding against personnel related problems, and locating the computer system in a good environment should be standard procedures for the small business.
