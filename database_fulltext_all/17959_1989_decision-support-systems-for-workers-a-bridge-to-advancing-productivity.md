# Decision Support Systems for Workers: A Bridge To Advancing Productivity

Lawrence F. Young

Quantitative Analysis/Information Systems Department, College of Business Administration, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio 45221-01301, USA

This paper explores how and why the use of computer-based support systems could effectively serve as the bridge to attain higher levels of worker responsibility for and involvement in quality and productivity improvement. Economic, technological, and competitive conditions appear to provide compelling reasons for moving toward more advanced modes of worker participation utilizing computer support systems. A Worker Support System is described which contains components of knowledge-based (expert) systems for methods engineering, creativity-idea processing support, a Worker Information Center, and an Analytical Templates component. Such components can be introduced in a phased experimental mode, with more advanced levels of support kept in tandem with more advanced levels of worker participation.

Keywords: Worker Participation, Decision Support Systems, Computer Support Systems, Quality Circles, Worker Support Systems, Work Productivity Improvement, Work Methods Improvement Organizational Computer Support

1. Introduction

Worker participation and Support Systems

Several studies and experiments, as well as informal observations, have demonstrated that production workers often possess the creativity and ability to identify changes in their work methods that significantly improve productivity, quality, and enhance their job satisfaction. Experience in Japan, Scandinavia, and some leading companies in the U.S.A. has demonstrated the value of providing means for workers to exercise these abilities.

Instances of training selected workers in methods improvement date back to the “Work Simplification” programs of U.S. Industrial Engineers in the 1940s and 1950s. However, until recently, U.S. management practices and attitudes have not helped in building and extending these ideas for encouraging worker participation. The international competitive environment and highly publicized successes of Japanese Quality Circles in using methods learned from American consultants, such as Deming, and from the I.E. literature, has renewed American interest in increasing the involvement of workers in productivity improvement. Several voices in the U.S., including that of Lester Thurow, Dean of the School of Management at M.I.T., are now furthering the recognition that quality improvement is particularly critical in increasing competitiveness. Changes in the management approach involving emphasis on worker participation have been credited for the Ford Motor Company’s radical improvement in quality and consequent increase in marker share in the last few years.

The cumulative evidence of past and recent experiences indicates that, given a generally favorable labor-management atmosphere, decent job conditions, and compensation perceived to be equitable, workers can and will significantly contribute to productivity and quality improvement, if they are provided with opportunities and training.

While we have seen a dramatic increase in the use of interactive computer support systems by managers and staff professionals in coping with semi-structured decision situations, these systems have not generally been made accessible to workers for identifying work methods improvements, except in a few leading edge companies.

Informal discussions with a knowledgeable staff member of Hewlett Packard indicate that the use of microcomputers to support workers is highly integrated into their company's mode of organizing and carrying out production operations. In such a company, it is perfectly natural for a worker or group to use a spreadsheet program, or a word processor, or production planning package, during an “off-line” group meeting or an “on-line” work-day. The microcomputers and software are accessible to workers in either case. Other leading edge companies apparently have integrated some degree of microcomputer support, including expert systems, into the regular work activities of production workers, but are not prepared to publicize these for competitive reasons. The large majority of companies may have some “off-line” worker activity, such as Quality Circles, but have not provided their workers with computer support facilities. We base this conclusion on informal observations and some reported experience, as well as a perfunctory review of research and practitioner literature. This review did not uncover any reports of computer support for worker participation groups.

On the contrary, a recent book by Zuboff [1988] claims that industry has largely failed to recognize that the traditional division of manual from mental work can now be transcended through information technology. Instead, according to Zuboff, computers have been used to enhance the traditional separation between decision making and manual labor through increased computer monitoring and control of workers.

It thus appears reasonable to assume that most companies have not yet provided computer support for worker participation groups. This seems to be due to three main factors:

(1) their production process has not yet advanced to the point where it requires a high degree of integrated worker decision making and direct control;

(2) management attitudes have favored only nominal participation;

(3) the lack of maturity of decision support systems (DSS).

This situation, at least with respect to the latter two factors, has changed and should now facilitate bringing computer support systems and worker participation together in more companies. The new situation can be characterized as follows:

(1) Recognition of a Need for Worker Participation The highly publicized payoff when workers participate by improving their work methods and the driving force of international competition are causing companies to rethink their attitudes. Under the “pull” of positive case reports and the “push” of competitive pressures, these reassessments are likely to lead to trying more meaningful forms of participation, including computer support.

## (2) Advancement into DSS Maturity

Micro-computer DSS software packages have become more widely disseminated among managers and staff specialists; microcomputers and accompanying software are now lower in cost per unit of computing power, and systems have become much easier to use. These trends contribute to economic and organizational feasibility of worker support systems.

## (3) Increased Focus on Knowledge-based (Expert) Systems

Availability of new, easier to use software “shells” for the development of Knowledge-based Systems and a new focus of attention on the potential value of such systems have decreased application development costs and led companies to explore new applications that may provide them with a competitive edge. This new form of computer support has the potential to enhance worker participation.

## (4) Applicable Modes of Group Support

Professional and managerial attention has been turning toward exploring methods for the support of group management decision processes. Some of these group computer support methods appear to be applicable to the support of worker groups, such as Quality Circles.

## (5) New Ways of Supporting Qualitative Thinking (Creativity)

New qualitative thinking and creativity computer support functions have been proposed. Some of these have been implemented in software and appear to be appropriate for use by worker participation groups.

In this paper, we identify a basic repertoire of worker support functions that can aid companies that have not already integrated worker decision-making into their production process. For such companies, the introduction of worker support systems could provide better production management involving facilitation of worker decision making instead of using computers merely to monitor and control workers who are not expected to think for themselves. There are, however, pre-conditions for the effective introduction of such a system.

## 2. Preconditions for Worker Support Systems: an Incremental Approach

A worker support system is not proposed as a cure-all for productivity and quality problems. It will not substitute for good labor relations, progressive management, and a willing and capable work force. Worker participation in any form has been found to require the following preconditions in order to succeed:

(a) A basic foundation of trust between management and workers;

(b) A reward system perceived to be equitable;

(c) A workplace culture in which open communications and cooperation is the general rule;

(d) Workers who are able and willing to read and think for themselves, even if they are not highly educated in a formal sense.

Given these preconditions, the objectives of participation and computer support are to increase existing opportunities for higher productivity.

Workers will require some time to become trained and acclimated to using computer support systems, just as they need preparation and time to adjust to participation itself. To facilitate training and the acclimation process, it is proposed that different levels of support be introduced in a phased, incremental manner. Each more extensive level of computer support can be used to facilitate a correspondingly higher level of worker participation, as follows:

## Level 1: Introductory Participation

Quality Circles or other modes of worker discussion groups are introduced. At this level, workers still have only a few opportunities to control their own work methods and can only propose changes to management. If the minimal preconditions exist, this phase creates a basis for increasing opportunities to recognize workers' contributions, to reward their improvement ideas, and to progress toward the next higher level of participation. If the preconditions do not exist, however, this form of support is likely to prove to be ineffective and nominal, and will fade away, with or without worker support systems.

## Level 2: Transitional Participation

Here, in addition to providing a forum for work group meetings, workers have a higher degree of autonomy in selecting their work methods and in controlling their own output, quality assurance, etc. Worker support systems at this stage can begin to be used in an “on-line” mode as part of normal work operations as well as to support worker discussion groups, such as Quality Circles. A larger scope of information and functionality can be made available to workers through a support system at this stage.

## Level 3: Advanced Integrated Participation

At this level, on-line integration of worker support systems in the workplace environment is completed. Workers have an extensive repertoire of support functions and access to a wider data base.

Progress toward the higher levels of participation described may also be accompanied by more advanced technology. The introduction of high technology often requires a different type of worker, one who is more capable of making on-line decisions in the control of work processes. This may absolutely require that workers use some form of computer support system as an integrated part of performing their regular tasks.

## 3. Functional Capabilities for Worker Support Systems

A basic generic Worker Support System may include the following:

## 1. Methods Engineering Expert Systems

Software to provide support to workers in methods analysis and methods improvement using industrial engineering techniques.

## 2. Creative Process Support

Support for the group creativity enhancement protocols of Brainstorming and Synectics can be provided through a locally linked set of microcomputers in a worker support meeting room. In addition, selected thinking aids and idea organizing functions can be provided.

## 3. Worker Information Center

A Center can provide generic support software and data bases for the workers. Data bases could include current and historical production information, production costs, machine rates and other technical specifications, materials specifications, and human factors data.

## 4. Analytical Templates

A set of spreadsheet-based templates can be created for work groups to ask “what if” questions about changes in their work methods, etc...

## 4. Methods Engineering Expert Systems

Both group and individual modes can be provided by Expert System modules that provide industrial engineering knowledge and make it available to the worker group in a non-threatening, objective form of impersonal consultation.

While many microcomputer software packages currently provide industrial engineering aids for queueing analysis, facilities layout, production blending problems, production planning, inventory and quality control (e.g., Banks, Spoerer, Collins [1986]), most of these would probably be too specialized and technical for workers not trained or already involved in analysis and decision-making in their regular job tasks. However, a few existing packages may be suitable for workers with very little training or special knowledge. Such a package is reported by Castles [1984]. Consultant, the package described later, could also be used in multiple qualitative criteria decision problems related to methods change. It provides a framework for organizing and weighting the several factors of a semi-structures problem situation but does not require any complex or hidden mathematical or logical algorithm.

Other problems could be handled by using or adapting existing Expert System packages for tasks such as process and quality control (Moore [1985], Alexander and Jagannathan [1986], and Miller [1985]) or project management (Sathi, Fox, and Greenberg [1985]). Many of these expert systems, however, may also not be generally suitable for workers. An alternative to using existing packages is to tailor-make more rudimentary and less specialized applications using Expert System “shells”. For example, rules-of-thumb that minimize material movement can be built into an expert system for facilities layout.

Similarly, basic methods analysis rules can be inserted to search for improvements by eliminating, combining, or re-sequencing operations. The level of expertise required, and hence the difficulty of developing such systems, may not be very great. For workers inexperienced in such analyses, a rudimentary system with a relatively low level of expertise is likely to be sufficient. Many authors (e.g., King and Morgan [1986] and Young [1988-2]) suggest that true expert systems must be capable of providing counsel that equals the proficiency of human experts. Knowledge-based systems may range from rudimentary intelligent assistance in limited problem domains through peer-level assistance at about an average practitioner's level to the proficiency of the top professionals in the field. There can be a hierarchy of progressively more expert systems for workers as they move from neophytes in off-line workplace decision-making to fully-fledged semi-autonomous participants in integrated on-line workplace responsibility. ## 5. Creative Process Support

These components support idea generation and reflective, creative activities. Idea Processing is the incubation and refinement of new ideas. Based on a review of non-computerized methods to enhance creativity, Young [1983, 1985, 1988-1] identified particular functions that could be included to aid in Idea Processing. These generally include the following functional components:

Divergent Search helps in generating alternatives. This centers on the classification of ideas, arranging and providing combinations of them.

Problem Redefinition helps examine where it is most desirable to define problem scope by identifying a hierarchy of related problem views from the most general to the most particular way of looking at the problem.

Idea Manipulation uses idea transformation operations (such as magnify, “minify” (make smaller), reverse, etc.) to convert the initial ideas into new ones.

Metaphorical Association helps to visualize familiar entities in new ways. Unusual associations or similarities between entities are discovered and later assessed for their utility.

Scenario Building/Analysis helps construct plausible sequences of events and situations. Building and assessing alternative scenarios may aid in understanding how to formulate policies likely to produce desired outcomes.

Computer support for Idea Processing can be used independently or it may be linked with an analytical DSS. If it is linked with DSS, as ideas develop, attain greater definition, and become more quantifiable, they can be transformed into mathematical model form so that spreadsheet analytical templates can be applied.

Not all modes of Idea Processing support have been implemented in software packages, but it seems likely that these will continue to be developed. Two existing microcomputer software packages that are in use are: Think Tank $^{TM}$ [Living Videotext] and Consultant $^{TM}$ [ODS]. Neither of these require mathematical sophistication or other specialized education. They are easy to learn, and may be useful tools for a basic worker support system.

Think Tank, known as an “outline processor”, has gained wide acceptance. As an indicator of market acceptance, it now has the competition of other packages such as Ashton Tate's Framework, which supports outlining as well as spreadsheets, graphs, and word processing, and Select Information Systems' Freestyle, an outline processor linked to word processing software. Applications of this kind of tool are unlimited and can apply to any subject of interest to the user. Common applications are planning the content and structure of proposals, reports, system specifications, or other written documents.

“Outlining” requires the hierarchical organization of ideas. It can be applied to the organization of a mental model of anything, including the design of a system, the formulation of a competitive strategy, a philosophy, or a policy. (Information systems designers are familiar with hierarchical documentation techniques such as Warnier-Orr diagrams, HIPO diagrams, or “visual table of contents”, in which the hierarchical structure of an information processing system or data set structure is represented in a symbolic hierarchical “tree” format that is logically equivalent to an outline.)

A well designed support system that aids in outlining is more than just a specially formatted word processor or automatic diagram generator. It is a kind of lever for the mind in thinking through hierarchical concepts. Its leverage derives from (1) releasing the user from the many manual tasks involved, so that effort can be extended in more comprehensive thinking about the subject matter, and (2) providing a visual highly dynamic “scratchpad” extension to the user’s working memory.

Consultant is a software package that represents a set of creativity aids in a basic form that could be used by individual workers or adapted for group use. Released in 1985, it runs on the MacIntosh. Consultant displays icon logos of a human skull divided down the middle, representing the right (“creative”) and left (“analytical”) brain hemispheres. The package displays the descriptive phrase “Helps you use your whole brain to think and work more productively.” See Exhibit 4.

There is a set of “Aid” functions associated with each half brain; one set consists of “Idea Generation Aids” and the other “Decision-Making Aids”. The latter includes quantitative methods of less interest in the present context.

“Procedures” are accessed by using “pull-down” menus in standard MacIntosh style. These include file and Edit menu headings for standard functions, such as naming and saving files, pringing out displays, cutting and pasting, etc. But the important thinking aid procedures are – Begin, Detect, Discover, Create, Plan, Sell, and Work.

Each Procedure menu consists of a list of prescribed steps to guide the user through a typical logical sequence, although the user can also usually start, procede, and stop anywhere. In following the procedure, the user only needs to respond to each menu item by typing in ideas as they may occur. However, the user can follow up by making use of one or more of the Aid functions develop these initial entries. This is especially useful for those with little experience in planning projects, problem-solving, etc. For this type of user, the frameworks are useful until they become so familiar that the user can create personal variations or skip them entirely.

The Plan menu, for example, consists of three groups of action items that represent a framework for a neophyte planner, as follows:

First Group Identify Goals, Establish Objectives, Determine Units, Quantify Objectives, Assign Time Limits.

Second Group Identify Tasks, Identify Interdependencies, Assign People, Identify Milestones.

Third Group List Resources, Quantify Resources, Determine Costs, Determine Benefits, Test Plan.

Two of the Idea Generation Aid are Brainstormer and Questioner. They are essentially aimed at stimulating the user to generate many ideas in response to a stimulus question, following the brainstorming method. According to this method, immediate assessment of new ideas is not desirable, but the user's responses can be saved and assessed later, with quantitative decision-making aids.

There is also a Combiner function, which is useful in aiding a divergent search for many alternatives. It differs from the previous two Idea Generation Aids in that it not only leads the user to respond, but also automatically generates combinations from elements from separate lists supplied by the user. These new combinations systematically present possibilities that mighty have otherwise been overlooked.

A fourth and last Idea Generation Aid is called Scenario. The Scenario function takes the user through separate steps of defining elements of an initial situation. It then asks the user to identify events that refer to these elements. This approach simplifies the development of a scenario by separating the definition of background elements from the events themselves. While scenario building and analysis has been generally thought of as a tool for high-level strategic assessment, it may be applicable to work groups who are considering the likely unfolding of events that may result from their own proposals to change work procedures and policies.

Additional software packages to aid thinking processes are emerging. One that concentrates on facilitating brainstorming is called The Idea Generator $^{TM}$ .

## 6. The Worker Information Center

Data bases accessible to workers could provide pre-formatted standard summary reports and listings (daily, weekly, monthly, etc.) as well as an ad hoc query capability. The extent of available information should be in keeping with the level of participation provided. If workers are involved only in Level 1 (Introductory Participation) and do not have direct control over work methods, providing much process monitoring information would, at best, serve little purpose, and at worst, would fuel management-labor disagreement. Thus, special care must be taken in doing requirements analysis for a worker information center in each organizational setting.

Within the limits of this caveat, information items to consider for a worker information center are: actual and standard production rates and volumes for different production operations and machines, machine utilization and maintenance histories, cost data for machines, labor, and materials, human factors such as fatigue effects on certain types of operations, and absenteeism and labor turnover rates. Confidential or privacy interests of individual workers must also be safeguarded in such a system and laws govern whether or not such data can be included. But summarized or categorically generic statistics may be provided in many cases. Performance data of individuals might be made available via the computer system only to an authorized individual, properly identified through a password or other safeguards. Such a feature might allow individuals to compare their performance to averages or standards, as well as to serve as guidelines in assessing proposed change. (The feedback of past performance, without any change in methods or monetary incentives has sometimes been shown to have a positive effect on future performance.)

Summarized regular performance reports have been a standard components of MIS. They are intended to provide managerial control of regular operations. In traditional organizations, this form of control has generally been the responsibility of managers rather than of workers. It has been claimed that, if work were organized differently and workers were given autonomy to control their own operations, the demand for and volume of control reports would radically decrease. The need for feedback, however, arises from the opportunity for unintended deviations (as well as from any intentional violations of rules by workers) to occur. Therefore even with the elimination of any purposeful misbehavior, workers will still require much of the feedback that would otherwise have been directed to their supervisors.

## 7. Analytical Templates

Several spreadsheet programs are available for industrial engineering functions (Kleinfeld [1987]). Some of these may be used by workers without highly specialized training. An example is given in Exhibit 5. This general format could also be used for regular actual reports provided within the Worker Information Center. By providing a spreadsheet template containing the same informational structure, the worker could substitute new values to replace actual values for such items as standard hours, down time, or actual hours, and perform a variety of “what if” analyses.

Other types of templates could include such functions as determining the effects of changing quality standards, varying test sample sizes, increasing labor rates on production costs, decreasing scrap rates on production costs, eliminating or combining steps in a production process, changing layout or process sequencing on materials travel time and costs, etc.

## 8. Group vs Individual Worker Support

Most software packages support individuals rather than groups. However, group support systems may be needed for worker participation. Two different modes may be applicable:

## (a) Group Communications Support

This provides group communications though actual processing remains under the control of individuals. Each user can selectively transmit results of their processing to others, with or without accompanying messages, and receive results and messages from others.

## (b) Support of Group Procedures

This includes special functions so that unique group decision and thinking processes are supported, rather than merely providing communication.

According to Huber [1984] and DeSanctis and Gallupe [1985], a true Group Decision Support System (GDSS) provides the second type of support for interdependent group decision processes, such as those in committees, project teams, or executive boards. Gray [1986] points out that although the evolution of GDSS concepts, research agendas, and initial experimentation has been rapid, it is still mainly in a laboratory stage. Its relationship to the support of qualitative forms of idea processing, however, is apparent, because some of the procedures were initially formulated as group processes. Among the GDSS applications mentioned by DeSanctis and Gallupe [1985] are brainstorming, Delphi (a process involving a sequence of assessment and compromises by experts), and nominal group technique (a method in which individuals work independently in the presence of others to prepare their own ideas before subsequently pooling these ideas for general discussion). Synectics, like brainstorming, was initially conceived of as a group procedure to support creativity, and would be a candidate for GDSS. Synectics use of metaphoric thinking, for example, could rely on group members to produce metaphors or could make use of a software generator within an Idea Processing Support system (Young [1987]).

## 10. Next Steps

Worker support systems can be implemented incrementally. The easiest first step appears to be a Worker Information System. This could use a microcomputer database software package, with some data downloaded from mainframe production management data bases. Other data could easily be key-entered; the data required is not likely to be voluminous.

The next step could be the introduction of selected spreadsheet templates, linked to the database. In parallel to this, existing thinking support packages could be introduced.

In a subsequent stage, basic knowledge-based modules could be developed using existing microcomputer software shells. Finally, more extensive expert systems, analytical templates, and Idea Processing support tools could be added to complete a Worker Support System.
