# Microcomputer Adoption – The Impact of Organizational Size and Structure

Mary R. Lind

North Carolina A&T State University, Greensboro, NC 27411, USA

Robert W. Zmud

Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL 32306, USA

William A. Fischer

University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC 27514, USA

Data collected from twenty-one firms were used to study the impact of organizational size and structure on the adoption of microcomputers in those firms. The results show that both size and structure had a significant impact on the adoption of microcomputers. Organizational size was measured as the organization's annual sales. The structural characteristics were measured as organizational links (lateral relations) between the information systems area and users of microcomputer technology.

Keywords: Linking mechanisms, Organizational size, Organizational structure, Microcomputer adoption, Infrastructure.

## Introduction

Among the many reasons given for such uninspired performance by larger firms has been the loss of the intimacy and flexibility inherent in smaller organizations. The purpose of this paper is to investigate the relative innovative behavior of large and small firms facing the same new technology – the microcomputer – while keeping in mind the mediating influence of organizational structure. $^{2}$

The principle virtue of smaller organizations with regard to innovative processes is that they possess a fairly simple organizational structure, e.g., many essential functions are performed informally. Larger organizations, on the other hand, are frequently ensnared in bureaucratic interrelationships which frustrate information sharing, risk-taking, or innovation. Galbraith $^{3}$ proposed that the development of strong lateral relations within an organization allows its members to cut across bureaucratic lines of authority, resulting in contact between functional areas fostering a more organic organization. The less formalized communication $^{4}$ encouraged by this organic structure enhances the climate for innovation.

In this paper the role of organizational structure in innovation will be considered in terms of linking mechanisms that facilitate lateral relations among an organization's units. These linking mechanisms are seen as structural alternatives to traditional bureaucratic structures through which an organization's units cooperate in performing work activity. Such links can reflect very personal one-to-one contact or impersonal mechanisms such as newsletters or reports. Often, they occur through collective, or group, efforts to bring members representing distinct organizational units together.

## The State of Organizational Information Technologies

Functional areas in organizations are increasingly using microcomputer technology in carrying out their day-to-day activities. Benjamin $^{5}$ stated in 1982 that 40% of the computer-based processing capacity utilized microcomputers and predicted an increase to 75% by 1990. This prediction is being realized as microcomputers have diffused throughout the business units of many firms and are used for a variety of applications ranging from simple, self-contained uses, such as word processing, to quite sophisticated uses, such as plant scheduling.

To support this rapid increase in micro-based computing, information systems managers are introducing support mechanisms that encourage the development of microcomputer applications across business functions. $^{6}$ As the information systems group becomes more involved in distribution and technology transfer activities as well as its traditional manufacturing activities, $^{7}$ it is increasingly important that lateral relations $^{8}$ be established between the users and information systems. Managerial techniques to promote lateral relations are referred to as linking mechanisms (see Table 1). Newsletters and user questionnaires are linking mechanisms that provide user support without any personal contact between the information systems staff and microcomputer business unit members. Internal consultants, user liaisons, user consultants, information centers, and training sessions involve one-on-one contacts between a linking mechanism representative and a user. Pilot programs, steering committees, task forces, technology assessment groups, and user groups involve representatives from the information systems staff and from user departments.

Examples of such user domination are clearly found in Von Hippel's studies of innovation in the manufacture of semiconductors and electronic subassemblies. $^{10}$ In these industries, the users typically perceived the need for the innovation, conceived the solution, built a prototype, tested the prototype, and diffused the innovation. It is probably important to note that many of the users in Von Hippel's study were engineers. Microcomputer users, as defined in this research, includes a broad base of administrative and operational personnel, most of whom are college educated. These users share with von Hippel's users an intimate knowledge of their work process. Because of this knowledge, these users should be essential to, and facilitate, the adoption of microcomputers in the organization.

The linking mechanisms examined in this paper support two distinct activities. First, in order for users to make effective use of microcomputers, there needs to be a microcomputer infrastructure in place consisting of generic hardware resources and software tools. Linking mechanisms play a major role in the establishment of, and are embedded in, such an infrastructure. Once this microcomputer infrastructure is established, however, the locus of concern is likely to shift to that of supporting users in implementing sophisticated applications.

The microcomputer infrastructure of an organization can be described as its core ability to support end-user information processing activities. This infrastructure consists of computers, software, databases, and communications capability that allow the organization to process information. The nature of this infrastructure can enhance or constrain end-user computing. The more munificent this infrastructure in terms of its accessibility and features, the more that end-user computing is facilitated. This is particularly true of microcomputer use in functional areas, where readily accessible technology enables users to integrate the technology as a part of their normal work activities. The importance of accessibility has been supported by studies where information systems use has been shown to be related to perceived accessibility. $^{12}$

In applying microcomputers to application areas, users apply microcomputer-based tools in performing their work. The raison d'etre of end-user computing is to facilitate user activities – decision making, factory and warehouse activities, and administrative tasks. Application software provides users with tools to increase productivity in performing these tasks. Thus, to ensure the adoption of microcomputer technology within the firm there must be not only an adequate technological infrastructure but there must be recognition of opportunities to apply appropriate application software to support these users.

## Hypotheses

The focus of this paper is to analyze the effects of size and structure on the adoption of microcomputers. As Schumpeter indicated, size is frequently thought to be the most important factor in technology innovation because large firms are seemingly better endowed with resources than smaller firms. In addition, it would appear reasonable to suppose that large organizations have more potential to use microcomputers than smaller organizations, simply because of their larger scale of operations. Thus,

Hypothesis # 1 Size is a significant predictor of microcomputeradoption.

Others, however, have argued that size can, in fact, be more of a problem than a source of advantage in innovation and that it is organizational structure, instead, which is the more meaningful explainer of innovative activity. Here, structure is represented by the presence of linking mechanisms which are used to support the adoption of new technologies. In the present study, such linking mechanisms facilitate both building the technological infrastructure and implementing sophisticated applications.

Hypothesis #2 Structure is a significant predictor of microcomputer adoption.

A third issue to be examined involves assessing which type of links are more effective – those used to introduce microcomputers (e.g., targeted at the microcomputer infrastructure) or those used to nourish and reinforce end-user computing (e.g., targeted at sophisticated applications). Organizations just beginning to adopt microcomputer technology will need linking mechanisms to develop the infrastructure in order to provide accessible microcomputer technology. Once this infrastructure exists, linking mechanisms that support sophisticated applications should prove more useful. For organizations just beginning to adopt micro-technology, such as those queried at the time of this study (1983), it is proposed that linking mechanisms used to support the building of a microcomputer infrastructure will be more effective than those used to support sophisticated applications. Thus,

Hypothesis #3 For these firms which are in the early stages of end-user computing, linking mechanisms supporting the technical infrastructure are more effective at promoting microcomputer adoption than those that support sophisticated applications.

## The Study

## Microcomputer Adoption

To measure the extent of technological innovation in the sampled firms, the dependent variable, microcomputer adoption, is determined by the total number of microcomputers in use in each firm. This measure indicates the degree to which the technology has been adopted. Although this measure gives no indication of the extent to which the microcomputer technology is being utilized, it does indicate the information processing potential in the firms that have acquired them and the extent to which they have invested in this technology.

Size

Organizational size ${}^{13}$ was proposed by Schumpeter ${}^{14}$ as an important factor in technological innovation. To measure the independent variable, organizational size, the annual 1983 sales for each organization was obtained from archival sources. Annual sales rather than the number of employees was used since these industries (Table 2) vary in their level of labor intensity. Also, using the number of employees as a measure of size might indicate the degree of technological automation of the organization (e.g., highly automated firms might be expected to have reduced labor costs) and confound the results when testing the effect of size on technological innovation.

## Methodology

The data for this study were collected between March 1, 1983 and July 30, 1983. Structured interviews were conducted by the same interviewer at twenty-one firms (Table 2) with information systems managers. Objective information detailing the numbers of microcomputers and linking mechanisms used to support the micro-technology infrastructure areas and the application areas was obtained during the interview. The size measure, the total sales of each organization as mentioned earlier, was obtained from archival sources. Examination of the correlation matrix for the variables considered in the regression equations revealed no multicollinearity problems.

## Discussion

The results of this study indicate that both size and structure are significant predictors of the adoption of microcomputers. Such results do support Schumpeter's proposition that size can provide a significant explanation for technological innovation even, in the early stages of a technology's development. $^{15}$ In addition, organizational structure (here, represented as the number of linking mechanisms used by the information systems function in support of microcomputer usage) also appears to be an appropriate variable for explaining technological innovativeness.

When large organizations provide linking mechanisms to support microcomputer users throughout the organization, they exhibit a higher adoption rate of microcomputer technology than firms that do not provide such linkages. Because of their size, large organizations are likely to possess greater slack and to exhibit a greater diversity of activities within their boundaries. These research results suggest that in order to manage and coordinate these slack resources and activities, lateral and hierarchical relationships between technology providers and users are needed to induce and facilitate desired behaviors.

At the time these data were collected, most of the firms surveyed were just beginning to apply microcomputer technology. Many of their micro-based applications were self-contained – not integrated with other user applications or with production information systems. For these organizations, primarily in this early stage of microcomputer adoption, the study's results suggest that linking mechanisms targeted at building and supporting the technology infrastructure are most effective. We expect that once this technology infrastructure is built, linking mechanisms targeted at sophisticated applications should grow in importance. Our research design, however, does not allow us to assess such a proposition. In summary, both size and structure appear to play important roles in microcomputer adoption. This suggests that both Schumpeter and the proponents of structural explanations of innovation are correct in their interpretations of the technology adoption process.

Research is needed that develops a better understanding of the alternatives for providing lateral relations to link together users and information systems staff in order that link attributes that contribute to technological innovation during both the early and later stages of technology adoption can be identified. Research is also needed that investigates exactly what size does add to technological innovation. Finally, it would be desirable to devise a measure of size that assesses an organization's potential volume and capacity for doing work rather than simply describing number of employees or amount of sales. Hull and Hage $^{16}$ offer scale as an alternative for measuring size. They argue that the 'number of repetitive events occurring within an organization over time', which they call scale, is more important than the number of employees, number of sales dollar, or number of patients and thus provides an indicator of the amount of work done rather than a measure of an organization's inputs and outputs. With such a measure, it might be possible to begin unraveling the intertwined influences of size and structure on technological innovation.
