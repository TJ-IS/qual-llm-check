# Business Graphics Trends, Two Years Later

John A. Lehman

School of Management, University of Alaska, 118 Bunnell, Fairbanks, AK 99775, USA

V. Samba Murthy

MIS Research Center School of Management, University of Minnesota, 271 19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

This paper is a report of a longitudinal study of computer graphics use by management. It documents the transition of graphics from a leading edge technology at the contagion stage, to a relatively mature technology. This transition is manifested by a slowdown in the rate of growth and by improved integration into the system of organizational tasks and the information systems required to support those tasks.

Keywords: Computer Graphics, Business Graphics, Personal Computers, End User Computing, User-Acceptance, Success Factors.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Background

The topic of business graphics has been considered an important issue for MIS research, at least since the publication of the Mason-Mitroff framework in 1972 [1]. In 1982, Ives [2] published a literature survey of research in the area; this was followed by another literature survey by DeSanctis [3] in 1984. The reader is referred to the latter two papers for summaries of the state-of-the-art as of 1984, both in terms of experimentation and of management practice.

To update knowledge of management practice, in November 1984 a research group at the University of Minnesota published the results of a survey of MIS managers concerning business graphics [4]. At that time, these managers were forecasting very high growth for business graphics within their organizations. In late 1986, this survey was repeated to see to what extent this growth had occurred. This article describes the results.

This form of longitudinal study provides interesting details on the cycle of growth and maturation for a new technology. Prior to 1982, the use of graphics for management was very much a leading edge technology – adapted only by a relatively few innovators. There are two major themes which arise from analysis of these studies, both consistent with the hypothesis that use of graphics by management represents a maturing technology. The first is that, while there has been substantial growth over the past few years, the rate of growth is slowing. As will be seen, it appears that this slowing may be due to organizational rather than technical factors. The other related theme is that, as the use of graphic technology has spread, it has become better integrated into the system of organizational tasks and the information systems required to support those tasks.

## 1.2. Study Details

The survey and sampling frames are described in Appendix A. Probability values given throughout this paper are based on matched T-tests between the two surveys, unless otherwise indicated. A cutoff level of p=0.01 was used; in other words, where a change is reported, there is only one chance in one hundred that the reported change is statistically spurious.

The 1984 study identified four major categories of information about the use of business graphics:

— the existing situation,

— user satisfaction,

— impediments to increased use, and

— the future.

The next four sections discuss changes in these categories over the period of the study. The final section summarizes the major changes and their implications for MIS managers.

## 2. Existing Situation

In both surveys, the respondents were primarily managers, directors, and vice presidents representing corporate headquarters. Most of the respondents came from manufacturing organizations, followed by financial services, utilities, retailing, and engineering.

The survey looked at business graphics use within these organizations in terms of:

— technological capabilities,

— motivation for use,

— functional area use,

— end users,

— applications profiles, and

— organizational responsibilities.

## 3. Technological Capabilities

In 1984, 90% off the respondents had business graphics capability, but many of them had had the facilities for a year or less. Less than half of the respondents had the ability to link microcomputers to mainframes other than as dumb terminals. Relative to the size of organizations, investment in business graphics hardware and software was relatively low. There were few graphics work stations.

In 1986, graphics were in use in slightly more organizations and, as expected, business graphics experience has increased considerably. The passage of time explains most of this increase (Figure 1). Extensive use of microcomputers and micro-mainframe links has grown considerably, but other technological factors had remained approximately the same.

In terms of graphics devices, personal computers with graphics displays have shown the most growth (almost tripling). In related trend, microcomputer-based packages have become much more available (p = 0.001) while growth of mainframe-based packages has been relatively flat (Figure 2). No single microcomputer-based software package dominates the market to the extent that Lotus 123 did in 1984. Use of multiple packages appears to be the emerging norm.

An interesting change which illustrates organizational adaptation to new technology in this area is the hardware strategy selected for business graphics. In 1984, there were three distinct strategies: graphics on microcomputers, graphics on mainframes, and a mixed hardware strategy. The factors associated with success for each strategy were somewhat different, as were the user profiles [5], [6]. Many of the users of PC-based graphics appeared to be in rebellion against their data-processing departments; many of the centralized systems were used only by data processing departments. Over the past several years, the use of graphics appears to have become better integrated into overall organizational information systems; there are few exemplars of the pure PC or pure mainframe strategy among the respondents to the followup survey. While a study of individual end users found that such users tended to use one or the other type of technology [7], the organizations now tend to support both. This is consistent with the spread of information centers and related efforts to both legitimize and to control end-user computing [8], [9].

## 4. Motivation for Use

In 1984, most respondents cited end user demand as the major factor influencing the decision to buy computer graphics, followed by desire to explore new technology. Since then, end user demand as a factor influencing purchase has increased in importance whereas desire to explore new technology has decreased (Figure 3). User departments are still the first to voice need for computer graphics, although top management's role has increased substantially in the past two years (Figure 4). All of these trends are significant at the 0.000 level.

## 5. Functional Area Use

In 1984, only MIS, finance, and marketing were regular heavy users of computer graphics. In 1986, MIS and marketing are still dominant. Finance is relatively less dominant than was the case two years ago. Use of graphics by planning and by research and development has grown considerably, while use by other areas has remained approximately the same (Figure 5). Changes by all except production, personnel, MIS, and planning are significant at the 0.001 level or better.

## 6. End Users

In 1984, very few end users had business graphics experience; since then the percent of users with such experience has increased considerably (p = 0.000) (Figure 6). Use by professionals has grown considerably (p = 0.000), use by managerial assistants has grown somewhat (p = 0.000), while use by managers, executives and graphics artists has stayed about the same (Figure 7; changes for these last areas are not statistically significant).

## 7. Applications Profiles

Computer graphics is now used more to support written reports (p = 0.000) than was the case several years ago and less to support informal communication (p = 0.000). Relative to the total, use for decision support has decreased by about one-third (Figure 8); while actual use has remained about the same. Lower and middle managers use graphics as a decision support tool somewhat less than in the past (p = 0.000) (Fig. 9); high level managers use them at about the same rate (Fig. 10).

This last set of responses is important because they provide field support for the results of a series of experiments ([10], [11], [12], [13]) and additional surveys (e.g. [14]) carried out by researchers in the management graphics area. These have found that graphics are not as useful for decision making and decision support as was previously thought, but that they are useful for persuasion and other forms of communication.

## 10. Impediments to Increased Use

In 1984, the major impediments to increased use were problems with database integration, lack of user knowledge, and system integration. These are still the major problems today: there has been no statistically significant change. Technical difficulty is now considered slightly more important than in the past (p = 0.000), whereas high cost is no longer a major impediment (p = 0.000) (Figure 14). Increasingly, the problems are personal or organizational: insufficient user knowledge (p = 0.000) and the fact that potential users do not consider the use of computer graphics (p = 0.000). These non-technical aspects, coupled with the decreasing importance of cost as prices fall, are consistent with the hypothesis that managerial graphics use in organizations is reaching saturation.

## 11. The Future

Consistent also with this hypothesis, the forecast growth rate for computer graphics is now considerably less than it was in 1984, when 40% of the respondents expected that graphics would grow substantially. Today only 24% forecast large growth (Figure 15).

Among the applications areas where respondents expect computer graphics growth, decision support is considered substantially less important than in 1984 (p = 0.000), while replacement of routine data processing reports is somewhat less important. Expected use for data analysis, informal communication, and oral presentations is about the same (Figure 16). This is consistent with the hypothesis that use of graphics for decision support did not prove as successful as expected, and that communication has proven to be a useful area of application.

## 12. Summary and Conclusions

Our study examined the penetration of management oriented computer graphics into organizations. The basic findings were:

\- Considerable penetration has occurred since 1984,

\- Graphics have become better integrated into the overall organizational information system,

\- The rate of growth has slowed, primarily due to organizational rather than technical issues,

\- Factors in the success of computer graphics remain the same: ease of use and data availability, and

\- Applications have shifted from support of decision making to support of communication.

## 12.1. Implications for Information Systems Managers

The research provides empirical support for what is probably common sense among most managers. There are three classes of activity that appear to foster greater acceptance of graphics technology for management applications:

\- organizational support,

\- access to tools, and

\- access to data.

Organizational support is the provision of support staff, training, user groups, newsletters, helplines, and other forms of handholding. It appears that training and the existence of a dedicated support staff are the forms of support most highly correlated to user satisfaction.

Access to tools is making graphics systems widely available. The availability of inexpensive PCs has probably contributed most to the spread of graphics, simply because a member of the organization is more likely to use a system that is geographically convenient. Centrally located systems are not likely to foster use, except in small organizations.

Access to data is a major impediment to use of graphics systems. Traditionally, most graphics packages have been either stand-alone or have worked only with a narrow range of data (such as those from a particular statistics or database package). Integration of graphics capabilities with the ability to retrieve any authorized data is probably the major remaining task in integrating graphics into the overall organizational information system.

## Appendix A

## Description of the Survey

The survey instrument is reproduced in [15]. In 1984, it was pretested on associates of the Management Information Systems Research Center at the University of Minnesota. No participants in the pretest were included in the final mailing (although some of the same organizations were represented). The final version of the survey was sent to the data processing managers of 789 organizations nationwide. All were members either of the Society for Information Management or affiliates of the Center for the Study of Data Processing at Washington University, St. Louis. Individual members, academic institutions, consultants, and other non-institutional users were eliminated from the mailing lists. About 25% of those sent the survey responded.

In 1986, all original respondents were mailed the same instrument as a followup. An additional judgement sample of National Computer Graphics Association members was selected, resembling the original group in job title and organization type. Overall usable response rate was approximately 30%.

There are several biases which are likely in the responses to the survey. First, the choice of sampling frame is not representative; members of the groups which were sampled are likely to be more state-of-the-art than data processing managers as a whole. Secondly, it is likely that information center managers were under-represented. Third, outside of checking for industry bias, we are not able to pinpoint non-response biases. Geographical distribution of the responses, as evidenced by ZIP codes, did not appear to differ substantially from that of the original sample frame.
