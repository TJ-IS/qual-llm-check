# Aligning MIS with the Business Goals

Ravinder Nath

Department of Management Information Systems and Decision Sciences, Fogelman College of Business & Economics, Memphis State University, Memphis, TN 38152, U.S.A.

A preliminary field study of MIS and non-MIS (general) managers was undertaken; it was designed to identify those factors which the MIS management and the general management perceived as important in aligning MIS with business goals of their organizations. The results are based upon a questionnaire study conducted in 1987. This involved managers/executives in a large metropolitan city in the southwestern U.S.A. The three most important factors which MIS management identified are: educating upper management in MIS, upper management commitment to MIS, and a strong set of organizational goals and objectives concerning MIS. On the other hand, the top three factors identified by general managers are: educating upper management in MIS, ability of MIS management to keep up with advances in information technology (IT), and educating MIS management in business goals and objectives. While upper management commitment is deemed critical by both groups in aligning MIS with their organizations' business goals, there is lack of agreement on the other issues. These issues are identified so that all management can take actions to achieve the alignment between the management information function and the business goals and objectives of the organization.

Keywords: IS Management, IS Alignment, Business Goals.

## 1. Introduction

To harness the potential of information systems (IS) as competitive tools, it is paramount to align the management information function with business goals and objects of the organization. Without this, the MIS department may develop IS incapable of providing the right information to the executive users. “Alignment” has ranked fairly high on the list of information issues, as evidenced by many published studies. For instance, Dickson, et al., in 1983, using the Delphi approach, identified and ranked several important issues [3]. According to their study of MIS managers, “alignment of MIS with business goals” ranked seventh. Brancheau and Wetherbe, in 1986, using the same survey approach, found alignment issue fifth in importance [1]. In a 1985 survey of sixty-three MIS managers in the Saint Louis area, Hartog and Herbert reported that “aligning MIS with the business goals” was perceived to be the second most important issue (the most important was “planning”) [5]. Their study published in DATA-MATION found alignment to be the most important issue [6]; it was based upon a sample of six hundred companies and was jointly conducted by DATAMATION and the Center for the Study of Data Processing (CSDP), Washington University, St. Louis.

Since “alignment of MIS with the business goals” has received such high rankings, the question is: what does it take to align MIS with the business goals of an organization? In order to answer this question, a survey was conducted of MIS and general managers. It was designed to (1) identify issues thought to effect the alignment (2) ascertain whether MIS and general managers agree or disagree on the relative importance of these issues, and (3) assess how their organizations are faring on each of the issues.

The results of this study are interesting. MIS and general managers differ in their relative rankings of the important issues. Also, there are significant differences in the way that they perceive the performance of their organization in these important factors.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. The Questionnaire

The data was collected using a questionnaire consisting of two parts. In the first, each respondent was requested to list five factors that they believed to be important in aligning MIS with business goals. Then they were asked to rank these five factors with one for the most important factor, two for the next, etc. For data analysis purposes, the ranks were converted to scores from five to one (score = 6 - rank). This information provided the importance scores and thus allowed us to identify the factors critical to MIS alignment.

The respondents also rated these factors: how well does the organization do this? The performance ratings used a 5-point Likert type scale (1 = very poor; 2 = poor; 3 = average; 4 = good; and 5 = very good). This information allowed a comparison of the perceptions of MIS managers with those of general managers on the organizational performance.

The second part of the questionnaire elicited information about the respondents and their organizations. This allowed formulation of the respondent's profile.

## 2.2. The Respondents and Questionnaire Administration

The questionnaire was mailed to one-hundred MIS managers/executives in a large metropolitan city in the southwestern USA. A list of MIS managers/executives was compiled using two sources. One was the DPMA directory. To assure that only MIS managers are included in the study, only those with job titles indicating that they were managers or executives, were selected for inclusion on the list. The second source was a list of the names and addresses of MIS managers of major corporations compiled by the author. The two sources were pooled and duplicate names and organizations were removed to guarantee that the same individual did not appear more than once, and also that only one manager was selected from an organization. From this final list, a sample of 100 managers was randomly selected. Sixty-two usable questionnaires were returned.

In order to collect data from general managers/executives, the author administered the same questionnaire to a convenient sample of two executive MBA classes, resulting in forty-six usable questionnaires. The executive MBA students attend classes during the weekends and all of them have at least five years of managerial work experience at the middle or top level management. In spite of the fact that this group does not constitute a random sample from the theoretical population of general managers, their responses should not be significantly biased because: (1) the questionnaires were administered before the beginning of any class work, (2) each respondent held a full-time managerial position while enrolled in the executive MBA program, and (3) the respondents represented various types of organizations and functional areas. ## 3. Summary of Results

Each respondent was asked to list their top five factors, and obviously, there were a large number of different ones listed. The computed importance (6 - rank) were averaged over all respondents to obtain an importance score. Similarly, the performance score was also calculated. Table 1 reports the top fifteen factors in terms of importance, as identified by MIS and general managers. The top fifteen are the same for MIS and general managers, however, their relative rankings differ substantially. Table 1 also displays the means and standard deviations of importance scores. In order to ascertain whether the MIS and general managers differ on their rankings of the issues, even though it seems unlikely to an observer, a statistical measure of agreement was employed between the two sets of rankings. Kendall's Tau is one statistical measure of such agreement [4]. Kendall's Tau of 0.35, with P = 0.072 is statistically not significant at the 0.05 level, indicating a lack of agreement between two groups. To identify those factors where significant differences exist, t-tests were employed. Out of the fifteen t-values (one for each factor), four were found to be significant at the 0.05 level. Figure 3 displays a bar chart of the differences between MIS and general managers' importance scores for the fifteen issues. An asterisk next to the bar indicates that significant differences exist; they are: cost justification by MIS (factor #6), ability of MIS management to keep up with advances in information technology (IT) (factor #10), inter-departmental planning for MIS applications (factor #14), and user friendly systems (factor #15).

Another issues for which significant disagreements exist are: cost justification by MIS (MIS rank = 6.5; GM rank = 15); interdepartmental planning for MIS applications (MIS rank = 14.5; GM rank = 7); and user friendly systems (MIS rank = 14.5; GM rank = 8). Surprisingly, general managers attach very low importance to cost justification by MIS. One explanation of this could be that IS is often viewed as a strategic or competitive tool as well as supporting routine functions; therefore the GM does not place high priority on cost justification, as was done in the past. This needs more amplification.

Inter-departmental planning for MIS applications also raises many questions. Perhaps, there is fairly good interdepartmental low-level planning, but less at the higher levels. Nevertheless, it is not clear why their perceptions differ so significantly.

Do managers rate factors with poor perceived organizational performance high on the importance scale? To attempt to answer this question, an analysis of the importance and performance scores is necessary. Also, the performance scores of MIS and general managers need to be compared, so that the factors with significantly different belief in organizational performance can be identified. Table 2 is a summary of performance scores assessed by MIS and general managers. Rankings are also included for the two groups.

Now, in order to ascertain whether factors with low perceived organizational performance are assigned high importance, Pearson's correlation between the two variables (importance and performance) for each group of managers is computed. For MIS managers, the correlation is -0.49 (p < 0.05), significant at the 0.05 level. This negative correlation indicates the presence of the suspected relationship, namely, assigning high importance to factors with low organization performance and vice versa. However, no such relationship exists for the general managers (Pearson's correlation = 0.068; p > 0.40).

## 3.4. Importance vs Performance

Figures 5 and 6 display a comparison of the importance and performance scores for general and MIS managers, respectively. To compare importance, values of a new variable $V_{i}$ are bar charted. This is

$$
\mathrm{V} _ {\mathrm{i}} = ((\text { Mean   Importance   for   factor   i / Mean   Organizational   Performance   for   factor   i }) - 1)
$$

Note that $V_{i} > 0$ indicates that organizational performance of a factor is below its importance. In Figure 5, only $V_{6} < 0$ , indicating that “cost justification by MIS” is perceived to have higher organization performance than its importance, as judged by general managers. Performance on other factors is perceived as falling short of importance in aligning MIS with business goals. ## 4. Conclusions and Comments

The alignment of an MIS enterprise with the business goals of an organization has consistently emerged as one of the key issues facing information systems (IS), as well as, general executives. Our study was conducted to find out what is considered important towards this alignment by both the MIS community and the non-MIS community. We were also interested in knowing what the current state of affairs was on these issues within the organizations. The results of this study has brought out the following points:

1. In an effort to align MIS with business goals, general managers consider the ability of MIS personnel to keep up with the emerging information technologies (IT) extremely important.

Furthermore, general managers give a low grade to MIS staff on this issue, as evidenced by the low organizational performance score. Cash and McLeod [2] recognize this issue and stated “...that the requisite processes of technology forecasting, tracking, research and development, introduction, and management of new technology are unwiedly and almost impossible to operationalize, given traditional administrative and organizational approaches.” Their recommendation is the formation of a new organizational unit with specific responsibilities for these processes: they call this unit the Emerging Technology (ET) group. Our research results support this recommendation.

2. Another significant area where MIS and general managers disagree is in inter-departmental planning for MIS applications. General managers rank it seventh, whereas IS managers rank it at the bottom of the list. Also, organizational performance scores are significantly different. Why this discrepancy? One plausible explanation is that from an MIS perspective, much lower level inter-departmental planning is occurring in the form of requirement analyses and implementation. However, the IS executive wants to see higher level interaction where the real advantages of information systems are.

3. There is a complete lack of congruence of the two groups on the organizational performance of the factors critical for aligning MIS with business goals. All in all, the general managers consistently score the factors lower on performance than do MIS managers.

Our research reveals differing perceptions and a lack of congruence between MIS and general managers on issues of what is important, and where the organizations stand in aligning management information systems enterprise with business goals. Many of the factors deemed important for the alignment have been studied extensively in the MIS literature. But some new factors have evolved and need attention. The most significant is a new factor: the ability of MIS management to keep up with advances in information technology.

It is imperative that the gap between general and MIS managers' perceptions be narrowed, if not completely bridged if the alignment of MIS with business goals is to be attained.
