# Organization Design Strategies To Enhance Information Resource Management

John T. Nosek

Computer and Information Sciences Division, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122, USA

To optimize their management of information resources, organizations are looking for higher payoff, higher risk systems to support important ill-structured problems. These systems use evolutionary development techniques. The success of such methods are critically constrained by technology, design and programming practices, and the organizational environment. But these constraints are actually variables, and given the fiscal and political realities of the organization, an optimum balance can be made among them.

Keywords: Organization Design, Information Resource Management, Evolutionary Development, Organizational Environment, IS Development, IS Reward Systems, Organizational Structure, IS Personnel Policies, Information Management, Ill-structured Problems.

John Nosek is an Assistant Professor in CIS at Temple University. He received his B.S. in Naval Science from the United States Naval Academy, his M.S. in Computer Science from Villanova University, and his Ph.D. in the area of MIS from Temple University. His main interest has been exploring technical and organizational environments that facilitate evolutionary development of IS. He is currently focusing on cooperative computing efforts to support information needs as

sessment and information requirements analysis.

## 1. Introduction

The notion of information as an organizational resource broadens its interpretation. A resource can be:

(a) a source of supply or support: an available means;

(b) a natural source of wealth or revenue;

![](/api/attachments/HVT4UWUP/fulltext/images/5f26e97f35fc8efd6f5ac447832367ec52f6e32648599b57b41f1d16eaefe74d.jpg)

(c) a source of expertise;

(d) something one turns to in the absence of a usual means or source or supply.

However, the notion of information as a resource creates the idea, maybe falsely, that it can be managed as other organizational resources.

In extracting the most from information resources, we see four major constraints:

(1) Hardware innovation;

(2) Software development;

(3) Design and programming practice (development methodologies); and

(4) The organizational environment.

Organizational design is the continuous monitoring and assessing of the fit between goals, structures, and rewards. Galbraith [17] lists five areas of choice in determining an organization [see Figure 1]:

(1) Task;

(2) Structure;

(3) Information and decision process;

(4) Reward Systems; and

(5) People.

The first three constraints in optimizing information resources are further constrained by what technical innovations (hardware and software) are available in an organization, and what design and programming practices are accepted and promoted within it. Decision makers should treat the constraints as variables, and, given the fiscal and political realities of the organization, attempt an optimum balance among them. For example, an organization may be fiscally limited to current hardware and software, however, choice may still be made in organizational design, and development methodologies to optimize the information resources.

Fig. 1. Organization Design Variables.

The greatest strength of IS managers is their technical expertise, and much has been written on technical innovation [5,7,9,31] and the use of various development methodologies and conceptual frameworks [16,21,33,35,38]. We focus on the choices in organizational design – specifically, on the choices in structure, reward systems, and people to support the information and decision processes employed in accomplishing organizational tasks. Human performance is determined primarily by ability and motivation [12]; and the organizational environment impacts directly on ability and motivation [see Figure 2].

This paper has two major purposes: (1) the synthesis of research literature to aid in organization design choice; and (2) to act as a catalyst for managers to view structure, reward systems, and people as organizational variables and for managers to use their innate creativity in designing innovative systems and structures.

## 2. Demands of a Changing Environment

The following are a set of facts on the organizational environment:

![](/api/attachments/HVT4UWUP/fulltext/images/f86dd433db007ee12453184b41a96ff84d35a259a09e1593eb8a178d9124b402.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Human Performance Determinants.

1: Changes in organizational goals change the motivation for developing an IS [12,13,27].

2: Using a system changes the organizational expectations more than the abstract specification of needs in an early phase of the design process, and these organizational expectations are particularly significant in changing system requirements [2,25,30].

3: For the most part, users can not specify in advance the criteria for judging the actual system. They use tacit knowledge to note instances of misfit of the system and to judge its acceptability [1].

4: The more competent domain experts become, the less they are able to describe the knowledge they use to solve problems [37].

5: When experts attempt to explain how they reached a conclusion, they often construct plausible lines of reasoning that bear little resemblance to their actual problem-solving activity [24].

6: As systems become more oriented towards decision support, it becomes more important to adopt an evolutionary, incremental, highly participative approach to system development [18,22,26].

7: Expert system builders do not have a series of well-defined steps that they follow. The inherent complexity of the system building process precludes laying out all the steps in advance. As a result, they use evolutionary development [37].

8: Lientz and Swanson found that user enhancements accounted for 42% of the total maintenance effort, and propose that IS requirements should not be thought of as static during any period of the development cycle [30].

9: Future information requirements are not merely unknown, they are unknowable [14,40].

As organizations begin to optimize their information resources, they are looking for higher pay-off, higher risk systems to support the ill-structured problems of decision makers. The growing inability to specify requirements in advance for such systems demands evolutionary development, which entails having the IS built incrementally. It is not a trivial idea that systems are developed piece by piece, but a conscious design strategy to shorten feedback loops between the user and the designer to develop and maintain a satisfactory system. At each cycle in systems development, users are able to live with a new, at least partially usable version of the system. information system [2,11]. Evolutionary development demands a more integrated, more interactive process; and this demands greater risk-taking to develop more flexible, more creative, organizational designs to support the process.

## 3. Choices in Structure

Decision makers can choose the division of labor, the organizational configuration, and the distribution of power. The level of these structural changes can be at the organizational and the departmental level.

In evolutionary development, the user is the expert who must model the decision process and data processing personnel must provide the support system for this model. Because of the changeable nature of the problem, integration is a continual concern which calls for organic organizational forms rather than the strict separation of tasks that are found in the mechanistic organizational form [6,23,28].

Figure 3 presents various structural options. (a) Hierarchical strategies maintain the strict levels of reporting structures. In decentralized organizations, the data processing professional works within a functional department; in more centralized organizations, functional experts are employed by the data processing department to assist in system development; the Theory Z organization promotes a structure where the title “programmer” is earned after as many as ten years of service within various organizational jobs. This is closely related to Japanese methods: the programmer expects to work for one company, and it invests in these programmers by providing comprehensive training programs. Licker suggests that these more experienced “programmers” will develop much more effective systems [29].

(b) We also present 2 sample matrix organizations. As users become more involved in the development of their own systems and assume more control over their IS needs, structural changes in the IS department must be made to provide a professional staff (a) when users realize the limitations of self designed systems; and (b) to transfer technology to users (Information Centers). Some organizations are also creating technology transfer organizations to provide support for their data processing staff.

If the IS is judged to be of a high enough priority, based on organizational goals, it will be developed under the second tier of the relationship – co-ownership.

Rather than the producing department trying to get the user/consumer to sign-off the job, the IS becomes the co-product of the user and the development group within the data processing department. ## 4. Reward Systems

Organizations can rely neither on voluntary adoption of goal related behaviors nor on willing compliance with directives – there must be a fit among the task, the behaviors, and the reward system for high productivity and individual satisfaction.

Figure 5 identifies five different behaviors, from join and remain to cooperative; in this direction they become cumulatively harder to develop and more costly. The greater the uncertainty of the task, the greater the required behavior.

While rule compliance and system rewards, such as vacations, retirement benefits, sick leaves, working condition, etc., can favorably affect join and remain and dependable role behavior, these types of extrinsic rewards have little or no affect on the higher order behaviors needed for successful evolutionary development.

## FIVE BEHAVIORS:

![](/api/attachments/HVT4UWUP/fulltext/images/a93c2346495562d7a6ee6fe833a99fc0e07d174ec2fa1e292913c10c3832ce6a.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Behavioral Goals of Reward Systems.

There is no doubt that when an individual's pay is clearly dependent upon performance, it is higher than when pay and performance are unrelated. However, when the organization defines which outcomes will be rewarded, it also defines those that will not. But for uncertain tasks it is difficult to make this distinction. Luzi & MacKenzie warn that the lack of adaptability of incentive systems to actual organizational and economic realities may cause performance based incentive systems to be counter-productive [32]. An alternative is to use more global, objective measures that reflect spontaneous and cooperative behaviors of the group. The smaller it is, the more effective the reward, however, while group rewards increase cooperation within a group, it decreases cooperation between them. In any case, necessary activities that are difficult to observe or measure and may lead to payoffs some time in the future are unlikely to occur under monetary reward systems.

Intrinsic rewards are those that are a natural consequence of the behavior itself and are independent of any external rewards. Galbraith identifies two intrinsic rewards: task involvement and goal identification. He stresses their importance in insuring higher order behaviors.

Goal identification occurs to the extent that individuals have incorporated, into their value systems, the goals or subgoals of the organization. This has the most positive affect on all behavior.

Task involvement is felt by individuals who have certain skills and derive satisfaction from the task itself. While this can lead to high effort, self-responsibility, and satisfaction; it can also lead to resistance to cooperation and differences of opinion between roles. "If the organization is to use motivation based on task involvement, then jobs must be defined, tasks undertaken, and individuals selected so that conflict between organizational goals and professional norms is minimized." [17]

Couger and Zawicki reported that DP professionals have substantially higher growth need than any of the job categories surveyed by Hackman and Oldham, yet they have the significantly lowest social need among professionals. They report that DP professionals are motivated by opportunities to increase their professional competence and feel strongly that their supervisors do not provide adequate feedback or performance expectation [10].

A recent study by the Management Research

Group confirmed that effective DP managers [8]:

(1) established clear performance expectations,

(2) were concerned with the welfare and career development of their personnel, and

(3) provided feedback on previously defined performance expectations.

Couger & Zawicki believe that a match is possible because the motivating potential of jobs match the growth needs of DP professionals. They propose that, contrary to Licker, the chief programmer team concept may fragment the DP professional's job, and that job design should probably be concerned with enlarging the scope of the job rather than reducing it.

However, while it appears that the growing challenges of systems development provide the potential of matching the growth needs of DP professionals, the increased challenges of evolutionary development generate the following paradox:

DP Professionals have the highest growth needs yet the lowest social needs.

Evolutionary development of high risk, high payoff, ill-structured systems require higher levels of “people” or social skills.

## 5. People

People are limited in performing by basic skills and capabilities, values, and knowledge relevant to the jobs. Intrinsic motivation is fostered by matching a job with an individual who derives satisfaction from performing it. Therefore, along with the design of jobs in the organization, recruiting and selection are primary policy variables. In addition to these variables, training and education of current workers can remedy the lack of knowledge of employees [12].

Looking to the future, where programming and design are more automated, Vitalari suggests that intensive training is required to educate DP professionals to specify requirements and in problem solving that anticipates, elicits and assures that the requirements satisfy the user's needs [36].

Federico et al, in a review of current literature, emphasize that there are overwhelming inadequacies in the knowledge and skills of system users today [15]. Ginzberg showed that users who hold realistic pre-implementation expectations are more likely to regard the developed system as satisfactory and use it; he also emphasized the need for education and training throughout the development process [20]. White proposes that perceptual styles be considered in selecting MIS project team members. She concludes that critical decision environments demand diverse project teams with the most diverse perceptual styles available [39].

## 6. Sample Scenarios

Next we discuss several scenarios to see how reward systems and people are related to them. Structure: Departmental project-groups.

## Advantages:

1. There is organizational memory. People who participated in the initial development know the process. They can interpret user requests better, and there is understanding of what has and has not worked.

2. The system should be easier to maintain. In addition to project history, people who have to maintain a system, are likely to be more careful in building a system.

3. By enlarging job design, we remove any stigma from maintenance. In fact, programmers who are doing maintenance on one system will better appreciate a good system. There should also be greater peer pressure to produce a maintainable system.

4. Group programming is more productive.

5. The group identifies with a system. This should lead to greater task involvement.

6. Directors of project-group managers should be freed of project details and focus on IS effectiveness.

7. There should be shorter feedback loops between users and the DP department. Each project-group manager prioritizes maintenance and enhancements.

Other design changes to foster success of this structure:

1. Rewards associated with groups.

2. Non-threatening peer evaluations used to improve programmer productivity.

3. Groups given the right to "hire and fire", i.e., as long as groups are successful, they can determine participation. To support this, training should be given to help groups correlate successful participation behaviors with selection criteria.

4. Task distribution determined by groups.

5. Allocation of training budget determined by groups. In fact, within some boundaries, the distribution of all project/system related funds is determined by them.

6. Hours for individual members determined by groups; only goals and boundary constraints presented to members.

7. Directors with absolute authority to remove project managers if groups violate defined limits. However, objective evidence of violations must be clear to avoid the problems of double-loop learning [3].

8. Users help determine satisfaction rating for project-group.

Difficulties with implementation:

1. There has to be a better idea of the goals and boundary constraints. Directors of project managers must have standards of controls and provide clearer performance expectations.

2. Directors who can work in a decentralized environment must be found.

3. Cooperation between groups may suffer.

4. There could be conflict between organizational goals and professional norms.

Scenario 2: Theory Z Organization

Advantages:

1. An organizational investment against the uncertainties of the future. The future is “unknowable". Organizations determine the need for information applications through an error-prone communication process between the domain expert and the analyst. It is difficult for one information specialist to know all perspectives and important functional areas of the organization.

2. Provides a degree of homogeneity to the company resulting in greater cooperation.

3. Most likely to attract the brightest, with long-term goals.

4. Organizations select the CEO most capable to guide the company while responding to uncertainty in their task environment. It is quite conceivable that now CEO's with information science backgrounds will be needed. Theory Z would provide a pool of information scientists who have organizational breadth.

Other design changes to foster success of this structure:

1. Career paths and advancement criteria must be determined.

2. Tasks of trainees must permit the maintenance of computer skills.

Difficulties with implementation:

1. This is a radical concept. A ten year training program may seem excessive.

2. Difficulty in committing resources when they become scarce.

Scenario 3: Two-tiered, co-ownership of high-valued IS

Advantages:

1. Initially, system proposals are evaluated on their merits.

2. High-valued, ill-structured systems are provided special support so that evolutionary development succeeds. If organizational resources are limited, this provides focussed support on the most important.

3. Reduced conflict between user and system development departments. There should be less emphasis on requirements for sign-off of these necessarily evolutionary systems. Also, if departmental success and resources are tied to a system, there should be greater incentive to participate and reduce requests for less-important features.

4. Communication should be improved when systems reach the second tier. The feedback loop is shortened, and there is less effort in establishing rapport on future changes. It is likely that users and developers will gain better understanding of needed change.

Other design changes to foster success:

1. Performance and reward structures must motivate greater cooperative behavior and greater system flexibility. Since these systems are expected to require continual changes, requests for change should not be construed as failure, and programmers must be judged on how much flexibility has been programmed into the system.

2. People must be hired/trained to work within this highly changeable environment. The elicitation of requirements may actually require semi-experts and coaches.

Difficulties with implementation:

1. Co-ownership would be radical and would require support at all levels of management.

2. Costs would need to be closely monitored.

## 7. Actual Organizational Examples

The following examples represent two ends of the spectrum in organizational design. The first involves minimal changes to an organizational design, while the second involves major changes in technical capabilities, designing and programming practices, and the supporting organizational structure.

Example 1: The Controller's Group for ABC Chemical wanted a DSS to estimate the financial performance of its fully owned subsidiaries and potential candidates for take over. Previous systems were built by the data processing department but never became operational because of the continual changes requested by the users.

An outside consultant was hired to design and implement a system which could be easily maintained. The consultant designed and programmed the DSS using existing software and hardware, as stipulated, providing the necessary flexibility and adaptability by interfacing existing database, modeling, and graphic software. At the suggestion of the consultant, a special user support group was then created to support immediate change to the DSS while any major extension could follow standard operating procedures.

Successful implementation of the evolutionary DSS was performed in an ad hoc manner by a single, integrating force with minimal changes to the organization. The consultant controlled the design and programming practice, the structure, the reward system, and the user training [34].

Example 2: A Large Insurance Company, LIS, embarked on an intensive effort to upgrade productivity in their Information Management Systems Division. The plan focussed on three major areas of improvement: use of a high level language as an alternative to COBOL; a project to employ reusable code as a way to streamline analysis and programming; and a standardized testing method with a variety of software tools and an inspection procedure to lend a teamwork aspect to application development.

To accomplish this, LIS has made and continues to make: substantial acquisitions of the latest data processing technology, profound changes in design and programming practice, and substantial change in organization design. LIS has purchased a fourth generation language to build DSS and prototypes, and automated documentation and testing tools; it is currently documenting and making available reusable code through a sophisticated online indexing and retrieval system and is planning sophisticated workstations.

LIS is committed to design and program practices which increase productivity. They have acquired data processing technology to make it easier to reuse, document, and generate code from design documents. They are emphasizing effective, readable, and standard code which makes the programs easier to maintain and document.

LIS has also made major change in their organizational design to support increased programmer productivity. The major structural change was the creation of a nine-member resource center which administers parts of the productivity effort and provides information on the use of tools. This offers a reference unit with: copies of DP periodicals, policies, and staffers who coordinate DP offerings in the company's expansive education plan while providing feedback to vendors.

The major change in the rewards system is the enforcement of designing and programming goals. LIS has managed to insure higher order behavior in creativity and cooperation by incorporating task accomplishment rewards. The company started an “inspection” system – formal structured walk-throughs that involves the opinions and help of peers in the design, coding, and testing process of the application process. One programmer has commented, “It’s no longer a thing of ‘my little program.’ Pride of authorship is replaced by team work.”

LIS is also committed to providing employees with the knowledge to permit them to perform at higher levels. The company has an effective education and training program to aid the staff in understanding the changes to the organization and to aid in usage of the new productivity tools available to them [19].

## Comparison of the Examples

In the first, minimal changes were made to the organizational design and evolutionary development was successfully applied the first time. In the second, major changes were made in the acquisition of technology, the objectives of design and programming practice, and the design of the organizational system.

The second example describes a much more fertile environment, however, the examples demonstrate that decision makers should view data processing technology, design and programming practices, and organizational design as variables to be adjusted in order to achieve the best fit.

## 8. Summary & Conclusions

To optimize their information resources, organizations are looking for higher payoff from higher risk systems that support critical ill-structured problems. The demands of such systems severely limit the usefulness of analysis and specification techniques that focus “on getting the requirements right in the first place”. Evolutionary development mitigates some of these limitations, however its success is critically constrained by technology, design and programming practices, and the organizational environment. Organizational decision makers should reconsider their concepts and change these constraints into variables, and, given the fiscal and political realities of the organization, attempt an optimum balance among them.

Decision makers can make choice of structure, reward system, and people to optimize human performance. Structural changes involve choices in the division of labor, organizational configuration, and the distribution of power. The changeable, integrated nature of evolutionary development favors organic rather than mechanistic organizational forms.

Reward systems must motivate higher order creative and cooperative behavior through intrinsic rewards. DP professionals are motivated by clear performance expectations, opportunities for professional development, and feedback on performance. While DP professionals have high growth needs, they have low social needs, and this creates a paradox in job design: high people, social skills required for successful evolutionary development versus the low social needs of DP professionals. In addition to job design, decision makers can use recruiting, selection, and training to insure DP professionals and users possess the necessary qualities for successful system development.

While the example of the large insurance company, LIS, may represent the ideal, changes of a large magnitude require great fiscal and political power. ABC Chemical demonstrated that evolutionary development can be successful, even with relatively minor permanent change.

If organizations attempt to optimize organizational resources by tackling high payoff, high risk, support systems for critical ill-structured problems, then evolutionary development is mandatory. The degree of success will depend on how well we provide, within fiscal and political constraints, the optimum balance of technology, design and programming practices, and organizational design.
