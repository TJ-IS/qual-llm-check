# Standardization of Hospital Applications and the Problems \*

Gene Thompson

N. 805 Surgent Road, Spokane, Washington 99206, U.S.A.

The University of Southern California Center for Health Services Research has initiated an "AHIS (Automated Hospital Information Systems) Workbook Project", for planning, design, evaluation and selection of computer systems for hospitals. A list of hospital computer applications was developed. Standardized descriptions of all of these were developed and validated by site visits and review by suppliers and users of hospital computer systems. The components of an Automated Hospital Information System were identified and categorized and means of evaluation of the completeness and utility of computerized hospital information systems were developed. These standardization hospital application descriptions are not a resource for planning and development of hospital information systems.

Keywords. automated hospital systems, computerized hospital systems, hospital information systems, computerized hospital applications, hospital application descriptions, standardized computer applications, systems standardization, hospital system evaluation, hospital application evaluation, hospital applications, hospital computer applications, hospital systems, computer standards.

Lack of a means of comparison and evaluation of hospital computer systems presents a problem. A hospital or any other enterprise wanting to acquire or develop computer applications needs good information. Surveys and studies of hospital computer systems do not provide that vital link which shows how they compare with an “ideal”. But the omission of such a comparative standard is not for lack of trying. Computer system developers and suppliers are constantly putting forward their “ideal”. This naturally has the relevant and necessary applications, with a capability for adding to or modifying the HIS later. Such a diversity of offerings and the obvious self-interest in the providers create a lack of confidence.

Gene Thompson is the president and owner of Thompson Consulting, a computer consulting firm in Spokane, Washington. His experience in the computer field dates from 1959 and his experience in health care computing dates from 1966. As Chief, Health Information Systems for the Los Angeles County Department of Health Services, he initiated and managed the introduction of computer systems for a large multi-hospital public hospital system and directed the development of the on-line terminal systems for the County hospitals. He was President of ECHO (Electronic Computing-Health Oriented), a national organization of health care users of IBM equipment 1975–1977, and is an instructor on Health Data and Information Management in the University of Southern California Master's Program in Health Care Management. Since coming to Spokane in 1979, he has expanded his horizons to include word processing and the use of microcomputer systems in health care and other organizations as a private consultant. He has a textbook published, with Ira Handelman, in 1976, Health Data and Information Management. He has published a number of articles on various aspects of computer use in health care and how to improve that use.

This paper is about an attempt to create standards for computer applications in hospitals. The initiation, development and outcome are described. These standards were intended for use in comparison and evaluation of applications for hospitals. Our intent is to give a clear idea of the approach used and the problems encountered, and to discuss the difficulties in gaining acceptance of those standards.

The project was termed the AHIS (Automated Hospital Information System) Workbook Project. It was initiated in 1978 by a Federal contract award to the University of Southern California Center for Health Services Research. The requirements were:

1. To develop an AHIS Workbook to provide hospitals with the methods, approaches, tools and references for the decision to develop and/or acquire an AHIS, for the collection and evaluation of information to select an approach or a product, and for the planning and initial development of an AHIS.

2. To develop reference tools in conjunction with the AHIS Workbook which would aid a hospital in the identification, evaluation and selection of an AHIS.

The contract was completed in 1981. It was found that both parts of the contract required the development of new tools for evaluation and comparison. The AHIS Workbook was not to be a textbook: methods of systems and procedure analysis filled several textbooks, which are notable for their lack of consistent methodology. It took several months to devise new methods. The basis was a technique developed in a previous project: the use of standardized definitions of applications in hospitals as the basis for defining the system.

The earlier project, the AHDMS (Automated Hospital Data Management Systems) Project, was a comparison of systems in hospitals of small to medium size. A set of 75 standardized application descriptions were developed, based on the applications listed in the yearly Application Survey of ECHO (Electronic Computing-Health Oriented), a national organization of health care users of IBM computer equipment. The standard application descriptions were tested by site visits to several hospitals; this worked well enough to produce a reasonably accurate description of the functions which were computerized, even though the programs, their method of development or acquisition and the detailed products differed from hospital to hospital. The application titles used in the AHDMS project are shown in Table 1.

The number and scope of application description in the AHDMS Project were limited: the smaller hospitals and smaller systems warranted this. The idea of using standardized application descriptions to describe hospital systems was validated, by hospital and vendors during this project.

The use of such standardized application descriptions as a means of comparing and evaluating systems and vendor-supplied AHIS in the AHIS Workbook Project required a much more solid base. Time had passed, there were new applications, some outside the scope of the AHDMS Project. The original bias toward IBM systems had to be corrected.

The first job of the AHIS Workbook Project was therefore in defining a process of identifying the important applications to be described. This was combined with the process of collecting data on the application for its description. This method involved:

1. Computer searches of the literature: finding key words which would select applications, functions and operations that had been computerized and should be considered.

2. Review of vendor literature on hospital applications: this required followups to vendors and customers on the utility of these applications.

3. Site visits to hospitals for the collection of information and verification of hypotheses: these were structured to include collection of information on existing applications, as well as any variations.

## 1.1 Inpatient Census and Census Reporting

## Definitions

Inpatient: A patient assigned to occupy a bed in the hospital.

Census: A register of patients and the hospital beds they occupy.

## Description

An automated Inpatient Census maintains an automated record of the names of patients occupying a hospital bed or bassinet, and an identification of the location of the patient, usually by bed, but conceivably by nursing station/ward or other medical/administrative/fiscal location grouping. The automated census must accept 5 basic transactions for updating: birth, admission, discharge, death and transfer. The census may be batch, on-line or real-time, but it must reflect the status of all inpatients for each legal census day, as established by the hospital, and it must be accessible to nursing, medical records and the business office on a current (this day) basis.

Minimum reporting from the Inpatient Census is:

1. Start-of-day census list by nursing station/ward/other bed or patient grouping, reflecting patients in beds at the start of the morning shift.

2. End-of-day census list by nursing station/ward/other bed or patient location grouping, as a posting of transactions up to the legal end of the census day.

3. Report of admissions/births and discharges/deaths by nursing station/ward/other bed or patient location grouping and summarized by facility.

All of the above reports must be printed daily. A parallel output, in computer-readable form may be required by the system as inputs to other processes: patient billing, medical records reporting, utilization review.

## Options

Additional capabilities which may enhance the basic application but are still included in the application definition are:

1. On-line collection of census transactions through terminals.

2. On-line updating of the census through terminals.

3. On-line inquiry to the census through terminals.

4. Inclusion of medical service (general medicine, surgery, OB-GYN, pediatrics, etc.) identification in the census and special census reports by service.

5. Inclusion of patient preference (smoking, non-smoking), bed classification (private, semi-private, ward), service level (intensive care, nursing hours level), medical staff assignment (attending physician ID, house staff ID) in the census and special reports by these data categories.

There is only one way to validate the application definitions, by exposure to the hospital computing industry and incorporation of feedback into the application definitions until there is a consensus. This process is now under way, through the publishing of the AHIS Workbook and other channels of publication of the application definitions.

Some of the areas that are unresolved are:

1. Developing the applications set and definitions that are independent of vendor terminology. Feedback gained in hospital site visits accomplished this to a degree, but validation is still needed.

2. Inclusion of infrequent or unusual applications, i.e. those special to a size, type, or locale. This was hotly debated during the development of the application definitions; it was resolved in favor of inclusion of most of the rarely-used applications for the sake of having a complete coverage. Nonetheless, there should be further analysis of the frequency of the use of the applications in practice.

3. Inclusion of applications that are not currently operational, but which are under test or have clearly drawn specification. New applications clearly have a place in the analysis of the existing or proposed applications for a hospital. These must be identified as separate from the current applications.

4. Inclusion of applications which are operational, but not fully described or published. It is obviously difficult to obtain such information. Many applications have never been described because the people developing or using the application do not take the trouble.

5. Finding better modifiers that show how the application is used, whether the users are satisfied, and any problems with the application. This was accomplished partially by comparing the applications in the AHIS Workbook with the applications implemented in hospitals. There is a difference, and there is a clear pattern of applications often supplied by more than one vendor, but infrequently used by hospitals. These modifiers would indicate where caution and more investigation of the application was needed.

6. Inclusion of applications special to one type of computer equipment, such as the electronic office or specialized communications. Such applications are not common today, but they do exist, and will be more numerous in future. The Word Processing application is really the only one of this type and even that is limited, since the word processing application operates in different ways on a dedicated microprocessor or on a large computer.

7. Provision of samples of the documentation, procedures, and products of the applications to go with the definitions. The definitions alone provide only a limited view of the application.

The main problems encountered were the time, money, and effort needed. Subsequent modifications of the definitions should be easier. However, some of the problems require a different approach than that used in the initial development. The application definitions must be exposed, used, and updated. Feedback after use must be collected. This is an effort which requires an organization and funding, but apparently none exists.

The vendors of AHIS, who might be considered the natural source of the effort to update and use the application definitions, are not so motivated as a group. Part of their competitive strategy is to emphasize differences between their products and those of their competitors. There is no strong user motivation to make improvements in the application definition, since the use of the application definitions is only occasional for any one. The hospital and computer organizations have other problems, and cannot be expected to put priority on a “peripheral matter”. Government and foundation funding for special projects is declining.

Unless a way is found to make the development, publishing, and distribution of updated application definitions self-supporting, the current set are likely to remain unchanged. This is unfortunate, since it is a first effort, with all of its flaws, but with great promise.

The processes, methods, and products described for this project are applicable to any well-defined industry. Banking, transportation, food processing, retail sales, and a number of other industries using computer systems deal with the same problems. Projects defining and publishing standard application definitions and processes using them would provide needed help to managers. The problem of finding initial and ongoing funding for such projects exists for these industries as well. Solutions will be found only when the need and priority is well-understood by the managers in these industries.

National and international standards bodies are unlikely to be a major factor in application definition and processes using them. The membership of computer standards committees and organizations is largely computer technicians and managers. Their concerns center on technical standards used in the development, integration, and interfacing of computer processes. Application definition and the use

of application profiles is aimed at the needs of the organizations and managers using computers. If a priority is to be established for this effort in any industry, it must come from the users of the computers, not the computer technicians.
