# Information Systems as Aids to Achieving Organizational Integration

Tony Moynihan

Irish Management Institute, Sandvford Road, Dublin 14, Ireland

The high performing organization appropriately matches the structure and culture of each part of the organization to the degree of uncertainty faced by that part. But the existence of such differentiation in structure and culture across the organization can lead to difficulties in achieving coordination of effort. To overcome this difficulty, high performing organizations also have corresponding mechanisms for achieving integration between the differentiated parts.

This paper describes a number of situations in which computer-based systems are playing a major role in achieving integration.

Keywords: Differentiation, Integration, Uncertainty

## 1. The Differentiation/Integration Model

It is common knowledge that there are differences in culture and structure from one department or division to another across almost any organisation. Sometimes these differences are very marked.

As an example, consider a company in the orthopaedic implant industry. In the R&D division, there is relative silence and an almost contemplative atmosphere. People are each doing their own “thing,” showing little apparent interest or involvement in the work of their colleagues. Most people’s projects have a long time scale, so there is little feeling of having to meet tight deadlines. Formal rules and procedures are practically non existent. Time keeping is not considered to be important. There appears to be very little direct supervision.

However, in the manufacturing division it is very different. It is noisy and full of bustle. It is all very "here and now", with people working to tight deadlines. There is much interaction between people. There are many rules and procedures and finally, there is a clearly defined supervisory structure with detailed guides defining people's jobs.

Lawrence and Lorsch [1] define such differences between parts of an organisation as "Differentiation". This is the difference in cognitive and emotional orientation among managers in different functional departments, and the differences in formal structure among these departments. Lawrence and Lorsch argue that the culture and structure within any part of an organisation should appropriately match the degree of uncertainty faced by it. This R&D division must cope with uncertainty in doing its job; there are no standard, programmed ways of doing their research. Indeed, much of the time they are not even sure they are working on the right problems, given the uncertainty about the future availability of some metals. In contrast, the manufacturing division faces relatively little uncertainty. It works to a specific plan, manufacturing procedures are well established, raw materials are well understood, and, if something goes wrong with a piece of plant or a process, it is quite clear what needs to be done.

It has been found that, in effective organisations, those parts of the organisation which must cope with a high level of uncertainty generally have a low level of formalised structure; they contain individuals who are more interested in the task than in people, and who are preoccupied with long-term rather than short-term problems. In parts of effective organisations that have to cope with only moderate uncertainty, a moderate level of formalised structure is likely to be found, members are concerned with both people and task, and have a preoccupation with medium-term problems. Finally, in parts of an effective organisation coping with a low level of uncertainty, one is likely to find a high level of formalised structure, a return to concern for task rather than people, and a preoccupation with short-term problems. “Level of formalised structure” is the degree to which a part of the organisation has a well defined management and supervisory structure. This also implies the degree to which there is clarity about people’s jobs, the degree to which the work is undertaken through rules, regulations, and standard procedures, and the degree to which decision-making is centralised at the top (rather than delegated).

"Goal orientation" is another source of differentiation across an organisation. The well worn stereotype of the production manager and the marketing manager locked in combat is an example of a consequence of a difference in goal orientation. The marketing manager wants to get the business, to do the best on price and delivery. On the other hand, the production manager is concerned about the production schedule and labour and plant efficiency figures.

## 2. Differentiation and Corporate Ability

High performing organisations have structures and cultures which are appropriately differentiated [1]. In other words, they have appropriately matched the characteristics of each part of the organisation to its degree of uncertainty. On the other hand, it has been found that less successful organisations are less likely to achieve this match. Imagine a production unit facing little uncertainty which has no standard procedures, where there is confusion about schedules, where there is little or no supervision, etc. It would be chaotic! On the other hand, imagine a research chemist working on a challenging problem in a laboratory where every decision has to be checked out with the manager, where there are tight time deadlines at each stage of the research and where there are many regulations and procedures. Little productive research could be done!

So, an appropriate degree of differentiation makes sense. But the existence of differentiation can lead to problems: it can lead to difficulties in achieving coordination of effort across units. It can also lead to friction and misunderstanding when individuals from very different cultures within the organisation get together to work on issues. The problem of obtaining unity of effort between differentiated parts of an organisation which must work together is described as the problem of achieving integration.

## 3. Integrating Mechanisms

High performing organisations not only have suitably differentiated structures and cultures, but also have corresponding mechanisms for achieving integration between the differentiated parts, for "putting humpty-dummy together again". Organisations use a number of mechanisms; the principal ones are:

• line management structure;

• cross organisation teams and committees;

• individual coordinators;

• coordinating departments;

\- plans and procedures.

The research in Ref. [1] was conducted prior to the widespread use of computer-based systems. I believe that there are situations in which computer-based systems can make a contribution to the achievement of integration. I now describe some instances where managers have found this to be the case.

## 4. Computer-Based Information Systems as Aids in Achieving Integration

A large meat producer near Dublin was experiencing many problems in achieving coordination across the organisation, presumably because of very marked differentiation. The company bought pigs, killed them, disassembled them (cut them up), and then processed the various parts and organs to obtain such items as bacon, ham, and sausages. The markets for the company products were very volatile. Levels of demand could suddenly increase or decrease by a factor of two or three. There was also uncertainty about the availability of pigs. Despite the existence of contracts for supply with pig "producers", there were large week-to-week variations in the number of pigs available for slaughter. In addition there was also unpredictable variation in the quality of the pigs. In fact, until a pig was slaughtered and disassembled, and its various parts examined, no firm decision could be made about the best products to make from the various parts of the pig.

In contrast, the manufacturing process was subject to very little uncertainty. Thus, there was great uncertainty at both ends of the process (obtaining pigs and marketing the final product), but once a production plan for the week was established, there was little uncertainty in the manufacturing process.

Management had great difficulty in achieving unity of effort across the company. They seemed to be constantly “firefighting” and coping with “surprises”. Also collaboration between departments was poor. Senior management felt that middle managers and supervisors had a low level of motivation because the latter two groups were working in a constantly changing situation in which it was hard to see the relationship between effort and performance.

It was decided that an integrated production planning and control system was needed for the organisation; this was designed and implemented. The new system required managers across the organisation to meet regularly in a structured, problem-solving manner. Whilst computers supported the critical planning and scheduling decisions by providing much of the data on which these decisions were based, the actual decisions were made by the group using specially developed heuristic procedures.

After some months experience with the system, managers were asked to identify ways in which they felt the system had contributed to the organisation. Among the points they made were:

\- there was a reduced level of turbulence and uncertainty at management level;

\- there was an improved level of motivation generally through working to explicit goals and then receiving feedback on performance in relation to these goals;

\- recurring problems received a high level of visibility at management level and so were tackled once and for all:

\- collaboration across organisational boundaries had improved; and teamwork had improved because it was being “driven” by the system.

Another example of computer-based systems improving integration appears in a recent article on manufacturing resource planning (MRP). Robert A. Abair, Director of Materials at the Smith Valve Corporation in Massachusetts describes the benefits gained by his company in using computer based MRP:

"The biggest accomplishment of MRP was that it caused management to work as a team. The departments that in the past had been autonomous have now been included. The Engineering people cannot operate in their own little sphere any more because their bills are so important to us. The Finance people have to work very closely with Production people in forecasting cash-flow. We have become a better company overall because of the cohesiveness's MRP has forced on us" [2].

The next example is slightly more discursive. It concerns a very large industrial company which had grown over the years through acquisition. It was a very “political” organisation as the vestiges of the original management occupied senior positions in many of the companies which had been acquired. At board and senior management level, there appeared to be a great shortage of “hard”, shared information on goals and performance. Indeed, even senior managers within a division did not know the budgets and plans to which their division was presumed to be working. This, in turn, made it impossible for them to receive feedback about how well they were performing. At board level there was a complete lack of objective data on the performance and plans of the company; e.g., no cash-flow forecasts were made available.

In the view of at least one senior board member, this lack of “hard”, shared data on the business had led to a number of adverse results. Politics and political manoeuvring had become a preoccupation amongst directors and managers. Lack of objective feedback on performance, and thus the sharing of this with senior colleagues, meant that an individual’s performance was invisible. This, in turn, had created a low level of motivation. A small number of senior managers had emerged as “gate keepers”, controlling the flow of information to and from the Chief Executive. The lack of objective data led also to the absence of a shared view, amongst the directors and top management, of the problems and opportunities facing the organisation. This reduced the ability of the organisation to respond in a rational, coherent way to threats and opportunities. It also meant there was no basis for company planning.

As a result of the efforts of a number of concerned board members, the situation was greatly improved by introducing a proper budgetary control system and by ensuring that targets and achievements were made explicit and visible. The directors insisted that they be given more factual information; e.g., cash-flow forecasts, which had always been produced but which had not been made available, were provided for board meetings.

Changes such as these led to a much improved level of integration across the organisation. As a director put it:

"we have all started pulling the same way. We really now have a shared view of the problems facing the company and of the opportunities there for us. Also, the fact that the board and top management are now getting information like this had made us all aware of the importance of strategic planning. It is amazing to think that simply sharing a few keys reports about the state of the company has led to so much change at the top".

## 5. Planning Models as Integrating Mechanisms

A company in the plastics industry bought a financial modelling package for their computer. After some experience in using the package to help in the budget cycle, the chief accountant described some of the ways in which he felt the package had contributed to the effectiveness of the organisation:

"When we were budgeting in the past, the amount of work involved in pulling all the figures together and producing the projections was huge. Requests for changes to the budget from the departments involved were discouraged or ignored because of the work they would create for the Accounts department. In turn, managers in the company began to feel that their efforts to budget were wasted and they felt that their participation was not welcome. We really have a much better situation since we started to use a computer for budgeting. First of all, doing the arithmetic and making the projections is no longer a hassle for my people once we've gathered the data. We are all far more relaxed now about the budgeting process and managers are really feeling that they are part of the process. We are getting more realistic budgets and the budgeting cycle is now working to pull us all together for the first time".

## 6. Conclusions

This paper has tried to show that there are instances in which computer systems can help achieve integration in organisations. It concludes by posing two questions to be asked when reviewing the effectiveness of systems: are there any problems in obtaining integration in the organisation? If so, can suitably designed systems contribute to the solution of these?
