# External Information Services: A Survey of Behavioral Aspects of Demand

Leif B. Methlie and Alf M. Tverstøl \*

Institute for Information Systems Research, Norwegian School of Economics and Business Administration, Helleveien 30, N-5035 Bergen-Sandviken, Norway

More and more of the information a company needs to know about its environment will be computerized. On-line databases will be available in remote locations and interrogated by persons being assigned gatekeeping functions in various sectors of the company. This paper describes some characteristics of the external information services sector of the information business. It reports the findings of a survey of demand of external information services among Norwegian industrial companies. It seeks to determine usage patterns on the basis of company characteristics, awareness, and experience. Attitude profiles are computed for users and non-users.

Keywords: Data base, data management, external data bases, external information systems, intelligence information, information services.

## 1. Introduction

In a recent issue of Fortune [1] the headline of an article read: Everything you always wanted to know may soon be on-line. A new information services industry is growing up around on-line databases, i.e., using information storage that can be interrogated from a remote location; these databases provide information to business as well as the public. The information services industry has been fairly well described in the literature (e.g., [3,4,5]) and several directories of these database services exist [10,12,13]. The demand side is not equally well understood or described. Some survey articles and conceptual work on business intelligence, primarily aimed at top-level management, have been published [5,8,9], but very little is reported on behavioral aspects that determine the demand for external information services. Is the business community, in general, aware of the many on-line databases now available? What is the experience of companies using these new media as compared to more traditional sources, such as books, statistical year books, journals etc.? This article reports on the results of a survey on these matters conducted on almost 300 industrial companies in Norway.

## 2. The Information Services Industry

The information business consists of a large range of companies – from large broadcasting networks to small market research consulting firms. We can broadly divide this business into the following segments: communication technology providers, information technology providers, communication channels providers, information content providers (e.g. publishing companies), and information facilitation services [1]. Information facilitation services have historically been provided by small companies: service bureaus, software houses, market research firms, etc., as compared to the corporate giants found in other segments of the information business, e.g., IBM, AT&T and McGraw-Hill. However, this picture may change in the future. On-line databases lie at the heart of the future information business. More and more information will be computerized in the future – the printing and publishing industry is well aware of this situation. Furthermore, to make the content of computerized databases available, information technology is required to process and store this information, and communication technology and channels are needed to distribute data to remote locations. Last, managerial skill is required to operate these large databases. On a small scale, these managerial and operating skills are already found in the computer services industry (service bureaus and time-sharing companies). Such skills are key resources in the industry growing around on-line databases. The small companies in the information services segment are therefore prime targets for acquisition by the giants of the information business; e.g., McGraw-Hill Publishing Company bought Data Resources Inc. in 1979 and Burroughs Corporation purchased Systems Development Corporation in 1980 ([1] has further examples).

## 3. External Information Services

Surveillance of a company's external environment is known as "business intelligence". It is performed by agents internal or external to the organization: the “gatekeepers”. They scan the environment for states and events that may indicate opportunities or threats to current activities. Groups of gatekeepers are usually interested in a particular sector of the environment; for instance, a marketing analyst focuses attention on markets and demographic changes, while a financial analyst focuses on financial markets. Gatekeepers provide pertinent information to the planning and decision-making processes. The information that is gathered may be of various kinds, such as: GNP and trades (national statistics), consumer and competitor behavior (demographic and markets information), vendors information (product information), profit and growth of an industry (company information), and stocks, bonds, and currencies (financial market information). We use the generic term “external information” or “business information” to mean any or all of these.

Business information sources fall into several broad classes, such as “directories, magazines, services, trade association publications, government publications, books, year books, encyclopedias . . .” [7]. In this study, we are particularly interested in services: external information services (EIS). Services are developed to meet particular needs and are adapted to their purpose through publication format and timing. Services are often considered to be costly sources of information because they incur direct expenses as opposed to more indirect costs associated with sources such as journals, books or even meetings. Here information is often considered “free” commodity. The direct costs involved in using information services also influence the search behavior and usage. Changes in search behavior arise from differences in format and timing. Scanning a newspaper for events that can be important is an intelligence activity in itself. The ultimate information obtained about an object may be composed of fragments from multiple reportings. Furthermore, the timing, i.e. when the information is distributed, is to a great extent determined by the medium and the receiver exercises little control on that aspect. Using information services is a determined act of the user; it is elicited by explicit needs and formulated as a request to the agent providing the service.

External information services are provided in one of the following three ways:

(a) Information generators. Some companies specialize in generating information that does not already exist; for instance, information about markets as generated by marketing research firms. These companies utilize computers in their own data processing, but seldom provide the information to their customers electronically.

(b) The information bank. This utilizes computer technology. Companies operating on this concept have skills and resources to manage large amounts of data, applying database technology and telecommunication technology. Information is seldom generated by these companies, but is bought from other sources. The information is often provided along with auxiliary services, such as models for forecasting, econometric analysis, etc. "On-line databases" or "external databases" are terms also frequently used for this concept.

(c) Information brokers. These companies do not provide databases of their own, but have access to a number of them. They specialize in converting customer requests (often quite vaguely expressed) to precise searches which access the databases to provide useful information to the customer.

## 4. The Sample and Questionnaire

The primary data for this study was based upon 258 responses from a sample of 400 industrial companies in Norway. A systematic, random sampling method was used to select this sample from 1000 Norwegian industrial companies listed in "Norges største bedrifter" (1979), a publication of Norway's largest companies listed in order of sales income.

As the concept “external information services” has no well-defined and unambiguous meaning, the following short description was provided in the questionnaire:

By EXTERNAL INFORMATION SERVICES we mean services that a company can buy to gather information about its environment. Examples of such services are: marketing research, searching in databases, advertising research, technology surveillance, and consumer research.

The questionnaire contained twelve questions. Two of the questions aimed at measuring attitudes and were formulated as a number of statements; the respondent was asked to indicate his/her degree of agreement or disagreement to each of these.

Since marketing is a function directed towards the external environment of a firm, the marketing department was assumed to be that most familiar with external information services. The questionnaire was therefore mailed to this department and some of the questions reflected information of special interest to the marketing function. The survey, however, was not restricted to marketing intelligence.

400 questionnaires were sent out and 268 were returned - a response of $67\%$ , which is regarded as satisfactory. Ten of the respondents had to be rejected due to invalid data, leaving a primary database of 258. Tests on the data show that all size groups and branches are represented, although the response-percentage varies from $49\%$ for companies with less than 50 employees to $85.7\%$ for companies with more than 700 employees.

## 6. The Findings

The purpose of this study was to get a better understanding of behavioral aspects determining the demand for external information services. In particular, the aim was to explore (1) how well Norwegian industrial companies know about sources of external information, (2) what kind of business information is gathered through these services; and (3), how companies rank various sources according to their importance. Here we will only present parts of the findings; complete documentation is given in [11].

## 6.1. Measures of Usage

50% of the respondents have utilized business information sources which, according to our definition above, can be classified as EIS; 30% subscribe regularly to at least one service. This suggests that more than 40% of industrial companies in Norway are not aware of these information services or do not find them appropriate for their needs. On the average, the respondents used 2.5 different sources during the last year of the survey (1979).

In terms of usage, the respondents are probably not representative of the population. It is fair to assume that the companies not returning the questionnaire have had little or no experience of these kinds of services. Table 1 shows the demand for external information. As shown, the highest demand is found for information about existing markets.

## 6.2. Who Uses External Information Services?

Usage is found to be influenced by company-size and industry category, where company-size is measured in terms of the number of employees. The usage, as a function of company size, is depicted in the bar chart of Fig. 2.

We also looked for significant differences in usage among industry categories. To eliminate the effect of company-size, the material was broken down to size-groups within each industry category. Although the number of observations in each group became too small to make statistically firm statements, the findings indicate a usage pattern which differs substantially among the industry sectors. Higher usage was found in the raw-material supply industry (lumber and wood products, chemicals and allied products, petroleum refining, and primary metal industry). Lower usage was found in food industries, textile and leather products, machinery and construction.

These differences can be explained by the greater turbulence in the environments of companies in raw-material supplies: e.g., the impact of the crisis in the ship building industry on the demand for steel. It is therefore fair to assume that the use of external information services is positively correlated to uncertainties in the external environment.

## 6.3. How Do Companies Rank Various Information Sources?

To explain differences in search behavior for business information, the respondents were asked: "When you need information about a new market of interest to your company, which information sources do you use? (rank the three most important)". A list of sources was provided. The respondents were categorized into two groups; one consisted of companies regularly buying or having bought information through external services (called "users") and the other consisted of companies having either evaluated but rejected or having never appraised these services (called "non-users"). The results are displayed in Fig. 3.

As can be seen, there are significant differences between the behavior of the two groups. Non-users are attracted by low-cost sources, such as libraries, newspapers, and journals, while users rely more on services, such as consultants or on-line databases. The ranking is, to some extent, influenced by company size. Large companies (with more than 500 employees) rank government agencies as their number one source of business information. Increase in use of external databases with company size is significant: of companies with less than 100 employees, only 2.7% report on usage of external databases – they rank this source as number 9. The corresponding figures for size category 101–500 employees are 3.3% (rank 8), and for companies with more than 500 employees, 17.1% (rank 8).

## 6.4. User Experience

Are the companies satisfied with the services? To measure the degree of satisfaction, as well as attitudes towards this way of gathering environmental information, two questions were formulated: one asked for a direct rating of satisfaction; the other indirectly measured satisfaction through attitude.

Attitude was measured by six statements related to qualitative attributes of information, such as relevance, usefulness, timeliness, completeness, and costs. The respondent was asked to rate each statement on a six-point scale. The average total score was 4.1, where 0 represents the lowest and 6 the highest degree of satisfaction. The direct question asked the respondent to rate how close the best known service had come to expectation. The rating was made with an itemized, five-point scale. The average score was 3.6 (equivalent to 4.3 on a six-point scale). The distribution of the scores is shown in Table 2. The two measurements give amazingly similar results, and indicate that users are fairly well satisfied. Their attitudes are generally positive; this indicates that further use can be promoted by marketing efforts on the part of the servicing industry. The attitude measurements give some insight into comparative differences among satisfied and dissatisfied users. The most satisfied users seem to appreciate the timeliness, precision and relevance that are provided by services. They are less concerned with costs than the less-satisfied users, who express dissatisfaction primarily in terms of relevance and cost. The relevance attribute can indicate difficulties in finding the right source. The information received has apparently not corresponded to their needs – their scores on the relevance attribute are comparatively low. The normative implications of this are twofold: (1) the marketing effort may not be good enough, and (2) the users need to be better organized to make use of these services.

## 6.5. Respondents Attitudes Towards Buying External Information

In the preceding measurements, we explored differences in attitude profiles among satisfied and non-satisfied users. In this section, we look at some measurements made to explore comparative differences in attitudes of users versus non-users. All respondents were asked to express their degree of agreement or disagreement with the nine statements of Figure 4. The respondents were divided into two categories (users and non-users) according to the previous definitions. Attitude profiles are depicted on the same figure, where individual items are sorted on the basis of disagreements between users and non-users.

Analyzing the two profiles with respect to comparative differences in scores on the specified attributes gives the same answers to the question: what makes a company a user or a non-user of external information services? First, a company's perceived need for business information is significantly greater among user than non-user companies. Second, non-users rely more on sales personnel as their prime gatekeepers than users (at least in the marketing sector). Third, non-users believe to a much greater extent than users that their information needs are unique and therefore cannot be provided by these services.

All respondents express a desire to have better knowledge of the services (sources) available. Therefore, it seems fair to assume that increased marketing effort is a prerequisite to better understanding and awareness of these services. Non-users view of their needs, both with respect to quantity and quality, is probably more due to lack of knowledge than any other reason.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

1. The skills needed to be successful in operating external databases are greatly valued, and the external information services (EIS) sector of the information industry does attract much interest from larger companies, especially the corporate giants in other sectors of our industry.

2. EIS is more supply (technology) pushed than demand (application) pulled.

3. The markets do not seem to be readily available, but must be developed. This explains why many on-line databases are provided by economically strong companies (usually through ownership) or are supported by governments in start-up phases, or are provided by data-rich organizations, such as the United Nations, OECD, etc.

4. The survey indicates that the business community in Norway is not well aware of the available kinds of information sources. Except for larger companies, their gatekeeper functions do not seem to be organized towards use of external databases.

5. The direct costing of external information services requires changes in user behavior. Non-users express clear aversion against information sources incurring expenses higher than more general sources (such as newspapers, journals, books, etc.)

6. Non-users rely on simple gatekeeping functions. They show less need for environmental information than users.

7. Business relationships and government are said to be the two prime sources of business information among both users and non-users.

8. Among larger companies (i.e. ones with more than 500 employees) 17.1% report external databases to be among the three most important sources in exploring new markets. However, the rank of this source is 8 among 10.

9. Users are generally satisfied with the services they have obtained. However, less satisfied users express concern about their costs in their ability to find the right sources.

10. To raise user awareness of external databases, greater marketing efforts must be made by the providers of these services.
