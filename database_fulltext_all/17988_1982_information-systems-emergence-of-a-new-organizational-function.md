# Information Systems: Emergence of a New Organizational Function

Philip Ein-Dor and Eli Segev

Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, Tel-Arcu, Israel

Data processing units have undergone a process of rapid advancement both in function and organizational location, maturing as a separate autonomous area devoted to information systems. This paper is a study of the dynamic change in the organizational location of information units. Fifty-three organizations from a wide variety of industries in a large metropolitan area were studied. Some consistency in the location of the information systems within organizations have been identified. Concrete advice presented here may save readers one or more disrupting relocations of the information systems unit in their organizations.

Keywords: Autonomous IS, information units, organizational location, organizational function.

## 1. Introduction

Case: A very large U.S. bank, the cornerstone of a large holding company, embarked more than five years ago on a program of development for information systems. The information system unit was first located within Banking Services, and the manager of the unit was an assistant vice-president, far below the top of the organizational hierarchy. Two and a half years ago, the information system unit was moved to the Finance and Administration Department and given the status of a functional area. The unit was then headed by a vice-president who reported directly to the executive vice-president of the bank. Within the past year, the chain of command was again modified; now the head of the unit is a senior vice-president who reports to the president of the holding company. The scope of the unit has shifted from that of a service unit within a specific department to a prestigious functional unit in its own right.

This case is typical of the rapid advancement of data processing units in organizations. From automating small, well-defined clerical tasks, they have been upgraded to the design and implementation of top-management control and planning systems. Gibson and Nolan [1] viewed this development as a stepwise process composed of four stages – initiation, expansion, formalization, and maturity. In the initiation stage, the EDP unit is located in the department whose applications are the first to be computerized, usually accounting. In the last stage, maturity of information systems, the information unit is a separate, autonomous functional area.

The general phenomena described above, and exemplified by the opening case, have not been studied in depth and are not well understood. Given the growing size, cost and importance of information systems, any insight which contributes to our understanding and more efficient use of them is of considerable value. Moreover, from the study of the dynamic change in the organizational location of information system units, some similarities have been identified and concrete advice derived.

## 2. Information System Unit Location

In the early sixties, most organizations placed the responsibility for computers in the accounting department. Computers were regarded as machines for automating clerical work. The nature of the work was perceived to be related to the skills extant in accounting departments, as were the processes that were automated. This practice was strongly rejected by Hanold [2], who distinguished clearly between accounting and finance and firmly advocated locating the information function in finance. Hanold viewed accounting as an evaluative system, rather than a management function. Because the “accounting fraternity” is bound by standard accounting procedures and has obligations to the board of directors, shareholders, and the public, accounting departments analyze and present rather than manage.

Neither the accounting nor the finance locations seem to be either necessary or universally apposite. Nor do these, as final locations, conform with the process described by Gibson and Nolan. These uncertainties motivated our attempt to study the dynamics of the organizational location of information processing units.

The study encompassed fifty-three organizations from a wide variety of industries in a large metropolitan area from November 1978 to May 1979. Of the 53 organizations involved, sixteen were in the electronics, computer, or communications industries, seventeen were in natural resources, utilities, manufacturing, construction, or trucking, three were in warehousing or retailing, and nine were law firms, banks, or insurance companies. There were also one university, one hospital, and six federal, state, or local government agencies. Table 1 exhibits the detailed distribution of the organizations by industry. The relatively small number of organizations does not permit meaningful study of subpopulations. Hints of differences between types of organizations (e.g., banks and insurance companies versus manufacturing) are to be found in the detailed cases.

The methodology is discussed elsewhere [3], but consisted essentially of interviews with officers, including the head of information systems, in each organization. Part of each interview was devoted to establishing the initial and current organizational locations of the information service function. Table 2 presents the raw data on changes in organizational locations of information system units between initial location and location at the time of the survey. Information on eight of the organizations is incomplete; these are excluded from subsequent tables and analyses.

The numbers on the main diagonal (bold) indicate how many units remained in their initial locations. Thus, of the 45 firms, there was no movement in 22. The remaining numbers represent organizations which changed location of the IS unit. The movement is nearly all to the right of the main diagonal, representing a systematic bias in the kinds of changes.

Table 3 exhibits the data on changes in location so as to emphasize the direction and volume of movement for each of the locations encountered. The locations in the table fall naturally into three groups. Group I, comprising accounting, planning, and production. Of ten units originally located within this group, only I remained by the time of the survey.

Group II comprises the controller, administrative services and finance. Nearly two thirds of the units were originally assigned to this location, and were still there at the time of the survey. This group exhibits dynamic behavior with high rates of exits and entries. Within the group, the controller location is notable for an extremely high rate of turnover, and a tendency to decline in overall numbers. Finance and administrative services exhibit somewhat lower rates of turnover and a slight tendency to gain in numbers.

The conclusion is inescapable that there is a general tendency toward the formation of independent information system units; that we are, in fact, witnesses to the emergence of a new organizational function.

## 3. Operating Units as Locations for Information System Units

Information systems frequently originate in operational or low level staff departments. The examples found in this survey are in production, planning and accounting. These locations are probably the quickest to require relocation because units in them clearly become anomalous as soon as the desire arises to implement systems for other functions. This explains why virtually none of the units which originated in these locations are still there.

Five organizations initiated their information systems within the accounting function. The current location of all five has changed: one reports to the controller's office, one is a part of finance, and in three organizations a new, independent function was created. All five started their computerization efforts in the late fifties or early six-ties.

Case: In a very large insurance company, the data processing department began as part of the accounting department with the IS manager reporting directly to the controller. In the late 1960's, following growth in information systems, a separate department was established and the rank of the executive was raised to the vice-presidential:

level. This case is representative of the process in all three units originally located in accounting and which are now autonomous. However, in other organizations the transition can take much longer.

Case: A division of a large multi-national corporation has been involved in information system development since the early 1960's. In the mid-sixties, there were four accounting and computer centers; each had its own hardware and systems and programming staff. The accounting functions, computer operations, IS development, and some of the users all reported to local managers. The four local managers reported to a division controller.

In the late sixties, a centralized planning and coordination function was established in an effort to plan, control and standardize IS development. However, with decentralized IS approval and development (within limits), this function did not have a significant impact.

During the early to mid seventies, the four accounting and computer centers were consolidated. Computer operations were separated from the accounting center and reported instead to the assistant division controller. IS development remained a function of the accounting center and the independent planning and control function.

Last year, the information development functions were detached from the accounting center. IS development and computer operations now report (through separate management) directly to the assistant controller, who in turn reports to the controller who reports to the U.S. division president.

Such experience seems to strongly vindicate Hanold's suggestions. It does little, however, to substantiate the second part of Hanold's argument – that finance is the best location. Only one of the five units moved to finance. The majority of the firms with experience of the accounting location opted for the autonomous solution.

## 4. The Controller's Office as a Location for IS Units

The data concerning the controller's office are among the most interesting. Table 3 indicates (a) that a relatively large number of units originate in this location and (b) that it is particularly unstable. Of the ten units originating in the controller's office, only three remained there, seven having relocated. But, concurrently, five units were moved in. This would seem to indicate that this location has considerable theoretical attraction.

Case: A medium sized department store network established a data processing function in the sixties. In the late sixties and early seventies, batch oriented transactions systems were developed. The EDP manager at this time reported directly to the controller, placing him at the third level from the top of the organizational structure. In 1974, a management information systems director was hired to report directly to the financial vice president. This change moved the director's rank to the second level. In 1978, the MIS director was promoted to vice president of MIS.

## 5. Finance as a Location for IS Units

It seems that the arguments made by Hanold have some merit up to a certain point in information system development, but beyond that, this location becomes untenable. Of the ten information system units in our survey which originated in finance, four exited; two moved in what seems to be the direction of greater autonomy – one into administrative services and the other into independent status. Surprisingly, two moved into the controller's office. The only explanations we can posit for this last move are either that the organizations realized that some change was necessary but misdiagnosed the reasons for the problems or else this is evidence of organizational pathology.

Case: In a large snack food company, with sales over one billion dollars, strategic decisions are centralized in the sense that all operations report to top management and depend on it for long range plans, goals, and decisions concerning capital expenditures. The operations themselves are somewhat decentralized with processing plants located throughout the U.S. There are a few zones of responsibility for sales, distribution, and processing. All major decisions are ultimately made by top management at headquarters.

In-house information system efforts began with purchase of the company's first computer in 1970. Prior to that, data processing for the company had been handled by an independent service bureau. The internal system was established in the finance department under the director for management services. The position of IS in the organizational power structure is virtually the same today as when the system was initiated and there has been little change in the rank and position of the executive responsible. The executive's position and authority have increased as a direct consequence of overall company growth, but the same is also true of sales and production. There is less opportunity for IS executives in the top echelons of corporate management than there are for sales and production executives. This is attributed to an emphasis on operations in general and to the emphasis of technical rather than managerial skills for information system personnel. The company name for the information system function, "Information Services", reflects top management opinion that IS is a service for management rather than a function in its own right. The director of information services reports to the vice-president for finance who in turn reports to the company president.

## 6. Administrative Services as a Location for Information Systems

Administrative services emerged as an initial location of considerable popularity. It is the only location from which there was no exit directly to an autonomous information system function.

The data concerning this location suggest that it is a good, but less than perfect substitute, for autonomous IS. Because administrative services is a staff function serving all operational functions. It has much the same kind of relationship with these functions as an autonomous information system unit would, being free of much of the interfunctional rivalry which seems to adversely affect other functional locations. That this is a less than perfect solution is indicated by the fact that 25% of the units originally in this location did leave it – a relatively low but significant turnover. It is hypothesized that this is so because, in administrative services, IS is one of a number of services provided; thus, there is difficulty in obtaining the level of independence which seems to make autonomous IS units so effective.

Case: In a multinational consumer goods corporation, the first information systems, developed over one hundred years ago, were manual systems (naturally) in the accounting and sales areas. In the early 1960's, the computerization of data bases was undertaken. At this stage of development, information systems were located in a data processing department which was administered by the comptroller's division; information systems were viewed as directly associated with the computer hardware.

In the late 1960's, with a growing awareness of the relationship between IS and other management support systems, data processing was relocated. The information systems group was merged with other management services to form the current Management Systems Division. While the executive in charge, until this change, had been three levels below the company president, the new manager was now one level higher, indicating the increasing importance.

## 7. Autonomous Information Systems

Most information system units seem destined eventually to become autonomous. If this view is accepted, it follows that the sooner this alternative is adopted, the fewer relocations will be necessary for the IS function. Since such relocations are invariably disruptive both for the IS unit and for the organization as a whole, the sooner the situation is stabilized, the better off the organization would seem to be.

Case: An oil producing and refining firm initiated IS in accounting in the early 1970's. Initially, nearly all of the data processing was done by a service bureau and the rank of the responsible DP manager was very low. Not until 1974 did the department achieve some modicum of independence, although it was still under the direct control of Accounting.

In 1976, an analyst was named manager of information systems and the finance department was restructured to control accounting and IS with parallel status. With continued growth and the need to develop advanced information systems, an executive was named vice-president and director of information systems; this was in 1978. The director of information systems is directly under a senior vice-president who reports to the president. The appointment of a director of IS was an important change for the department of information systems, as well as for the entire organization. It took much time, money and effort, but it was felt that the need justified the costs.

Case: A local branch of a large insurance company employs about 600 people and handles about 350,000 claims, totalling \$4,000,000, annually. Information system applications include claims processing, master file maintenance, payroll, and financial operations. The organization is highly centralized; all major decisions are made by the executive vice-president who has three senior vice-presidents to oversee execution. Mid-level management handles only day-to-day operations.

Prior to 1970, information systems consisted mainly of purchased software packages for financial applications. Claims were processed entirely manually. There were only a few programmers whose responsibility was to maintain the packages purchased from software vendors.

Beginning in 1970, computer hardware was acquired and a vice-president for data processing was appointed. Reporting to him are four EDP managers in charge of General Systems, Claims Systems, Computer Operations, and Special Claims. Each EDP manager has reporting to him two assistant managers, four project leaders, systems analysts and programmers. The vice-president for data processing reports directly to a senior vice-president.

## 8. The Dynamics of Information System Location

On the basis of the data and case studies presented above, it is possible to hypothesize the dynamics which determine the location of information service units in organizations. The most common practice seems to have been to establish information units at the area needing the initial applications. As a result, there is a wide distribution of initial locations. This practice, convenient at the outset, seems to create serious problems. First, the location within a specific function inhibits and delays application outside this jurisdiction. Integration of applications across functional areas is also inhibited, so that the process of transforming the data processing group into an organizational information system function is also hindered.

These problems seem to be most apparent when the information group is located in accounting, production and planning, and the instability of these locations over time has been demonstrated. As the demand for the services of the group are extended to additional functions and as management begins to realize the potential for integrating systems, it becomes apparent that identification of IS with a particular function causes difficulties. The obvious solution is to relocate the information system group into a more central and more staff oriented location with fewer ties to any particular function.

The data indicate that there is a natural hierarchy of locations above the operational level in which the degree of centrality increases and functional dependence decreases. This hierarchy begins one level above operations in the controller's office, then progresses through finance, administrative services, and, finally, to autonomous information services. Of 23 organizations in which relocations occurred, only four were in a direction contrary to the progression of the hierarchy. Exactly how far up the hierarchy any particular relocation carries the IS group seems to depend on the planning range of management and their ambitions for IS development; the longer the planning span and the more ambitious the plans, the higher up the hierarchy will the relocation be made.

Case: In a small, 400 employee, cheerleader supply company, information systems originated with a small computer and one system analyst/programmer who reported directly to a vice-president. Because of complexities unforeseen in the original plan it was decided that a larger staff was necessary and three programmers, three computer operators and two key punch operators were added. At the time of the survey, this group was headed by the analyst who then reported to the comptroller who in turn reported to the vice-president. In this case it would appear that poor planning led to the relocation of the information system group two levels below the vice-president instead of the original one level.

Case: A medium-sized electronics company with approximately 1100 employees is part of a corporate organization with well over \$300 million in annual sales. The organization is characterized by stable growth within well defined objectives and policies. Management Systems has been lodged at various levels within the organizational structure since the information system effort was initiated in the 1960's.

The information system chief first reported to the vice-president for finance and was one level below the division chief executive. Apparently, there was an inclination towards empire building. The IS staff grew rapidly and by the late 1960's, the IS executive reported directly to the general manager. This arrangement became cumbersome both for the IS director and for upper management. When the IS executive left the company in 1969, Management Systems was reorganized and the manager of accounting became the new IS executive. At the time of the survey, the information system director reported to the manager of accounting, who reports to the division controller who in turn reports to the financial vice-president. Thus the IS unit was relocated four steps below its previous eminence. In this case, the power exerted by one individual led to a location which apparently had no connection with the real content and status of the information system unit; with his departure, the unit was assigned a more realistic location.

## 9. Conclusions

Two major conclusions emerge from this study:

1) For the first time since the creation of the marketing function some thirty years ago, there is clear evidence of the emergence of a new organizational function: information systems. As this function constantly grows in scope and importance, and as it tends more and more to parallel the concept of management itself, management should be aware of the process and to plan for it. In many organizations, the growth of information systems is somewhat out of control and excessively directed by political pressures and temporary situations; many ineffective locations are the result of such unplanned development.

2) The eventual establishment of autonomous information system units seems to be as close to a natural law as any developments in organizations ever are. This being the case, it would seem that this should be the first alternative considered for information system location and should be adopted wherever feasible. A decision against this alternative is an implicit decision for relocation at some time in the future. In order to avoid the cost and disruption of such relocations, solutions other than the autonomous unit should be adopted only when dictated by circumstances. Where an autonomous unit is infeasible, the next best solution would seem to be in administrative services, followed by finance. It appears that the controller's office is (at best) a short-lived solution.

Some time has elapsed between the field study and publication of this paper. In the meantime, significant changes have occurred in information systems, including the rapidly expanding use of mini and micro-computers, as well as the tendency towards distributed systems. It is yet too early to observe the effects of these changes on the organizational location of information systems, but, based on informal observations, the following hypotheses are suggested:

a) There will be a tendency to differentiate between purely local information needs and organization-wide needs. The former will be generally served by local mini and micro computers, the latter by centrally managed systems, often geographically distributed.

b) If this is indeed the trend that emerges, the central information system function will deal more and more with problems which are organization-wide, of critical significance and require organization-wide coordination. This should enhance even further the status of the central information system function. The IS function will probably also play an important staff role as consultant and trouble-shooter of last resort to the local systems. Based on the data presented above, local information systems will tend to report directly to top local management.
