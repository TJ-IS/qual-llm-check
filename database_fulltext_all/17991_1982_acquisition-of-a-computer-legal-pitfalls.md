# Acquisition of a Computer: Legal Pitfalls

Hugo Gevaert \*

Sasstraat 14, B-8480 Vennec, Belgium

I is clear that the content of an agreement to purchase a computer is very important. The main reasons are the high purchase price of a good configuration and the important damage if an implementation fails. It is furthermore obvious that the contents of the standard agreements used by the manufacturers are as aggressive as their high pressure sales techniques.
Some particularly exorbitant clauses of these standard-agreements make it necessary to defend the customer more than usual.
a. Many have a clause that excludes: (1) the contents of the request for proposal, whenever they exist; (2) other written or verbal agreements and representations; (3) specifications in publicity of the supplier; and (4) even the contents of the supplier's offer.
It is clear that such a clause is dangerous, because there is often a great difference between the vendor's publicity or offer and the contents of the standard-agreement.
b. There are also clauses in most contracts aiming to reduce (even to annihilate) the obligations of the supplier or warranties normally provided in other contracts.
c. There are even standard-agreements allowing the vendor to alter unilaterally some clauses of the contract by sending a registered letter to the customer.
These examples should be enough proof to make sure that the vendee reads a contract carefully before signing, or at least takes advice of a good technical consultant before completing a deal.

Keywords: Computer Acquisition, Contractual Prevention, Contractual Hardware Clauses

## 1. Purpose

This article will not make a study of legal doctrine or jurisprudence (i.e. case law). In fact, it would be necessary to analyse both American and French court-decisions; there is not much published jurisprudence from other countries. The main reason for this is that most litigation is ended by arbitration (and hence unpublished), and that the clauses of a standard agreement are so water-tight and so favourable to the interests of the supplier that the customer often gives up in despair.

The purpose of this article is to be practical by pinpointing some clauses which are very dangerous for the vendee and to suggest some clauses more appropriate to the purchaser's interest. The aim is to produce some counter-weight against unilaterally drawn standard clauses. The aim is not to attack vending techniques nor name any suppliers.

## 2. General Contents of a Contract

Standard agreements are seldom explicit in providing specifications on reliability or results. General clauses defending the rights of the vendor are extremely well drafted and contrast sharply with the short clauses concerning price, specifications, results or purposes for which the customer wants to buy. It is therefore not without interest to mention these purposes clearly; e.g., invoicing, and how to achieve it; pay-administration, and how to do it; stock-control or stock-management; accounting, etc.

It is certainly important to detail the purposes of the client; by doing so, the vendor issuing a tender obliges himself to find a solution to these problems.

## 3. The Entire-Agreement or Integration Clause

The short contents of the agreement should not be considered as solely binding the parties. Seldom are good lists of objectives and specifications drafted.

If a good request for proposal exists, the client will be better protected. It is furthermore important to insist that the contents of the vendor's offer become part of the agreement. This will induce the vendor to be prudent and to make no untenable promises.

A possible clause stating the contrary of the integration clause is:

"the request for proposal (containing the objectives and specifications issued by the customer) and the contents and specifications of the vendor's tender are to be considered part of this agreement by reference".

## 4. Quality of the Hardware

Manufacturers or suppliers very seldom deliver defective hardware. It is, nonetheless, important to describe the desired quality and specifications of all equipment, since vague descriptions are usually causes for future trouble. The contract should therefore contain a clause such as:

"The hardware-parts forming the configuration are:

type of machine;

\- capacity of C.P.U.;

\- capacity of external memories;

\- description of peripherals.

"The vendor warrants that all equipment supplied will be new and of original manufacture. "All parts conform with the specifications set forth in the vendor's publicity. The vendor warrants good working conditions of the entire equipment on the day of delivery in accordance with the standard qualities published by him".

## 5. Performance

It is clear that warrants of performance are seldom described in any standard-contract. Because they are, none-the-less, indispensable, all efforts must be made to include such clauses.

One excellent clause is the so called “lemon-clause” used in all purchasing contracts of the U.S. General Services Administration. This relates to the reliability of the equipment and specifically deals with the warrants given by the vendor concerning the effective availability of the equipment. Such a clause is usually structured in terms of percentages of availability.

It procures the possibility for the customer to change all defective parts immediately, without being obliged to call for vendor's maintenance service.

Such a clause could be:

"The equipment will be at the use of the vendee not less than $90\%$ in any 24 hour period, not less than $94\%$ in any 7 days period and not less than $96\%$ in any 30 days period.

"If these percentages of reliability are not met, such defective parts as found by the vendee causing such unavailability must be replaced after notice to the vendor by registered letter. "The vendor shall replace the component within 48 hours of notice received".

In the context of the reliability clause, it is important to mention all run-time specifications and to refer explicitly to all specifications described in the vendor's offer and prior publicity.

## 6. Price

Clauses concerning price are as varied as are standard agreements. A fixed, invariable price is rare, but is nevertheless in the interest of the customer. All but a few vendors refer to possibilities for increasing the price due to augmented import-cuties or devaluation occurring between the signing of the agreement and the time of delivery. Some prices are even expressed in foreign currency and are attached to some sort of stability clause. Such clauses are often contrary to the interests of the customer.

## 7. Cancellation

It seems normal that manufacturers are allergic to clauses that provides the customer rights of cancellation: too many purchases are completed in an atmosphere of inconsideration (e.g., aggressive vending techniques and publicity). As a result, the purchaser often purchases a computer which is too big or too small.

It is a duty to bargain hard on rights of cancellation; it is however reasonable to provide for liquidated damages to be paid to the suppliers. Meanwhile, the purchaser should be able to reduce the configuration; e.g., by purchasing only some components, or by reducing the capacity of the C.P.U.

A potentiel clause is:

"Cancellations by the purchaser of the whole configuration or of some components of it, or reduction of the capacities of the CPU, etc., will be accepted by the vendor.

"At cancellation, liquidated damages will be paid to the vendor as follows:

\- 5% of the price, ex VAT, if cancellation occurs within two months after the date of the agreement;

\- 10% of the price, ex VAT, if cancellation occurs within the period of 2 to 4 months from signing;

\- 15% of the price, ex VAT, if cancellation occurs after 4 months from signing up to the date of delivery.

"If components are cancelled, such percentages will be calculated and applied to the price of the component".

It seems very useful to detail the price of all components in the agreement. In most agreements however they are not.

## 8. Terms of Payment

It is no secret that payment schedules in standard contracts are often tight. High payments are scheduled at early stages, resulting in loss of leverage when the contract is completed. It is clear that clauses on terms of payment have to be drafted to protect the rights of the purchaser and to create some leverage if the vendor is in default.

Thus a clause must be drafted to spread payments:

\- a part at delivery of the hardware.

\- a part at delivery of the software.

\- a part after completion of necessary tests.

\- a part when the whole configuration can be used in a reasonable and reliable way.

## 9. Acceptance Tests and Methods

It is not easy to obtain written obligations with respect to acceptance methods and tests. It is, and always will be, a golden rule to describe the tests and to provide for payment of a substantial part of the purchase-price only after completion of acceptance-tests.

These examples deal with cases where a vendor is supplying hardware and user software in a single agreement.

Then, an ideal clause seems to be:

"After delivery of the hardware and upon implementation of the software, "our acceptance tests will be performed in a period of 30 days.

“(1) A test to determine whether all specifications of hardware and software are met and whether all operating characteristics of both match those issued by the vendor.

"(2) A test to determine whether performance matches the 'bench mark' prior to the installation of the equipment. (Such benchmark is only performed in cases of purchases of rather big computer configurations).

“(3) A test to determine if the system is compatible with the prior system of the user.

“(4) A test to determine if the system meets reliability and availability standards.

"In the event that the operating performance fails to meet the established specifications, the vendor shall modify or adjust the equipment".

It is furthermore necessary to specify a means for resolving the issue if the vendor continues to fail in these tests.

## 10. Compatibility

More and more computers are purchased to work together with existing systems. A purchaser must obtain a warrant of compatibility to ensure that both systems can work side by side or complement each other.

The following clause is sufficient to warrant this compatibility

"The vendor warrants that the equipment sold is both hardware, datastorage, and software compatible with the existing equipment used by the purchaser.

"If the vendor has committed an error, he warrants to take all measures to restore hardware-, data- and software-compatibility".

## 11. Modularity

More and more people are buying smaller computers but want to be able to enhance the purchased system. It is mandatory to ascertain that such opportunities are real and not mere phantasy (as is too often the case). There are cases where modularity was warranted but remained dead because the components where not available. There are even cases where the manufacturing of a computer system was stopped and its components where unavailable shortly after a purchase.

There is even a case where modularity for system A was warranted through use of components from system B or C. It later became clear that system B was no longer manufactured, and that system C never existed (!). A clause to avoid such nuisances is thus needed; it may also be an advantage to retain a good technical consultant.

The following clause may avoid such trouble: "Further enhancement of the purchased system is possible with the components described above up to a total CPU-capacity of (x)K Bytes and external memories of (y)Mega Bytes".

## 12. Interface

Some buyers wish to utilize interface peripherals or other equipment manufactured by other manufacturers than the vendor. Vendors generally do not like such arrangements: they try to avoid interface clauses and don't wish to warrant any interface connection. However, it is possible to obtain a warrant of interface with equipment specially and specifically accepted as such by the vendor.

In such case, however, the vendor attempts to be both party and judge at the same time.

A valuable clause is therefore:

"The vendor warrants that the system permits connection in interface with the following components or peripherals: [a specific list of such equipment]".

## 13. Conveyance of Risks

It is clear that the risks of loss may only be conveyed at the moment that the configuration is really delivered at the premises of the client and not when the computer arrives in some harbour or leaves the premises of the manufacturer.

This does not, of course, deal with problems concerning delivery. Too much depends on the contents of the agreements; there is a great difference between the purchase of hardware alone and of hardware with software.

There is even considerable difference between the contract for delivery of a software package and a contract for development of special software. There even is a difference between the delivery of software in development and the delivery of a system where several hardware and software component are functionally interrelated, and as such delivered in stages.

## 14. Cession of Rights

In some standard contracts, cession of rights by the user is forbidden or a preemptive right of the vendor is at least provided. The preemptive right will not be discussed, but ways and means of price-fixing must be detailed.

The user has much interest in selling the system. Some problems can arise if the user or the bidder wants reconditioning of equipment. Also most maintenance-agreements are not cessible. The parties may not be able to find some third person able to recondition the machines or to procure maintenance.

As an example: currently, in Belgium independent maintenance-services are not available.

The following clause seems important:

“If the vendee wants to cede the configuration, which he may at his own discretion, then the vendor warrants to recondition the equipment at the following price...

"With or without reconditioning, which can thus be demanded at the sole discretion of the vendee, the vendor warrants that all maintenance services will continue to be available to the subsequent buyer at conditions present on the time of cession".

It is not without interest to fix a determinable price for reconditioning, such as: the most economic price of materials and manhours used by the vendor at the moment of cession. It is better to provide for such a so-called favoured-nation clause.

## 15. Termination of Business

It is nearly impossible to prevent a vendor from terminating business.

This termination may create trouble with respect to maintenance and component availability. It is important to have access to all documentation, including maintenance, if termination occurs. It is equally important to state that the vendor shall retain enough components and spare parts during the life-time of the system in order to ensure good working conditions, repair, and replacement.

If one insists, it is possible to obtain a warrant that components and spare parts be held in escrow by a third-party (or present at one's own premises).
