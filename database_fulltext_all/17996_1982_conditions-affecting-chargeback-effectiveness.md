# Conditions Affecting Chargeback Effectiveness

D.H. Drury

Associate Professor of Accounting and Information Systems, McGill University, Faculty of Management, 1001 Sherbrooke Street West, Montreal, PQ H3A 1G5, Canada

A continuing problem of electronic data processing is allocating computer services so that management personnel use them more effectively. A frequent approach is a chargeback system whereby users are charged for data processing services. Data obtained from questionnaire responses indicate significant relationships between organization characteristics, data processing variables and management practices supporting chargeback systems. The focus of the analysis is intended to determine, empirically, the mix of management practices which lead to user awareness of computer applications and their costs and ultimately, improve data processing resource allocations in complex organizations.

Keywords: Chargeback Systems, EDP Control, Systems Integration, Information Systems Management, Cost Control, Accounting Control, Management Practices, User Awareness, Resource Allocation, Implementation Issues.

## 1. Introduction

According to Dearden [1], rapid Electronic Data Processing growth has frequently been accompanied by a decline in efficiency, cost effectiveness and responsiveness to computer users needs. These consequences are reflected in a survey in 1976 by a major public accounting firm which revealed that only one of three financial executives are satisfied with current EDP service levels [2]. The problem most frequently mentioned by 150 executives in 1974 during another study bore the all encompassing title of “poor communications” [3]. Eighty percent of the respondents stated that users do not know enough about computer operations and systems managers do not understand enough about user’s requirements.

In many firms, data processing is primarily treated as a service to the company. A steering committee normally determines the data processing center budget, which is absorbed into the general overhead of the organization. This approach to data processing management has several advantages, including simplicity, avoidance of additional accounting costs, encouragement of user computer experimentation, and a tendency to insulate data processing from fluctuations in the organization. Unfortunately, there are several serious disadvantages including the following:

1. When processing becomes a free service, it is unlikely that management will allow uncontrolled growth, and informal or intuitive means of control are inevitably adopted.

2. The only in this type of system is the upper limit of the EDP budget.

3. Users tend to overuse the system, running jobs of even slight value or interest because they are not charged.

4. There is no feedback which permits users to evaluate how efficiently projects have been handled and who is responsible for the projects.

In order to overcome these difficulties, to allocate scarce computer resources, and to ensure that each application is cost effective, many firms have adopted chargeback systems whereby users are charged for their usage of computer services. Various cost bases and methods of allocating data processing costs to users have been encountered in practice [4].

In the technical literature, the authors overwhelmingly favor the implementation of chargeback systems [5-8]. User involvement in systems development and control of operations costs are often cited as reasons for implementing chargeback systems. A chargeback system promotes effective data processing since users can control their total costs only by controlling their data processing costs [9,10]. The relations between users and the data processing function become more businesslike, since the type and level of service a user gets can be related to the bill paid for the service. The chargeback system provides an economic information link between users and the data processing function. This assists in reducing communication difficulties thereby promoting cost control and the effective usage of resources.

An essential point is that the presence of a chargeback system represents a different philosophy and approach to control of data processing than does the absence of a chargeback system. A chargeback system by itself, however, can be ineffective in achieving cost and resource effectiveness objectives. In order to examine complementary and supporting management conditions and practices in a chargeback environment, data was collected from firms by means of a questionnaire.

## 2. Research Structure

A central problem facing most organizations is taking advantage of specializing through departmentalization or divisionalization while simultaneously coordinating or integrating their collective efforts [11]. A result of this proposition is that highly specialized departments such as data processing, would be expected to adopt mechanisms and structures so that activities can be coordinated and controlled. Viewed from this perspective, the use of a chargeback system is not only a control mechanism, but also, it is part of the mix of the integrative mechanisms adopted by the organization. Of course, some mechanisms would be preconditions while others are mutually supporting.

User computer and cost awareness in this study are therefore assumed to be a function of these variables in the research domain: organization structure, EDP structure, and EDP integrative mechanisms.

A questionnaire was used in order to collect the data. The dependent and independent variables used in the questionnaire are presented in Fig. 1. They are grouped according to the structure of the research domain.

The twenty-three factors form the set of variables used in the construction of the questionnaire. In the initial trial stages, various academics and practitioners examined the wording and instructions for clarity and length, and pretest was conducted. In these trials, the correct answers were previously known and after comparisons were made, the questionnaire was adjusted to eliminate inconsistencies. The final edition of the questionnaire was distributed by mail.

## 3. The Sample

The questionnaire was sent to the senior data processing officer in 239 organizations and 173 responses were received providing a 72% response rate. Included in these were 29 organizations which primarily used external data processing and 60 which did not currently use a chargeback system. The remaining 84 firms do have a chargeback system.

The firms surveyed were from a wide variety of industries, including manufacturing, distribution, service, utilities, oil, food and construction. The firms are in the medium to large size category and most are growing. They are split approximately equally between those that operate out of one location and those with branch offices or divisions. Most of the firms have used computers for longer than ten years, employ more than ten people in their systems and analysis staff, and spend approximately 2% of their total expenditure on data processing. Although several firms did spend more than \$1 million per year on hardware rental, these were the exceptions. Finally, as is true in general, the use of IBM equipment outstrips the total of all others; it represents 63% of the sample.

## 4. Relative Importance of Conditions

Since chargeback systems are currently in vogue, it is our intent to focus on the sub-set of firms which have adopted this management practice. An initial concern, however, was whether the independent variables are important in only a chargeback environment or whether they are also important in a non-chargeback environment. In order to make this determination, the data from the two sub-sets, chargeback and non-chargeback firms, were evaluated using a discriminant function. Discriminant analysis distinguishes statistically between two or more groups of cases by forming combinations of the variables. The analysis separates the variables which are important from those that are unimportant in distinguishing between chargeback and non-chargeback cases [12].

Two-thirds of the firms in each group were used to develop the discriminant function. The coefficients obtained were used with the remaining one-third of the sample in order to evaluate its validity. In developing the discriminant function, 97% of the cases were correctly classified as either chargeback or non-chargeback firms. Also, the set of variables contained 90% of the variance between the two groups. Finally, we further classified them so that four of the twenty-three variables were statistically removed. They are depicted in Table 1.

These four variables are unaffected by the presence, or lack of, a chargeback system. This indicates that organizations with large numbers of programmers and analysts, with dispersed data processing facilities, and having used data processing longer are equally likely to use or not use a chargeback system. In addition, a more detailed evaluation of DP operations budgets is no more likely to occur in a chargeback than a non-chargeback environment.

The remaining sub-set of 19 variables did discriminate firms into chargeback and non-chargeback cases. Using these variables, organizations were classified correctly in 84% of the cases attesting to the discriminant power of the remaining variables. Consequently, these variables have different effects and are related differently in chargeback as opposed to non-chargeback cases.

## 5. Relations of the Variables

As many organizations are moving towards employing a chargeback system for data processing, the relations between these variables become important if chargeback objectives are to be achieved. Two of the variables which were found to have a high discriminating power were (#1) user cost awareness and (#2) user computer awareness. Higher levels of these variables were promoted by, or consistent with, a chargeback environment. The effects of these two variables do not occur in isolation from the others.

Indeed, the variables combine to discriminate in a chargeback environment.

In order to determine how these variables are related and consequently, to examine reinforcing conditions in a chargeback environment, the nineteen discriminating variables were correlated for the subset of firms employing chargeback systems. When higher levels of one variable are consistent with higher levels of another variable, the correlation coefficient is positive. Variables are negatively correlated when higher values of one variable are consistent with lower values of another variable. The direction of the relationships are indicated in subsequent figures by (+) and (−) signs. For example, a larger organization is consistent with higher total hardware rentals (+).

The related pairs of variables whose correlation coefficients were significant at the 5% level were formed into a correlation map which was constructed by connecting pairs of correlated variables. The relations and signs of the relations (+ or -) are identified within the maps. Three major chains of groups of variables emerged from these procedures. The hardware rental cost links all three groups of variables exhibited in Figures 2, 3 and 4.

The first major group of variables appears in Figure 2. It is evident from these relationships that organization and data processing variables interact in a chargeback system environment. As would be expected, larger organizations spend more on data processing. When larger amounts of money are spent, the executive responsible for data processing is placed higher in the administrative structure of the organization. This condition may be important considering the coordination and control problems created by large computer operations. For example, advances towards distributed processing by the organization may still require centralized authority over the data processing function at a high enough level in the organization to justify and influence resource allocation in data processing.

Another consequence of larger organizations is that the frequency of review of data processing with management and users tends to decrease. The reaction is to place more emphasis on the planning function expanding the length of the planning horizon. It should be noted that any connection with this effect and the level in the organization of the senior data processing executive is not evident from the analysis.

Data processing and organization variables generate other business reactions in the second major group of variables exhibited in Figure 3. Starting again with the hardware total cost in the upper left of the diagram, higher hardware costs and the percent expenditure on data processing by the organization are positively linked. This should be expected since more applications of data processing are possibly with a longer time frame and further, the success of these previous applications leads subsequently to higher spending on data processing resources [4]. Costs of data processing can be expected to rise and with the added computer and personnel resources, applications expand into areas where users no longer have common needs. The complexity of user needs becomes particularly apparent in decentralized environments, where for example, divisions set their own budgets and personnel policies. Special interests of user groups surface creating complex user priorities to be handled by data processing.

As Figure 3 indicates, more decentralization and specialized needs of users are positively linked with an increase in data processing being done outside the firm. This result should be expected since decentralization leads divisions to seek out efficient sources of their specialized processing needs which may increasingly, be external to the firm. Centralized controls or strict rules may have to be enforced so that the internal computer resource remains economically viable.

Consistent with the result that there is less frequent review of data processing performance, in the previous figure (Figure 2), there is less detailed review of data processing budgets. Several reasons are possible including the final acceptance of the computer systems as a necessity in the business, or stability in the growth of data processing spending over time or simply, lack of attention due to pressing issues in other parts of the organization. More likely, longer term planning as referred to in the discussion of the first chain, supplants in importance the detailed, incremental budgeting approaches used in the early history of the firm's experience with data processing. A longer planning horizon is also consistent with the fact that significant changes in the level of data processing costs are difficult in the short term because most data processing costs tend to be fixed over that period of time.

The last major group of variables also leads from the hardware cost variable in Figure 4. With a data processing chargeback system in place, the frequency of reporting by data processing increases. A larger operation is more complex, plays a greater role in the efficient management of the firm and consequently, more frequent reporting by data processing of chargeback statistics should be expected. This increased frequency of reporting has two effects. First it is negatively correlated with the use of steering committees. Both are frequently used integration devices with the rest of the organization. The analysis suggests that either one or the other of these is needed but in general, both do not occur together in a chargeback environment. Neither does the level of the senior steering committee executive assist in increasing the frequency of reporting although, if there is a steering committee there is likely to be a senior executive chairing it. Consequently, where chargeback systems are in use, a steering committee may conflict with it.

A second and perhaps more important implication also arises from examination of Figure 4. One of the more difficult problems facing the organization is making users aware of data processing costs. The problem is how to achieve this awareness.

While a chargeback system may be implemented with this objective, the indication from Figure 4 is that a chargeback system cannot achieve this objective in isolation. In the first step, more frequent reporting of data processing performance is necessary. Then, in the second step, users are assigned their own data processing personnel. This can serve several important functions. First, data processing personnel dedicated to specific departments are aware of unique user problems and thus, integrate data processing with user departments. Second, chargeback statistics can be interpreted and explained locally and finally, users have the means of acquiring knowledge of computer operations.

In order to understand the meaning of reported costs, it is essential that there is understanding of the activities to which they relate. Knowledge or computer operations is an important prerequisite in data processing. Only then, and in that order, do users achieve awareness about the costs of data processing. Consequently, it should not be expected that a chargeback system alone will achieve the desired awareness, detailed consideration, or commitment objectives. The preconditions and concurrent conditions in conjunction with a chargeback system are important in achieving chargeback objectives.

## 6. Conclusions

An empirical study is designed to indicate the experiences of businesses. In this study of firms which use data processing chargeback systems, organizational and data processing variables were found to interact. While chargeback systems are intended to overcome user and data processing management difficulties, they are not a panacea. In fact, the results of the analysis indicate that there are organizational and data processing factors which when present, may mitigate against their effective use. The results indicate that the mix of mechanisms is important and some may be inconsistent in achieving the desired objectives.

Based upon the results of this study, there is an indication that firms have achieved user computer and cost awareness with chargeback systems. This has been accomplished through implementing supporting EDP integrative mechanisms. The process contains a series of steps which include the appropriate budgeting and review techniques with users and senior management and the assignment of data processing personnel to the sub-units of the organization. The implementation sequence and the necessary concurrent conditions and factors should be given serious consideration. Unsatisfactory performance of chargeback systems may be attributable to these factors rather than to the chargeback system itself.

There are several unresolved issues remaining from these analyses which deserve consideration. These include the optimal mix of control devices, sub-unit autonomy versus controls on external processing, coping with complex requirements in differentiated sub-units and the changes in budget and review controls as EDP moves through various growth cycles. Finally, while the steps to achieve results have been indicated, the duration of the steps may be a critical element. The firm which moves too quickly to achieve integration and effectively control data processing through chargeback systems may end up not achieving them at all.
