# Human Factors in User Documentation

R.K. Toms

"EWES", Wasbeeklaan 15, 2361 HG Warmond, The Netherlands

Writing user documentation is a job most programmers do not enjoy and are not normally trained in. As computer users become aware of the importance of ease of use, so must computer suppliers be aware of ways to improve user documentation. As well as abilities in technical communication, people who generate high quality computer user documentation require system programming experience and human understanding of computer users. The paper relates the importance of good documentation to user productivity, analyzes the process of generating documentation, and discusses the skills needed by persons employed in that industry.

Keywords: Application program, Availability, Human Factors, Information Dissemination, Interactive Computer System, Online Information, Printed Documentation, Text Processing, Usability.

He has held a variety of positions in the computing industry, and has been in the business of computer documentation since 1971. Amongst a number of reports and articles about programming documentation, Mr. Toms has written a programmer's debugging guide for a major operating system, operation and programming guides for a data communications net-

## 1. Introduction

One hardly picks up a trade magazine nowadays without finding at least one article on human factors, but seldom do they mention human factor aspects of computer documentation. Documentation, whether displayed at terminal screens or printed in manuals, is often the first view that potential customers have of a new system or application program: Poor documentation can result in an unacceptable system [1].

## 1.1. A Change of Approach

As computer users become aware of the importance of ease of use, so must computer suppliers be aware of ways to improve user documentation. Instead of supplying several volumes of paper containing anything from detailed logic descriptions, to typed memos, telexes, and even scribbled notes, we can, with the aid of text processing systems, produce integrated sets of usable manuals where each manual is written for specific user tasks. In meeting this requirement, manuals must be given the proper titles, and the mass of information generated by programmers, designers, and analysts, at various levels of detail, has to be placed into the right manual. The process of sorting through complex information and processing it into usable material, requires the application of human factors [2].

## 1.2. The Business Case

Many people ask, "What is the cost justification?" Companies either set aside large numbers of man-months for documentation and education, or simply struggle along with what they have, resulting in user frustration and irritation, and inevitably a loss in productivity. A possible equation is:

Productivity = Availability and Usability.

If a screwdriver can not be found when it is needed, owning it is of not much use. If it has a rounded tip, finding it will not help. In the first case the screwdriver is UNAVAILABLE; in the second it is UNUSABLE.

In an automated system, availability may be represented by:

● JOB TURN AROUND TIMES

● TERMINAL RESPONSE TIME

● DOWN TIME

Great progress has been made in the inspection and testing of items relating to the AVAILABILITY of a computer system.

However, little progress has been made in USA-BILITY testing. This is because usability, or ease of use of software, includes the human interface to program operations.

The following methods are used to inform people about how to use and maintain computer programs.

\- Formal education and on-the-job training. Education packages are sometimes generated during the development of a product, or made locally after delivery of it.

● Printed documentation. This includes:

introductory and summary explanations;

\- explanations about how to use a system;

\- installation details;

\- program internal logic descriptions;

\- management and error reports.

● Online Information. Nowadays, communicating with programs is through data entry/inquiry at remote terminals. This includes:

\- man/computer dialogs;

\- HELP screens;

\- menu selection techniques.

## 2. Usability of Documentation

There are several ways to measure the readability of documents, but usability is rather difficult to measure [3]. The factors affecting the usability of documentation are listed in Table 1.

## Table 1

1. Retrievability

This depends on:

\- subjects/topics covered – as suggested by the title of a manual;

\- organization of the subjects/topics;

● quality of the table of contents;

● quality of the index;

● use of section markers such as 'running' tabs;

● dependency on information in other manuals;

● appropriateness and accuracy of cross references (pointers) to related information in other manuals.

2. Understandability

This depends on:

● style of writing:

\- uncomplicated phrases;

\- 'everyday' words;

\- unambiguous statements;

\- defined and explained terminology and abbreviations.

● method or presentation:

\- neat page layout;

\- clarity of figures;

\- Consistency in size and shape of symbols;

● quality of printing/copying; amount of reduction.

3. Appropriateness

This depends on:

● technical completeness;

● technical accuracy;

● level of detail.

## 3. System Programming Documentation

To most people, system programming documentation means:

● Program specifications;

● Workbooks;

● Program listings.

These contain detailed descriptions, primarily written for programming development and project control. If a totally usable system is desired, documentation designed and written for users must be added.

## 5. Some Typical Documentation Problems

In an effort to reduce the quantity and size of the printed documents, as well as the work necessary to make them, it has been suggested that all documentation be put online. This is a misinterpretation of the problem. Information displayed online needs a great deal of attention to human behavior, much more so than printed documents.

## 5.1. Printed Documentation Problems

If manuals are not designed properly, it is difficult to find information, and it is often not at the level of detail required, needing a further search of the documentation. Descriptions may be written in a variety of styles, requiring careful study of the text before it can be understood. All this constitutes a loss in productivity to the person requiring information; it also leads to “experts” who tend to leave a department sooner or later.

## 5.2. Online Information Problems

Information displayed at remote terminals is particularly sensitive to human factors. For example, HELP information screens require layout, amount of text, and level of detail, to be considered together with the guiding instructions about scrolling, and returning. Keys and commands must be simple to use and clearly defined. This requires, during design, a knowledge about the tasks, skills, and experience of people who will use the screens.

The problem of retrieval (indexing through menu selection screens) to find the 'right' information at the required level of detail becomes a major design problem involving human factors [5].

Screen displays generally require the use of computer terminology and abbreviations in message text as part of the displayed information. In most cases, system designers and programmers are responsible for defining these, with the result that emphasis is on ease of programming, rather than ease of use. To illustrate the effect of that statement, a private address in Holland typically has the form:

## 6. The Mechanics of Humanizing Program Descriptions

Getting the right information in the right place at the right level of detail consists of a number of activities. Figure 2 shows these activities as “Information dissemination and humanization”, together with the inputs to it, and the resulting output.

Detailed programming information is supplied by programming developers and analysts. The descriptions can be in the form of handwritten notes, memos, telexes, project documents, etc. This information is disseminated and user documents are generated.

## 6.1. The Humanizing Process

Once the information has been gathered, a rather complex operation takes place. All the information must be categorized and put into a consistent and usable form according to the definition of user tasks. This implies an involvement of a process (human or automated) which can determine what the application is supposed to be doing for whom, when, and why. Tight control is also required to avoid duplication of effort and technical errors.

By examination of the block labeled "DISSEMINATION & HUMAN FACTORIZATION" in Fig. 2, we see the various pieces of system information listed in a random manner. This is exactly how it arrives from programming, and it requires continuous scanning to select "how to use" information from "explanatory" and "how the system does it" information. This initial categorization forms the outline of the manuals, the titles, and their general organization. As more input arrives, each section is built up into chapters. Review cycles can be planned for either individual chapters or complete manuals.

Actual writing is confined to writing introductory and overview material. This is because most of the descriptive text is generated by programmers in the form of text files. Editing and rewriting these 'text files' has to be done carefully since a slight change of wording in technical descriptions can sometimes change the technical accuracy. Sections of the manuals are checked for grammar, and for consistency in terminology and abbreviations. Simultaneously, online documentation is checked for the proper use of terms and abbreviations used in message text and responses. The latter is also checked for ease of use.

Presentation of information is also important, so attention is given to page (or screen) layout (headings, paragraphing, line spacing), and to illustrations [6]. It is worth noting that illustrations present a problem in text processing environments; they require special techniques to generate and transmit them over data communication links.

Ideally, the “human factor process” should start when users are initially defining their requirements, and continue throughout development. Trade-offs between timeliness and quality sometimes have to be made. Deciding what to trade off is another human factor problem.

## 6.2. User Feedback

The feedback loop allows monitoring the work, and provides a source of information about use of systems. The feedback also gives information about interests, training, experience, and abilities of users; this helps in obtaining a better understanding of user needs. The information can be gathered by personal visits to customer locations, or else by surveys which ask questions about the content, level of detail, value of diagrams, and technical accuracy of descriptions in the manuals.

This has at least two positive effects:

● Potential customers have a chance to comment on the planned documentation.

● The human factor analyst stays up to date with the user world.

## 7. Resources

## 7.1. Machines

The entire generation and production process is made easier by the use of text processing systems. In the future, we shall see the integration of the following technologies:

● computer graphics and plotters;

● high resolution image copy printers;

● data dictionaries;

● multiple and split screen terminals;

\- automatic program documentation generation. These technologies extend today's text processing systems and are essential to meet the increasing demand for high quality documents.

## 7.2. People

Making the human factor process work properly depends on an expert interpretation of what information is required by users. Careful scrutiny of information gathered from programmers is necessary. For example, we must be sure that terminal operator's are never asked to search through program listings to complete terminal operations. To complete the PROCESS block of Figure 2, it is worth addressing the skills required by persons who work in this area.

The use of online text processing systems is, to some extent, changing the work of technical writers. We seem to be moving into an environment where it is necessary to compile text files created by programmers and analysts, into organized, logical, and usable material.

The online documenter must at least:

\- Be a “communicator”; be able to describe complex information to other people in ‘their’ terms;

\- Be a good listener;

\- Be able to define the audience for a system and view it from each audience position in a practical way;

\- Be creative (to 'see' apparently abstract connections):

\- Be able to analyze complex information and organize it into categories at various levels of detail:

\- Maintain a global picture, yet be able to understand the system technically, at several levels; This may infer a system programming background plus an appreciation of cost effects;

● Plan and coordinate documentation reviews;

\- Be able to stimulate 'all parties' into cooperation;

\- Spot check documentation for usability and carry out customer feedback surveys to maintain a common and reasonable degree of quality:

\- Be prepared to use advanced techniques, for example, text processing.

## 8. Conclusion

We have addressed the usability aspects of user documentation for complex interactive applications. For less complex systems, such an exercise may not be justified. When documenting a system, it is important to realize that the amount of documentation is not proportional to the size of a system, whether that be measured in the number of 'black boxes' that constitute the hardware, or whether measured in the number of lines of code that constitute the software. It would be ideal to prove that a well designed manual actually produces a saving, but there are too many dependencies such as: How often a particular description is needed. This in turn, depends on the ability, interests, and experience about the topic, of the person requiring the description.

Because of the importance of documentation to program operation and maintenance, it is essential to have technical communication specialists on the staff of programming development departments.

Summarizing the main points:

● There is a relationship between the usability of programming documents and the productivity of persons using computer programs.

● Factors affecting the usability of printed information are applicable to displayed information.

● Online information and printed documentation complement each other. To get optimum usability, both must be subjected to human factors.

● A blend of programmer/technical communicator skills is essential to generate either printed or displayed information.

As a final word: "Documentation to a computer system is like a double bass to a dance band. It's only noticed when it's missing or played badly".

## Appendix A User Document Library

The following manuals, or a subset of them, have been identified for a 'large' online application program:

## General Introduction Manual

This is a ‘management’ summary discussing main features and options, and possible impact on current operations. This manual is written for all users who wish to know what the system can do for them.

## Description of System Functions and Options

An explanation of all functions, and options provided by the system as viewed by the business users for whom the system is designed. It should not contain program logic descriptions.

The audience includes all business users (the people who use the computer system as a tool to aid their work), systems analysts, system planners, education staff, application programmers, and technical support functions.

## Business User's Guide

This includes "How to use" information. The emphasis is on how the function and facilities can best be utilized in the business environment. By definition, this manual describes at an appropriate level of detail, the modifiable parameters and user exits.

The audience is the same as the previous manual.

## Terminal Operator's Guide

Explains LOG ON and update/inquiry operations to be done at terminals.

The audience is anyone who sits at a terminal and inquires into or updates data maintained by the system.

## Messages and Error Codes Reference Manual

Describes the identity (unique number) and the text of all messages generated by the system (online and batch) with explanations of their cause, and recommended response. Also error codes and indications displayed on screens or printed on reports.

The audience consists of all users.

## Screens and Reports Reference Manual

Pictures or layouts of the various types of screens and reports generated by the system.

The audience is all users who need to know what kind of information is shown through the screen displays and reports, yet who may not have immediate access to the system.

## Central Site Operations Guide

Descriptions of batch and online applications with job streams required to maintain the central data bases, and network operations at the "host" computer. Descriptions are best presented in the form of summary lists and flow diagrams.

The audience is operations personnel at the central site.

## Applications Programmer's Reference Manual

This manual includes programming detail to aid maintenance and debugging teams. It is a "counter" to the program listings. It includes such topics as:

\- Data base structure;

\- Program/module invocation: per system function;

\- Messages issued per module,

\- Transactions generated per module;

\- Screens generated per module;

\- Summary of user exists together with their pseudo code and examples of their use;

\- Summaries of debugging hints and problem determination utilities;

\- Naming conventions of modules, data bases, messages, etc. The audience is technical support personnel, and users requiring details about program/module relationships and user exits.

## Installation, Planning, and Conversion Guide

## Describes:

\- Pre-installation activities and prerequisites;

A checklist of items that must be on site, and items that must be implemented in an existing system to prepare for the new application. (Hardware/software, disk space, terminals, etc.)

\- Parameters and user exists required for the correct installation of the system;

\- Test samples, and result comparisons;

\- Job detail and reports generated during the installation phases;

\- Performance estimates and results.

The audience is primarily local (country) technical support who have to install a system.
