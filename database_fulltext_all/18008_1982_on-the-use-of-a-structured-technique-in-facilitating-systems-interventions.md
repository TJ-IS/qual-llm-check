# On the Use of a Structured Technique in Facilitating Systems Interventions \*

Edward J. Lusk

Social Systems Sciences and Operations Research, Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Vance Hall CS, 3733 Spruce St., Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

and

Michael Kersnick

Pennsylvania State University, Delaware County Campus, University Park, PA 16802, USA

The paper discusses the effect of the systems age on problem solving. The problems which exist in organizations usually require discussions among the decision makers. Such discussions are intended to lead to a dialogue which hopefully results in effective corrective actions. This paper suggests that a structured information processing model can be used to help motivate the dialogue necessary to develop a design to cope with the systems problem. A case is discussed.

Keywords: Information Processing, Intervention Strategies, Analytic Hierarchy Model.

## 1. Introduction

To cope with dynamic and complex organizational problems, managers are often required to engage in discussions. The effectiveness of this rather time consuming means of communication is related to how information is processed during the discussion. Experience suggests that such discussions must be managed so that a dialogue develops in a timely manner. Rarely do discussions which do not use guidelines for processing information produce decision relevant information. For example, a group of regional directors of Family Planning Agencies were interested in organizing a meeting to discuss various long-range planning alternatives. In discussing how this meeting should be organized, the regional coordinator remarked that these meetings were rarely productive. She said that the meetings seemed to be verbal endurance contests and usually ended without the discussion "getting off the ground." She felt that because of this, the Regional Family Planning Agencies routinely missed significant opportunities to plan for the future. She wondered if there was a way to utilize the time at these regional meetings so that the discussions "produced something."

The purposes of this paper are to (1) discuss the traditional role of systems consultants in organization interventions, (2) suggest how a structured model may be used to facilitate the intervention, and (3) discuss a case. Consider now the nature of systemic interactions in organizations.

## 2. Systems Considerations in Problem Solving

The last thirty years have seen the emergence of the systems age. The transition from the Machine Age to the Systems Age has been subtle but has had a profound impact on the nature of problem solving heuristics. As Ackoff [3] notes:

"No age has a starting point; it emerges imperceptibly in bits and pieces that eventually combine, first to produce an awareness that something fundamental is happening, then to provide a new world view.

...By the 1950's interdisciplinary scientific activities proliferated. These included the management sciences, decision sciences, computer sciences, information sciences, cybernetics, policy sciences, peace science, and many others. The overlap of interest among them and the similarities in their practices led to a search for a theme common to all of them.

By the mid-1950s it was generally recognized that the source of similarities of the interdisciplines was their shared preoccupation with the behavior of systems.

A system is a set of two or more elements that satisfies the following three conditions: 1) the behavior of each element has an effect on the behavior of the whole; 2) the behavior of the elements and their effects on the whole are interdependent; and 3) however subgroups of the elements are formed, each has an effect on the behavior of the whole and none has an independent effect on it."

In such dynamic and complex environments, the problem solving routines that previously were adequate in facilitating organizational problem solving often are no longer adequate [1-9,11,12].

In fact, the inability of previously useful problem solving routines to perform adequately today is often cited as evidence of the systems age.

## 3. Traditional Role of Systems Consultants

In the systems age, the emphasis seems to have shifted from isolated solutions to “intervention strategies” which lead to designs. The term often used in systems interventions for a solution is a “design.” This terminology is preferred because a design implies an on-going evaluation and redesign while a solution implies a finality not likely to be realizable. Such designs usually require a sustained interaction of most members of the organization. Although these designs depend upon the generation of technical information, such as forecasts and detailed financial analyses, the processing of such information to achieve a solution is generally attempted as a dialectic, i.e., a verbal interchange intent upon articulating points of view in order to reach a common understanding. However, dialogues develop from discussion groups. Creating and sustaining a dialogue from a discussion group is a complicated task. Since the success of the systems interventions depends upon the quality of the dialogue, motivating a dialogue becomes an important issue.

In our experience, systems consultants first try to insure that they will have enough time to motivate a dialogue by sensitizing management to the possibility of a “crisis.” The role of the systems consultant in this phase is to collect information which can be assembled and projected to suggest that if things do not change, a catastrophe will befall the organization. In many cases, such a strategy is effective in insuring that decision makers will enthusiastically commit the time necessary to deal with the “documented crisis.” Systems consultants employ this strategy to (1) avoid the complacency and disinterest which inhibit discussion groups from developing a dialogue and (2) to insure that sufficient time will be available. In our experience, the magnitude of the crisis and the amount of managerial time committed to the systems intervention are directly related.

Even though the perceived crisis in a sense "guarantees" a substantial commitment of time by the managers, time alone is not sufficient to transform a discussion into a dialogue. In fact, discussions which drone-on interminably are often counter-productive. The longer the discussion continues, the less likely it is that the initial systems problem will exist in the form initially perceived. In this case, the dialogue may result in a design which "solves a problem" that no longer exists, thereby creating further difficulties [4]. Therefore, once the crisis is established, the systems consultant must work efficiently to utilize the enthusiastic commitment of time to transform the discussion into a dialogue.

In our experience, the next phase finds the systems consultant requiring the discussion group to articulate (1) their sense of the difficulty, (2) the mission of the organization, and (3) a set of criteria to evaluate the design. These criteria must relate to both the system's problem and to the mission of the organization. Specification of such criteria is critical if the design to be developed from the dialogue is to be evaluated in terms of the mission of the organization.

The way that this information is usually elicited is that a discussion group is organized and the systems consultant "charges" the group with the task of specifying the system's problem, the mission, and evaluation criteria. Then the consultant attempts to transform the discussion into a dialogue by monitoring the logical relationships articulated and implied during the ensuing discussion. For example, if an individual states $x$ and $y$ which logically imply $z$ and subsequently not- $z$ is stated or implied, the consultant requires this inconsistency to be resolved. This inconsistency feedback, which then must be resolved, is iterative in nature which presumably involves the decision makers in the focused resolution of the issue leading to a design. In this way, the consultant attempts to move the discussion to a dialogue.

In summary, the system's consultant collects information which documents a likely catastrophe. This initially motivates managers to commit enthusiastically a significant portion of time to dealing with the systems problem. Then the consultant attempts to transform the discussion about the difficulty, mission, and evaluation criteria into a dialogue which leads to a design by monitoring the logical relationships articulated during the discussion. The intension is to use the logical monitoring as feedback to the group which hopefully expedites the transition from discussion to dialogue to design.

## 4. Use of a Structured Model to Facilitate a Dialogue

In our experience, sometimes this logical monitoring works. However, sometimes the logical monitoring is difficult to do and the apparent inconsistencies articulated during the discussion seem to diffuse the discussion, polarize various individuals, and in some cases seem to be responsible for the disintegration of the discussion. There are a myriad number of reasons why this may happen. However, one principle reason may be the act of intervention itself. Often when there are problems in motivating a dialogue, they seem to be caused by the person responsible for the logical monitoring.

As an alternative to logical monitoring by an individual, we have been most impressed with the analytic hierarchy model [10] which has proven useful in motivating a dialogue from a discussion group. The analytic hierarchy model assigns priorities to alternatives and develops a measure of inconsistency regarding the specific judgements rendered about the alternatives. In our experience, this model is to be preferred to logical monitoring by a specific individual because by allowing the group to proceed without reliance on an individual monitoring the logical relationships articulated during the discussion, a sense of “group ownership” of the process is produced. This sense of ownership seems to produce a dialogue relatively rapidly compared to the individual monitoring method. Consider now a discussion of how the analytic hierarchy model is used in a systems intervention.

## 4.1. The Analytic Hierarchy Model

The analytic hierarchy model (Appendix) is used to develop a priority score for a set of alternatives.

In developing the priority score, judgement matrices are formulated. Each judgement matrix has a measure of inconsistency associated with it. If the entries in the judgement matrix are not logically related, the inconsistency measure is close to its maximum value. If the entries in the judgement are logically related, then the measure of inconsistency equals its minimum value. This measure of inconsistency may be used by the group as the logical monitor.

The way that we have used the analytic hierarchy model in structuring a systems intervention is that at some point in the discussion the group is requested to formulate criteria they believe to be useful in characterizing the systems problem under consideration and are related to the mission of the organization. When the criteria are proposed, rather than having an individual monitor the logical relationships in the ensuing discussion, the group develops a judgement matrix regarding the relative preference or importance of the criteria regarding the mission of the organization. The inconsistency measure associated with the judgement matrix is used as feedback regarding the quality of the judgements recorded. This self-monitoring feedback usually motivates the group to reconsider the relationships and refine their analysis. The process of refinement usually marks the beginning of a dialogue.

Consider now a case where the analytic hierarchy model was used in the design of an investment evaluation system.

## 4.2. Case: Development of Investment Evaluation System

A group of physicians and managers who owned a chain of hospitals were dissatisfied with the way capital investments were evaluated. They were concerned because after the capital budget was approved, it was difficult to adequately explain why certain projects were approved while other projects were not approved. According to the decision makers, the projects seemed to be evaluated and approved randomly or on some unknown basis. Management believed that the major problem was the absence of clear criteria for differentiating among investment alternatives. The president of the organization was concerned because in the past this and similar groups never were able to develop and agree upon a way to evaluate investments.

After some preliminary discussion, the group formulated the following criteria for differentiating among investments:

$C_{1}$ : Improve Patient Welfare (PW)

$C_{2}$ : Provide for Maximization of (LRP) Long-Run Profitability, and

$C_{3}$ : Improve the Status of the Hospital Group (S).

## 5. Summary

The use of a structural method to motivate the dialogue in a systems intervention is primarily an attempt to reduce the amount of decision maker time required to develop a dialogue. However, structural methods also promote the self-organization of groups which often further saves decision maker time. This is particularly important when a number of small groups of individuals within the organization are required to be engaged in a dialogue related to the intervention. Since in most cases, small groups should meet during the same general time, a structural method provides a way of organizing such small groups without requiring a number of systems consultants. In this way, the differences between the nature of the intervention can be minimized relative to the disparate experiences often introduced by various systems consultants. This is an extremely important point. In our experience, creating small groups is inevitable. In about half of the cases where research associates or organizational members were used to monitor the logical relationships articulated during the discussion, the discussion groups never seemed to develop a dialogue. In most cases, the person monitoring the logical relationships was clearly the source of polarization resulting in the breakdown of the group. In our experience, using a common structuring method such as the analytical hierarchy model has reduced the number of undesirable outcomes.

The most telling instance that we can recount relative to the issue of the use of a structural method to provide for logical monitoring is the comment made at the conclusion of the intervention by the president of the hospital group:

"With our feeling and understanding of what we all have been through, I think we can take it from here. "Jack" understands the model and if we need more sessions he can work things through."

This group self-confidence is necessary if the intervention is to be self-sustaining. We believe that a model greatly enhances this self-confidence and therefore is an important aspect of the systems intervention.

## Appendix

The Intransitivity Aspect of Analytic Hierarchy Model

The analytic hierarchy model, which was developed by Saaty [10], generates a set of priority weights for a set of decision alternatives given a specified set of criteria. In this paper, our interest is focused primarily on the ability of the model to generate a measure of intransitivity.

The model uses a measurement scale which has only the following elements:

(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9).

The numeric descriptors of this scale are taken to mean:

1 if alternative $a^x$ and alternative $a^y$ are equally important on a particular criterion.

3 if $a^x$ is weakly more important to $a^y$ on a particular criterion.

5 if $a^x$ is strongly more important to $a^y$ on a particular criterion.

7 if $a^{x}$ is demonstrably more important preferred to $a^{y}$ on a particular criterion.

9 if $a^x$ is absolutely more important to $a^y$ on a particular criterion.

Intermediate values 2, 4, 6, and 8 may be used if the primary descriptors (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) are not adequate to represent an individual's judgements.

The result of this evaluation will be noted by specifying one of the scale descriptors: 1, 3, 5, 7, or 9 or an intermediate value and entering that value in the appropriate row and column of a judgement matrix as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left[ A \right] _ {j} = a _ {j} ^ {k h}, \quad h, k = 1, \dots , n, \quad j = 1, \dots , m, \\ n \times n \end{array}
$$

where the superscripts k and h indicate that the kth alternative is compared to the hth alternative and the result of that comparison quantified using the scale system and recorded in the kth row and hth column of A, and j denotes a particular criterion, $1 \leq j \leq m$ ; for any $a^{kh}$ , if k = h, then the value of $a^{kh} = 1$ and for any alternative, the following inverse relationship is assumed: $a^{kh} = 1/a^{hk}$ .

The largest eigenvalue of $[A]$ , is then abstracted and used as the measure of intransitivity of the entries of the judgement matrix $[A]_i$ .

The central feature of the model regarding logical monitoring is the computation of the eigenvalue of a judgement matrix. The way a judgement matrix is constructed allows the computation of an eigenvalue which is directly related to the relative intransitivity of the entries of the judgement matrix. For example, assume that criteria A, B, and C are to be evaluated respecting the pair-wise scaling system. If A contrast B is given a 3, and B contrast C is given a 3, and A contrast C is given a 9, then all the pairwise comparisons in that $3 \times 3$ matrix are transitive. In this case, the eigenvalue would equal 3 which indicates that the judgements were transitive. If the judgements were not transitive the value of the eigenvalue would be greater than the trace of the judgement matrix, 3 in this case. The greater the intransitivity the greater would be the value of the eigenvalue until it reaches its maximum at 91/9 for the case of a $3 \times 3$ matrix. Since the eigenvalue measures intransitivity, it can be used in evaluating the logical relationship of the rendered judgements. Therefore, the analytical hierarchy model may be used to motivate a dialogue by providing for the group a means of monitoring the logical interrelationships as recorded in the judgement matrices.
