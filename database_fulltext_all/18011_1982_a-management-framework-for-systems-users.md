# A Management Framework for Systems Users

Julian W. Riehl

Information Systems Department, School of Business, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia 23284, USA

Many business managers today are in need of practical guidance in determining which information systems management practices are appropriate for their particular organization. Of central importance is the management of the information systems development process. A field research study was conducted at five major U.S. corporations to determine those information systems development management practices that had proved successful. The research method consisted of in-depth interviews with managers and technical people at each company. In addition, the reputation of each company as a successful manager of the information systems function was validated using a set of organizational performance criteria developed by a panel of prominent information systems professionals. The results of the research are presented in the form of a planning and control framework of successful management practices. The framework describes and classifies those practices common to all companies and clearly differentiates those practices which varied significantly among the companies studied. The framework is designed to enable management to readily identify those information system development practices that have proved successful and to select from among them those most appropriate to the local situation. It can also serve as a guide for self-study on the part of the individual manager.

Keywords: User, management involvement, user organization involvement, top management involvement, systems development, planning and control, strategic planning, project planning, project control, project management, system life cycle.

## 1. Introduction

Much emphasis is currently being placed on the importance of effective user-management involvement in the development of computer-based information systems in business type organizations. The subject is being addressed with increased frequency in management and information systems literature. Despite the importance being attached to the subject, however, a survey of the literature reveals little information being provided in a form that is of practical benefit to the average non-technical manager seeking to increase his individual competence but lacking the time for extensive personal research.

Among the questions of central importance to the business manager interested in defining his proper role in information systems development would be the following: What user-management practices have proven successful in real-world business environments? What generalizations can be drawn from successful practices that would be useful to any business organization in designing an approach to effective user-management involvement in informations systems development?

An in-depth field research program designed to begin to answer these and related questions was conducted at five major U.S. corporations. The corporations involved were selected primarily because of their widely recognized reputations for successful management of the information systems function. They were also selected so as to represent a wide variety of those basic organizational characteristics identified as primary indicators of corporate activity, i.e., organization size (medium or large), organizational complexity (single or multi-product), philosophy of organization (centralized or decentralized) and strength of competition [1]. The inclusion of such a range of corporate characteristics in the survey sample was designed to give the research results as broad a base of applicability as possible. The major characteristics of each company in the study are summarized in the following section of this article.

The results of the research provided a comprehensive and detailed comparative analysis of the user-management planning and control practices employed in information systems development within the five companies studied. The purpose of this paper is to synthesize the research findings and present them in a format that is of practical value to the interested user-manager. This takes the form of a framework of management policies and procedures designed to aid individual companies (or managers) in the selection of those proven information systems management practices most appropriate to their particular situation. The framework is presented in section 4.

## 2. Company Characteristics

Significant organizational characteristics of each company in the study are summarized as follows.

## 2.1. Company A

Company A is a major surface transportation corporation providing passenger and freight services to the chief population and industrial sectors of several geographical regions of the United States. Total annual operating revenues are in the range of one to five billion dollars. Company A is essentially a single product (transportation) company and is organized along traditional functional lines of operating management responsibility. As a result, the top management control of the company is highly centralized; financial control is exercised through standard cost centers; a form of relatively tight central control is in effect over investment and budget decisions.

## 2.2. Company B

Company B is a multi-national diversified manufacturing and services corporation engaged in the production of automotive, construction, and agriculture equipment; electronic equipment and appliances; basic materials including glass, steel, and plastics; insurance and financial services. Total annual operating revenues for the year are in excess of ten billion dollars. The company is organized along multi-divisional lines with over twenty major operating divisions operated as profit-centers. The degree of financial and investment control that is exercised by central corporate management is described as “moderate” in terms of the degree of delegated decision-making exercised.

## 2.3. Company C

Company C is an international multi-service corporation specializing in traveler lodgings, food services and leisure-time activity services. Total operating revenues are between one billion and five billion dollars annually. The company is organized along multi-divisional lines with over twenty companies, world-wide, operated on a decentralized profit-center basis. The type of financial and investment control that is exercised is described as highly centralized with respect to annual budget formulation and capital investment decisions.

## 2.4. Company $D$

Company D is a multi-national corporation engaged in manufacturing and services related to information-handling systems. The primary emphasis of the business operation is in those systems that are computer-based. The company is organized into five autonomous operating groups and thirteen divisions world-wide. The total gross operating revenue for the company exceeds ten billion dollars for the operating year. The management philosophy of the company is described as one that combines strong staff direction in planning and financial control with autonomous operating authority on the part of the operating groups. Financial control is exercised through the treatment of the individual operating divisions as profit-centers.

## 2.5 Company E

The company is a multi-national, diversified corporation engaged in providing a broad spectrum of products and services related to the general functions of business and education administration including office machines and supplies, information processing systems and learning materials. The company is highly decentralized in its organization, with four major operating groups and over sixty separate companies worldwide. The total gross annual operating revenue of Company E is in excess of five billion dollars. A high degree of financial control is delegated to the operating line companies which are operated as profit-centers.

## 3. Methodology

The survey was conducted using in-depth interviews with user-managers, systems managers, project managers, analysts and programmers. The personal interview technique of data gathering was adopted despite the obvious disadvantages of time, cost and manpower involved in order to assure the validity of the responses. The questions used were derived from a conceptual planning and control framework for information systems development based on the work of Anthony [1,2] and generated from a search of the literature. A list of about 450 subject-area questions was developed for use by the interview team. The extensive degree of the questioning plus the multi-positional approach to selecting respondents were designed to enhance the accuracy and objectivity of the information obtained.

The selection of the five companies in the research sample was tested through the use of a set of effectiveness criteria designed to evaluate whether or not each company could be considered to be an effective manager of computer-based information systems. The criteria were developed by a panel of seven prominent information systems professionals representing university, computer industry, business consultant and computer-user communities. The criteria set consisted of 120 performance standards in six major groupings: User-Management Attitudes Towards Systems Support, Degree of User-Management Knowledge and Participation, Top Management Commitment and Involvement, Exploitation of Potential of Information Systems Technology, Application of Effective Resource Control Techniques, and Organization of Information Systems Management. These effectiveness criteria were used both to screen possible company candidates for the study and to validate the final selection of the five companies in the research sample.

The formal research results are summarized here in the form of a planning and control framework for information systems development, shown in section 4. The framework is divided into three major categories: Strategic Systems Planning, Project Planning, Project Control. Each category is further divided into “principles” and “issues.” For our purposes a “principle” is defined as “a combination of policies, procedures and concepts that conforms to the actual practices of the majority of the companies studied.” An “issue” exists whenever a significant divergence was observed among the management practices of the five companies or when a principle statement requires further amplification.

Several caveats should be noted in interpreting the results presented in the framework, below.

1. The management framework is intended for the non-technical manager. It is essentially introductory and is designed to orient the user-manager to the proper role that he should play in the system development process. In addition, because of space limitations, the research findings are presented in an abbreviated form. The framework, therefore, is lacking in much essential detail needed for complete understanding of the material presented. Such key documents as functional specifications, detailed design specifications, the project plan, and conversion plan are examples of where an immense amount of pertinent detail, both in content and underlying process, is hidden by the summary descriptions of the framework. Fortunately, a large number of professional and text books are available on the related subjects of systems analysis, design, and project management. Here, the framework can provide an invaluable service in highlighting for the manager those areas in which further personal study will be most productive.

2. The framework concentrates on the system development process which is only a part - although a very critical one - of the wide scope of activities making up the total information systems function in a modern business. Specifically missing are subjects related to the configuration and selection of hardware/software and the management of data processing operations. These were omitted because of the desire to avoid subject areas peripheral to the central focus of the model - information systems development.

3. The class of information system that is addressed in the framework is primarily the medium-to-large scale system that supports business operations or management decision-making functions. Such systems are typically long term in development (i.e., one month to several years) and are produced as the result of a formal system project development effort. This kind of system is emphasized because it consumes the bulk of information systems resource expenditures in the business world today. An important type of system omitted from consideration is the short-term, ad-hoc response to the immediate needs of the decision-maker. This type of capability, nevertheless, should be considered to be a requirement for any well run and properly responsive information systems organization.

4. The purpose of the framework is to present accepted and experience-tested practices. It has therefore excluded, by definition, some of the more recent innovative trends in system design and development such as top-down design, prototyping and user-oriented, non-procedural programming languages. These are still being tested and evaluated in real-world business operations. One of the major impacts that these new methods undoubtedly will have will be on the execution of the system development life cycle (Project Planning Principle 1). Such variations as the “segmented approach”, where the project is divided into carefully segmented modules each with its own mini-system development life cycle, or a hierarchical form of development which involves the execution of the system design in successive levels of increased detail, each with its own partial system development life cycle, have found their way into practice [3], [4, pp. 347–351]. Despite such modifications, however, the basic concept of the life cycle, the nature and sequence of the activities involved and its importance as a fundamental management tool still remain valid.

## 4. The Planning and Control Framework

This framework consists of a set or proven management practices grouped into three major categories of “principles” and “issues”: Strategic Planning, Project Planning, and Project Control. An additional category of “Macro Principles” is introduced as prefix to the framework. This presents two principles supported by the research that apply across all three of the planning and control categories.

4.1. Macro Principle 1. General Management Principles Apply

Those universal concepts, principles and practices that have historically proven successful in the management of general business activities are equally valid for application to the development of information systems. This principle directly contravenes the view taken by many managers (and technicians) that information systems development is a unique process not amenable to normal management procedures.

## 4.2. Macro Principle 2. Line Management Responsibility

The information systems development process involves a major investment in resources toward the support of the organizational mission. It involves an end-product (information) that is critical to the success of any management function. It therefore follows that the development process should be directed and controlled by those responsible for the attainment of the organizational mission (i.e., line management).

## 4.3. Strategic Planning Principle 1. Master Systems Planning

Strategic planning for information systems should be conducted at an organization level above that of the senior functional and operational users of data processing. A corporate-wide master systems planning policy should be formally defined and a formal master systems plan produced.

4.3.1. Issue: The organization structure used to conduct master system, planning.

Two basic approaches were observed. Companies A, C and E used a central top-management planning group to direct the master system planning process. The formulation of the plan was coordinated and implemented by the central corporate systems staff. In Companies B and D the corporate systems staff coordinated and developed the master systems plan based on guidance derived from the normal business planning process.

## 4.3.2. Issue: Whether a "top-down" or "bottom-up" approach to master systems planning should be used.

All companies in the study used some form of the "bottom-up" approach. Two basic variations existed. In Companies C, D and E the individual operating divisions developed their own master systems plans within a long range framework of strategies and technical architecture established by the group responsible for central systems planning. Companies A and B developed a master systems plan based largely on inputs initiated by the operating divisions.

## 4.4. Strategic Planning Principle 2. Management Involvement

The master systems planning process should be so structured as to insure the active involvement of top management and senior operational and functional management in the systems development process. This includes the key activities of project proposal, review and approval.

## 4.4.1. Issue: The form and degree of senior management involvement.

In Company A top management involvement took the form of the senior operating vice-presidents participating in a corporate-level steering committee. This practice was modified in Company E by the addition of key representatives from the operating divisions on the steering committee. In Company C the steering committee was composed, primarily of the operating division controllers (all vice-presidents). In Companies B and D senior management involvement took place within steering committees established at the operating division level. Top corporate management was briefed on the master systems plan by the head of the corporate staff.

## 4.4.2. Issue: The extent of involvement of the Chief Executive Officer.

In only three companies did the Chief Executive Officer become directly involved in the master systems planning process. In Company A, he actively participated in the monthly steering committee meetings; in Companies C and E this participation was on an ad-hoc basis.

## 4.5. Strategic Planning Principle 3. The Master Systems Plan

The master systems plan should include, as a minimum, the following types of information: a statement of strategy and concept for the future development of the corporate information systems; a summary description of each planned project, project goals, development schedules and resource requirements; a summary cost-benefit analysis for each project; a priority assignment for planned projects (needed only if resource constraints are anticipated); summary estimates of hardware requirements; hardware and software purchase schedule; some form of long range technology and environmental forecast with impact estimates for information systems development. The master systems plan is most effectively expressed using a summary level of detail with detailed back-up provided in individual project documentation.

4.5.1. Issue: The time-span of the master systems plan.

Companies A, B and C used a time frame of the current operating year for the short range plan and five years for the long range plan. Companies D and E both used the operating year plus one as the time frame for the short range plan and seven years for the long range plan.

4.6. Strategic Planning Principle 4. Planning Coordination

Master systems planning should be formally coordinated with the overall business planning objectives and goals of the organization and should be in consonance with them.

4.6.1. Issue: The extent to which master systems planning is integrated with the formal business planning process.

Two of the companies (D and E) integrated master systems planning with the general business planning process. The master systems plan was a formal part of the companies' long and short range business plans. Companies A, B and C essentially conducted systems planning separately from the general business cycle. Responsiveness to business plan objectives was achieved through direction supplied by corporate systems steering committees in the case of Companies A and C and through guidance provided by the head of the corporate systems staff in Company B.

4.7. Project Planning Principle 1. Systems Development Life Cycle

The basic framework within which the planning and control of information systems projects should be exercised is that provided by the system development life cycle. This concept provides the basis for the comprehensive description, monitoring and controlling of the systems development process. A satisfactory model of the life cycle consists of six time-sequenced phases:

1. System Study (includes activities of problem identification, project proposal, feasibility study).

2. System Analysis (includes determination of detailed user information requirements, writing functional specifications).

3. Detailed Design (translating the functional specifications into technical specifications).

4. System Implementation (programming, procedure-writing and testing).

5. System Conversion and Cut-Over.

6. Post Implementation Audit.

4.7.1. Issue: Variations that exist in the system development life cycle phases.

All of the companies studies employed a version of the life cycle that was some variation of the six-phase model stated above. In addition, they all differed somewhat from each other. The variations observed ranged from an eight-phase model (Company B) to two-phase model used by Company A. The remaining companies all used five-phase versions. While considerable differences existed in the formal division of the phases and in the terminology used, the types and sequence of activities included in the life cycles were essentially the same as that of the six-phased model described in the principle statement, above.

4.8. Project Planning Principle 2. Feasibility Study and Project Proposal

The information system development process should be initiated by the activities of problem definition, preliminary determination of information requirements, feasibility study and project proposal. The accepted practice is for the completion of these activities to result in a single report, or set of reports, summarizing the findings of the preliminary study and presenting recommendations.

The primary purpose of this document is to provide an assessment of the proposed project feasibility from an economic, technical and operational standpoint; to provide initial guidance for the development of the system design to follow; and to present a formal proposal describing the nature and scope of the recommended system. The development and submission of this document should be the responsibility of user-management with technical support provided as required by the systems group.

4.8.1. Issue: The threshold level criteria for subjecting system project proposals to a full feasibility study.

The thresholds above which full feasibility study and corporate-level approval were required for information system project proposals were as follows: Company C - total development costs of \$150,000; Company E - \$200,000 total development costs; Company D - total development costs of \$200,000 or estimated development time of two years or more; Company B - total development costs of \$750,000. Company A required a full feasibility study for any project over five days or which would result in a new report.

## 4.9. Project Planning Principle 3. Economic Analysis

The economic analysis portion of the feasibility study is a key element in management evaluation of the proposed system. It should be as accurate as possible at this state in the development proceedings and should be updated as additional information becomes available throughout the remainder of the development cycle. The economic analysis should allow for the comparison of the estimated costs and benefits of the proposed system with those of the current system. The cost-benefit analysis should be quantified using monetary values wherever possible. The system proposal should be evaluated using similar financial analysis techniques as those applied to other corporate investment proposals.

## 4.9.1. Issue: The use of present-value discounting.

Present-value discounting procedures were applied to cost estimates of proposed systems under the following conditions: Company E applied present-value discounting as a standard practice to all life-cycle cost estimates; Company C, whenever the development time period exceeded two years; Company B usually applied it only to equipment proposals. Neither Company A nor D normally used present-value discounting in the cost estimates of proposed information systems.

## 4.9.2. Issue: Methods used to estimate the value of intangible benefits (i.e., those benefits not readily quantifiable).

Company E used a procedure for estimating the dollar value of intangible benefits whereby a series of “informed management judgments” were obtained from knowledgeable managers. The estimates were then reviewed by the senior responsible user-manager and a form of weighted average developed. None of the other companies made any sophisticated attempt to apply dollar values to intangible benefits. All companies had a general practice of describing significant intangible benefits in narrative form.

## 4.9.3. Issue: The criteria to be used for the evaluation of proposed system projects.

All of the companies used some form of financial analysis as the primary basis for judging the worth of system proposals. Companies B. C and D all used “pay-back” as the primary method of evaluating proposals. The time-period standard applied was usually not more than two years. All companies emphasized the importance of obtaining return as quickly as possible. Companies A and E used return-on-investment as their primary criterion for evaluating system proposals. The interest rates used were the same as those for capital investment projects.

## 4.10. Project Planning Principle 4. Project Management

Upon project approval, a formal project management organization should be established for the purpose of directing the system development effort. Project management responsibilities include developing a project plan and exercising control and accountability for project performance. Key tasks are those of scheduling, internal project budgeting, implementation of phases, technical review and sub-project control.

4.10.1. Issue: The management orientation and reporting chain of the project manager.

In most of the companies (A, B and C) the project manager was normally drawn from the ranks of senior system professionals and operated under the authority of the systems organization. In Company B a user-manager would occasionally be assigned as project manager but reported to the systems manager. Company D placed the project manager under the joint direction of the systems manager and a user-constituted system requirements review group. In Company E normal practice assigned a user-manager to the position of project (“program”) manager. In this role, he reported to the user-manager responsible for the system project.

4.10.2. Issue: Whether some form of project-status audit should be carried out by a group outside the project organization.

Two of the companies (D and E) established a centralized corporate audit function for the purpose of auditing selected key development projects, their status, their effectiveness, their documentation and compliance with standards. In Company E the audit function was carried out by an autonomous organization reporting to the chief financial officer of the corporation; in Company D, by a specialized group within the corporate systems staff. Companies A, B and C had no formal procedure for the audit of systems under development.

4.10.3. Issue: The criteria used to determine when a formal project structure should be established.

In four companies the policy for the establishment of formal project organization was based on criteria of estimated project development costs plus organizational impact. The cost thresholds varied as follows: Company B - 40 to 100 man-days of development effort, depending on the operating division standards; Company C - \$100,000 to \$150,000 total development costs; Company D - six man-months was the normal threshold. but this varied with operating division practice; Company E - \$50,000 in total development costs. Company A established a formal project at the threshold of five man-days programming costs or the generation of a new recurring report.

## 4.11. Project Planning Principle 5. The Project Plan.

The project plan is the framework upon which the project management system is based and is the foundation for project control reporting and user-management review. It should consist of documentation pertaining to four basic and related activities:

1. Estimating - the compilation of man-hours, computer capacity, and other consumable resources that will be required.

2. Budgeting - the estimation of costs of resources used in the project.

3. Scheduling - the determination of how long tasks are to take and when they are to be performed.

4. Resource allocation - the assignment of specific resources to project tasks.

4.11.1. Issue: The level of detail to be included in the project plan.

The majority practice of the companies studied (B, C, D and E) was to complete the project plan at a sufficient level of detail to allow planning (and control) down to the work-task level. Company A developed project plans to a summary level of detail, only.

## 4.12. Project Planning Principle 6. Project Progress Reporting

A project progress reporting system should be installed as part of the project planning activity. It is required to allow management control of the system development process. It should provide the status on expenditure of all resources consumed by the project and have the objective of focusing attention on problem areas in time for management to take appropriate and timely action.

4.12.1. Issue: General categories of information to be reported.

The formal and specific detail for reporting varied widely among the companies studied. The degree of detail reported also varied with the importance and size of the project. The majority practice, however, was to report on at least the following general categories of information for control purposes: resource expenditures-to-date versus budget; key check-point and milestone dates; comparison of planned versus actual manpower expenditures; statistical variances for key categories. Status information was provided at the level of individual programming task and on the individual person's performance if needed. Also, a summary of key problems plus overall project assessment was typically provided. Two companies (C and E) established minimal formal corporate standards for reporting. Companies B and D allowed the details of the project control reporting system to be determined by the systems or project manager. Company A employed a highly simplified standardized project control reporting system consisting only of percentage of completion of tasks against key milestone dates plus summaries of special trouble areas. Status information to assess individual performance was also provided.

## 4.12.2. Issue: Levels of management review and the type information provided.

The general practice was to provide a weekly project status control report at two levels of detail:

1. A detailed format, reporting down to the level of specific tasks and individual performance, for the use of the project manager and systems management.

2. A summary report describing overall project status and highlighting problem areas for user-management. A monthly status summary report including highlighting of significant problem areas was also submitted to user-management, systems management, and, for major projects, to the corporate or division steering group and to the corporate systems planning staff. In addition, Company E, for selected major projects, provided quarterly status summaries to the corporate systems steering group.

## 4.13. Project Planning Principle 7. Change Control

Prior to the commencement of formal project work, some positive system for control of change should be established so that the amount and degree of modifications applied to the various specifications, e.g., functional specifications, design specifications, programming specifications, are carefully evaluated and the changes implemented in a controlled manner with resource cost and system implications clearly understood.

## 4.13.1. Issue: Procedures for review and approval of proposed changes.

The general practice (Companies B, C, D and E) was to accomplish effective change control by means of formal change requests, technical assessment by the systems group, joint review-approval by the project manager and user-management, and full-cost allocation to the user organizations of all incremental costs due to changes. The management level at which review action was taken depended on the magnitude of the change. Company D purposely introduced a strong bias against changes through a policy of allowing user-management to approve changes only if scheduled costs and times are not increased. Company A followed a formal procedure for requesting, reviewing and approving changes but allowed the project manager to be the final authority for approval. No cost allocation was made to the user. In addition, all companies, except D, followed the practice of applying a general “freeze” to the implementation of changes once the actual programming commenced. Approved changes were then held in batches and released for implementation once system testing of the affected programs had been completed.

## 4.14. Planning Principle 8. System Development Standards

A comprehensive set of published data processing standards should exist pertaining to information systems development. These should be embodied in a standards manual which specifies policies, procedures and practices that govern all the significant tasks dealing with system development and implementation. Such a set of standards provides a common frame of reference for evaluating system proposals, reviewing and judging performance and determining specific areas of performance deficiencies. It is a key element in establishing effective management control of the system development process.

## 4.14.1. Issue: The form and level of detail of system development standards.

The general practice of Companies B, C, D and E was to provide a set of corporate standards that pertain to system development and implementation. The degree of detail and the organization level from which standards were issued varied with individual companies. Companies C and E had corporate standards that addressed in detail such subjects as description of the system development life cycle, definition of the key activities and procedures for each phase, output products and reports for each phase, documentation standards, procedures for project management and management review procedures and responsibilities. Companies B and D provided corporate standard for system development in the form of general policy guidance, only, and relied on the individual operating divisions to issue the detailed standards. Company A had no formal set of standards for the system development process.

## 4.15. Planning Principle 9. Cost Allocation

A cost accounting and allocation system should be installed for the resource costs related to the system development process. It should be designed in such a manner as to make management aware of the costs to the organization of system development and operations activities. It should be implemented so as to influence user-management to view system project approval decisions in the same cost-effectiveness context as pertains to other corporate investment decisions.

## 4.15.i. Issue: The level and extent of cost allocation.

Companies B, C, D and E followed the policy of allocating all system development costs to some level of the user organization. The level to which this allocation was made, however, varied with the company. Company B charged all system development costs to the individual operating divisions. Companies C and E charged all system development costs to that level of the user organization that sponsored the project. Company D charged system analysis and design costs to the operating division and the programming and implementation costs to the sponsoring user organization. In Company A all costs were charged to the individual project account only. No costs were allocated directly to any level of the user organization.

## 4.16. Project Control Principle 1. User-Management Control

User-management control of system development can be exercised through three types of key activities:

1. Active personal management participation in the early formative stages of the systems development cycle, i.e., problem definition, feasibility study, project proposal, functional specifications.

2. Quality control of the project implementation process through review and sign-off at established key check-points.

3. Monitoring of resource utilization against the project plan.

## 4.16.1. Issue: The level of user-management exercising review and control authority.

All companies studied followed the practice of having the user-manager responsible for the commitment of resources to the project oversee and direct the early study stages and exercise review and approval authority in all three key activities listed in the principle, above. The level of user-manager involved would vary depending on the importance and cost of the project. In two cases (Companies C and D) the user-manager was assisted in his responsibilities on major systems by the formation of a special design review group composed, typically, or representatives of all key user groups plus functional and auditor specialists having interests in the system being developed. Such review groups would assume the bulk of the detailed review work and submit review summaries and recommendations to the responsible usermanager. Company A followed the policy of assigning systems-trained personnel as key staff assistants to senior user-managers to assist in the review and direction of projects.

## 4.16.2. Issue: The key check-points that should be established for management review.

Three companies (B, D and E) established check-points at the completion of the activities of the feasibility study-project proposal, the functional specifications, the detailed design specification, results of final system tests, and the report of the completed system installation. Company C followed the same practice with the exception that a single check-point was established for the combined activities of feasibility study, project proposal, and functional specifications. It was common practice for all of these companies to reduce the number of check-points for smaller and less important projects. Company A did not follow the practice of carrying out formal check-point reviews and depended, instead, on continuing informal interaction between the user and the systems organizations to accomplish the necessary user-management overview. This approach was made feasible by the companion policy of segmenting all system designs into separate small projects of not more than one man-year each (total programming resource costs).

4.16.3. Issue: The general objectives and procedures for the check-point reviews.

In the four companies employing formal check-points for project quality control (B, C, D and E), the review of each check-point typically included the examination of the output products of that phase, a review of the project performance to date, and an evaluation of the time and cost estimates for the next phase. Required sign-off procedures on the part of responsible user-management were carried out on the key output documents of each phase. In three companies (C, D and E) the concept of “incremental funding” was employed; i.e., the actual release of funds for each major phase of the system development life cycle was withheld until specific approval was given as part of the check-point review procedure conducted for the preceding phase.

4.17. Project Control Principle 2. Information Requirements Definition

The identification of the user information requirements should be the initial step in developing functional specifications, one that is critical to success of the entire system development process that evolves from it. It should involve the active participation of those elements of the user organization (including user-management) needed for an accurate and comprehensive definition of requirements.

4.17.1. Issue: Those methods that have proven useful in validating the user statements of requirements.

All companies acknowledged the chronic difficulty of establishing a valid set of user information requirements. Some common methods used to assure meaningful user inputs included extensive cross-checking of the statement of information requirements for each key user position with other users who were involved in the specific decision or information-flow process. This practice was coupled with that of holding joint “walk-through” involving appropriate user-managers, users, functional specialists and members of the system design team to assure common understanding of the implications of information requirements as they were developed.

Special user-oriented system modelling techniques such as HIPO [4] and the "structured analysis" approach popularized by Gane and Sarson [3] had been used with success by Companies B, C, D and E to assure user comprehension and meaningful participation. This proved particularly effective in improving the completeness and logical consistency of the user's statement of requirements. Companies A and E employed systems-trained functional analysts as key assistants to senior user-managers. Because of their knowledge of both the user and systems functions, such specialists provided invaluable service as liaison between user-management, functional specialists and the systems support group. They were particularly helpful in the establishment of valid information requirements. Company D obtained a similar benefit from the use of the system design review group to validate the user's requirements statements.

4.18. Project Control Principle 3. Functional Specifications

The completion of the system analysis phase provides a key check-point for user-management. The end product of the system analysis phase – the functional specifications – builds on the content and detail of the system description presented in the feasibility report and provides a more accurate description of the proposed system in terms of its processes, inputs and outputs. Also included are updated cost-benefit analysis, cost, time and resource estimates. Properly drawn, the functional specifications will represent a contract between the user-manager and the systems organization detailing exactly what he expects to receive as the end product. Functional specifications should be expressed in user terminology and should be completely comprehensible to him.

4.18.1. Issue: The extent of user-organization participation in the development of functional specifications.

In Companies B, C and D systems management, in the person of the assigned project manager, assumed the direct responsibility for developing the functional specifications. User-management approved and signed off on the final product. User organization representatives were heavily involved in the specifications development. In Companies A and E user-management assumed direct responsibility for the development of the functional specifications with technical support provided by the systems group. User organization representatives were actively involved as needed.

4.19. Project Control Principle 4. Detailed Design Specifications

The detailed design phase serves the function of translating the functional requirements of the user (Project Control Principle 3) into technical specifications which spell out in detail how the requirements will be met and provide specific technical direction for the completion of the system development process. The output of this process is a set of detailed design specifications which provide the basis for a key management check-point. Included in the specifications should be an updated cost-benefit analysis and time, cost and resource estimates.

4.19.1. Issue: The extent of user-organization participation in the development of design specifications.

In Companies C and E, user management reviewed an executive summary of the detailed design specifications including detailed facsimiles of all forms and reports plus manual procedure documentation. Company D followed the practice of a user-directed system design review board evaluating the design specifications in detail and preparing the executive summary plus recommendations for user-management action.

In all three companies user-management directed planning for manual procedure development and user representatives assisted the systems group in writing programming specifications and in conversion planning, as needed. In Company B user-management reviewed the detailed design specifications on an exception basis - only in those subject areas where significant changes had occurred in relation to the statement of functional specifications. The systems group (i.e., the project manager) assumed responsibility for all phases of the development of detailed design specifications, assisted by user representatives as needed. Company A did not formalize design specifications or present them for user-management review.

## 4.20. Project Control Principle 5. System Implementation

The phase of system implementation involves the activities of programming, procedure-writing and testing in conformance with the detailed design specifications described in Project Control Principle 4. User-management should exercise responsibilities in four major channels of effort during this phase:

1. Assuring adequate user-organization involvement in the production of manual procedures and operating instructions.

2. Directing active participation by user representatives in system testing and conversion.

3. Assuring that adequate training is being provided all future system users.

4. Monitoring the progress of the project through status control reporting.

4.20.1. Issue: The extent of user-organization participation in the development of manual procedures.

In all companies the general practice followed was that of user-management assuming the full responsibility for the writing of manual procedures and user operating instructions, with user representatives and functional specialists heavily involved. Assistance was obtained from the systems group as required.

## 4.21. Project Control Principle 6. System Testing

Conduct of the system testing is a time-critical activity upon which the ultimate success and timely completion of the project will depend. The test plan and schedule is a key document which enables user-management to review progress during the programming phase in such key dimensions as quality, resource and schedule performance. The plan should define responsibilities, establish standards for test data and testing procedures, detail the sequencing of testing at the individual program module and various levels of system integration.

## 4.21.1. Issue: The extent of user-management involvement in the system testing process.

In all companies the user-manager with project sponsorship responsibilities reviewed and signed off on the results of tests conducted at the integrated-system level.

In Companies A and C the conduct of the system testing was considered a joint-management function with both systems management (the project manager) and user-management sharing the responsibilities for direction and outcome of the system testing phase. The project manager, however, retained operational responsibility for the conduct of the testing process.

In Companies B and D the project manager assumed the major responsibility for the system testing function and its successful outcome. In

Company D the user-directed system design review group held responsibility for monitoring the conduct of all system testing and the detailed evaluation of the test results prior to user-management final review.

In Company E user-management assumed complete responsibility for the conduct and results of the system testing function. In addition, the practice of establishing a “base case” condition was followed wherein a standard set of test files and test data representing all known logical conditions were created under the direction of user-management. The “base case” thus provided the basic criteria for judging system test performance.

## 4.21.2. Issue: The extent of user representative participation in the system testing process.

Among the majority of the companies (A, B, C and D) the common practice was for the systems personnel to assume primary responsibility for the individual program-module and program testing with user representatives providing assistance in the manual system interface functions. The user representatives' role in testing became greater as the level of program integration increased through the system testing process. The user organization was responsible for providing operationally valid test data.

Company E followed the general practice outlined above with the respect to the division of responsibilities at the lower levels of program testing. However, for the conduct of final system testing a special quality assurance group directed by user-management was established. Membership was made up entirely outside of the regular project development team.

## 4.22. Project Planning Principle 6. Conversion and Cut-Over

The conversion and cut-over phase is designed to bring the new system into operation with a minimum of risk, cost and disruption on the part of the user organization. The conversion and cut-over process is often a very difficult and complex operation. It requires extensive planning and significant participation on the part of the user organization to assure success and avoid serious disruption on the functional operations of the business. It encompasses the following types of activities: creation of new files, conversion of data to new files, completing manual procedures and operating instructions, education and training of users and operators of the new system, establishing and implementing a strategy and schedule for the final system cut-over into full operational status. The key document is the conversion plan normally initiated prior to the commencement of the system implementation phase.

## 4.22.1. Issue: The form of organization established to conduct the conversion process.

Four of the companies (B, C, D and E) followed the practice of establishing a specially designated conversion team under the direction of a senior analyst to plan and direct the various activities included in the conversion process. The team was typically a subset of the design team and composed of systems personnel and user representatives. The conversion process was carried out under the direction of the project manager as an extension of the programming and system testing activities.

## 4.22.2. Issue: The degree of user-management involvement in the conversion process.

In Companies A, B and C the conversion and cut-over process was normally conducted under joint-management responsibility of the user and systems organizations. The technical direction of the conversion and cut-over activities remained under the authority of the project manager.

In Company E the user-manager responsible for the system development assumed primary responsibility for the conversion and cut-over of the system, operating through the program (project) manager who was, in most instances, also a member of user-management.

In Company D the project manager exercised primary control of the conversion and cut-over process and was closely monitored by the system design review group acting under the authority of the user-manager.

All companies followed the practice of user-management exercising final approval on all conversion and cut-over planning and formally signing off on the operational results of the cut-over phase.

## 4.23. Project Planning Principle 8. Post-Implementation Audit

In order that the actual performance of a new information system be known to management, the final stage of the completed system development effort should be some form of post-implementation audit. The purpose of this activity is to compare anticipated system costs/benefits and performance criteria described in the functional specifications with actual results realized in the operation of the new system. The audit should be undertaken as soon as the operation of the new system has stabilized – normally in about six months.

## 4.23.1. Issue: The Organization and authority for conducting post-implementation audits.

A variety of audit practices were observed. In Company A the corporate steering group selected two to three high interest information system projects each year for post-implementation audit. The project manager and selected members of the design team of the development project normally conducted the audit.

In Company E an autonomous corporate audit and operations-research staff group held the responsibility for selecting and conducting post-implementation audits on six to nine major projects each year. The audit team was directed by members' of the special corporate staff group; care was taken to select audit team members who were not directly involved in the system project being audited.

In Company C the corporate systems manager, in coordination with user-management, selected one or two major projects a year for post-implementation audits. The systems group conducted the post-implementation audit using, where possible, the same analysts who conducted the original system study and proposal.

In Company D the systems planning and control group of the corporate staff conducted post-implementation audits of all designated key systems; i.e., those with total development costs greater than \$200,000 or total development time in excess of two years. The audit team was headed by members of the corporate staff and made up of technical specialists drawn from the corporate staff and operating divisions not directly involved in the system project being audited.

Company B did not carry out post-implementation audits as a regular part of the system development management process. However, post-implementation audits were carried out by the corporate internal audit group on systems selected by them with special financial or asset control implications.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

The planning and control framework detailed above served to identify and classify a wide variety of successful information systems management policies and procedures tested in real-world business environments. The primary purpose of the framework, and its underlying research, is to provide a practical means for improving the information systems management competence of business organizations by using proven management practices as models for study and selective implementation.

Several aspects of the framework, in particular, are designed to enhance its usefulness to the general management of a business organization:

1. The basic planning and control context of the framework was chosen because it represents the types of activities that the business manager typically conducts in the course of his daily work and which he therefore should be able to easily relate to.

2. The category of "principles" presents those management practices that had gained common acceptance among the companies studies. The fact that these practices have proven themselves in the operational experience of information systems competent companies with a wide variety of industry and organizational characteristics should make them likely models for emulation by the individual company seeking to improve its information system management performance.

3. The category of “issues” provides a comparative analysis of those management practices that varied significantly among the companies studied. Since the practices thus identified differed among the companies, all of which enjoyed proven reputations for successful information systems management, it can be reasonably assumed that the observed variances were largely due to individual business environmental factors such as industry characteristics, organizational factors, style-of-management. A comparison of such variant practices with the individual characteristics of the companies employing them should provide a basis for selecting out those practices most likely to be appropriate to the local situation of any company under study.

There is a secondary purpose, of perhaps equal importance, that the framework can serve. Its major subject divisions and areas of emphasis should provide a guide to the individual manager as to productive areas for further personal study. It is foreseen that in this manner, also, the framework can operate as a catalyst and accelerating factor in the process of improving user-management competence in information systems.
