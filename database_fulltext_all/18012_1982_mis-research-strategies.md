# MIS Research Strategies

Scott Hamilton

Senior Project Manager, Product Development, COMSERV Corporation, Mendota Heights, Minnesota 55120, USA

and

Blake Ives

Assistant Professor, Dartmouth University, Hanover, New Hampshire 10273, USA

Different strategies for conducting empirical research in the Management Information Systems (MIS) field are discussed. Strategies employed in 532 MIS articles published in 15 journals during the 1970–79 time period are analyzed. The trends are analyzed over the ten year period with respect to the differences between articles authored by practitioners and academics, differences between articles that are cited frequently versus infrequently, and the relationship with the type of research conducted.

The analysis points out that more than two thirds of published MIS research by either academicians or practitioners has utilized non-empirical approaches and focused on a single variable. Case studies are the most commonly employed empirical strategy and most studies do not measure the impact of independent variables on the process of using, developing, or operating information systems. The analysis suggests that MIS journal articles employing empirical research strategies are cited more frequently than nonempirical ones.

Keywords: Research methods, MIS research, empirical vs. non-empirical research

He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota and holds an M.S. in computer science from the State University of New York at Albany. His current interests include knowledge utilization, the design of graphic user interfaces, and the management of the computing resource.

## 1. Introduction

Research in Management Informations System (MIS) has been criticized as overly conceptual [5], lacking in rigor [20], and noncumulative [15]. To address these problems, different research strategies have been proposed. While each of these has contributed to the general body of MIS knowledge, they differ in their usefulness in answering particular questions. Discussions of empirical methods are typically illustrated by actual studies, but a comprehensive analysis of MIS research methods is lacking.

The purpose of this paper is to provide such an analysis. Answers to the following questions were sought:

1. How frequently are various research strategies used in MIS research?

2. Are there discernible trends in the use of these strategies?

3. Have practitioner authors employed different research strategies than academic ones?

4. Have articles which have made important contributions to MIS utilized different strategies than less important articles?

Chervany [3] suggested the need to make a study of MIS research strategies with respect to the variables investigated (question 5). Cherns [2] hypothesized that the choice of a strategy would influence diffusion of the results (question 4). The conflict between respectability in academia and relevance to management suggests that the researchers' affiliation (question 3) may influence the strategies. The maturation of the MIS field (question 2) and the development of firmer research paradigms [17] may also influence the use of various strategies.

## 2. Empirical MIS Research Strategies

Alternative strategies for conducting empirical MIS research have been defined, illustrated and compared by several authors. Van Horn [22] examined four methods – case studies, field studies, field tests, and laboratory studies – and presented numerous illustrations and arguments for each. Van Horn suggested that field tests and rigorous field studies are rare, that rigorous field studies are useful for studying MIS design, development and implementation, and that valid laboratory studies offer the most promise for MIS research. Dickson, Senn and Chervany [6] similarly note the paucity of empirical studies, especially field tests. They illustrate the four methods and emphasize the usefulness of man-machine experiments (one type of laboratory study) for assessing information support of the decision process. The usefulness of laboratory methods of studying information support of the decision process has also been discussed by Jenkins [13] and Schneiderman [19]. Action research has been discussed and advocated by Gibson [9] and Keen [14] for studying MIS design and implementation. Action research methods ease participant learning and use, and may be combined with case studies, field studies, and field tests.

The methodological foundations of each strategy have been discussed by authors in different disciplines. For example, discussions of strategies in organizational psychology include field tests [4], field studies [1], and laboratory studies [7]. Tradeoffs between alternative strategies have been compared in terms of internal and external validity [16] and in terms of diffusing research results [21].

Problems in the methodological foundations of empirical MIS research have been discussed by Turner [20]. The lack of empirical research is another problem. Authorities suggest that the published literature primarily reflects non-empirical research methods. For example, Van Horn [22] characterized 78 percent of 27 papers presented at two MIS conferences as nonempirical. Approximately 33 percent of 430 MIS-related doctoral dissertations were nonempirical in nature [10].

## 3. Analysis of MIS Research Strategies

This analysis dealt with relevant MIS articles pushed in fifteen journals. Fig. 1 identifies them within discipline area. For issues of each journal published between 1970 and 1979, articles within the MIS discipline were identified. A detailed discussion of the journals, the journal selection procedure, and the article selection procedure was previously published [11]. The article selection procedure identified 532 MIS articles published in the 15 journals over the 10 year period. The number of MIS articles appearing in each journal during the publication time period is also shown in Fig. 1.

Each article was classified as utilizing one of five MIS research strategies. The five are: case study, field study, field test, laboratory study, and a nonempirical approach. Nonempirical approaches (e.g., tutorials and conceptual works) rely on secondary sources or the author's experience to support conclusions. Fig. 1 characterizes each journal in terms of the percent of MIS articles which utilized an empirical research strategy.

## 3.3. Research Strategy and Article Importance

A rough indicator of article importance is citation frequency. The more an article is cited, the more the research community is expressing the fact that the article contains useful information. The threshold for defining important articles appears to be a citation rate of 3 or 4 times a year, a number which only a few percent of all articles ever achieve [8]. Comparing the research strategies employed in frequently cited articles with those employed in less frequently cited articles provides a measure of the relative contribution of a particular research strategy to MIS research.

A subset of 370 articles, published during the 1970 to 1977 time period, were selected from the 532 articles published between 1970 to 1979. Articles from 1978 and 1979 would not yet have been commonly cited and were therefore excluded. Citation frequencies were calculated for the subset (370 MIS articles) based on an analysis of bibliographic references attached to the total set (532 MIS articles). The cited articles in the subset were broken into two groups. The first group contained articles cited two or more times while the second group included articles that have not been cited or cited only once. Fig. 4 summarizes the analysis of research strategies utilized in the two groups of articles.

## 3.4. Research Strategy by Research Type

The variables investigated in each article were characterized by mapping them into a comprehensive framework for MIS research developed by the authors [12]. The framework defines three variable groups (environment, process, and information system) and major variable classes within each group. This model permits an examination of MIS research from a number of alternative perspectives. The five different types of research derivable from the model are identified in Fig. 5 and briefly described in Fig. 6.

Fig. 5. Five Categories of Information Systems Research.

The authors previously published [12] a detailed analysis of research strategies by research types for 331 MIS-related doctoral dissertations completed during the 1973 to 1979 time period. Fig. 6 presents a replication of their analysis for MIS journal articles published during the 1970 to 1979 time period. Each type of research reflects different approaches in the research strategies taken, as summarized below:

Type I articles generally employed case studies or used nonempirical research strategies, primarily conceptual studies relying on secondary sources or the author's experience.

Type II articles employed field studies, field tests, and laboratory studies. In most cases, the Type II articles examined relationships between the user environment and the use process.

Type III articles were uncommon and typically employed empirical strategies. In a few cases, laboratory studies were used to test the impact of information system characteristics (via simulation) on the use process.

Type IV articles were also uncommon but typically employed conceptual approaches. Lab and field tests were preferred when empirical strategies were used.

Type V articles studies commonly employed empirical strategies. In many cases, a laboratory study was used to test the impact of various information system characteristics (via simulation) and various user characteristics on the user process.

In comparison with the research types examined in the 331 MIS-related dissertations, the MIS journal articles focused on a specific variable (Type Ia research) far more frequently (47.4% versus 29.6%). In most of these cases, the article presented a development approach and relied on a conceptual analysis to support conclusions. The empirical studies typically failed to consider process measures as dependent variables. Only 46.5% of the 159 empirical studies measured the impact of independent variables on the process of using, developing, or operating information systems.

## 4. Conclusion

The analysis of MIS research strategies provides some empirical ammunition for those assessing the present state of MIS research. The majority (70.1%) of MIS research published in journals over the last ten years (1970–79) by either academicians or practitioners has been nonempirical in nature and has typically focused on a single variable (e.g., a new development technique). Case studies are the most commonly employed empirical strategy and most empirical studies failed to consider process measures as dependent variables (e.g., the impact on user performance).

The analysis of the MIS journal articles does provide hope for future research. The analysis of article importance suggests that use of empirical research strategies should be rewarded by greater recognition.

A need exists to establish a balance and integration between the research strategies in the MIS field. Past conceptual work and case studies have indicated important hypotheses which can now undergo more rigorous testing in laboratory settings. Field experimentation can serve as a testing ground for significant laboratory results. The key to good research, though, is not just in choosing the right research strategy, but in asking the right questions and picking the most powerful method(s) for answering the questions given the objectives, research setting and other salient factors.
