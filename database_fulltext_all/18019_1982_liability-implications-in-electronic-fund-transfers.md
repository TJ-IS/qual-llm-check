# Liability Implications in Electronic Fund Transfers

Charles R. FRANZ

Management Science Department, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

and

Sharon A. ASBILL

Bankers Trust of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29201, USA

The purpose of this paper is to present an overview of Electronic Funds Transfer Systems, the Electronic Fund Transfer Act (EFTA) of 1978 and liability implications for financial institutions and customers. Models of payment transfer prior to EFT are presented followed by a review of the EFT Legislation. Various aspects of regulatory authority, disclosures, pre-authorized transfers and error resolution procedures are examined. The paper ends with a discussion of three types of liability: consumer, financial institution, and civil and criminal. An extensive list of references is provided.

Keywords: Liability, electronic payments, electronic banking, electronic funds transfer, EFT, Electronic Fund Transfer Act, EFTA, electronic fund transfer float, stop payment, value dating, preauthorized transfers.

## 1. Introduction

The history of payments systems has been one of continuous evolution. Initially, trade was based upon the barter system, but this soon proved to be cumbersome and was replaced by a system which utilized universally accepted symbols for value – money. Later, money was deposited for safekeeping, and depositors would write their goldsmith bankers directing them to pay a certain amount to a named individual – the forerunner of the check. In recent years, the plastic credit card has emerged. This has led to the advent of the latest stage, the Electronic Fund Transfer System (EFTS) (see Fig. 1).

## 2. Models of Payment Transfer Prior to EFTS

Three general methods of payment exist to transfer funds: currency or cash, checks and credit cards. Cash is a highly efficient medium in the conduct of small transactions. It is cost effective and provides costs per transaction far below that of any other means; receipts provide records, but the danger of theft is great, and the consumer bears all the risks.

Checks account for the bulk of value transferred, are relatively expensive, and cancelled checks serve as proof of payment. However, the bank must bear the loss of forged checks, unless the consumer's negligence contributes to the forgery. Checks create a level of float that is due primarily to the time required to deliver, clear and process them. Error resolution procedures, however, are a function of the individual policy of the bank.

Credit cards provide convenience. Four main types of credit cards are: retail store, gasoline, bank, and travel-and-entertainment cards. The primary cost weakness of credit cards has been the large credit losses of the card issuers and the high costs of credit verification, although this is being reduced drastically by new technology. Some important advantages for consumers are the reduced fear of robbery and opportunities for using the float, as well as return of unsatisfactory goods or obtaining service on faulty new equipment. Credit transactions do, however, open the door to accounting errors and fraud. But laws and regulation have shifted many of these risks to the service provider (e.g., in the USA). The consumer is not responsible for unauthorized use exceeding fifty dollars. Also many procedures exist for resolving disputes concerning credit card usage.

## 3. What is EFT/EFTS?

EFTS is a payment system which enables routine financial transactions to be conducted via electronics. At its most basic level, it is simply the replacement of a promise of payment with an electronically-generated message. The current state of the art in electronic banking services in the USA has produced roughly seven different types of electronic fund transfer services:

1. Automated teller machine (ATM), which has proliferated in the past few years. Consumers withdraw cash, make deposits, transfer funds, and make payments. Customer access is generally achieved by inserting a special card and pushing buttons to enter a (secret) personal identification number (PIN).

2. Debit card, which resembles a credit card, except that it does not finance purchases. It causes funds to be immediately withdrawn from the user's account. A PIN must be used to initiate any transaction.

3. Electronic "check guarantee" system, which combines features of an electronic transaction and check payment. The financial institution issues a card, often with a PIN, to a consumer who presents it at a terminal installed at a merchant location. The consumer's paper check may thus be "guaranteed".

4. Preauthorized payments and credits, which generally go through Automated Clearinghouses (ACHs). Preauthorized debits permit payments for regularly recurring obligations like mortgages, insurance premiums, or utility bills. A consumer may also receive regularly recurring payments from others (for wages, dividends, Social Security benefits, or annuities). There are presently thirty-two ACH's in the USA [8,28].

5. Automated telephone payment, which is commonly known as "pay-by-phone" or "telephone billpayer" services. Consumers pay bills by instructing the institution to transfer funds to accounts of firms or businesses. If touch-tone phones are used, the bill-payer can be linked directly to the computer, and the buttons used to punch in payment information. Phone payment gives the consumer control over the timing and amount of payment.

6. Wire transfer, which is probably the oldest. There are two major wire systems in the USA: the Bankwire and Fedwire. The former is a private network serving commercial banks. The latter is run by the Federal Reserve System and allows electronic settlement between banks. Transfers of funds are generally between banks, other financial institutions, and large corporate customers.

7. Check truncation, which serves only institution-to-institution electronic orders. Because of various technical problems required to truncate checks on a large scale, the idea has not yet been put into widespread use. Recently, however, plans have been announced for pilot programs for certain corporate checks [8].

## 4. Overview and Context of EFT Legislation

The wide variety and diversity of EFT services and systems raised formidable problems in the development of comprehensive statutes governing all consumer aspects [8,52]. EFT systems were fraught with dangers unknown to consumers – the legal protections applicable to checking accounts, credit cards, and other payment systems did not encompass EFT [9,53,54,55].

EFT creates new transactions which have no traditional legal counterparts [3,7,14,21,24,30,42,44]. Electronic fund transfer performs "financial transactions normally executed by a check;" therefore, potential Uniform Commercial Code (U.C.C.) Articles 3 and 4 problems exist [5,32] in the USA, however, EFT may also have a credit function; thus there are both federal and state consumer credit protection statutes to consider. Since a single electronic transaction could include characteristics of money, checks, and credit cards, there was no easy resolution when there was conflict between the applicable bodies of law [14,24,44]. Consumer liability for unauthorized withdrawals (consumer fraud) or other system violations remained undecided [9,55], and the liability of financial institutions for loss due to incorrect transferral of funds was unclear. Furthermore, disclosures to consumers were incomplete and inconsistent [9].

In 1978 a comprehensive financial regulatory reform package (the Financial Institutions Regulatory and Interest Rate Control Act) was signed into law by the US Congress. Title XX of that act is the Electronic Fund Transfer Act (EFTA). Two provisions governing consumer liability for unauthorized electronic fund transfer and EFT card issuance became effective February 10, 1979 and the remainder of the legislation took effect on May 10, 1980 [41].

The EFTA allocates risks and liabilities for such matters as the institution's failure to respond correctly to a consumer's instructions, losses caused by unauthorized access to an account, and disputes concerning alleged errors. The Act also provides a detailed set of procedures for resolving such problems [8].

Preliminary efforts have been made to coordinate the laws relating to various payment systems. Professor Hal Scott of Harvard Law School has promoted this concept [5,32,40]. The Federal Reserve Board has also proposed additional legislative action to reconcile the federal laws covering credit and debit cards. This is likely to be a major theme in the evolution of consumer financial services regulation [8].

## 5. Regulatory and Enforcement Authority

The EFTA grants the Federal Reserve Board authority to promulgate regulations implementing the Act [8,20] and to issue model clauses for optional use of financial institutions. These clauses describe the disclosure requirements of the Act, and help in protecting those who utilize them from civil liability.

The Federal Reserve Board is also empowered to modify the requirements of the EFT Act for small financial institutions if the modification is necessary to alleviate an undue burden and is not inconsistent with the purposes and objectives of the Act. Finally, the Board is instructed to extend the regulation under the EFTA to persons holding consumer accounts if those third parties offer EFT services [8,13].

## 6. Coverage of the EFTA

All account transfers that are initiated through electronic terminals, telephonic instruments, computers or magnetic tape, but not by paper instrument, are defined as electronic fund transfers. Certain types of transfers are specifically excluded such as check guarantee or authorization services, that do not directly transfer fund to or from an account, and automatic transfer services which transfer savings to checking accounts to cover overdrafts.

The Act does not define the legal relationships among the various institutions participating in an EFT service or system. It is applicable only to the relationship between consumers and financial institutions [18]. While the Federal Reserve Board has attempted to allocate some EFTA responsibilities, these matters remain largely a matter of private contract [8,33,49].

## 7. Disclosure and Documentation

## 7.1. Disclosure

Disclosure requirements are among the most valuable protections provided by the EFTA. Because of consumer unfamiliarity and because of the serious financial and privacy hazards, consumers need information on their rights, responsibilities, and liabilities. Plain language disclosures are required by the EFTA when a consumer contracts for EFT services, when accounts terms are changed, or when transfers are made [10].

The EFTA requires institutions to disclose changes in terms or conditions of an account, if these changes increase consumers' costs or liabilities, or decrease consumers' access to accounts. Generally, written notification of these changes must be given to consumers at least twentyone days before their effective data [9,35].

## 7.2. Documentation

Perhaps one of the greatest hazards of EFT is its reliance on electronic impulses rather than paper $[10,23]$ . No paper documentation of transfers is generated. EFT systems can malfunction or be manipulated to wrongly withdraw funds. Thus consumers are vulnerable because they have no proof of payment for use in disputes with merchants or in filing tax returns. Furthermore, they have no basis for identifying errors and requesting correction.

To provide consumers with proof of payment, the EFTA establishes that receipts which indicate a transfer “shall constitute prima facie proof that such a transfer was made” [12]. The statutory language, however, could be interpreted as allowing an institution that generates an erroneous receipt to use it as proof against the consumer. Therefore, the Federal Reserve Board needs to clarify that this cannot occur [9]. Also, the EFTA mandates a periodic statement for each consumer account that may be accessed through electronic fund transfer [8].

## 8. Preauthorized Transfers

Because of their special nature, preauthorized regularly-recurring transfers are given independent treatment by the EFTA. Such transfers must be requested by the consumer in writing; a copy of this authorization must be provided to the consumer at that time. Although POS and ATM transactions need not be reversible or provide for any stop-payment privilege, preauthorized transfers may be stopped by notifying the financial institution orally or in writing at any time up to three business days preceding the scheduled data of transfer [44]. If preauthorized transfers to the financial institution or a third party payee vary in amount, the financial institution or designated payee may be required to provide advance notice of the amount to be transferred and the scheduled date of the transfer prior to each transfer.

## 9. Error Resolution Procedures

Electronic fund transfer services are not error free. Incorrect information, improper computer programming, or machine malfunction may occur as well as incorrect, mistaken, or unauthorized transfers, documentation failures or omissions, and any consumer request for additional information, clarification, or documentation.

Quick error resolution is required by the EFTA [2]. As a matter of policy, it was necessary to favor the consumer since a financial institution is better able to cope with delay in payment. The consumer is required to give oral or written notice which identifies the account, states the error, gives the amount and states the reason [41,44].

Safeguards are provided to assure that both parties of EFT act in good faith. If a court finds that a financial institution did not provisionally recredit the consumer's account and either did not investigate in good faith or did not have a reasonable basis for "believing the consumer's account was not in error," or the the "institution knowingly and willfully concluded that the consumer's account was not in error when such a conclusion could not reasonably been drawn, the financial institution is subject to treble damages, statutory penalties, attorney fees, and court costs" [9,35]. Because the burden of suing rests on the consumer, there is protection against frivolous and unjust claims [44]

## 10. Consumer Liability for Unauthorized Transfers

There are two basic models for loss due to unauthorized EFT transfers [2]. The credit card model operates on the principle that there should be a customer risk of \$50 with the rest born by the institution. The check model follows the maxim that the depository institution is contractually obligated to follow the order of the depositer; thus to charge the drawer's account for any unauthorized transfer would be a breach of the contract. However, the U.C.C. carves out exceptions to the general rule in the case of "fictitious payees" and the "imposters." This assumes that the burden of liability should rest with the party who is best able to discover the unauthorized use [44].

Because of the sophisticated technology, neither the consumer nor the institution is in a good position to detect unauthorized use of EFT at the time it occurs. The EFTA therefore blends features of both models. A consumer has potential liability for the unauthorized use of the account only after accepting the access device and the issuer has a method “whereby the user can be identified” [41,44].

The liability of the account holder may be altered by negligence. Failure to notify the depository institution within two business days after learning of the loss, theft, or unauthorized use of the access device makes the consumer liable for any losses within the two day period, subject to the \$50 limit; moreover, .ne consumer is liable for any losses after the two-day period and until notice is given, up to an overall \$500 limit, if losses could have been prevented by timely notice [35,44]. Also, the consumer is liable for transfers which the depository institution can show were preventable unless the institution is notified within 60 days after the receipt of the statement showing unauthorized use. Finally, the financial institution has the burden of proof.

## 11. Financial Institution's Liability

Financial institutions are not liable for any actual or consequential damages they cause by erroneously withdrawing funds. Such transactions were excluded from the definition of “unauthorized transfers,” presumably so that individuals would not be liable for a loss up to a maximum of fifty dollars. Even so, consumers may use the EFTA error resolution procedures to obtain reimbursement for wrongfully withdrawn funds. Then, if reimbursement is not made in accordance with the correction requirements, the consumer may bring a civil action. If the consumer fails to use error correction procedures, no remedies are provided by the EFTA. However, individuals may be able to obtain redress under common law, such as unjust enrichment, or negligence, or possibly under the Uniform Commercial Code.

## 12. Civil and Criminal Liability

There are two basic provisions regarding civil liability under the EFTA: one governs the liability of a financial institution for failing to make or stop an electronic fund transfer in accordance with appropriate instructions; the other sets forth the general civil liability of institutions which otherwise fail to comply with the provisions of the EFTA.

Institutions are generally liable for all damages they cause by not properly completing a transfer or stopping a preauthorized transfer. Specifically, they are liable if they do not transfer the correct amount in a timely fashion when properly instructed, or if they do not complete transfers in accordance with the terms and conditions of the account. They are also liable if the funds in an account are insufficient to complete a transfer, but the funds are insufficient because an institution did not properly credit a deposit [9].

The EFTA does not establish an institution's liability for damages that are proximately caused by dishonors that result when an institution erroneously withdraws funds or when an unauthorized withdrawal has been made. However, the Senate Report on the EFTA explains that the same liability would be set for financial institutions in EFT that now apply to checking under the Uniform Commercial Code. Applying the standards of U.C.C. § 4-402 to EFT would render institutions liable for either proximately caused damages or actual damages that result when they wrongfully withdraw funds from an account.

When transfers are not properly completed due to a bona fide error and an institution proves it had good procedures to avoid that sort of error, the institution's liability is limited to actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees. However, institutions are completely absolved of any liability if they can show three facts: first, that the failure to complete a transfer resulted from an act of God or other circumstance outside control; second, that they exercised reasonable care to prevent that type of circumstance; and third, that they exercised diligence, as required [41]. Thus, they must anticipate circumstances such as storms, earthquakes, cyclones, blackouts, and possibly bombings, and have protections against their interference (e.g., alternative back-up systems [9]).

The EFTA also shields institutions from liability when a transfer is not properly completed due to a technical malfunction known to consumers at the time they tried to initiate the transfer or, with a preauthorized transfer, at the time the transfer should have occurred [9].

The general civil liability provision specifies certain exculpatory circumstances also. If the defendant can demonstrate that its violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error, it may not be held liable. Also, a financial institution that discovers that it has inadvertently violated the Act may preempt any lawsuit by notifying the consumer of the failure, complying with the requirements of the Act, making an appropriate adjustment to the consumer's account, and paying actual damages [8].

## 13. Miscellaneous

Under the EFTA, whenever a malfunction prevents the completion of a transfer, the consumer's obligation is suspended "until the malfunction is corrected and the electronic fund transfer may be completed, unless such other person has subsequently, by written request, demanded payment by means other than an electronic fund transfer [35]." However, the EFTA ignores the problem of stop payments except in regard to preauthorized transfers.

One substitute for the stop payment is value dating wherein the consumer and the payee agree on a future data when payment becomes due. In the interim, the funds are held in "escrow." Value dating permits the payor to time the final payment. Another method is building in a reversal period. This period permits the consumer to request reversal if purchased goods are returned. Merchants accept either value dating or reversal provision because collection expenses and bad debt losses are cut.

Congress in the EFTA adopted a “relation to state laws” provision which preempts only state law which is “inconsistent” with the federal legislation. However, state law is to be deemed not inconsistent to the extent that it affords any consumer greater protection that that afforded by the EFTA.

There is not statutory provision regarding float. Float is not a legal right, but simply a characteristic of the check and credit card systems; it is the period of time between the writing of a check and its presentation for payment, or the period of time between a charge and the due date of the bill. The consumer has the use of the funds during this interlude. A payment system which accelerates the finality of payment, shifts this earning power to the payee and eliminates float. Some "float-like" substitute may have to be programmed into the system if the consumer will not accept EFT without float. Value dating, deferred payments, or discounts are all suitable replacements.

## 14. Conclusion

Recent technological advances have drastically altered the payment systems. The UCC and U.S. federal credit card legislation do not adequately assign the rights and liabilities of the participants in an EFT system. Consequently, the U.S. Congress passed the EFTA of 1978 to provide protections to consumers who choose to pay electronically. The EFTA represents a significant development in the volution of consumer financial services. For the first time, comprehensive federal regulation will govern the rights, responsibilities and liabilities of U.S. consumers and service providers in EFT systems.

Prior to the enactment of the EFTA, consumers were not given basic information about their EFT accounts and their liabilities when using EFT systems. Now, the EFTA requires disclosure in plain language of key information about consumer rights and liabilities. Also, the EFTA requires institutions to provide consumers with paper documentation of transfers. Because EFT systems are vulnerable to computer fraud and unauthorized use, the EFTA limits consumers' liabilities for resulting losses and provides consumers with error correction procedures. The EFTA allows the states to enact laws that are more protective of consumers, and such laws are needed to fill the gaps in the protections afforded to consumers by the EFTA.
