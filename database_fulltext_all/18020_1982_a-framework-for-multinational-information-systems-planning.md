# A Framework for Multinational Information Systems Planning

Gad J. SELIG

Director, Technology & Strategy Consulting, Contel Information Systems, 130 Steamboat Road, Great Neck, NY 11024, USA

The purpose of this study was to develop and identify formal strategic planning and control approaches and requisites to better manage and integrate the information systems resources (e.g. information systems, telecommunications and office automation) more effectively with the business plans and processes in a large multidivisional, multiproduct and transnational corporate environment. Additionally, an examination of certain external and internal environmental factors and pressures was conducted to assist business and information systems executives, and professionals to focus on those factors which influence the successful management and direction of the multinational information systems resources and functions (MNISR) within their environments.

Twenty-five U.S. based multinational organizations participated in the research. Most of the organizations were selected from the manufacturing (e.g. energy, pharmaceuticals, consumer products and electronic) industry with some representation from the banking and financial services industry. In addition, four case studies were conducted to provide in-depth analysis, comparisons and verifications of actual MNISR planning and control practices.

A pragmatic strategic planning framework and structure consisting of multiple but interrelated components and activities is suggested as the formal end product of this research. In addition, five general conclusions stand out from the research. First, the MNISR plan structure must accommodate both the similarities and differences between the domestic and international MNISR environments and the key factors which influence them. Second, business and MNISR plan linkages must be established by addressing strategic business and MNISR support issues and strategies at several organizational levels based on the involvement of multiple personnel across business and geographic unit boundaries. Third, the utilization of a uniform MNISR planning language, process, cycle and structure similar to the business plan language, process, cycle and structure is critical. Fourth, the role of the corporate (central MNISR) staff should be well defined and limited to only those activities where its expertise is either clearly recognized and/or established by corporate policy. Fifth, corporate MNISR should provide funding for MNISR strategies that are either mandated by corporate headquarters or have a demonstrable comparative advantage to the individual multinational business components as well as the corporation as a whole.

## 1. Background

Many multinational corporations have, as yet, to come to terms with the interaction and complexities that nationalistic, geographic, linguistic, technological, political, management and functional differences have imposed on their transnational Multinational Information Systems Resources (MNISR) functions of planning and control. A growing number of MNCs are utilizing computer based information systems, office automation and administrative support systems, and telecommunications systems as major and increasingly critical tools in managing and monitoring their global businesses and environments.

More senior managers are focusing their attention on managing strategic change in the MNISR environment by identifying significant issues, opportunities and vulnerabilities; and developing and managing proactive MNISR change strategies and methodologies that address the strategic and tactical directions of the business and MNISR functions [1].

## 2. Objectives

The major purpose of this study was to develop and identify a blend of formal and incremental strategic planning and control processes and strategies to more effectively manage and integrate the information systems resources based on an examination of the practices of large multinational corporations with a reputation for being well managed. Additionally, an examination of key external and internal environmental factors and pressures was conducted.

## 3. Methodology and Participants

Twenty-five U.S. based multinational organizations out of a total of fifty (see Table 1) participated in the research. Most of the organizations were selected from the manufacturing industry with some representation from the banking and financial services industry. In addition, four detailed case studies were conducted.

Data used in this study were primarily obtained by the survey and response research method and consisted of mail questionnaires, personal and telephone interviews, an extensive literature search and a review of source documents. An expert panel, consisting of prominent professionals, was also employed to review the research.

## 4. Major MNISR Planning and Control Concepts and Framework

Strategic planning, is a difficult process to implement on a meaningful and consistent basis. Tilles [2] suggested that there are five major dilemmas, which face large multidivisional companies:

1. The need for both individual initiative and multi-group consensus.

2. The need for divisional and/or national entity autonomy and a total corporate entity.

3. The need for commitment to but objectivity about goals and objectives.

4. The need for vigorous direction of internal operations and awareness of multiple external environments.

5. The need for incentives which motivate to success on short term performance and those which motivate to success on long range goals.

There is no single MNISR planning and control framework to reconcile theory with reality. Rather, there are a multitude of approaches and dimensions which may not only differ from company to company, but also between divisions of large multinationals. Many uncontrollable and sometimes conflicting factors, both internal and external, interact in a complex way to affect results. MNISR management must arrive at a set of multiple strategies in the face of this complexity.

## 4.2. MNISR Strategic Planning Process

A synthesis of a strategic planning process (see Fig. 1) for the multinational information resources has been developed to include six successive and iterative phases. Each phase includes a series of activities which are described below:

1. Environmental Reference Base. This provides an external and internal environmental profile, both domestically and internationally, of the factors influencing existing business and MNISR conditions in terms of problems, stages of penetration, resource levels, strengths, weaknesses, and pressures for change. It answers the question of where are we today?

2. Issues and Opportunities. This phase identifies or relates business issues and objectives with MNISR issues, opportunities, goals and objectives from a corporate, domestic and international (country and region) perspective. This phage generally answers the question of why change?

3. Strategic Analysis of Alternatives. This phase involves an evaluation of various strategic MNISR alternatives in terms of organization structure, mission statements, benefits, strategies, advantages, disadvantages, risks, obstacles, vulnerabilities, resource requirements, key assumptions and contingency plans. This phase should encompass business and MNISR posture considerations and should generally address the question of what could we do?

4. Strategic Objective Setting and Commitment. This phase involves the actual plan and the development of strategies. Implementation strategies (e.g. hardware, software, migration of applications, telecommunications, shared systems, personnel development, office automation, etc.) are established. This phase answers the questions of: what should we do?

5. Systems Development Methodology. This phase attempts to translate the strategic MNISR plan into work components and projects through a systems development methodology, which consists of multiple phases (e.g., Project Initiation, Feasibility Study, Design, Approvals, Resource Allocations, etc.). This phase answers the question of How do we get there?

6. Follow-Up and Measurement. This phase involves a review mechanism of the plans based on a series of preestablished requisites such as budgetary reviews, capital expenditure linkages to the plan, major project checkpoint reviews, periodic performance audits and adherence to standards and/or guidelines. This phase answers the question of "Did we get there?"

## 4.3. Business and MNISR Plan Linkages

Another component of the framework is to create an environment which parallels and links the business planning process with the MNISR planning process. This provides a mechanism for the involvement and, perhaps, greater commitment by multilevel and multiunit management, personnel and organizations, to a more effective linkage between the business and MNISR worlds.

The linkage mechanisms should start with independent, but parallel and synchronous business and MNISR planning phases (e.g., Environmental Assessment; Issue Analysis; Strategic, Tactical and Annual Support Strategies; Joint Checkpoint Reviews) and culminate in joint business/MNISR planning and review sessions at the division, country and corporate levels. The key point to avoid, if linkages are to be successful, is not to complete the business plan or the MNISR plan process separately, but to encourage joint reviews.

## 4.4. Proactive MNISR Posture and Strategies

Historically, the traditional long range MNISR planning process looked at the business environment predominantly from a technical or functional view. Rarely was there an attempt to relate the MNISR opportunities and resource levels with the life cycle of the business such as embryonic, growth-oriented, mature and declining [5]. The traditional long range MIS plan process was an extension of the budget process. This involved an extrapolation of past and current projects and financials, making allowances for inflation and other factors. Some zero based budgeting concepts have recently been introduced into the process.

To alter these traditional MNISR perspectives, several purposeful and incremental change strategies have to be implemented.

First, due to the all pervasive planning horizon, many more levels of people, organizations and functional groups must be involved in the process through multilevel and multipurpose (functional as well as strategic) task forces, quality circles, workers councils and other such tools.

Second, to improve the MNISR strategic planning and control functions, the perception of what the MNISR functions contribute to the business and why MNISR exists must change. In strategic MNISR planning, the plan process, not the final plan, is most important. As organizations and people try to refine the planning process, they have to understand their reference base, what options or alternatives they have in different host environments and in the relationships between the home and most MNISR organizations, and where they are going or their directional thrust. Instead of just focusing on the development of traditional MIS applications as part of the planning process, the perception must focus on the new or changing roles MNSIR has to play, such as: to harness new bottom line automation opportunities at all levels of the organization it serves; to provide services which enable users to generate and communicate information on a transnational as well as a domestic base; to design and implement computer-based information systems that will deliver cost effective information complete and reliable; to establish a relationship between MNISR and the business objectives of revenues, expenses and profit and to achieve success, as uniquely defined and measured by the objectives of each MNC. Thus, strategic and proactive MNISR planning tries to bridge the gap between tactical (short term) and long-range strategies by answering the question of how and where an MNC will go from the present into the future. Strategic planning is concerned not with inventing the future so much as identifying the objectives to be achieved and of developing the strategies and capabilities to meet these objectives.

Third, MNISR strategies should be based on parallel business postures. For example, depending on the overall business climate, the MNISR planning environment can and should assume one of several of the following macro-level strategies:

1. Passive or Reactive Strategies. The following substrategies would apply to this area:

a. Continue Present or Business as Usual Strategy. This strategy is characterized, for example, by a company with a low visibility MNISR. MNISR is not only a service unit, but largely a financial after-the-fact service unit. The second characteristic of this strategy is that it tends to thrive in companies that are growing very fast and are increasing their share of the market rapidly in the existing business.

b. Defensive Strategy. In this posture, the typical MNISR attitude is defensive. MNISR has lost its credibility and is, therefore, vulnerable. Typically, it reacts to fire-fighting situations, is operationally oriented with little or no strategic business support focus.

c. Major Contraction Strategy. In this posture, the MNISR strategy would parallel the contraction or divestiture strategy of a business. Mature or declining businesses would probably adopt this strategy.

2. Proactive Strategies. The aggressive or opportunistic MNISR posture would encompass an active role in seeking new bottom line MNISR opportunities with several sub-strategy postures:

a. Modest Improvement Strategy. This option would apply in a business where systems have become technologically obsolete and require major redesign efforts at a minimum investment.

b. Aggressive Expansion Strategy. In this mode, MNISR penetration would occur in areas that have, heretofore, not been penetrated. From an MNC perspective, shared systems and facilities, as well as technology migration, could be significant strategies.

c. Radical New Direction Strategy. This MNISR strategy would examine new opportunities, and change organizational responsibilities and structures to enhance MNISR penetration to the point of adopting MNISR investments as a major internal business strategy. The degree of investment and risk would probably be commensurate with the potential realization of opportunities.

Fourth, to actively pursue a better MNISR integration philosophy with the business, the following questions must be critically addressed with respect to the MNISR planning framework:

1. Is there a business-like attitude towards MNISR?

2. Does the right MNISR organization structure exist to satisfy the objectives?

3. What capabilities does the MNISR organization need versus what it currently has?

4. What mechanisms can assure that present and future environmental factors are assessed in developing strategies to accommodate both with the business direction and external threats?

a. Is the business environment, both internally and externally in which MNISR operates, understood sufficiently, to develop strategic alternatives?

b. How are future technological opportunities exploited and evaluated to support the business alternatives?

c. Are the MNISR policies coexistent with the management style and policies of the business?

d. Do policies exist that ensure the sharing or migration of MNISR resources?

5. How are realistic and measurable objectives set for MNISR?

a. How are the business objectives translated into meaningful objectives for MNISR? At what level? By whom?

b. Are realistic and measurable objectives set within MNISR that support the business objectives?

c. Is there an appropriate mission statement defining the role of MNISR within the business organizational levels?

d. Is the scope of MNISR sufficiently broad to allow it to achieve its mission now and in the future?

e. Does MNISR have a defined mission at the corporate level? At the operating group, country and/or divisional levels?

6. Are operational MNISR strategies and policies in place that determine how the MNISR services will be provided to support the business?

a. Are there strategies in place governing the acquisition, use and disposition of the key MNISR resources?

b. Are there strategies in place defining what and how the MNISR services will be provided? Who will provide the services?

c. Are there appropriate strategies in place governing how MNISR services will be charged or expensed and how post implementation audits are conducted?

7. Is there an MNISR plan process in place that communicates and obtains commitments to the strategies?

a. How are critical MNISR issues identified and translated into objectives?

b. How are business strategies translated into MNISR strategies?

c. Who is involved in the generation of the MNISR strategies? At the strategic and functional levels? At the transnational level? At the corporate and business unit levels?

d. Who commits to the MNISR strategies?

e. How are the strategies communicated throughout the organization?

f. Who translates strategies into action programs establishes priorities?

g. Are the risks inherent in new MNISR strategies

= recognized? Have contingency plans been established?

h. Is there a linkage between the strategic MNISR plan cycle and the systems development methodology cycle? Between MNISR plans, budgets and capital allocations?

8. Who ensures that long-range and short-range plans are developed within the strategic guidelines? What monitoring mechanisms exist?

Fifth, effective administration of the MNISR planning and control activities will not be attained unless a compatible, and flexible organization structure is established in consonance with the organization style. Any organization will be influenced by the current politics and characteristics of the company in which it exists. However, it is becoming more evident that the importance of computer based activities and systems is growing rapidly. Therefore, since one can expect the activity to rise significantly in the organization structure, several transitional stages of organization should be anticipated.

Traditionally in the U.S., the top computer executive has been responsible for computer systems design and development and computer operations. In many cases, these functions were later supplemented by the additionals of management science and policy and procedural responsibilities. Still later, the telecommunications functions were added to provide some coordinated management planning and control responsibilities. Historically, the MIS executive predominantly reported to the finance function, although other reporting structures have emerged and are growing. The concept of the Chief Information Officer (CIO) is becoming a reality.

The above organizational pattern does not necessarily hold true in foreign operations, particularly in Europe, where the computer and telecommunications managers tend to report into different functions.

In the real world, since the MNISR planning, development, control and operational responsibilities are so fractionalized, the MNISR strategic planning process must be so designed as to either overcome the territoriality problem or ignore them.

It appears that a practical organizational form for effective MNISR administration should have the following common characteristics as suggested by King [6]:

Centralized MNISR policy direction based on explicit strategies (e.g., planning standards and guidelines; worldwide systems migration; vendor relationships; compatibility levels; sharing of facilities such as data networks, hardware, research and information exchange) and other;

decentralized operations administration based on operating and technical standards and guidelines;

communicate new ideas and technologies to the affiliates on a continuous basis;

create sufficient coordination mechanisms to detect potential problems early enough so that corrective action can be planned early enough and initiated before a crisis can develop and

evolve a quality of leadership which achieves consensus on both strategy implementation and operating standards.

Sixth, the completion of the MNISR strategic plan should initiate implementation activities in the form of a structured MNISR development methodology. The MNISR development methodology, which can be used both globally and for information systems, telecommunications and office automation projects, assumes that all projects, large or small, consist of similar generic phases. These phases should be linked together by a uniform format to provide a structured model for MNISR developments. Typical phases in the MNISR development methodology should include: project initiation, initial survey/feasibility study, depth study, business system or service design, computer systems design, programming (where necessary), conversion, documentation, implementation and evaluation.

## 6. Select External and Internal Pressures and Factors Influencing The MNISR Planning Process

Sixteen external and internal environment factors were examined. The external factor included: the international computer and communications industry, nationalism, technology proliferation, governmental and regulatory factors, labor and unionization, socio-cultural factors, competitive factors and uncertainty. The internal factors which were examined included: multinational company characteristics, management style and the corporate role, business management implications, MNISR organizational responsibilities, MNISR opportunity areas, standards and protocols, vendor coordination and technology sharing. Some of the conclusions of the key factor analysis follows.

## 6.1. Regulatory Factors

Regulatory implications on the MNISR functions primarily impact the following areas: provide many more non-common carrier alternatives; potentially restrict or make difficult the transborder data flow of information; privacy restrictions on the processing and transmission of personnel data and import restrictions on certain classes of hardware and software in developing countries. Over 25 national governments, primarily European, have either passed or have under study, transborder information legislation. To date, personal privacy information has dominated the discussions, but such other factors as national defense, employment, economics and culture are starting to be considered [7]. As more MNC's voice their opinions on this issue, governments will be obligated to take those factors into consideration if they desire to attract more multinational businesses.

Despite the uncertainty regarding particular regulatory and competitive actions and directions, interconnected domestic and international networks exist and are continuing to grow.

Multinationals must also deal with multiple host government regulations that require the retention of a large amount of information for long periods of time. For example, information that must be retained includes data to: combat antitrust and restrictions of trade (legal) suits; prove the level of foreign subsidiary profit repatriation; pension and benefits data; product safety data and other information. This can pose a significant challenge to the MNISR executive and his or her multinational counterparts.

## 6.2. Management Style and the Corporate Role

The management style of a given corporation has a major impact on the relative success or failure of MNISR planning efforts. The issue is related to the conflicts between centralization, decentralization and the corporate role. One of the key questions is whether or not MNISR functions are significantly different to justify a different management style than the business environment. If the business is profit center oriented or decentralized, can the MNISR functions remain centralized with respect to planning, development and operations? The MNISR plan and management style must reflect and coexist with the business plan process and the business management style.

It is the research's contention that the very dimensions and complexities associated with multinational corporations force a degree of MNISR decentralization. As part of this trend, the responsibilities of the corporate executive office and the role assigned to the corporate staff functions (which include the MNISR functions) is critical to the effectiveness of the MNISR planning and administrative activities. To be successful, the corporate MNISR role must be consistent with the specific corporation's management style.

## 6.3. Commonality, Standards and Protocols

One of the major inhibiting factors for effective transnational information integration is the relative weakness of both external industry and internal MNISR standards and protocols. In addition, there is always the danger of externally imposed de facto standard; which are not necessarily desired by many MNC's.

The greatest possible opportunity for facilitating multinational sharing must eminate from a strong standards and protocols program. Progress, in this area, is sometimes easier to achieve in the external environment through such international organizations as the International Standards Organization (ISO) and other groups. However, the political infrastructure in a decentralized environment tends to counteract any corporate-wide MNISR standards.

The issue and level of MNISR standardization in a decentralized environment is difficult and relates directly to such factors as: the corporate role, the degree of compatibility and commonality required for corporate management and external reporting purposes; achieving optimum vendor pricing and service relationships and achieving lower unit cost in the development and operations of MNISR systems and resources. However, the key issue of compatibility may be applicable when evaluating worldwide management reporting systems involving common definitions, uniform chart of accounts, telecommunications considerations, automated corporate financial consolidations based on geographically dispersed divisions and business sectors and a host of technology tools (e.g., data dictionaries, data bases, inquiry and report generation packages, electronic mail, teleconferencing, etc.)

## 6.4. Technology Sharing

One of the conclusions of a multinational information systems workshop held at the National Computer Conference in New York in June, 1977 was:

It is surprising to find that there are only a very small number of specific instances that could be quoted as a deterrent to multinational systems development and sharing. Even then, specific situations (mostly in the currency and balance-of-trade areas) could be solved by means of planned accommodations. Without doubt, these findings were the most unexpected results and helped significantly in arriving at an overall assessment of the potential for pursuing multinational systems development.

The role of the corporate MNISR function has a significant impact on the success or failure of the technology sharing strategy. Broad multinational systems strategies may range from aggressive, centrally developed systems programs to locally, independent systems developments. A predominant form of coordination is a multinational task force.

## 7. Conclusions and Key Success Factors

The MNISR planning and control framework and structure as outlined here should adequately fulfill their intended purposes of providing a practical planning and control framework to guide

MNISR managers and practitioners in the establishment of a more effective strategic MNISR planning and administrative environment.

Rockhart, in his work with critical success factors [8], suggested that there are perhaps five to ten factors that are critical to the success of an executive in business. In applying the concept to the MNISR planning and control environment, the following critical success factors are synergized from the material in this study:

1. Understand the similarities and differences between the domestic and international MNISR environments and provide for them in the plan process.

2. Develop a plan and MNISR strategies at multilevels based on a uniform format. Make a commitment to generate a plan. It may take two to three cycles to achieve a desirable product, but start it. Involve as many MNISR and user personnel at each level as possible.

3. Parallel the business plan process, cycle and format in a purposeful, proactive, incremental and iterative way.

4. Address strategic business and MNISR support issues and concerns from different perspectives (corporate, country, regional, division and others).

5. Understand the business and MN!SR strategic postures – where you are, where you came from, why you want to change, where you want to go and how you want to get there, either directly or indirectly but always in a purposeful and conscious manner.

6. Understand the business plan process and establish MNISR/business plan linkages through joint planning and monitoring activities.

7. Understand the human behavioral factors and interfaces between people, departments, divisions, groups, countries and the corporate office.

8. Define and communicate the corporate MNISR role in planning and coordinating global MNISR activities at various levels.

9. Develop a series of MNISR requisites for plan monitoring and/or control purposes and perform periodic effectivity audits of the business unit in a constructive and professional manner.

10. Relate the strategic MNISR plan to the business plan, capital expenditure plan, the annual budget cycle and the systems development methodology cycle.

11. Recognize that there are constraints to the MNISR planning constructs and develop strategies to overcome them. Examples of the constraints include: the excessive lead time differentiating the plan process from the implementation process and the definition of executive and other user MNISR requirements in only vague and general requirements.

As a concluding remark, Machiavelli said [9]: There is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things, because the innovator has for enemies all those who have done well under the old conditions, and lukewarm defenders in those who may do well under the new.
