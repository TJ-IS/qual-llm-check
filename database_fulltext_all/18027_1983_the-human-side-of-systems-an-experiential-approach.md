# The Human Side of Systems: An Experiential Approach

Madeline Weiss

The American University, Center for Technology and Administration, Massachusetts and Nebraska Avenues, N.W., Washington, DC 20016, USA

Research is presented on the effect of human relations training on the interpersonal orientation of information systems professionals. The results indicate that training can make a difference.

Interpersonal orientation is significant because the more systems professionals can effectively express their feelings on substantive issues and help others to do so, the more likely they are to be effective in problem solving and decision making. Increased effectiveness is a meaningful step towards the important goal of effective management information systems that truly meet the needs of their users.

Keywords: Information systems professionals, data processing professionals, management information systems, interpersonal competence, human relations training.

## 1. Introduction

As both organizations and computer technology become more complex, it is increasingly more difficult for systems professionals to keep abreast of new technology. As a result, they require more frequent training to update their knowledge and skills and less of their time is spent with users discussing their needs. Yet this same increasing complexity has led to greater utilization of interactive information systems for clerical and decision-making needs. Consequently, systems professionals must work very closely with users to insure that the information systems truly meet their needs.

Such close collaboration requires that systems designers have people skills, technical skills, and time. Not only do systems professionals have difficulty finding the time, but they often feel more comfortable working with the technical aspects of their jobs. Couger and Zawacki provide evidence to confirm this often-expressed feeling; they discovered that this group had a low “social need” score. They define “social need” as the individual’s need to interact with others. The average score for all other professionals was 5.48. For the 1,600 data processing professionals in their survey, the average score was only 4.19 [4].

Yet, all too frequently, new information systems fail to meet the needs of their users because the systems were designed and implemented more from a standpoint of technical feasibility rather than the need of the users.

## 2. Approaches to Resolving the Dilemma

Several approaches have been suggested and implemented to resolve this problem. An effective approach is to help systems people see themselves as organizational change agents – helping to bring about changes to the organization. Systems professionals need to appreciate that a change to one system of an organization generally will precipitate change to others. If systems designers are more aware of such interactions and interdependencies of the “technical” and “people” systems, they can better plan the changes the information systems so that there are fewer unexpected and unwanted consequences. Once their awareness of the human dimension is increased, they should be given the opportunity to acquire the interpersonal skills needed to be more effective.

Many authors have written about the need to take a more behavioral approach to the design and implementation of management information systems (MIS). Lucas summarized the results of some empirical research involving over 2,000 information system users in 16 organizations as: "It is our contention that the major reasons most information systems have failed is that we have ignored organizational behavioral problems in the design and operation of computer-based information systems" [7].

This same view is supported by others. London states that systems people are generally well versed in the technical aspects of their jobs, but their knowledge of and training in the people side of systems are inadequate [6]. His book presents a practical step-by-step approach to designing humanized systems by showing how to interview users, how to involve users in the design and implementation process, and how to handle a project team. Many other authors take a similar approach.

Argyris is one of the few authors who goes a step further. He recognizes the clash between the rationality of MIS and the emotionality of people's responses to it, concluding that both managers and MIS professionals must be trained to deal with emotionality and strain in interpersonal and intergroup problems. Telling systems professionals how to consider the human dimension is not enough. The systems professional must first change values and perceptions towards interpersonal relationships. Argyris says that behavioral science research

"suggests that in order to increase his interpersonal competence the individual needs to be aware of his self and his defenses. Next, it is helpful if the individual strives to attain a minimum of psychological conflict and an acceptance of his self so that he can create conditions that lead to trust, openness, risk-taking and effective confrontation of conflict." [3]

This level of interpersonal competence is needed by the systems professional to explore or take adequate account of the feelings of users of new systems who may resist the changes. The systems professional who does not achieve this level will tend to sell the new system by using rational reasons, largely missing or ignoring users' feelings (such as fear, dependency, powerlessness, and loss of control), and will tend to bypass interactions with users that might be fraught with conflicts in favor of working out the technical details in private [1].

Argyris advocates creating a learning situation in which the individual can become more interpersonally competent by increasing awareness and acceptance through experiential means. We are implementing this experiential approach to increasing the interpersonal competence of systems professionals at The American University through courses in the graduate and the undergraduate programs. Recently, we conducted a study using the graduate course.

## 3. Hypothesis Tested

The hypothesis of this research is:

Human relations training of information systems professionals is associated with a greater interpersonal orientation in change.

This hypothesis is based on experience in teaching human relations to information systems professionals, the experiences of behavioral scientists, and the previously discussed theories.

Argyris splits the administrative competence of an organization into two components: “intellective, rational, technical competence” and “interpersonal competence.” To the extent that individuals in the organization value rationality and getting the task done, they will tend to focus on the intellective and suppress the emotional aspects, especially those that do not seem immediately relevant to achieving the task. A serious consequence is the refusal to consider (either consciously or unconsciously) ideas and values which might expose feelings. Such a defensive reaction might set up barriers to the consideration of intellectual ideas as well as values in the organization, since individuals might tend to limit themselves to those ideas and values that are not threatening [1].

Agyris describes the concept of interpersonal competence by describing the three conditions of individuals which relate positively to it. These are:

1. Self-acceptance: the degree to which the individual has self-confidence. The higher this is, the greater the person feels valuable, and the more that person values others.

2. Confirmation: a sense that occurs when others confirm a person's own feelings about himself or herself. The more frequently the confirmation, is experienced, the more confidence the person feels about the ability to behave competently and the greater the probability of helping others confirm themselves and their efforts.

3. Essentiality: the ability to utilize abilities and express needs allows the person to feel essential and, consequently, more committed to the organization and to its effectiveness [2].

Having discussed the conditions, Argyris describes the behavior that individuals exhibit when they exist:

1. Owning up to, or accepting responsibility for one's; ideas and feelings.

2. Being open to ideas and feelings of others.

3. Experimenting with new ideas and feelings.

4. Helping others to own up to, be open to, and experiment with their ideas and feelings.

5. Accomplishment of these behaviors in such a way that one adds to individuality (rather than conformity), concern (rather than antagonism), and trust [2].

Such behaviors tend to be associated with an increase in both interpersonal and technical competence because increasing interpersonal competence provides a situation that allows greater technical competence [2].

We now turn our attention to the issue of how to increase interpersonal competence in information systems professionals. Argyris suggests some learning conditions that allow individuals to increase their interpersonal competence:

1. The experience must emphasize the participant's responsibility for self-development.

2. It should help the learner to become aware of self-attitudes and behavior and then help unfreeze them before making the decisions on any possible change in attitude and behavior.

3. Effective development occurs as the individual becomes more self-aware and more self-accepting.

4. As the learner's sense of self-respect, self-esteem, and self-acceptance increases, that person will become more understanding of others and show greater esteem for them. This will tend to decrease all participants' defensiveness and tend to increase openness and tolerance of new ideas [1].

My own experience confirms this, but I would go a step further. Once the individual has increased awareness and acceptance for himself or herself and others, that person should be given the opportunity to learn specific skills to use in interpersonal relationships. The learning situation should both teach these skills and provide practice in a safe environment prior to their use in ongoing work situations.

## 4. Research Design

The research used the non-equivalent control group design. The experimental group consisted of the students enrolled in two sections of the “Human Relations Aspects of Management Systems” course at The American University for the spring 1980 semester. This graduate level course is offered

Table 1

by The Center for Technology and Administration of The College of Public and International Affairs. Students were primarily enrolled in the masters program and are employed full time as computer systems professionals while attending classes in the evenings. The control group consisted of students enrolled in another masters level course titled "Systems Design for Management." This course focuses on systems design, not human relations; the instructional method is didactic rather than experiential.

The independent variable in the hypothesis, "human relations training of information systems professionals," was the first course. Its purpose is to provide an opportunity for students to increase their awareness of human processes and to develop interpersonal skills. The primary written assignment is a "journal" in which students record their reactions to the reading and class experiences and record their attempts to apply these concepts and reactions in their work situations.

The dependent variable in the hypothesis, “a greater interpersonal orientation in change” was measured through a questionnaire. The predefined categories into which the data from the questionnaire were coded were taken from Argyris’s behaviors that are associated with interpersonal and technical competence. The interpersonal behaviors were combined into two categories:

1. Owning up to, being open to, and experimenting with, ideas and feelings.

2. Helping others to own up to, be open to, and to experiment with their ideas and feelings.
The third category deals with behaviors which indicate a primarily technical orientation.

Each fixed alternative question posed three alternative behaviors or responses that correspond with these three behavior categories. The instructions asked the respondent to mark a "3" for the alternative that is most likely, a "2" for the alternative that is somewhat likely, and a "1" for the one that is least likely.

The questionnaire was administered twice to all students of the two sections of the experimental group and to all students of the control group. In all three classes, the questionnaire was administered at the first session (the pretest) and the last (the posttest).

## 6. Discussion: Implications and Limitations of the Results

The data collected offer support for the hypothesis that human relations training of information systems professionals is associated with a greater interpersonal orientation in change since the experimental group significantly increased its interpersonal scores on the questionnaire after the Human Relations Aspects of Management Systems course, while the control group did not.

We must be aware of several factors in this research before drawing conclusions from it. First, the sample size was small: 17 participants in the experimental group and 13 in the control group. While it is harder to achieve statistical significance in so small a sample, we have to be more cautious in generalizing to the population of information systems professionals from only 30 people. Second, while the questionnaire created for this research was pretested on another group, it has not been widely used and tested by others. Wider use would test both its validity and reliability. Moreover, any questionnaire that asks respondents to predict their response in various situations runs the risk of invoking desired behavior rather than actual behavior. Third, there may be differences between participants in the experimental and control groups related to their willingness to change their interpersonal orientation. Since the course is not required, those who register for it may be more disposed to changing. Because of high collinearity in the data, it was not possible to test for this difference beyond a simple calculation of the mean pretest score in each group. That calculation indicated a negligible difference between the mean pretest scores of those in the experimental group and those in the control group. Undoubtedly, there are other exogenous variables involved; e.g., personality factors, previous experience with human relations training, and other experience during the course. Additional alternative explanations are always possible when a controlled experiment cannot be performed.

Despite the limitations of this research, there is reason for optimism. These results lend support to the belief that information systems professionals can increase their interpersonal orientation. Such a gain is particularly significant in light of Argyris's theory that an increase in interpersonal competence brings with it a concomitant increase in technical potential. My experience with information systems professionals and their organizations supports this theory. The more systems professionals can effectively express feelings related to the substantive issues and help others to do so, the more likely they are to be effective in problem solving and decision making. Such increased effectiveness is a meaningful step towards the important goal of more effective systems that truly meet the needs of their users.
