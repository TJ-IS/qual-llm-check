# The Role of the Champion in DSS Implementation

Kathleen F. Curley

Management Science Department, School of Business, Northeastern University, 360 Huntington Avenue, Boston, MA 02115, USA

and

Lee L. Gremillion

Operations & Systems Management, Graduate School of Business, Indiana University, Tenth and Fee Lane, Bloomington, IN 47405, USA

Adoption of a DSS usually involves changes or innovation in an organization's way of doing things. The process of innovation has been the subject of study in other contexts, and research has indicated that the efforts of various champions can be critical to successful innovation. Observations of DSS implementations in several organizations suggest that a system champion can likewise be a key factor in implementation success. Such persons play a variety of roles, including that of reference leader, of missionary or change agent, or of top management surrogate. Implementation strategies can be designed which capitalize on the presence of a champion.

Keywords: Decision Support System, Implementation, Innovation, System Adoption, System Champion

## 1. Introduction

Information systems implementation has often been studied and depicted as a process of organizational change $[5,6,10]$ . The goal is usually to develop a description or explanation of this process from which prescriptions or recommendations can be made for improving its likelihood of success. This goal becomes increasingly important as organizations turn more and more towards computer-based information systems for support in all phases of their operations, including management decision making.

Adoption and use of the Decision Support System (DSS) in particular involves change or innovation in the organization's way of doing things. Innovation itself has been the subject of study in other contexts, particularly that of new product or process development. In this context, it has been shown that a common factor to the success of innovations is the presence of one or more Champions – individuals in the organization who make decisive contributions to the innovation by actively and enthusiastically promoting its progress through the critical stages of its development and adoption. Observations of DSS implementations in a number of organizations suggest that a “system champion” can be a key contributor to implementation success. Such persons typically have relatively high organization standing, are perceived by the system users as being knowledgeable in both the system workings and the organizational functions it supports, and demonstrate commitment to and enthusiasm for the system in a variety of ways. Often they become “internal change agents” or “missionaries” for the DSS, both influencing the attitudes of others towards the system and aiding them in understanding and using it. Implementation strategies can be designed to discover and make use of such individuals.

## 2. DSS and Innovation

As Alter [1] and others have pointed out, the term DSS has come to mean different things to different people. For the purpose of this discussion, we adopt a very broad definition of DSS as a computer-based tool whose primary purpose is to help managers make decisions. The DSS may be considered to be at one end of a spectrum of computer-based systems, at the other end of which are computerized record-keeping or transaction processing systems. Whereas these latter systems automate, usually for purposes of efficiency, some well-structured or clerical tasks, the DSS usually aims at improving a manager's effectiveness, often in making decisions involving unstructured tasks, by extending his or her ability to deal with data, models, calculations, and so on. The further a particular system lies towards this end of the spectrum, the more appropriately it is termed a DSS.

Implementation of a DSS can result in significant changes in the way members of the organization do their jobs. Many cases of this phenomenon are documented in the DSS literature. In one typical example, Joyce and Oliver [9] describe how a planning group changed its way of defining and managing projects as a result of its use of a DSS. Cinzberg [6] postulates that changes in the manager's approach to (and even definition of) the tasks performed are intrinsic to successful DSS adoption, and again, the literature provides many cases to support this. Haseman [8], for example, points out that the real problem in implementing GPLAN (a planning support DSS) was not in teaching the users how the system worked, but rather in getting them to make the role change from programmer/analyst to decision maker that was inherent in the system.

A successful DSS implementation can be viewed as an organization innovation – the organization is adopting a new decision-making “technology” just as it would adopt a new product or a new process for making or marketing a product. In fact, adoption of data processing technology has been used as an indicator of administrative innovation in earlier studies $[12,16]$ . Thus, ideas and observations concerning the process of innovation in general should be germane to this particular instance.

## 2.1. Research on Organization Innovation

The study of innovation in organizations is not new: in The Wealth of Nations Adam Smith noted the central importance of technological innovation to economic progress. Since that time economists have generally identified the process of innovation as one of the critical factors contributing to economic progress. Especially in this century serious research efforts have been devoted to understanding this process of innovation. Schumpeter [21] first stated the important distinction between inventions and innovations which has since been generally incorporated into economic theory:

An invention is an idea, a sketch or a model for a new or improved device, product, process, or system. Such inventions may often be patented but they do not necessarily lead to technical innovations. In fact the majority do not. An innovation in the economic sense is accomplished only with the first commercial transaction involving the new product, process, or idea.

Expanding on this distinction, Schmookler [19] likened innovation to the blades of a scissors:

On the one hand it involves the recognition of a need or more precisely in economic terms a potential market for a new product o process. On the other hand it involves technical knowledge.... Design, development, and marketing involve a process of matching the technical possibilities and the market.

Analogously, implementing a DSS in an organization is a process of matching system capabilities to the organizational “market”. A system itself is like an invention – no innovation flows from it unless it is eventually used. Implementation, the process of getting a system into use, is thus a parallel to the process of taking an invention to the stage of becoming an economic reality. In studying this process of innovation, a number of factors have been suggested as important to its success. One of the most interesting, and most applicable in the information systems context, is the champion. This is an individual who assumes responsibility for the various technical and marketing risks present at each phase of a product’s development. A number of studies have revealed the presence of one or more such individuals as an important reason for the success of the innovation.

## 2.2. The Champion

Schon [20], reporting the results of a study done by Arthur D. Little on radical innovations, asserted that at least where radical innovation was concerned, the champion was critical. As he stated it, “the idea either finds a champion or it dies”. He quotes Arthur K. Watson on an episode which occurred within IBM as an example of this role:

The disk memory unit, the heart of today's random access computer, is not the logical outcome of a decision made by IBM management. It was developed in one of our laboratories as a bootleg project – over the stern warning from management that the project had to be dropped because of budget difficulties. A handful of men ignored the warning. They broke the rules. They risked their jobs to work on a project that they believed in.

In a well-known British study called Project SAPPHO, conducted between 1968 and 1971, the characteristics of the innovation process were studied in great detail [17]. The basic idea of the study was to substantiate or refute generalizations about technical innovation by the systematic comparison of pairs of successful and unsuccessful attempts to innovate in the scientific instrument and chemical industries. Of the 200 measurements considered, only a small number differentiated clearly between organizations which had succeeded and those which had failed. Among the most important was the role played by key managers and technologists in the innovation process. Four specific roles were identified:

1. Technical Innovator: the individual who made the major contribution on the technical side to the development and/or design of the innovation.

2. Business Innovator: the individual within the managerial structure who was responsible for the overall progress of the project.

3. Product Champion: any individual who made a decisive contribution to the innovation by “actively and enthusiastically promoting its progress through the critical stages”.

4. Chief Executive: the "head of the executive structure" of the innovating organization (but not necessarily the managing director or chief executive officer).

Project SAPPHO results indicated that the business innovator's power, respectability, status and experience were particularly important correlates of innovation success. It also confirmed a correlation between the presence of a product champion and innovation success.

Maidique [14] combined the concept of the various roles with a model of organizational stages of development to further refine the notion of the champion. Specifically, he suggested that firms grow through distinct stages: those of (1) the small technological firm; (2) the integrated firm; and (3) the diversified firm. Empirical data from his own work in the semi-conductor industry, as well as that reported by Scott [22] and Bower [3], suggest that the characteristics of the champion differ according to the firm's stage of development. For the small technological firm, the roles of technological innovator, business innovator, product champion, and chief executive may well be filled by one individual. As the firm matures and grows larger, these roles are likely to be split. A common pattern is for the technical innovator and product champion role to be played by one person, with another individual playing the business innovator/chief executive role. Finally, at the third growth stage, separate individuals in each role are likely to be required for a particular innovation to get through the many layers of management approval.

## 3 Roles of the System Champion

In observing a number of organizations implement systems, we have been struck by the applicability of the idea that a champion is required in order for success to be achieved. In particular, a system champion can be an important determinant of implementation outcome. In some cases the system champion may resemble the product champion most closely. In other cases, the business innovator; taking an idea developed by the technologists and providing funding and political support for its diffusion. In all of the cases, the system champion is a key individual, whose personal efforts in support of the system are critical to its successful adoption. The champion role is also often found in the implementation literature, sometimes reported explicitly (as in the cases mentioned below), sometimes merely implied. The system champion role appears in several forms.

## 31 The Opinion Leader

One form of the role is that of “reference” or “opinion” leader – someone to whom others in the organization naturally turn for information and guidance. Lucas [13], for example, found such individuals to influence the degree to which their colleagues in a medical research facility made use of a computerized information storage and retrieval system. Wetherbe, Davis, and Dykman [24] found a similar phenomenon in studying the implementation of office automation systems, commenting that “informal power may reside within component departments where various operational experts’ may influence the acceptance of the systems implemented.” We have encountered instances of this in our studies of information systems use in the U.S. Forest Service. Cale [4], for example, found the presence of a reference leader to be a factor significantly correlated with the degree to which forests in one region used a computer-based planning support system.

## 3.2. The Change Agent

Often the champion plays a much more active role than merely that of reference or opinion leader. Kraemer noted this more active role in a study of the implementation of a DSS in a public sector organization, noting that the advocacy of key individuals was a critical factor to the outcome [11]. In fact, the champion is most effective when he or she serves as an internal change agent, particularly in assisting in “unfreezing” (to use the Lewin–Schein [18] terminology) the organization to make it more receptive to the change. The following example illustrates this role.

Over the past few years, in response to legislation concerning land management planning, a number of national forests have had to build land management databases using a commercial DBMS. These databases contain information on basic characteristics of the lands within the forest, broken down by geographic location. The primary purpose of the databases has been to “feed” a linear-program based land allocation model, which forest planners use to help formulate forest management strategies for the next decade. The use of the database in this manner had been mandated by higher levels in the organization.

The DBMS in use, Intel's System 2000, has a very powerful and easy-to-use "query" language facility, which has been made available to the forest personnel. Use of this facility for on-going decision support has been mildly encouraged by higher organizational levels, but not required in any sense. While most forests made little or no use of this tool, one did, in fact, begin to use it very heavily. Literally dozens of staff members on this particular forest learned to formulate queries for retrieval on a regular basis.

We visited this forest and interviewed its personnel to see if we could determine the reasons for its success. Without exception, each person interviewed would begin the explanation of his or her database use by relating two dramatic incidents in which the database query language was used to support management decision making. In one case, a fire broke out in one of the forest's wilderness areas, necessitating a decision, within a few hours, on a suppression/containment strategy. Rather than evaluating the threat by flying over the region as he normally would, the forest supervisor (the top line officer on the forest) directed the fire analysis team to query the database to get the information needed to formulate and evaluate various fire scenarios. The information so obtained indicated that an all-out suppression effort was clearly justified, and a \$1.5 million effort was launched. In the second case, a U.S. Senator wanted to know the consequences of routing an oil pipeline across the forest lands. Again, at the supervisor's direction, forest staff used the information from the database. They found that it enabled them to make a response in a few weeks, instead of several months as such an analysis would normally take.

On this forest, the supervisor played a combination role, that of chief executive and product champion. He “struck when the iron was hot” – i.e., he promoted system use precisely when the most opportune moment presented itself. In both cases, he forced use of the system under circumstances in which that use would be both successful and visible. This was much more effective in motivating the members of the organization to un-freeze from their accustomed ways of doing things than any amount of “selling” activities by higher organizational levels.

## 3.3. The Top Management Surrogate

Another way to view the role of the system champion is as an expression of top management support for the system. To state that management support is important to implementation success is almost a platitude, but the manner in which this support is to be expressed or demonstrated to the members of the implementing organization is not a trivial issue. Usually the top manager involved - president, chairman, director, or whatever - is not personally in a position to explain to each member of the organization the rationale for the implementation and his or her particular role in it. Some other mechanisms for expressing this support must be found, and they are often not obvious. In fact, Weick [23] has shown that this is a common problem: directions initiated by top management are often diluted, weakened, and even lost as they are disseminated down into the organization.

The activities of a system champion can serve to project and reinforce management support, particularly when the individual who plays the role is perceived by the members of the organization to be prestigious or influential. The implication is that if this person, who is close to the “powers that be”, is pushing the system, then that person must be doing it with the blessing of those powers. The champion may act as top management’s deputy or surrogate in dealing with unforeseen contingencies which arise during implementation. This model of the champion is very similar to Bardach’s notion of the “fixer” – a person or group with the prestige and power to combat counter-implementation efforts effectively [2].

Gibson [5] reports a case which illustrates this. It involved the successful implementation of a minicomputer system in a bank with little or no apparent support or involvement on the part of senior management. He points out, however, that "the key was an unusually strong middle manager who had the confidence and backing of his bosses and of his workforce." This manager was essentially given the charter to champion the system and then left alone by top management.

## 4. Implications for Implementation Planners

If the activities of a system champion can enhance the likelihood of a DSS being successfully adopted, then the implication for the implementation planner is simple: he or she should try to either find or make a champion for the system. If no one in the implementing organization can be found to champion the system, then the planner should consider either changing the system to make it more attractive, or delaying or abandoning the implementation. In other words, if no one of high status and prestige in the implementing organization has any interest in pushing the system, then its chances of success are probably quite low.

Searching for a potential champion is most appropriate when one has a choice of organizations in which to initiate implementation. For example, it is becoming increasingly common for multi-unit organizations to "pilot test" systems in one or a few units as a prelude to implementation at many sites. Among the criteria for selecting a pilot size is its amenity to the system – one definitely prefers success to failure in the pilot so that it can serve as a reference selling point. By selecting those units in which there is an individual to play the role of champion, one increases the likelihood of that success.

When there is not a natural system champion in the organization, it may be possible to develop one as part of the implementation activities. This is particularly the case when top management supports the system: the role of the champion will be to make that support operational. Here we are looking for some influential individual who can be trained or otherwise prepared to lead the system through. Two cases illustrate this.

The first involved the phased implementation, over a three year period, of a planning-support DSS in eleven forests of one US Forest Service region [7]. After pilot installations on three forests during the first year, the implementation planners became convinced that a champion ("honcho" in their terms) on each forest was a prerequisite to successful system use. Accordingly, the Regional Forester (the top line manager in the region) directed each forest to designate a primary staff officer as process leader for system use. The individual designated was then given extensive training on the system, and was made responsible for all aspects of its use on the forest.

In another case, a deliberate attempt is made to generate a champion; this involves office automation systems marketed by Wang Laboratories in Europe. In an effort to improve customer satisfaction, Wang has developed a “human factors” program aimed at helping companies enhance their use of the systems they purchase. One of the key elements is the identification of managers who will assume accountability for overseeing various phases of system implementation. Wang representatives describe this as a way to enhance the robustness of the implementation plans – a manager who feels some degree of “ownership” in the plan will rescue it when it happens upon unforeseen problems [15].

## 5. Conclusions

The MIS implementation literature has appropriately moved away from a relatively naive “rationalist” tradition [10] to an understanding of the process as basically political and social. The notion of the “system champion” suggests a means for improving implementation management which is consistent with that model of the process. The role of the champion is essentially political and social – to support, to sell, to lead, to urge use of the system, to respond to and help overcome resistance to the change involved in its adoption. Were users truly “rational” in the traditional sense, they would adopt a system on the basis of its empirically demonstratable merits, and no champion or other political or social change agent would be required. Of course users are not truly rational, and the literature is full of cases in which users have rejected apparently “good” systems.

Our observations, both in the field and from cases reported in the literature, suggest that the presence of a champion is often a significant factor in determining implementation success. This conclusion is supported by a considerable body of research on innovations, of which DSS implementation may be viewed as a special case. In fact, to the extent that the adoption of an information system involves organizational change or innovation, the champion's role is likely to be important whether or not the system is properly termed a DSS. We have focussed upon DSS implementation because such systems almost by definition involve innovation.

Finally, we have seen cases in which implementation planners have actively exploited this idea by attempting to secure champions for their systems. Further study of this phenomenon, particularly toward refining understanding of its most effective use, is clearly indicated. We now recognize implementation as a political and social process – the challenge is to work from that recognition to develop effective prescriptions for action.
