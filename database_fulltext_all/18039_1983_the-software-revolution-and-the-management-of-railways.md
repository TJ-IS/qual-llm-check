# The Software Revolution and the Management of Railways \*

K.H. Spring \*\*

Lariden, Wellington Avenue, Virginia Water, Surrey GU25 4
QZ, England

The performance of railway trains is tending to reach a limit, set by energy and maintenance costs. Road and air competitors are making efforts to increase their market share. Control, communications and information processing are making rapid improvements because of current developments in electronics. The market and competitive environment of railways is changing, usually adversely. The software revolution offers railways new means and opportunities towards better performance, raising significantly utilization of assets. Human resources can be used more effectively and with less drudgery, releasing skills and initiative. Most importantly, it should be possible to develop a much higher standard of customer-care at all stages of the journey.

Keywords: Railways, Technology, Markets, Competition, Performance, Productivity, Human Skills, Customer-care.

Dr. Kenneth Spring started his career in the National Physical Laboratory in England. He subsequently moved into defence research, including a period at the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough. He then worked on novel methods of power generation and transmission at the Central Electricity Generating Board. He joined the British Railways Board in the early 60's, at a time when their Research and Development Division was being greatly expanded in scale and scope.

He was Head of Research 1970–1978, then becoming Director, Strategic Studies. He retired in 1982, and is now an independent consultant.

## 1. Introduction

1982 was designated as Information Technology year in Great Britain so it was appropriate to look at the many changes which are under way and the consequences which they may have for railways. These changes will present many challenges and opportunities in the way railways are operated and managed. Also there are likely to be profound changes in the social, technical and market environment in which they seek to operate in an increasingly competitive world.

## 2. Objectives for Railways

Modern railway trains are tending to reach a limit of performance on present track infrastructures. Passenger train speeds are limited by safety, and by energy and maintenance costs, which rise rapidly above speeds of sav 200 km/hr. Similarly, for freight trains, axle loads and speeds have been pushed near to the limits. Although some railways have constructed new lines permitting higher speeds, their economic justification often depends on capacity limitations of existing lines. Perhaps, therefore, this is a useful time to see whether the software revolution might come to the rescue of hard pressed managers.

The expression “total cost” (or cost of ownership) has come to be used to describe all the costs associated with a particular piece of equipment. In the case of a high speed train, for example, it would include not only the capital cost of construction but also annual maintenance costs (suitably capitalised) and other operating costs, including track damage and more intensive signalling.

The productivity of all resources, human and physical, must be improved and this is where the software revolution can make its impact. To stay competitive, railways must match the standards of service and customer expectation provided by the competition. Any time saving by faster trains is worthwhile only if matched by quick and easy-to-use terminal and interchange facilities.

## 3. Characteristics and Opportunities of the Software Revolution

The software revolution is characterized by the growth of mini and microcomputers to rival and challenge large mainframe computers: also by the development of techniques for digital as well as, or in place of, analogue data transmission. Recently there has been rapid development of optical fibre transmission with very high data transfer rates, low error rates and freedom from interference. There are important developments also in data display systems. There is a need for flexibility to adapt to these changes – but new technical systems possibly have a shorter life, higher system capital costs and possibly more sophisticated maintenance than conventional equipment. Railway management will thus have to attune to much shimmer and more highly skilled staff.

It is convenient to think of the opportunities at three levels. First is the generation, storage and use of information for running the railway on a real-time basis, especially to cater for non-timetabled or unplanned movements and to recover rapidly from disturbances. The second is to provide information for customers on a preplanned (timetable) basis and also on a real time, updated, 'actual' basis. The third is provision of information for longer term business and system management, e.g. for billing customers, for planning new resources, for financial analysis, etc.

A good deal more thought on the strategy for information systems seems to be needed. Too much information can be as costly and embarrassing as too little. An information and software strategy must start from the nature and purpose of the information needed, and those who need it — and only then proceed to the technology of how it is generated, stored and transmitted. However, the very act of specifying what is really wanted, with a knowledge of the costs involved, is a powerful force in refining the real needs and problems.

## 4. Changes in Communications and Electronics

Increasing amounts of data are being transferred between computers, and new facilities such as electronic mail and facsimile are being introduced. All these use digital information coding techniques, and they do not mix readily with voice analogue techniques. Digital technology is gradually taking over from analogue, and this new technology embraces both transmission and switching. In due course, this is bound to affect the relationship between the traditionally separate areas of railway signalling and telecommunications.

The range of technology is wide and growing rapidly, embracing co-axial cables, one pair of which can carry about 1,000 telephone channels, and, more recently, optical fibres. Multimode fibres can now give data transmission rates the equivalent to about 2,000 telephone channels, with repeater station spacings of almost 10 km. Mono-mode fibres in the next few years, will allow these or higher data transmission rates, with repeaters at spacings of about 20 km. The practical problems with optical fibres now revolve around radiation sources and detectors which are cheap, reliable, long lived, and are responsive in the low attenuation part of the optical spectrum, 1–1.3 nm.

In parallel with these developments in data transmission, the costs of both processing and storing data are reducing, in real terms. The information packing density of magnetic disc storage is being improved, by using thinner magnetic coatings and improved reading-heads, and there is a strong possibility of an optical, rather than magnetic, disc memory, read by a laser. The cost of processing data is being reduced as the number of elements on a macro-chip is being increased. The limit with today's technology (optical masking) is approaching 300,000 elements per chip. Over the next decade, this limit is likely to rise by about 2 orders of magnitude, to around 30 million per chip, using electronic beam, or ion-beam, technology.

Summarising, we can say that the cost of employing people is rising at typically up to 5% per year in real terms, the cost of data communication is falling by about 10% per year, the cost of processing logic is falling by about 25% per year, and that of memory by almost 40%. The implications are at least twofold. First, do not employ people unless you have to! Secondly, data-processing and memory costs are falling more rapidly than transmission costs, so it is essential to undertake local processing and storage, and transmit only refined data.

## 5. The Competitive Environment

Railways face an increasingly harsh and competitive environment. The increase in real price of primary energy has led to a slowing down or reversal of economic growth. On the other hand, the pace of technical change has perceptibly increased, and it is not clear which of these forces will dominate. In any event, mankind will opt for some kind of 'growth'.

The big cities, such as London, grew because they were convenient points for physical transfer. Later they grew as the 'Headquarters' towns of Government, administration and business. Much business still depends on personal contacts, but nevertheless with cheap and easy communications these functions are now tending to diminish. Recent surveys, moreover, have tended to show that many people do not like to live in very large cities, or, at the other extreme, in rural isolation.

The gradual and now familiar convergence of electronics, data-processing and telecommunications into 'telemates', is producing far-reaching changes in banking and retailing, to name only two activities. The implication could be that traveling to shops could be levered, and that some new means of delivering may be devised. The convergence into telecommunications seems in turn, to be producing a further convergence - telecommunications, information processing, electronic type-setting, letter sorting, publishing and broadcasting. The future of the printed book (though not its method of production) is probably secure - but what of newspapers and their physical carriage by rail?

The advent of new communication technologies and automation in manufacture will probably lead the 'post-industrial' nations to move to concentrated, capital-intensive production of many goods, but at the same time the population may expect more decentralised living and working conditions - an agrarian society in an automated world. A national economy might come to be divided into large, productive manufacturing and commercial services; a labour-intensive service sector; small-scale entrepreneurial businesses; and perhaps a revived small-neighbourhood sector, possibly with 'craft' industries and activities, some part-time.

The first implication for railways is that many of their traditional bulk traffics (coal, steel, cement...) are likely to decline irreversibly. In surveying the strategy for the future, it is vital to distinguish between trade cycles (boom and slump) and longer-term, irreversible structural changes in society. The second implication is that the daily tidal flow of commuters into the big cities will gradually subside. Since such intensive peak services are very difficult to provide economically, their foreseen and gradual reduction should be financially beneficial. Modern telecommunications will probably reduce not only travel to work, but also travel during work, to business meetings.

Developments in technology, coupled with the upward squeeze on energy prices, are producing some remarkable changes in rail's physical competitors – road and air. The capital cost per seat of a car of given size is now, in real terms, about two thirds that of 1955. This has been achieved, partly through economics of scale (mass-production) despite greatly improved steering, road holding, comfort and safety generally, in line with people's continually rising expectations.

A revolution in automobile technology is taking place as electronic devices and microprocessors supplant mechanical and electromechanical equipment. Apart from automation of design and manufacture, this change is taking place in sophisticated systems of engine control, to improve power, reduce fuel consumption and exhaust emissions; in braking systems to control skidding;

and in display systems providing the driver with a variety of new diagnostic information.

The capital cost per seat of most aircraft has also fallen, again to about two-thirds of the 1955 cost in real terms. The operating cost per seat km has also fallen dramatically, apart from fuel costs. This is partly as a consequence of larger aircraft, partly because higher speeds and minimum-maintenance jet engines have permitted much larger annual distances to be flown by each aircraft.

In contrast, the capital cost per seat of the average later City rail trailer car has gone up by a factor of between two and three over the same timescale. This is because most of the world's railways have made massive strides in speed and comfort, riding, sound insulation, air-conditioning, etc., but have not been able to take full advantage of the economies of scale of their competitors.

## Energy Costs

For conveying freight, it is generally accepted that the lower rolling resistance of a train, and its lower aerodynamic drag, produce an energy advantage of about 2:1, comparing rail with road. For heavy bulk freight by train, the energy consumption\* varies from about 0.25 to 1.0 MJ tonne-km with an average of perhaps 0.6 MJ tonne-km, for containerised freight it is in the region of 1 to 1.5 MJ/tonne-km, whereas the figure for road haulage is in the region of 2.4.

In the case of passenger trains, however, the energy advantage is much less clear cut, as shown in Table 1. In making comparisons, to be as objective as possible, energy use is expressed per seat-km out per passenger-km, so that assumptions about load factor need to be made. In comparing the example, intercity bus with train, both the average space per passenger and the average speed are less. On a comparable basis, the 2:1 advantage of the bus would be reversed, and this ignores the further advantages of train safety and amenity.

Nevertheless, the competitive message is clear - and it must be emphasised that the car makers of the world are predicting a nalving of fuel consumption by the end of the century, without too much loss of internal space or performance. The makers of road freight vehicles and of aircraft are also both, independently, claiming about 30% increase in fuel efficiency in the same timescale. Driven by these pressures, the so called 'world-car' has almost arrived. There is little sign so far of the world-train, but more specialization amongst the world's railway manufacturers would help to reduce unit costs.

## 6. The Software Revolution on the Railways

Let us turn to some of the beneficial changes in operation and management of railways which are becoming possible through technological and other innovations.

The automatic provision of information to passengers, both of the planned timetable and any deviations from it, is now a well-established practice. There are two significant current developments which may influence future systems. The first is synthetic speech, based originally on digital recording and reproduction of human speech. The second is that of large-area solid-state display systems. Both liquid crystal displays and electroluminescent panels are possibilities. In the longer term these offer the prospect of bright, multicolour displays of high intensity and long life. Thus, they should, when fully developed, avoid the reliability and maintenance problems associated with rotating-flap and movable-mosaic electro-mechanical displays.

A modern ticket issue machine which has to work in a rather harsh environment, needs most of the following features: an ability to issue tickets for all types and class of travel between all points on the network, flexibility to respond without long lead times to particular marketing needs (e.g. a special cheap fare scheme designed to fill capacity on a particular route or time-period); the ability to be programmed remotely from a central source when, for example, fare-levels change; the ability to be interrogated remotely from a central computer, to provide frequent, and high integrity management information concerning the transactions it has produced; the ability to accept credit cards, certainly at principal stations; the ability to be at least compatible with automatic revenue control schemes, with seat reservation systems, and with information systems. Such a ticketing system produces significant benefits, by freeing staff for more productive work, including face-to-face contacts with customers. Printing costs are reduced and there is a basis for a much more flexible market environment. Many of the above requirements are also needed for a portable ticket issue machine for use in trains. A similar kind of technology can be used for the routing and destination of parcels. The advent of the microprocessor has made such systems practicable, by providing the essential requirements of low volume, low power consumption, and extremely high reliability.

The British Telecom viewdata system, known as Prestel, has become a very useful service to the travel trade in the U.K. with approximately 1600 retail outlets being equipped. Most of the main transport operators and tour firms in the U.K. are now information providers to the System. Developments planned for the System include a Train Service Data Base and a greater amount of 'real time' information through a gateway facility. This could add considerably to its domestic potential, by enabling the customer to make a complete transaction from home.

## 7. New Signalling and Control Technologies

The most important role of railway signalling is to ensure that trains reach their correct destinations safely and on time. The “view” of the signalmen and controllers has gradually widened, permitting the setting up of routes over long distances, and the monitoring and control of a larger and larger pattern of trains. All of this has been accomplished by what might be called a track intensive system of control. However, the possibility of a new phase is now opening up, with every train in direct contact with a control centre, its performance being compared with that of real-time simulation of the whole system. Optimization of the task of driving, either in an advisory or supervisory way, is possible to maintain timetabled movements with minimum conflicts and energy consumption. On a very busy commuter line, for example, energy savings in the region of 20–30% might be possible.

Present-day interlocking for safety purposes is achieved by electro-mechanical relays, which are reliable and 'fail-safe'. The use of solid-state interlocking with no moving parts is being seriously considered, because of its expectation of substantial cost reductions. The basis of the system is similar conceptually to that used in aircraft automatic landing, with three identical systems acting in concert. A major effort is now being made to prove the safety, integrity and reliability of both hardware and software. Solid-state interlocking will not alter the appearance or performance of the equipment, as perceived either by signalman or driver and it is fully compatible with current signalling and operating practices.

British Rail (B.R.) has been active for many years in the use of computer-based optimization techniques to improve the running and regulation of trains. Work is now in progress on B.R. to install automatic route-setting in 1984 as an integral part of the Brighton line resignalling. Such a scheme is capable of being developed to provide computer-based interlocking, and dynamic schedaling of trains. High integrity radio signalling, already used on low-density rural lines, is a logical development for major lines if it can be proved cost effective.

CAPT (Control-Advanced Passenger Train) is a system of transponders placed along the track at intervals of about 1 km, based on solid state technology. They are entirely passive devices interrogated by a train passing over, and reflecting a digitally-coded radio message. This is identified (using fail-safe logic as in solid-state interlocking) as the driver's authority to exceed the normal line-speed e.g. on curves where APT is allowed to do so. The principles in CAPT can, of course, be extended to convey other information from the track to the train.

The train describer was originally introduced into signal boxes to enable the identity of a train to be passed automatically 'down the line' from signal box to signal box, to aid the signalman. Train describers and their associated computer logic have now been expanded to interface with timetable storage, platform announcements and automatic indicators. On the Southern Region of British Railways in particular, with its intensive train service, this concept has been developed to give automatic train reporting by exception, printing out only those trains not within two minutes of right time, and setting wayside indicators in accordance with the running of trains.

## 8. Automation in Manufacture and Testing

The current revolution in electronics and information handling and processing will contribute greatly to the intelligence of automatic machines and robots. As these techniques gain ground in the competitive automotive industry, the greater will be the spur to the railways to reduce their own costs. The use of simple programmed industrial robots already permits the automation of many potentially dangerous or unpleasant jobs. Examples are the heavy cleaning of the undersides of vehicles such as locomotives before maintenance and repair; shot-blasting of castings; and the painting of vehicles, permitting the use of materials such as polyurethanes.

British Rail Engineering Limited has recently invested in a modern Computerized Numerical Control (CNC) bogie production line, followed by inspection on a CNC automatic inspection machine, and finally robotic paint spraying. Further possibilities are being examined for the use of robots to weld bogie frame sections utilising optical sensing of the weld to provide closed-loop feedback information to the robot on its path and position.

Moves towards the automatic factory bring about two very different but extremely difficult problems. The first is the financial justification of the investment required -- typically at least ten times that of a like-for-like machine-tool replacement. The second is that of recognizing, and allowing for, the human interface in the displacement of people and the acquisition of new skills.

Allied to automation in manufacture is the use of automatic test equipment to check whether new or repaired equipment is performing to specification. It has made its name in the defence field for testing complex avionic equipment, and is used in the automotive industry for testing gearbox and transmission units. Its acceptance into the railway industry probably depends on a considerable reduction in vehicle-types, and 'parts-count' per vehicle.

## 9. The Software Revolution in Civil Engineering

The requirements and constraints which need to be built into the planning of track maintenance and renewal can now be formalized into computer programmes. Computer-aided design of bridge structures is now also widely used. More recently, mini and micro-computers have been used to help measure two important aspects of track quality. The geometric parameters of the track can be measured, with optical sensors, at up to 200 km/h, with real-time, on board processing of data giving an instant print-out of track quality.

The material quality of the track can be similarly measured, using ultrasonic techniques, but the fluid coupling needed between probe and rail limits the speed to about 70 km/h. Most flaws can be compared, in real time, with standard defects using specially developed software algorithms. Bolt-hole flaws, however, need to be stored for later analysis.

The measurement of clearances between vehicles and fixed structures, such as tunnels, can also be undertaken in real time using laser probes and micro-processing.

## 10. Automation on the Train

The locomotive offers many opportunities for automation, to assist or replace the human function. Reduction in energy use is one obvious benefit.

Much time is currently spent in preparing a loco for its journey, and again in shut-down afterwards. By contrast, a road driver presses the starter-button and drives away! A rail driver's manual may run to about thirty pages of complex procedures, and there are further restrictions, for example, about special wagons on the train, or speed restrictions en route. In principle, the 'smart' locomotive could be provided with information for the driver, direct from a data base, in the form of a written schedule containing only the information needed for his particular train and journey.

## 11. Energy Control

Space-heating can benefit from intelligent control, which produces tomorrow's heating load on the basis of today's programme, suitably modified by changes in external conditions and internal utilization. Computer programmes are also available to ensure that a structure is designed and sited for minimum cost of ownership, with respect to construction cost and capitalized energy and maintenance costs. Railways, with their own high-capacity communication networks, should be able to impose centralized control on their use of energy and especially electrical Maximum Demand. The same general energy-management principles are applicable to the trains themselves.

## 12. Office Systems

Railways grew in the 19th century, and at that time were organized along 'military' lines, as there was no other model for such large and complex businesses, dominated by a concern for safety of staff and customers. Now, the software revolution offers an entirely new way to address the problems of organization and management of a large business system.

Until very recently, office productivity had stagnated, while manufacturing productivity had advanced dramatically. The typical factory employee has about ten times as much capital equipment vested in him as the typical office worker. Again, until very recently, office automation has tended to duplicate existing work processes and practices, "automating the quill pen"!

The computing fraternity has had insufficient awareness of business needs. Now that we know what is technically possible, the next phase of office automation will need to look critically at managerial productivity, using less people but bringing them together much more effectively - making a large system look like a small one. A healthy information flow should be second only to a healthy cash flow!

At present, there are no fully agreed standards, so choosing suitable equipment is a difficult task in a fast-changing scene. It is probably best to start with a small pilot scheme, ensuring, if possible, that it is upwards-compatible and part of a wider strategy.

## 13. Organization

This question of strategy is the key point, for we are not talking about a typist's or even a manager's productivity, but about a new managerial way of life. Railways are still largely labour-intensive, but with coming developments in information technology there should be a need for less layers of management, and traditional departmental boundaries will need to be re-drawn. The road to the electronic office and its full implications is thus likely to be strewn with many problems, both human and technical. Senior managers need the right amount and nature of information, so that, in developing an information strategy, the most important problems to be faced have to do with management style and culture, and only secondly with data processing and transmission.

There are many aspects to consider at the more individual level, not least the way people relate to one another in the 'social office'.

## 14. The Need for Change

Both the hardware and software of information technology are changing rapidly, and permeating all aspects of life and business. On the whole, railways' competitors, perhaps being smaller, have been able to change relatively quickly. Railways, too, must accept that the competitive and market environment will change and that they must adapt and change – at a time when most are pre-occupied by cash-flow and investment problems.

In the main, the road and air competition have been able to meet people's rising expectations, but the problem for railways, with their generally limited market share, has proved much more difficult. Yet their own rights of way and systems of guidance and control provide a basis for the application of modern technology, with, in electronics, diminishing real costs.

Human nature is slow to change, and so the technological shock produced by the software revolution can produce defensive reactions more difficult to deal with than the impact of technology itself. The management of change, at both an individual and an institutional level, will become a primary task.

In addition to these major changes in scale and style of organization and management, it is possible to see some more specific effects. Much less human labour will be needed in areas such as time-taoling, financial analysis, vehicle-design and manufacture. On the other hand, there will be a need for a higher general level of skills: system analysts, software specialists, electronic technicians, and above all, managers with interpretive skills in the analysis of data, and the ability to take the required action.

The speedy access to a wide range of information should enable a railway to be operated in a much more flexible, demand-sensitive manner, and also able to use its data base to plan the utilization of its resources of people and equipment in the most effective way.

In engineering, the reduction in cost of ownership of all assets - track and signalling as well as vehicles - is very important. To the extent that this reduces complexity of equipment, there should be beneficial effects on system reliability.

## 15. Marketing

The changes in external environment discussed earlier are likely to lead to major changes in railways' market opportunities, both freight and passenger. The 'good' commodities for railways – the heavy bulk traffics – will in many cases decline in total amount and market share. The nature of passenger traffic will change too, with some loss of business and commuter traffic, but the possibility of capture of more leisure traffic. A more "user-friendly" environment in information for, and attitudes to, the casual customer is thus vital, and ticket price may be more important than speed or space. The natural advantages of railways for stress-free travel need to be explained and exploited. Internationally, railways need higher and more uniform standards of customer care.

## 16. Conclusion

From their earliest days, railways were innovators, managerially and technically, generating new levels of safety and service. Now, they need these qualities more than ever, to manage change so that they can continue to prosper. These changes needed now are more subtle, but all-pervasive. The emphasis has to be on software in its widest sense, in customer care and managerial productivity, as well as that of the physical assets. Above all, it has to be concerned with human relations, to broaden the vision, enhance the skills, provide an atmosphere of enthusiasm and encourage creativity among the employees. The highest ideal is to aim for the human use of human beings. Managers must be concerned, not with how to react to change, but, much more positively, how to generate the will to seek change - to seek to emerge slimmer, tougher, but more responsive.
