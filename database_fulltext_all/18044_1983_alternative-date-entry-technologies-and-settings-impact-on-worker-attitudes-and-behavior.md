# Alternative Date Entry Technologies and Settings: Impact on Worker Attitudes and Behavior

Blake Ives

Dartmouth College\*, Nathan Smith Building, Hanover, NH 03755, USA

and

Norman L. Chervany

Director, Executive Development Program, BA Tower, University of Minnesota, 269-19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

Changes in technology and the movement of key entry functions into user areas are altering the traditional work environment. These changes can effect worker attitudes and associated behaviors, such as absenteeism and productivity. This study examines the effect of differences in key entry settings and technologies on perceived job characteristics and worker attitudes and behaviors. The results are based upon a study of 350 operators working in 87 key entry environments drawn from 29 organizations. It is found that new technologies and the movement of key entry into user areas affect perceived task characteristics, including task significance, autonomy, and feedback from the job. Job satisfaction, worker motivation, absenteeism, and productivity are also influenced (but to a lesser degree) by changes in key entry technology and setting. The implications of the findings for the design of key entry equipment and jobs are discussed. The conclusion is drawn that designers of key entry technologies and environments will benefit from a knowledge of the dimensions comprising meaningful work.

Keywords: absenteeism, attitudes, data entry, job characteristics, job design, job satisfaction, key entry, motivation, productivity, task design.

The changes can be organized into two categories: (1) the key entry function which is being moved closer to the data source (decentralization), and (2) key entry activities which are being automated (technological change).

## 1.1. Decentralized Key Entry

Organizations with data processing facilities have traditionally located most key entry personnel within the data processing department. The major reasons for centralizing key entry include:

1. Keypunches are noisy; they are a disturbing influence on other people.

2. Operators need considerable special training; economics of scale are possible with centralized facilities.

3. Centralized repair is convenient and faster; key entry systems tend to break down frequently.

4. Centralization generally minimizes the time to transport cards to a central computer.

5. Centralization permits efficient utilization of relatively high-cost equipment and people.

The term “decentralized” or “distributed” is applied to key entry personnel located outside the data processing department. In recent years, organizations have begun to decentralize key entry operations. The potential benefits (see, for example, [16]) include:

1. Users retaining control of their data.

2. Users assuming responsibility for their data.

3. Immediate error detection (often at the time of creation) and correction.

4. Reduction of long processing delays.

5. Minimization of redundant handling of data.

Recent advances in key entry technology have begun to make decentralization a reality [21]. This new equipment is reliable and relatively easy to operate; user personnel can be quickly trained. Additionally, the relative costs of people and machines are changing. Many organizations now see computer terminals in the same light as typewriters: it is no longer necessary to utilize it 100 per cent of the time. Instead, the focus is on efficient use of labor. Furthermore, because of training and/or experience in computer applications, user managers are more receptive to computer equipment in their departments.

## 1.2. Technological Change in Data Entry

Between the development of the computer card in the late 19th century and the modernization of the early 1960's, there was little change in key entry technology. While internal computing power of computer systems was increasing at massive rates, key entry technology was essentially dormant. The development of key-tape systems in 1965 led to radical restructuring of people's thinking about key entry. Today's systems often have a programmable microprocessor, often permitting sophisticated reformatting of data, complex error checking procedures, simple table look-up operations, and the collection of operator productivity statistics. The evidence suggests [3,4] that considerable gains in productivity can be realized when key-to-disk systems replace keypunches.

## 2. The Research Problem

The decision to decentralize key entry or to move up to a new level of technology is normally made after a thorough cost/benefit analysis. One important consideration is, however, often ignored: the key entry operators themselves may experience changes in attitudes and behavior as the result of new key entry technology or decentralization. The traditional key entry job typically is regarded as repetitions and dull. Each operator enters (unfamiliar) data; another verifies the work. Operators tend to be isolated from the system and normally are not permitted to make even the most obvious corrections. In general, absenteeism and turnover rates for personnel are high and operators' attitudes poor [8,13,14,23].

Gilb and Weinberg [9] describe the plight of the key entry operator and the attitude of the designers toward them:

To most systems designers, this cheap labor force is essentially invisible. Of the many books now on the market concerned with "computers and society", not one even mentions this frightening waste of humanity.

The move to more sophisticated technologies and to decentralized key entry permits an opportunity to enhance this demeaning job. But the new key entry jobs may be as bad or worse than the traditional ones they replace.

## 3. Research Framework

Numerous researchers have examined the impact of computer-related jobs on worker attitudes and behaviors [7,24,27]. In these studies, however, no general research framework has been used. Several theories model the general relationship between job characteristics and worker outcomes. The Hackman and Oldham [10] model was selected as the theoretical basis for the research.

This model hypothesizes that certain intrinsic characteristics of a job (e.g., variety, significance) influence worker attitude (e.g., satisfaction, motivation) and behaviors (e.g., turnover, absenteeism). Fig. 1 shows three “critical psychological states” – experienced meaningfulness, experience responsibility, and knowledge of results – these become “positive” and improve behavior and attitudes when a worker’s job provides the following “core job dimensions”:

1. Task Identity; the opportunity to complete a meaningful, whole piece of work.

2. Skill Variety; the opportunity to use a number of different skills on the job.

3. Task Significance; the opportunity to perform a job that affects the lives or work of other people.

4. Feedback from the Job; the degree to which the job provides direct information about the worker's success in performing the job.

5. Autonomy; the opportunity to make decisions relating to the process.

The model has been validated in a large field study [11]. Strong relationships have been found between the core job dimensions and most work outcomes. The model has been used in actual job design efforts [12,18] and in analyzing numerous occupations.

For this research, the model has been extended in Fig. 2. The core job dimensions are hypothesized to co-vary with one organizational setting variable (centralized versus decentralized) and one technology variable (sophisticated versus unsophisticated technology).

1. Key entry operators working in user departments will be more likely to have jobs characterized by work they consider to be (a) significant, (b) highly skilled, (c) clearly identifiable, and (d) providing a better knowledge of their results than those in a centralized key entry installation.

## 4. Research Design

The following section describes the research design of the study.

## 4.1. Classification of Key Entry Setting

The following classification was made:

1. Centralized key entry - operators physically located in a data processing department that serves two or more user departments.

2. Decentralized key entry - operators physically located in a user department outside the data processing department or within a data processing group serving only a single functional department of an organization.

In each organization in the study, this categorization key was made after discussions with at least two levels of management and an onsite visit.

## 4.2. Classification of Key Entry Technology

No precise boundaries exist between increasing levels of key entry sophistication. The study classified each system into one of three:

## 1. Unintelligent operator machine interface

Key entry equipment which cannot be programmed (except by use of a program drum card or similar "hard-wired" technique).

## 2. Semi-intelligent operator-machine interface

Including all key entry equipment which is software programmable or which is interfaced (either directly or via communications facilities) during key entry operations to some other programmable facility (e.g., a central computer system) - except it does not, however, include those systems described below.

3. Highly-intelligent operator-machine interface Including equipment meeting the requirements above, but also accessing and updating master files. Key entry operators working with online systems normally meet this requirement, though some online systems are more analogous to key-disk systems and permit no access to master file data.

This characterization was determined by interviews with the key entry supervisors and, if necessary, by observing the key entry function and questioning operators.

## 4.3. Dependent Measures

Worker perceptions of their job's characteristics were measured by the Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) of Hackman and Oldham [10]. This measures five "core job dimensions". Operator attitudes - general job satisfaction and the degree of internal motivation - are also measured. The validity of this measure has been extensively examined [6,11].

Worker absenteeism and productivity ratings were provided by supervisory personnel; absence severity included the number of days the worker had been absent in the previous six months, while attitudinal absences included the count of one-day absences. Huse and Taylor [15] found attitudinal absence to be a considerably more reliable measure than absence severity.

Worker effort was measured by a rating scale completed by the supervisor. This scale provides a perceptive-based measure of productivity. It was considered more appropriate than a behaviorally anchored measure (e.g., keystrokes per hour), because some differences between subjects would be directly traceable to the technology (e.g., a buffered keyboard is faster than a nonbuffered keyboard), while we were looking for productivity differences attributable to differences in the operators' perceptions of their jobs.

## 4.4. Subjects

Participants in the study included 350 key entry personnel working in 87 key entry environments drawn from 29 organizations in the Minneapolis-St. Paul metropolitan area. Subject organizations were selected by the following criteria.

1. The key entry facility must have existed in its current organizational location with current technology for at least six months.

2. Only key entry operators who had worked there for at least six months were included.

3. Operators must have spent at least half of their time working at a computer-related keyboard.

4. The majority of an operator's keying time must have been spent performing data input rather than inquiry.

Table 1 presents the systems studied. Four of the six cells have a similar number of systems reported, but the decentralized unintelligent and centralized highly-intelligent systems are sparsely populated. Because of the relative unimportance of these two key entry environments in the business community, they are not included in the analysis.

## 4.5. Data Collection Procedures

Key entry personnel were asked to complete the JDS form. The key entry operators were informed that participation was strictly voluntary and that their individual responses were to be held in the strictest confidence. A description was given of the double-blind procedure used to match the supervisors and operator responses; only two people elected not to complete the forms. In several cases, because the participating organization was unwilling to give up that much production time, the researcher randomly selected at least five operators.

## 4.6. Analysis

The statistical procedures used to analyze the research hypotheses are described briefly in Appendix 1. First, we determined if there were statistically significant differences in the dependent measures. For those for which a significant difference was found, we determined which cell-to-cell differences account for the variation. In this second step, three cell-to-cell comparisons were examined:

1. The centralized, unintelligent vs. centralized, semi-intelligent cells.

2. The decentralized semi-intelligent vs. the decentralized high-intelligent cells.

3. The centralized semi-intelligent vs. the decentralized semi-intelligent cells.

Comparisons 1 and 2 examine the hypotheses concerning the effect of key entry technology on job characteristics and worker outcomes. Comparison 3 examines the effect of centralization-decentralization on those same variables, but is limited to the semi-intelligent technology level.

The hypothesis that job characteristics would be more enriched in user departments received some support. As predicted, workers in decentralized (semi-intelligent) environments did find their work more significant. The other predicted differences in skill variety, task identity, and feedback from the job could not be proved. However, the autonomy variable showed very significant differences. Decentralization of semi-intelligent equipment seemed to result in workers perceiving more autonomy.

The second hypotheses predicted task characteristics to vary with key entry technology. Again some support is shown. The relationship, however, is complex. The autonomy variable showed differences between cells; the traditional keypunch environment allowed more autonomy than key-to-disk type technology; but online distributed systems also were typified by higher autonomy scores than were key-to-disk type distributed systems. Feedback from the job behaved more predictably – operators in centralized key-to-disk environments received more feedback than their keypunch associates.

The last two hypotheses, anticipating better worker outcomes with both decentralization and increasingly sophisticated technology, were not supported. Only total-day absenteeism showed a significant difference among cells. It seems unlikely, however, that these differences can be accounted for by differences in worker job satisfaction among cells. First of all, single-day absenteeism (i.e., attitudinal absenteeism), anticipated to be more susceptible to attitudinal variations, showed no significant differences. Furthermore, there seems to be little relationship between the differences in total-day absenteeism among cells and the general job satisfaction differences.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

Although the initial research hypotheses received limited support, the study provides considerable insight into the key entry job.

## 6.1. Utility of Hackman and Oldham Model

The fact that relatively minor differences were noted in worker outcomes between cells, despite fairly strong job characteristic variations, makes us question the use of job design models like Hackman and Oldham's for enriching key entry settings. If worker outcomes and behaviors do not vary with worker perceptions of task characteristics, then designers may not need to give these variables much attention; this argument runs counter to much prevalent literature [1,19,20,22]. Additional analysis, shown in Appendix 2, provides more insight. Although not statistically significant, this shows that our results are in the same direction as those in the original Hackman and Oldham study. To be sure, however, the relationships are weaker.

Several attractive explanations for the lower correlations can be postulated. First, women made up 95 percent of the sample. There is evidence to suggest [11] that the relationship between task characteristics and worker outcomes may be weaker for women than men. Secondly, the absolute variations in job characteristics is considerably less than that for the Hackman and Oldham sample. This may have reduced the strength of the relationship between job characteristics and worker outcomes. Finally, the job design model may be of limited utility in the design of key entry work; designers and supervisors may find that efforts spent on improving job characteristics [2.8,9,12,17,28] could be better spent elsewhere (e.g., better supervision, working conditions, social conditions, and pay) or in selecting more compatible workers. We believe, however, that systems designers with training in the fundamentals of job design could have avoided some of the “worst case” systems of our sample.

6.2 Implications of Job Characteristic Differences Found

In general, decentralization did not result in all of the improvements initially postulated. However, the key entry settings that were both most enriched and depressed were in user departments. In several cases, decentralized operators sat at their equipment eight hours a day, keying data for a single application. They had little contact with other workers in the user department. At the other extreme, operators were performing key entry activities in conjunction with their jobs (e.g., dealing with customers, maintaining control totals, validating and correcting data). The decentralized environment does appear to offer the most flexibility of job design.

Changes in technology produced more noticeable differences in job characteristics. The most interesting was the decrease in autonomy and increase in feedback as the focus went from traditional to the more sophisticated technologies. Apparently, some inverse relationship exists between the feedback and autonomy variables. Sophisticated key entry equipment can quite easily maintain very elaborate productivity statistics (e.g., keystrokes per hour, minutes active per hour, errors detected through verification). Although this feedback may go directly to the operator, it is normally filtered by the supervisor. Decreased worker autonomy seems to be an unfortunate consequence.

## 7. Conclusions

The use of the job design model of Hackman and Oldham helps in the redesign of present key entry jobs. In particular, it provides useful guidelines to designers and managers who wish to avoid creating poor worker attitudes about the system. Decentralization is not improving job characteristics as much as might be expected: the sample was limited to people keying over four hours a day and this may have biased the results towards an unfavorable conclusion. If most workers interface for only an hour or two a day, the results might be better.

State of the art key entry technology also seems to have done less to improve the key entry job than might be expected. Although feedback from the job has improved, it has done so at the expense of worker autonomy. The designers of future systems and equipment need to provide meaningful feedback to the person who can best use it.

## Additional Data Analysis

The relationship between task characteristics and worker outcomes is not as strong here as in other studies. Table 5 presents correlations between worker outcomes and task characteristics contrasted with these same correlations from a study done by Hackman and Oldham [11] using workers from a wide variety of occupations. The correlations for the current study are generally lower than those reported by Hackman and Oldham. This is particularly true for the behavioral measures where only "autonomy with rated productivity" is significant while Hackman and Oldham found seven out of ten to be significant. However, 16 of the 20 correlations in the present study are in the direction predicted by the model and many are significant.
