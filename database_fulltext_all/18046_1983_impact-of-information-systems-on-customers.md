# Impact of Information Systems on Customers

Vasant Raval

College of Business Administration, Creighton University Omaha, NE 68178, U.S.A.

The distinction between “users” of information systems of an organization and “customers” of the organization has not surfaced clearly in the literature on information systems planning and implementation. Also, the impact of the organization’s information system on its customers is neither assessed nor clarified. This paper seeks to expose and, to some extent, fill these gaps. Based on his experience, the author derives three classes of assumptions likely to be made by organizations in managing the interface with their customers. Possible implications of such assumptions are discussed, and suggestions for planning the interface are offered.

Keywords: Interface, customer interface, customer interface management, assumptions regarding customers, customers' role in systems design.

## 1. Introduction

Whereas a great deal of attention is given to users of information systems, very little is known about the impact of such information systems upon the customers (i.e., users of services or products) of the organizations controlling and operating such systems. This article provides an initial assessment of the issue and suggests a framework for examining and improving problem areas in the interface between the customer and the servicing organization.

Following clarification of the issue, decisions affecting the customer and their effect on the design of the servicing organization's information system are examined. The assumptions made by organizations about the effects of information system on their customers are discussed next, along with the implications of considering such assumptions during system analysis and design.

## 2. The Issue

It is important to note the distinction between the user of an information system and a customer of the organization. The customer is one to whom services are rendered or products sold by the organization that controls and operates the information system. In a university, for example, the student is a customer; the registration system is the information system, while the registrar's staff and other employees of the university are the users of information from the registration system.

An information system should facilitate servicing the customer. Organizations differ in their customer profiles. Some have a single, predominant, and more or less permanent customer. An example is feeder industries, where the organization supplies products or services to a large company such as General Motors. In such cases, the customer can be expected to have significant input in the design of the organization's information system. But here we exclude such cases and consider the more frequent cases where there is a large number of customers, each separately having very little influence on the organization; the customer base may be more or less constant (e.g., bank customers in a suburban branch) or may be changing over time (e.g., students in a university). Finally, the article primarily considers service-rendering organizations, although most of the discussion applies equally to product-selling organizations.

## 3. Role of Customers in System Design

The customers are seldom represented in the analysis and design of an organization's information system. This situation occurs for a variety of reasons. First, the customers may have a rapid turnover rate (e.g., students graduate and move on). Second, either the organization, the customer, or both tend to think that customers have no legitimate influence on the design of the organization's information system. Third, even where customer involvement in the design stage is desired, it may be difficult to obtain direct representation from such a group.

This lack of customer representation does not automatically imply absence of attention to their interest. In fact, many changes in information systems are made with a view to benefiting the customers. Air travel [4], banking [7], credit granting [3], and retail merchandising [5] are some of the industries where system revisions have benefited the customers. As an example, the Universal Product Code in the retail merchandising industry has produced customer benefits such as a detailed sale slip, greater accuracy in billing, time saving, and greater protection against error [1,5].

The cost of providing information that seemingly is useful to customers is borne by the organization and can be predicted fairly accurately. On the other hand, the determination of value to customers of any information supplied or withheld ordinarily requires the organization to make its own decisions. Since there is usually no formal representation of customers, errors in judgment are likely. Customers might consider some information valuable and therefore would like to have it supplied by the organization. For example, when the Universal Product Code was introduced, many retailers felt it was possible to remove price tickets from products, but this was based on the assumption (perhaps unrecognized) that such price tickets are not important to buyers. When buyers complained about their inability to comparisonshop in the absence of price tickets [1], the tickets were reinstated or the price was stated on the shelves rather than the products. In contrast, customer reaction is not predictable to useless but continually supplied information.

In addition to judging the value of information to users, the organization may have to make several other related assumptions. These include the preferred decision processes of customers, efficiency in service to customers, and their understanding of the information supplied or required.

## 4. Assumptions and Systems Consideration

There is little or no material available on ways an organization attempts to bridge the gap caused by lack of meaningful customer input. As noted previously, systems designers try to make reasonable assumptions. Here we present three classes of assumptions, with illustrations of each drawn from our knowledge of certain systems. For each class of assumption, information system considerations also are presented.

## 4.1. Decision Model of Customers

Assumptions are often made about the decision model of customers.

Illustration #1. A university registration system (Spring 1983 enrollment, 8200 students) required that the students submit a preregistration form indicating requested courses and class times. These forms were later batch processed. If the class requested by a student was full, the program assumed that taking requested courses is more important to the student than the class time--thus where multiple sections were available, the program found another section, perhaps at a different time. If this time conflicted with other class times, the procedure continued to ignore requested times and attempted to register the student in as many of the requested courses as possible.

The students were not aware of assumptions made (in the computer application) about their decision-making process. Nor was any effort made by the university to determine how the students search for courses and times.

Students were often shocked when informed about their registration in courses and times. Some found they had courses concentrated on two days of the week; a part-time, evening student might have been given day classes; a long-distance commuter might have been given courses over all five days. Consequently, there was much confusion and further work (by both students and university staff) following distribution of registration notices.

Illustration #2. A bank (total assets, Oct. 31, 1982, \$45,038 million) offers multiple accounts (checking, daily interest, monthly interest, and so forth) to a customer. When a customer has overdrawn the checking account, the bank assumes that the customer would prefer to transfer an amount from another account (say, daily interest account) to the checking account to keep the check from being “returned to drawer.” The bank assumes that the customer would not prefer the monetary and other costs of a check returned for lack of funds. The bank keeps transferring amounts needed from the other account as more checks arrive for payment, and each time the customer incurs service charges. Further, the bank assumes that the customer does not desire to transfer enough funds to maintain a minimum balance and thus avoid monthly service charges on the checking account, even though this might be the profile of the customer over the past several years.

An information system is essentially an allocation of tasks between man and machine [2, pp. 19–20]. Which gets the task depends upon the comparative advantage of one over the other [6]. If there is a single, predominant model, the task incorporating the model can be handled by machine. If, on the other hand, several competing models exist, it might be preferable to assign responsibility for the task to a person within the organization, or allow the customer to make the final decision by simply communicating pertinent information. However, this option might also be expected to increase the cost of servicing customers.

The allocation of task to customers is quite visible in the student registration systems of large universities where students personally assess classes and times available at the time they register and make their choices. Regardless of the final decision to allocate the task to man or machine, it is important for the organization to know at least the customers' decision models.

## 4.2. Efficiency in Customer Service

There is tacit assumption that changes made in providing services are more efficient and facilitate transactions for both the organization and the customer.

Illustration #3. A trust company (total assets, Dec. 31, 1982, \$9,242 million) changed the rule for determining maturity dates on one- to five-year term deposits. Instead of maturing at any desired date, the new rule provided that every deposit mature at the month-end of the depositor's month of birth. While this may be convenient and economical for recordkeeping function within the organization, it takes away considerable flexibility from the depositor.

Illustration #4. With the use of the Universal Product Code for customer billing, price ticket on a product seemed to be useless. With its removal, however, the ability of customers to comparison-shop was diminished.

The concern here often boils down to organization costs cutting. However, in entertaining such moves, the organization should carefully search for the consequent sacrifices of its customers. It is important to note that such changes may have market implications. Therefore, any cost reduction or other simplification should weigh the benefits to the organization (e.g., cost savings) against costs to the organization resulting from sacrifices by customers (e.g., loss of goodwill, loss of revenue).

4.3. Information Exchange between Organization and Its Customers

Assumption is made that information exchange between the organization and its customers is complete and well-organized.

Illustration #5. Several different air fares are available for the same trip with some planning and manipulation of travel times chosen. If the traveller does not have knowledge of available alternatives, an excessive fare may be paid – and the customer may find out from the person in the adjacent seat.

Illustration #6. A university distributes an “advisement” (prior to pre-registration) for the following term. Among other things, the report lists alternative courses which would equally satisfy a specified, mandatory degree requirement. The students are confused, since they have no knowledge of the implications of the alternatives on their programs, even though any one of them is acceptable for fulfillment of the requirement.

Illustration #7. It is assumed that customers know what information to supply. People who use a service that completes their income tax returns "while they wait" may lose because of this. The preparer works only with information supplied by customers and may not attempt to elicit any more than the basic information necessary to file a tax return. Consequently, errors such as not claiming certain deductions are likely.

Sometimes, a representative of an organization is surprised when the customer accidentally transmits some relevant information. The representative's response to such a situation may be, "Oh, I'm glad you mentioned that. Your case will require different treatment..."

Note that this class of assumptions differs from the preceding ones because this assumption entails matters of communication of information, as opposed to its processing (and perhaps communication) in the first two classes (4.1 and 4.2). Also, the first two classes are related when efficiency measures of the organization affect customer's information needs stemming from their decision processes.

If the knowledge levels of customers differ widely, it is appropriate to supply or receive information hierarchically (or in IF...THEN form) so that any interested customer can begin from that point where he/she would provide or obtain the incremental information. Sometimes legal constraints impose a requirement that all of the information must be communicated to the receiver, regardless of the receiver's knowledge level. Air travel safety procedures, for example, are given to passengers at the beginning of each leg of a flight.

To the extent that the responsibility to inform is not shouldered by the organization, the customer interest must be served in other ways. This has resulted, for example, in the birth of travel agencies – to ease travel decisions. Tax consultants also appear on the scene to serve the taxpayer who cannot, or does not have time to, understand the implications of information communicated or required by the Internal Revenue Service in the U.S.

## 5. Planning for System Interface with Customers

The organization should make a planned, conscious effort to consider its customers during the design of the information system interface. In the absence of empirical evidence, an analytical approach is used to suggest the possible routes to follow.

The planning begins with the best possible identification of the interface. In particular, careful analysis should be conducted of historical patterns of significant interactions between the organization and its customers. This is done to identify the nature and characteristics of major types of interactions and how these are managed by the organization. In the process, the nature and extent of involvement of the organization's information system should surface. Assumptions, if any, about customer behavior should be traced and analysis should be conducted on how these were selected and justified. In each case, an inventory of accepted and rejected assumptions should be maintained.

The study of future system modifications should include the identification and evaluation of possible impact on the customer interface. The input can be obtained in various ways: a manager may be asked to play the role of a customer, or suggestions from employees in contact with customers can be invited and analyzed. In addition, a planned collection and careful review and interpretation of customer complaints can help determine problems at the interface. An ideal course of action is, of course, to solicit direct representation of customers on the system design team--however, such representation may not always be feasible.

Any time a major modification affecting the interface is planned, a systematic program should be undertaken to familiarize customers with the implications of the change. The alternative to a planned effort is that the customer will have to learn how to “use” the system, or that the customer will ask (if he/she cares). Implicit in the first alternative, often chosen by default, is the idea that the first-time services are not crucial and that the repeat customer will learn by using the services from the organization repeatedly. The second alternative seems to encourage asking and learning by trial and error, which may increase costs in providing customer services. There is also a risk that the customer misses an important question, or a representative of the organization provides an incomplete answer.

Since each individual customer has very little influence on the perceived “undesirable” aspects, a concerted action on the part of customers might be warranted to achieve a change. In such cases, customers tend to form consumer groups, or else a consumer advocate is born.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

The absence of customer input in the design of an organization's information system does require the organization to make reasonable assumptions about the customers' decision making processes, the value to the customers of the information supplied, the knowledge level of the customer, and so on. To the extent that these assumptions deviate from reality, problems arise in providing satisfactory service. To minimize such deviation, organizations select one or more approaches to derive or modify these assumptions. The approach selected may range from complete disregard to a planned effort to learn about customer needs. Apparently, the selection of an approach maybe influenced by the extent to which an organization can afford to irritate its customers.

The evidence presented in this article is anecdotal, and therefore has limitations in terms of generalizing the conclusions. At a minimum, it is reasonable to expect that there is room for clarification as well as improvement. It is possible that organizations systematically manage (or even modify or manipulate) the interface, especially where it affects significantly the quality of service as perceived by their customers. The question of the interface management is fundamental and multidisciplinary. However, very little is researched in this area; even a systematic survey, analysis, and documentation of existing practice is lacking. Studies of the interface can be expected to provide significant insights to all parties concerned, including system designers, marketing managers, public relations managers, internal auditors, and customers.
