# Going outside for MIS Implementation

Michael A. Kole

Rider College, Lawrenceville, New Jersey, 08648, USA

Smaller businesses have limited experience with information systems and limited resources to spend on new developments. To overcome these limitations, they have begun using outside consultants and packaged software. This article proposes a strategy for MIS implementation based on the use of outside expertise. This strategy is directed at helping small businesses achieve effective and productive MIS in a fairly short time.

One firm's successful implementation of this strategy is used for discussion. This firm leveled their administrative workforce while continuing their significant increases in sales. Their information system was put in on time and within budget. It is being used by managers and clerks from all functions within the company.

Keywords: MIS, Implementation, Strategy, Small organizations, Consulting, Productivity, Field study, Change process, Packaged software, Performance.

## 1. Introduction

Effectiveness and productivity are the twin goals of Management Information Systems (MIS). MIS are installed to help improve managerial performance and to hold down growth in “the office”, i.e., of white collar workers. However, success is usually very gradual, taking longer and costing more than anyone planned. Often a by-product is growth of “white rabbits” as they are called by the President of one company. These are the support staff that seem to multiply in response to information available or needed.

The traditional approach has been to develop the MIS with in-house computer specialists, then having them implement the MIS. However, small businesses cannot afford the specialists, the investment or the time for the traditional approach. Decentralized units of larger firms object to using centralized MIS staff and can not or will not add computer specialists to their own staffs. The advent of small business computers and distributed data processing has made smaller businesses, and decentralized plants and offices, the new growth areas for MIS in the 1980's.

## 2. The Small Business Environment

All businesses have a strong need to improve management and office performance. Small as well as large businesses situations change quickly when information and knowledge play vital roles. Smaller businesses are set apart by their limited human and financial resources.

Managers of small businesses generally have limited experience with information systems and little time to become educated. These firms do not have systems specialists and they can not or will not want to add them. These specialists are scarce, expensive and are often more motivated by the technology than the business problems they are solving. Small businesses and business units want their MIS to be under their managers' control.

Smaller firms need to limit their financial commitments. They fear the unknown development costs and have read about large cost and time overruns. They are willing to capitalize or lease software as well as hardware when the costs are essentially known.

These small business conditions become even more important when the firm is growing rapidly. Faster growth means quicker and greater changing conditions, which increase management's need for better information. Thus the smaller rapidly growing firms, such as found in the high technology area, have some of the most pressing needs to implement new MIS, while minimizing costs, time, and staff expansion.

## 3. Field Study in one Specific Company

A small rapidly growing high technology company will be used to illustrate the proposed strategy and to show the tangible benefits that can be achieved. The author was the consultant to The Company for this project.

In mid-1979, The Company's sales had reached five million, having grown about $40\%$ per year over the prior three years. They produce state-of-the-art spectrometers. To stay ahead of competition they place a high priority on the rapid development and introduction of new products. They maintain a high level of customer service through short lead times from receipt of customer orders through shipment. They sell to businesses, governments and institutions through a worldwide variety of distribution channels.

In 1979, the President decided that the implementation of an MIS was necessary to support their continuing growth. They ordered a computer, an IBM/34, and hired a consultant to manage the implementation.

The Company was acquired by a much larger firm in 1980, about seven months into the project. They are now a subsidiary operating relatively independently.

## 4.1. Top Management Commitment and Participation

Top management commitment and participation is vital to success. The project should be a major goal of one key manager. This manager should have clearly defined overall responsibility, get involved at the start and see the project through implementation.

The President assumed this responsibility for The Company. His role and commitment were keys to the project's success. He provided guidance and direction while delegating specific responsibilities. He insured that goals and a strategy were developed and followed. He mediated disagreements, made decisions and conducted progress reviews as necessary to keep the project on target.

## 4.2. Outside Expertise

Smaller firms should seek outside assistance in project management and software selection. Independent consultants can provide the objective advice and project management that is needed. With contracts negotiated in advance, the costs are known and the firms have strong inducements to reach their goals on time. Divisions of larger firms can substitute corporate staff for the consultants, if necessary. The terms and relationships for using corporate staff should be similar to using outside consultants; e.g., negotiated contracts and charges for services rendered.

At The Company, the consultant's goals were those of top management, i.e., get the system in use in a timely fashion so it effectively supports the organization. The project's objective was overall company effectiveness. The consulting role focused on assistance and implementation, not design and development. The consultant introduced the new MIS and helped the users overcome their fear of failure and the unknown. The consultant guided the user's learning, change and acceptance of the technology while managing the project. Requirements were translated into codes, controls and functions so the MIS would perform as expected.

Important characteristics of consultants for such MIS implementation include:

a. Experience with a similar business function in a computer environment

b. Facility with communication and the change process

c. Flexibility and creativity in project management

## 4.3. Packaged Software

Packaged software should be used whenever possible. New programming should be minimized and delayed. Progress will come faster by using what has been developed and tested. Management will learn how to take advantage of the MIS capabilities and what new or changed features they need – then software changes can be made. A year before revising the packaged software is a good guideline.

The Company decided to use IBM/MAPICS software. Because MAPICS was widely used most of its bugs had been shaken out and there were other firms to contact if problems arose. The President decided that no programming would be done for the first year. When they became proficient and learned what additional features they really needed, then software changes could be made. This decision helped immensely in meeting the ambitious initial schedule.

## 4.4. Key Functions

The MIS should be built for key activities. It should focus on business functions, not technology. The MIS should provide management support while being integrated into the clerical and administrative functions.

The Company chose inventory control as their first application. Work on bill-of-material and order processing were to begin while inventory control was being converted to computer use. These applications were at the heart of the business. Leading in the development of new products, rapid response to customer needs and control of material kept The Company on its rapidly rising growth path. Management felt that computer assistance was necessary to enable them to maintain growth and profitability.

Implementation of inventory control, bill-of-material processing and order processing were scheduled seven and one-half months after project initiation. This was an ambitious schedule since all of these were manual activities and the computer would not be delivered for almost five months. A smaller computer was leased during the interim period to help with file development and conversion.

## 4.6. Implementation Process

After initial decision making and short term goals setting, attention must be directed toward positive project initiation. The consultant faces three immediate tasks when starting:

1. Selling himself or herself by establishing rapport and trust with all key people.

2. Helping to define the outputs and requirements of the system.

3. Building support and desire for the system among other managers.

At The Company, the President began this process by introducing the consultant to his staff at a meeting and expressing strong support for the project. All managers were given ample time for questions and discussion. The consultant continued this approach meeting individually with the key managers, and then with these managers and their staffs. The discussions ranged over requirements, needs, opinions and fears. A process of top down training in discussion oriented sessions was intermixed with detailed project definition.

After definition, a written plan was prepared, distributed and discussed. The plan was divided into modules. Specific tasks, names and dates were assigned to each module. From the beginning, these were the responsibilities of company employees, not the consultant. Employees in each department used a computer terminal to enter their data, do their own inquiries and generate their reports. The consultant coordinated the project, providing advice, training and assistance when needed.

Regular written progress reports kept the project on target and all concerned people informed. The reports were prepared by the consultant from information and commitments supplied by the people responsible for each task. The reports were brief, comparing progress with plans, noting accomplishments and delays. The reports helped management know when to hold meetings to investigate changes or delays, make plans or mediate problems. The brief written comparison with plans served as preludes to verbal discussions of significant issues.

## 5. Results

The MIS at The Company has helped improve effectiveness and productivity. Growth in support personnel and inventories have been modest while sales have continued their dramatic increases. The MIS cost/sales ratio is the lowest of any division of their Parent Company. The MIS has been integrated into the regular operations and control processes of the firm, and is used by managers and clerks from all functions. The information system was put in on time and within budget. Virtually all of the first year's goals were achieved. The President said "We couldn't have met our goals without this information system." These results in productivity, cost, use of the MIS, and progress against the plan are presented in more detail in the following subsections.

## 5.1. Productivity

Growth in the number of administrative employees at The Company leveled off dramatically after the implementation of the MIS. Administrative employees include all clerical and supervisory people in departments affected by the MIS: accounting, general administration, order entry and indirect manufacturing. Figure 1 illustrates this cost avoidance while showing the continued increase in sales. Managements' goals and efforts were the force behind this leveling. The MIS enabled the company to increase the productivity of its white collar personnel. Management feels that they will be able to continue increasing sales while avoiding significant increases in administrative personnel.

## 5.2. Costs

The cost of the entire MIS operation is running between 1% and 1 $\frac{1}{2}$ % of sales for The Company. This cost as a percent of sales is the lowest of any division of the Parent Company. Management is projecting a significant sales increase, but only a marginal MIS cost increase for its next fiscal year. They expect their cost/sales ratio to be 20–25% under the average of the other divisions of the Parent Company next year.

The Company could have to into their Parent Company's time sharing computer and used much of its information systems capability. The Company believes their MIS provides much better support than if it depended upon their Parent Company's system.

## 5.4. Progress Against the Plan

Progress can best be considered during three periods: the first seven months, through the end of the first year, and the start of the second year.

## 1. The First Seven Months

The end of the first seven months, June 30, 1980, was chosen because that was the goal for implementation of the first three applications, inventory control, bill-of-material processing and sales order processing. Inventory control met the goal, it was in use and working. Bill-of-material processing using chained product structure files was put into use during the seventh and eighth months. It took a few more months to shake out the bugs and learn to make it effective. Sales order processing proved more difficult because the packaged programs did not meet all of the Company's needs. Creative use of the contract price file permitted a number of price structures to be used. New salesmen's numbers were introduced to jointly identify sales order type and salesmen. But mostly it was the difficulty in cleaning up old and partial backorders that delayed full implementation for five months.

The first seven months was a period of significant change, surprise and halting progress. The personnel had to undergo a period of learning and dependency, then transition, finally acquiring knowledge and mastery of the new MIS. There was mixed resistance and acceptance during this period. The operator hired to enter the data and run the computer quit after four months. This reinforced the need for each department to enter their own data and the commands for report generation.

## 2. The End of the First Year

During the first year all of the original goals had been accomplished. The first three applications were being used as planned. Standard costing and invoicing were selected as the second set of functions to be implemented. Again it was decided to use standard software, IBM/MAPICS, for these applications. These were also implemented and being used by the end of the first year.

By the end of the first year most of the administrative employees had accepted the MIS. There was enthusiastic support by a few and reluctant use by some others. On the whole, The Company was using its computer information system for transaction processing and decision support.

## 3. The Start of the Second Year

The start of the second year saw the gradual withdrawal of the consultant. An MIS manager was hired and a full transition was made to self-reliance by company employees. The new MIS manager concentrated on analysis, new developments and advice to management. Production control, accounts receivable and order analysis were being implemented. Regular operations of data input and report generation were carried on by the users, not MIS personnel.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

The Company is using their MIS. They have avoided costs by holding down growth of their administrative employees while continuing to expand their sales. The cost of their MIS to their sales is among the lowest of any division of the Parent Company. They met their timetable, going from no computerization to a substantial computer based MIS in a year. Thus by several measures The Company has achieved a successful MIS. They did this by going outside for expertise and following the proposed non-developmental MIS implementation strategy.

The Company was one application of this strategy. Perhaps another strategy would have produced equal or superior results. Certainly the people played a major role in contributing to the system's results. In analyzing the implementation of real MIS, control over many factors is sacrificed. What is needed is to study the use of this strategy in many organizations

We are still in the exploratory phase of examining strategies, factors and conditions that will produce successful MIS. This is especially true in our smaller organizations and organizational units. This article attempts to show a way for small businesses to achieve desirable results when implementing MIS. By going outside for consultants to direct the implementation, by using packaged software, and by using a strategy such as the one presented, small organizations may have a way to successfully implement their MIS.
