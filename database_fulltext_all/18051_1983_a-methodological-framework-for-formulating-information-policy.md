# A Methodological Framework for Formulating Information Policy

Reuven Karni

Faculty of Industrial Engineering and Management.

Technion-Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa 32000, Israel

Effective operation, control and management of the information service within an organization are essential to its success. Nevertheless, many management decisions that effect this service are made by default, or through a play of forces, rather than in accordance with general management policy. This paper presents a framework for formulating an information policy in an organization. Its architecture is built up of four components: information policy goals, measures to determine how well these are achieved, policy actions and alternatives, and the information services system itself.

A list of policy actions, outlining the decisions which should be made by management, in cooperation with information service department and its users, is organized into an Information Policy Matrix. This provides a structured approach.

Keywords: Information Systems, Information Policy, Users, MIS Planning, Organizational Effects of Computing Systems, Management Decision-making.

## 1. Introduction

"Just as information systems are achieving recognition as one of the most powerful tools at the disposal of the modern manager, they are also becoming notorious as one of the most difficult tools" ... "the problems of information systems are caused primarily by improper or inadequate management" [3]. These words reflect the feelings that usually accompany "computerization" of an organization. Management finds it hard to control, whilst the organizational units struggle to find a means of communication, cooperation and coexistence with the information service department. This probably arises because:

1) The technology base is highly dynamic, bringing with it changes and adjustments in operating habits and system accessibility. This is not typical of other units.

2) Information systems are intended to support organizational processes rather than units [6], and thus cut across organizational boundaries, requiring more interdepartmental cooperation.

3) The "job mix" of the information service department encompasses repetitive operation, the development of new applications, and the need to respond to extraordinary requests. This leads to interactions which are not typical.

"In fact, the EDP function in a company is almost like a company within a company. It is a highly complex, technically-oriented function ... that operates both a job shop and a continuous production shop. It provides services to all segments of the organization within which it resides" [5].

Some of the problems are due to a conflict with the traditional method of organizational problem solving, fragmented along departmental lines, and solved in each department according to its own professional methodologies. This “professionality viewpoint” approach to problem solving is implicit in the way the operation, control and management of information systems are achieved; many of the problems are technical in nature, and must be solved within the information service department. However, it is clear that a large number of decisions made (by default) in the information service department are truly the concern of the organization-as-a-whole. These decisions must be made by upper management, and expressed as its “Information Policy”. Without a clearly enunciated information policy, management will not be able to control its information services.

Fried [5] quotes a late 1970's Diebold Research Program report “[which] indicates that top US corporations are groping in the dark because of a lack of an overall information policy. Nearly 84 percent of such firms … indicated such a lack, and they are only now beginning to get an inkling that such a policy is necessary. Only 26 percent … had a central group or individual responsible for information policy”.

This paper is concerned with describing a formalized methodological framework for formulating information policy. Many of the concepts derive from research that has been carried out in the field of Energy Policy (see [1,2,8]).

## 2. Methodological Basis

The proposed methodology or information policy is built up of four components:

1) A consistent set of definitions which provide a basis for its analysis

2) A framework for describing and classifying the areas in which it is to be made

3) A procedure for specifying the decisions to be made in each area

4) A procedure for evaluating the impacts of the decisions so that the correct decisions will be taken

The set of basic definitions is given in Appendix 1. We now focus more sharply on the Information

Policy issue:

1) The Information Issue. Users in organizations require data as one of the inputs into the many functions that they carry out. This data must be delivered as required by each particular function; the information service system must be operated, controlled and managed towards this end.

2) Information Policy. Information policy involves a series of statements made at the organizational level, directed towards the required functioning of information services within the organization.

3) Information Policy Areas. The set of decisions compromising information policy may be subdivided according to areas which facilitate the decision making process. Within each area, several alternatives may be possible, the alternatives adopted constitute the information policy for that area.

4) Information Policy Measures. Each information policy action is intended to have some impact on the functioning of information services and systems. The effects must be capable of being measured. By means of these criteria, alternatives can be evaluated; those adopted must be consistent with that balance of benefits and costs considered appropriate by management.

## 3. The Information Policy Matrix

As a first step, we define those areas in which decisions are to be made at the organizational level. In a previous paper [7] the author has shown that a Nadler Systems Matrix [12,13,14], originally developed as a tool for defining a “system”, may be profitably utilized as a basis for an extended definition of management information. In such a matrix, the rows delineate the fundamental components, or elements, whilst the columns portray the attributes, or dimensions, of the subject being defined. As the components of information policy should be related to the structure and characteristics of information systems, an appropriate INFORMATION POLICY MATRIX provides a framework for incorporating and arranging all policy areas relevant to the information issue. As information services constitute a system, the elements of Nadler's original matrix give a satisfactory breakdown of information policy elements. These elements are defined in Table 1(a).

## 4. Policy Actions and Alternatives and an Example

The next step is to detail the actions, alternatives and measures for each policy area, and relate these to the information service components required for implementation. This is carried out in two stages: drawing up a list of actions that have to be taken, and then developing feasible alternatives that could be adopted. It is important that the opinions of all those involved in each specific policy area should be taken into account before decisions are made; this makes it more likely that cooperation is achieved.

We illustrate the process in the area of providing hard copy reports to usesrs. This overlaps several areas in the output/operations and output/development cells.

The actions to be taken encompass how reports are to be requested, how their content is to be defined, and how the results are to reach the user. These actions, and some alternatives, are given in Appendix 3(a). The policy alternatives is dictated by management attitudes to free user access to data, and to the resources to be allocated for this.

The alternatives incorporated into the overall policy must exhibit internal consistency.

The information service system components involved include report request procedures, output distribution procedures, processing schedules, application and report generation software, information services personnel, end-user or centralized hardware, and the information resources directory.

Policy alternatives may be measured against three objectives: to provide good data, as fast as possible, and as cheaply as possible. These may be evaluated by measures such as availability, quality and efficiency. These goals are measures are detailed in Appendix 3(b).

## 5. Policy Analysis Architecture

All these aspects may be consolidated in a methodological scheme - the "Policy Analysis Architecture" of Fig. 2. This groups together the four constituents: goals, measures, actions/alternatives and the system. Each constituent participates in two of the four sub-matrices. This matrix is similar to the business/systems relationship matrices used by IBM in its Business Systems Planning approach [6].

Policy analysis in matrix form has several advantages:

## 6. Case Study

The Paz Oil Company is the largest supplier of petroleum products in Israel. At present, the information processing resources of the company are rather modest, in line with current policy of restrained development of application systems. In view of increasing internal pressure for expanded services, a survey of information needs and resource requirements was commissioned. The result was a farreaching proposal (i) for extensive computerization of clerical activities (with some decision support) within the company; (ii) for an increased rate of development activity, utilizing contracted manpower if necessary, to make this possible in two years; and (iii) for the establishment of a powerful centralized computing facility with full telecommunication links to all locations branch offices, fuel storage and distribution depots, and manufacturing plants. The proposal thus represented a complete departure from current policy, and its feasibility was questioned by many departments. In order to focus attention on the issues, and to obtain explicit decisions from management before proceeding with the design and implementation of the recommended plan, a list of eleven policy actions and alternatives was drawn up (Table 2(a)) and submitted to management. A parallel list of impacts (Table 2(b)) was prepared in order to portray the likely effects. The preferences expressed by management [M], the authors of the survey [S], and the information service department [I] are indicated alongside each policy alternative. The results are highly instructive:

1) A clear mandate was given for accelerated development of transactional systems

2) Management agrees to decouple the central and peripheral work regimes, except for some priority at the branch offices

Management has, however, not accepted all the recommendations; and, more significantly, a divergence of opinion between management and the information service department has surfaced. A reorientation will be required in order to carry out the plan in the light of this new policy.

## 7. Conclusions

This paper has described a method for defining an information policy. It has, as a cornerstone, the principle that conscious and deliberate decisions must be made at upper management levels on the operation, control and management of corporate information services. Moreover, a series of alternative decisions should be considered (and reviewed and adapted over time), rather than the enforcement of hard-and-fast rules.

Traditionally, decisions on information processing have been regarded as technical in nature, and thus best left to information service personnel (or even to inter-group power conflicts). In contradistinction, the development of an explicit policy architecture makes it possible to consider each aspect of information services from a management, user and technical point of view, thus selecting that course of action most appropriate for achieving the goals stated. Furthermore, policy can be developed by canvassing opinions from those involved in providing and receiving information service, thus encouraging cooperation and creativity.

Finally, this paper has been oriented towards the information service viewpoint. It is probably more valuable to management if the policy areas – and related actions and alternatives – were grouped according to its viewpoint. It is the responsibility of policymaking bodies within the organization to ensure that policies and policy alternatives are arranged and presented in as effective manner as possible – after all the analytical groundwork has been carried out. Moreover, information policy must be considered within the broader context of organizational policy, so that organizational and information goals are closely linked.

## Appendix 1

Basic definitions for information policy analysis

1) ISSUE. An issue is a manifestation in the real world which leads to a situation in which some reaction is required and some decisions must be taken.

2) OBJECTIVE, GOAL. An objective is an aspiration or ideal, expected to be achieved or maintained in the long run. A

goal is a quantified (or qualified) expression of the desired level and timing of the attainment of a specific objective.

3) POLICY ACTION. A policy action is a specific activity directed to manipulating one or more aspects of the (information services) system - or any other related system - with the intention of stabilizing or modifying the system.

4) POLICY ACTION ALTERNATIVE. A policy action alternative is an alternative method by which a policy action may be carried out

5) POLICY. A policy is a combination of one or more policy actions directed towards the achievement or maintenance of a specified set of goals or objectives.

6) POLICY ALTERNATIVE. A policy alternative is a set of policy action alternatives, directed towards the achievement or maintenance of the same set of goals or objectives.

7) MEASURE (CRITERION). A measure is a quantitative or qualitative characteristic or effect resulting from the implementation of a given policy action alternative within a given policy alternative.

8) POLICY AREA. A policy area encompasses a related set of policy actions about which decision; can possibly - but not necessarily - be taken independently of decisions taken in other areas.

9) INFORMATION SERVICES. Information services (or the information service system) is the ensemble of inputs, outputs, processes, organization, hardv are, software and data banks dedicated to the provision of information as input to those organizational functions (or, information-related services) requiring information as an input.
