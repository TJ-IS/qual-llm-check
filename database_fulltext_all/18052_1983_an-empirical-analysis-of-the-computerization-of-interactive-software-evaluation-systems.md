# An Empirical Analysis of the Computerization of Interactive Software Evaluation Systems

Department of Management Science and Computer Information Systems, University of Miami, Coral Gables, GL 33124, USA (Tel. 305-284-6595)

This research studies factor analysis of traditional survey-questionnaires for interactive software evaluation in order to construct a Principal Factor Conversion Function (PFCF). Such a PFCF may be used in several ways: first, for exploratory purposes, to discover the principal factors of the traditional questionnaire; second to test the potential of data reduction within significant loss of information; and third to compare the traditional manual evaluation to a condensed computerized interactive software evaluation. A CAI system was used as the software to be evaluated in this research.

Keywords: Empirical analysis, computerization, interactive software evaluation, computer users, factor analysis, computer assisted instruction, data collection, business electronic data processing, BASIC programming, data reduction, screen design, software clarity, instructional innovation, user-perceived quality, user involvement, user evaluation of software, software management

Because of the decline in hardware costs coupled with substantial rise in software costs and the rapid proliferation of computerized systems, the evaluation of software maintenance, modification, and upgrading costs becomes increasingly necessary for the viability of the entire organization.

A survey-questionnaire is the traditional method used to provide input for software evaluation, especially for larger software producers and computer user organizations such as IT&T, IBM, CDC, UNIVAC, DEC, HP, etc. The research discussed here develops and examines the feasibility of switching from a traditional, manual survey-questionnaire to a condensed, but comparable and consistent, computerized interactive software evaluation technique.

Here, the perceived “usefulness” of software is estimated by the end users. Swanson [23], Gallagher [15] and Zmud [24] argue that the user perception of information has four basic dimensions:

1. its significance, usefulness, or helpfulness;

2. its accuracy, factualness, and timeliness;

3. its quality of format or physical representation and reliability;

4. its meaningfulness or reasonableness.

These four may be reduced to two primary dimensions as applied to the usefulness of financial information [7,14,15]. They are:

1. usefulness (including characteristics in the above first pair of dimensions);

2. clarity (including the content of the second pair).

Some inadequacies can be identified in prior research: one results from attempting to measure usefulness with only one item. This, as suggested by Nunnally [5], does not provide sufficient domain sampling for a complex construct, and therefore tends to have a very low reliability; our study uses multiple items in order to provide sufficient domain sampling. Another weakness concerns the lack of validity testing; in our study, the correlation between the attitude items and three measures of performance are used to determine the validity of attitude (as a measure of perceived usefulness).

Still another weakness of many studies can be attributed to the manual nature of the evaluation process. Participation in a manual evaluation is tedious and time consuming; it tends to create a negative bias on the part of the participants. Therefore, this study proposes to computerize the evaluation process and shorten the questionnaire to its essential factors. A shorter questionnaire will therefore be administered interactively (using a computer terminal) in order to reduce this negative bias.

Construct validity of the major dimensions of perceived usefulness can be tested by factor analysis. As Kerlinger [16] says: “factor analysis may also be called the most important of construct validity tools.” Runkel and McGrath [21] assert that when several measures are proposed as means of measuring a construct, the measures should relate to other measures of the same construct.

As an example, Romaniuk and Montgomerie [19] studied the use of CAI as a supplement to a BASIC introductory computer course taken by first year computer systems students. Eighteen students took the course with CAI while another eighteen took the course without. Romaniuk and Montgomerie compared the students' performance and their time to complete the course. However, they did not evaluate the quality of CAI software prior to the experiment, and we content that the qualities of that CAI software may have strongly affected its use. Moreover, numerous other studies report on successful applications of CAI [1,2–10,12,13,19]. However, none of them considered computerizing the traditional manual evaluation. The advantages of computerizing such evaluations include time savings by the users as well as improved precision and reliability. The following are problems of manual software evaluation, as reported in the above studies:

1. Incomplete evaluation forms.

2. Illegible evaluation forms.

3. Biased sample of system users.

4. Low response rate.

5. Long elapsed time between program execution and evaluation,

6. Time-consuming transcription of data from evaluation forms onto data-entry forms,

7. Time-consuming key-punch or key-entry operations,

8. High rate of errors in data transcription, and

9. User resentment at excessive paper work.

The present study suggests that most of these problems can be mitigated, and then the evaluation will be more complete, legible, less biased, representative, prompt, timely, accurate, and more pleasant for the user-evaluator.

In summarizing, it can be noted that the computer assumes varying roles of an instructional nature. Two significant findings appear in the literature: first, very few of the projects and the studies reported any extensive testing or evaluations of the CAI quality to the instructional process; second, CAI studies have relied almost totally on manual survey-questionnaires for their evaluations and data.

## 2. Objectives and Hypotheses

Frequently, end-users do not participate or have sufficient input in decisions concerning the acquisition, implementation, maintenance, and modification of interactive software. As a result, vast economic resources are wasted in developing software which is underutilized due to user dissatisfaction. One of the obstacles to resolving this problem is the lack of effective and efficient methods for software evaluation. The reported study presents a specific case of the more generic problem: CAI, with novice computer users as test subjects.

The first task is the measurement of the attitude users have to each of the items on the tutorial evaluation. This is an important step in evaluating perceived usefulness, given that the CAI software utility has been measured using a Tutorial Evaluation Form (see Appendix A), factor analysis can be performed. The present study, in a manner similar to Dzida, Herda and Itzefeldt [11], applied factor analysis. However, here the factor analysis is used not only to identify the major dimensions of software utility (as in [11]), but more importantly, as a means of resolving problems in the process of computerizing the evaluation. Although some of the problems have been discussed elsewhere, no attempt has previously been made to resolve them.

The second problem, therefore, is to establish that there are reduced major dimensions of perceived utility and to prove that the reduced factors are as informative as those previously used six items. The data reduction was perceived to be the first step in computerizing the evaluation.

The third problem deals with the computerized evaluation. The first stage is concerned with naming the reduced variables and designing the computerized evaluation. The variable names are based on the factor names (Table 1) of the computerized evaluation (Appendix B) that followed the use of the manual evaluation.

The second stage compares the results of the manual and computerized evaluation, in order to maintain a sense of continuity in evaluating the software. A major difficulty results from the different number of evaluative variables; the jmanual evaluation has six variables, the computerized approach two. The solution to this can be obtained by converting the “manual” six variables into two “computerized” variables by applying the factor loading from the varimax rotated factor matrix. Accordingly, the two factors were computed as weighted averages of the six manual variables using the factor loadings as weights.

The null hypothesis of this study states that each of the items on the manual Tutorial Evaluation Form represents a different significant factor, i.e., there is no possibility of data reduction. Conversely, the alternate hypothesis states that the some of the Tutorial Evaluation items are redundant and the number of items can be reduced without loss of information. According to the factor analysis of [11], one may expect that the null hypothesis would be rejected in favor of the alternative hypothesis, leading to a substantial data reduction.

## 3. Methodology and Procedures

The subjects in this study were 146 novice computer users receiving instruction in the BASIC programming language via formal classroom lecture supplemented by CAI tutorials. The instructor had no part in the creation and implementation of the CAI programs or the study: therefore, he had no vested interest in the outcome.

A time schedule for the completion of the CAI tutorials was recommended, but the users were able to repeat any tutorial an unlimited number of times. They were presented interactively on a Digital Equipment Corporation DEC-10 computer. Each user had the choice of using either a Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) or a teletype (TTY) terminal.

The instructor first covered the material in lecture; the material was then reviewed and supplemented by the tutorials. Before using the CAI tutorial programs, the users were given a demonstration and received handouts on the use of the CRT and TTY terminals.

## 4. Factor Analyzed Software Evaluation

As stated earlier, a major objective of this paper was to discern the underlying pattern of relationships among the attitude subscales (Clear, Interest, Easy, Useful, Enjoy, and Too-Short) of the Tutorial Evaluation Form. Such information is valuable in evaluating the efficiency of such an Attitude questionnaire; i.e., how much is superfluous and can be eliminated without loss of information.

Another important objective is the construction of an “index of attitude” to be used for comparison between this experiment and subsequent experiments with a more efficient and condensed questionnaire. A condensed version of the modular evaluation form may be added to the CAI program and administered much more efficiently than by the present method.

Table 1 presents the final orthogonally rotated factors in our research. Because it is an orthogonal rotated matrix, it stands for both pattern and structure matrices, that is, the coefficients in this table represent both regression weights and correlation coefficients.

The highly significant factor loadings in Table 1 (accented by asterisks) indicate possible data reduction. Hence, the null hypothesis is rejected in favor of the alternate hypothesis. Accordingly, one may conclude that four of the six (manual) Tutorial Evaluation items are redundant. The factors can be named by the highest loading variables: Factor 1: Interesting, Useful, and Enjoyable Factor 2: Clear and Easy.

The loading of numbers in each row represent regression coefficients of factors to describe each variable. The two columns in Table 1, therefore, stand for two factors or hypothetical constructs and the number in each row represents regression weights.

By using the factor weights, one may be able to construct the normalized score of the subscale Clear. Hence, Z score of (Clear) = 0.21392 (Factor 1) + 0.65133 (Factor 2) + Unique Factor. By reading each row of Table 1 in the same manner, the linear composition of each subscale can be described in terms of the two hypothetical factors. Note, however, that only the subscales Clear and Enjoy load moderately on both factors, thus approaching a factorial complexity of more than one factor. For example, the loading of Clear on Factor 1 is 0.2139 while Factor 2 dominates with a loading of 0.65133. Accordingly, the rest of the variables load significantly on only one factor, have a complexity of one, and are simpler than Clear and Enjoy.

In a regression equation, the independent hypothetical variables, Factor 1 and Factor 2, control or account for the variance in the dependent variables, Clear through Too-Short. The importance of each of these factors (Factor 1 and Factor 2), for each of the subscales, can be expressed in terms of the subscale variance accounted for by a given factor. For instance, the variance of Clear (the variation in Factor 1 and Factor 2) is:

$$
0. 4 6 9 9 9 = (0. 2 1 3 9 2) ^ {2} + (0. 6 5 1 3 3) ^ {2}
$$

The sum 0.46999 is the communality of Clear which indicates the fraction of the variability of Clear accounted for by the combinations of the two common factors. The rest of the variability, the complement 0.53, denotes the unexplained variance. Table 2 shows the communalities of all the subscales and indicates, based upon the cumulative percent of variance (CUMP) explained, that Clear and Interest are the most important subscales.

This suggests that this six-item scale can be condensed into a two-factor scale which encompasses most of the information. ## 5. Summary, Conclusions and Implications

It was found in this study that the Tutorial Evaluation Form indeed measures perceived usefulness of the interactive software – the CAI system. The validity of the attitude scales was established by a factor analysis which lead to the construction of the two main factors of the construct of perceived usefulness. The two main dimensions of the perceived usefulness are the usability dimension and the clarity dimension.

It was also demonstrated that no significant loss of information occurs as the amount of data is reduced from six to two data values. This implies the same evaluation process can be repeated more efficiently and more effectively, with significant reduction of data.

Evaluation can be achieved more effectively by performing data collection interactively (immediately following the use of the software to be evaluated) rather than manually. Thus, the results of the evaluation can be assessed promptly and corrective action taken. Such an operation will be greatly improved by the use of the two main dimensions rather than the original six items.

For evaluation of the acquisition process, this software may be appended to the newly acquired software. Furthermore, interactive software evaluation would be even more useful for maintenance and modification evaluation. A modified or “upgraded” version may also be compared to the original, in order to measure the increase in user satisfaction brought about by the modifications.

This study also indicates that the most important criteria for CAI users (students) is clarity and their degree of interest in the tutorials.
