# On the Assimilation of Laboratory Data by General Medical Practitioners \*

H.W.K. Acheson

Senior Lecturer, Department of General Practice, Rusholme Health Centre, University of Manchester, School of Medicine, Walmer Street, Manchester M14 5NP, England

## and

## R.H. Mole

Lecturer, Department of Management Studies, Loughborough, University of Technology, Loughborough, Leicestershire LE113TU, England

The authors briefly consider the system by which General Medical Practitioners in the UK can obtain laboratory investigation as a supporting technical service for their medical practice. A descriptive model is advanced for the way in which laboratory derived data can be integrated with clinical data. The model incorporates the practitioners prior assessment at the time the laboratory investigation is initiated, the intention behind the investigation, the nature of the investigation, the interpretation of the result, and the value and outcome which can be attributed to the result of the investigation. These were found to be interlocking themes in the results of a large scale survey of how General Medical Practitioners actually responded on receipt of laboratory data. The results provide a scenario against which changes in the allocation of resources can be better judged.

Keywords: General Medical Practice, Clinical decision making, Medical data, Pathology investigation, Patient assessment, Data utility.

A pathology laboratory provides reports on the results of investigations initiated by a patient's doctor for the purpose of diagnosis or control of therapy. The reports are objective and are usually based on tests performed by technicians using routine, well-established, analytical procedures. They are interpreted by the initiating doctor in the light of his knowledge of the patient's clinical state and his reason for requesting the investigation. A Consultant is in overall charge of a laboratory and is available to advice a clinician in instances of difficult interpretation or upon the choice of more informative, or more specialised investigations.

A hospital doctor's perception of the standard of laboratory service depends upon the range of investigations which it provides, and partly upon a demonstration of technical competence (accuracy and precision). It also depends on the extent of delay in receiving reports. A laboratory normally finds it expedient to institutive a weekly programme of tests, which is a compromise between the several advantages obtained from batching tests for analysis and the maintenance of an adequate response to both urgent and routine request for investigation. A General Practitioner's (GP's) perception of service is influenced additionally by the accessibility of the laboratory to patients who need to attend, and the ease with which specimens can be transported to it.

All GPs in the United Kingdom have access to laboratory investigation services. In urban areas the transmission of specimens and laboratory reports usually presents little difficulty. In rural areas most specimens and reports are transmitted by post. A GP will select the investigation to be performed, collect the specimen and arrange for it to be forwarded to the laboratory, taking advice from a Consultant if necessary. The cost to a GP is small, being mainly the time to collect the specimen and the cost of delivery or sending it to the laboratory. However, the total cost of laboratory investigation to the National Health Service (NHS) is not, by any means, a minor matter.

Between 1968 and 1972, the number of requests for pathology investigation [1] received by laboratories increased by 33%; in the same period, the number of requests initiated by GPs increased by 51%. It is probable that the increase from general practice reflects previous under-use of laboratory services and that eventually the rate of increase will level out. But there is a tendency for the annual volume of laboratory requests from all sources to increase [2,3]. Each investigation has a cost, and Barnard et al. [3] estimated that in their laboratory in 1968 a serum folate estimation cost £3.19. With the general tendency towards rising costs and an increasing volume of requests, the total cost must be considered in relation to total NHS finance. The financial resources in the UK are not unlimited and there are many competing demands, of which the NHS is only one. There is, of course, competition for money within the NHS itself.

As Cochrane [4] has pointed out: “The main savings occur when patients receive adequate treatment without admission to hospital.” Laboratory investigation can be an aid to rational clinical management, and management by GPs is less costly than the alternatives. It is appropriate therefore to examine the circumstances surrounding GP’s requests for investigation and the decisions that result.

If laboratory services are to be used effectively and efficiently, they must be used only when appropriate, the tests selected must have a direct relationship to the information required, and the result should be speedily available. With the growth of clinical science a doctor's observations have become increasingly quantitative [5], but the basis for the selection of laboratory tests is the patient's clinical state and the physician's qualitative observations. A physician should have a clear objective in mind; such as the confirmation of a tentative diagnosis based on clinical observation, to refute a possible diagnosis that cannot otherwise be excluded, to monitor the progress of previously noted abnormalities, or to measure the concentration of a drug in blood.

It would be wrong to imply that a clear and unequivocal diagnosis necessarily follows from laboratory investigation. Whether or not the result of a test can be considered to be normal is based on estimations derived from large random samples of healthy people, from which a mean value is calculated. The normal value is regarded as lying within two standard deviations of the mean. Five per cent of “normal” people yield results outside this range. Therefore the proper assessment of the significance of a result can only be achieved when the patient’s medical history and clinical state is taken into account.

Some laboratory investigations yield abnormal results which are not specific to a single disease. Such indicators are valuable. They help to re-define the set of probable and possible diagnoses; e.g. a raised cholesterol level in blood serum may be attributable to several causes such as diet, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or biliary tract disease.

For economic reasons most laboratories now use automatic equipment for the more frequently requested analyses. This equipment is constructed to perform the same series of tests on each specimen. Thus a doctor may receive more results than he requested, some of which may yield unexpected abnormalities. Each of these has to be judged in relation to the clinical context and may pose an ethical, economical, and managerial dilemma: is it right to expose a patient to additional investigation if there is a likelihood that the cause of an abnormality found by chance will remain obscure?

Operational research workers have attempted to identify the factors which lie behind the disparity in rates of laboratory usage per year by GPs. The principal factors can be classified [6] as:

(a) individual characteristics of the doctors

(b) characteristics of the practices, and

(c) accessibility of the laboratory.

The GP who uses the laboratory most frequently tends to be young, with a postgraduate qualification, works in an urban area in partnership with other doctors, has easy access to a laboratory, and may have an additional hospital appointment. Typically, only a small proportion of GPs are high users of laboratory services. If the majority of GPs were to adopt the intensive usage of the minority, the annual rise in laboratory workload would be considerable.

It was necessary to attempt the division of each of the stages into mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive categories which could be used as the basis for recording the experience of a group of GPs. The concomitant definitions proved to be the most elusive, and constituted the major task inherent in the work reported here.

The developed form of the model is apparent from Fig. 2 which illustrates the method of recording. The responses to a series of six multiple choice questions and other data could be recorded on one line of the recording sheet. For practical purposes each investigation request was entered in chronological order. An opportunity was available (in principle) to re-sort the data to correspond with longitudinal patterns of investigation within patient episodes. This potentially valuable information had to be sacrificed: GPs were reluctant to divulge data which could be related to named patients, and to record data for a sufficient interval to encompass a representative set of patient-episodes of illness.

One thousand six hundred GP principals, comprising all those practicing in the area of the North-Western Regional Health Authority, were invited to record the laboratory investigations requested by them during two consecutive weeks. Two hundred and thirty (14.4%) returned completed documents, giving details of 4,500 investigations requests, together with information about themselves and their practice. Thus, two populations of doctors were potentially available for study: those who responded to our invitation and those who did not. It is probably that there were significant differences between them but our budget and the time available did not permit us to investigate non-responders. This would have been important if the objective had been to determine why some GPs used the laboratory more than others. However, because the objectives of the study were to find out the intention of the GP when requesting an investigation, his interpretation of the result and its contribution to patient management, it was perceived that a population of 14.4% of the whole was sufficiently valid for our purposes.

At the time that each investigation was initiated the GP was asked to indicate his prior assessment, based on clinical findings, from a list of alternatives (Table 3), and his intention when ordering the test (table 4). Most of the tests (59.9%) related to patients in whom the presence of disease either had been previously established or was considered probable.

The three principal reasons for laboratory investigation are to assist diagnosis, monitor the progress of a known illness, and to “screen” patients for possible disease. Table 4 shows that 68.4% of the investigations were intended to assist diagnosis and 19.8% were concerned with monitoring the progress of disease. Only 11.6% were arranged in the absence of clinical evidence of disease and intended as screening tests. Most of the latter group related to the possibility of blood disorders (55.7%) or abnormal cervical cytology (24.0%).

As mentioned above, the interpretation of investigation results requires that the patient's clinical state be taken into account. Some results outside the normal range may be accepted as “normal” if the patient’s clinical state does not indicate that the result is significant in the context of that particular patient. On this basis 1257 (27.9%) of the investigation results were interpreted by GPs as abnormal. A further 195 (4.3%) were interpreted as equivocal. Previous investigators [8–10] have reported a proportion of abnormal results ranging from 21% to 42%, suggesting that the yield of abnormal results should not be regarded as a sensitive index of effective laboratory usage.

After receiving the investigation result, GPs recorded their opinion of the clinical value of the investigation in relation to the management of each patient (Table 5), and their subsequent action (Table 6). Only 11.7% of investigations were considered to be of no clinical value.

Laboratory investigation provides only part of the data obtained about a patient's clinical condition. Thus, the management action taken as a result of investigation will depend upon the physician's judgement in relation to other information in his possession. It may not be possible to attribute a particular action solely to the investigation. Moreover, Table 6 shows that subsequent action is not dependent upon the normality or otherwise of the result.

The only directly attributable actions were: to close the episode (patient no longer ill), to arrange for the test to be repeated, to recall the patient for consultation earlier than expected, or to refer the patient for specialist advice.

## 3. Discussion

The general picture in regard to the set of 4,500 investigations is displayed in Figure 3. No single theme is evident. Indeed, the results of this survey into the circumstances surrounding the use of laboratory investigation illustrate a number of interlocking themes, rather than an obvious single criterion for establishing appropriate use or for identifying gross misuse. Our purpose was rather to elucidate how GPs integrate the services of the laboratory into daily clinical practice.

In this survey, 44% of the abnormal results were obtained when disease was probable, and 31.6% when disease had been established previously. Only 8.4% of the abnormal results occurred when disease was considered improbable (Table 4). Secondly, most of the abnormal results confirmed the GP's prior assessment, although a significant proportion (15.3%) led to this being changed. It is interesting to observe that comparatively large numbers of normal results and correspondingly low numbers of abnormal investigations were associated with this change ( $X^{2}=37.6$ , $V=1$ , $P>0.001$ ). This certainly calls into question the utility of the "yield" of abnormal results. Further, almost a quarter of the abnormal results were associated with the management decision to refer the patient for a specialist opinion, contrasting with only 5% of the normal results.

When evaluating the use of laboratory investigations by GPs and their relationship to clinical decisions, perhaps the most important factor is the expectation of the GP at the time he arranges the investigation. Our findings suggest that GPs were using laboratory investigation primarily as an aid to diagnosis, secondly for monitoring the progress of established disease, and thirdly for screening patients in whom no disease was suspected. A wholly appropriate order of priorities.

Laboratory investigations provide only part of the total data required for effective decisions in medicine. Diagnostic and management decisions are based on clinical and para-clinical data. The laboratory supplies some of the para-clinical data; other para-clinical data includes, for example, radiology. Every laboratory investigation request may be viewed as a purchase of a service which entails a monetary cost. Taken collectively, they represent a significant proportion of available Health Service finance and economy of use is desirable. Economy is best achieved by careful consideration of the contribution of the test result to clinical decisions. The circumstances under which it would be desirable to purchase additional tests effectively, that is where the benefits of risk avoidance would exceed the costs of incremental testing expense, cannot be inferred from our data. Such circumstances would depend upon the clinical factors concerning each individual patient, a study of which would be extremely complicated because it would involve a detailed continuous assessment of each patient throughout each episode of illness; a patient being a biological organism and therefore subject to continual change, never in a static state.

Clinical decisions are based on scientific knowledge and value judgements, which, in turn, are based on the experience of the physician. Our survey suggests that the value judgements made by the participating GPs were appropriate in relation to the contribution of the laboratory investigations arranged by them.

One unexpected aspect of the work is the usefulness of the record cards to GP trainees and their tutors. The record card forces the trainess to make explicit many of the mental notes in relation to pathology data, and thus provides a convenient vehicle for self assessment.
