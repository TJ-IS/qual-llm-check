# The Relationship Between Computerization and Performance: A Strategy for Maximizing the Economic Benefits of Computerization

William L. Cron

Assistant Professor of Marketing, Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

and

Marion G. Sobol

Professor of Management Science and Computer, Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

The relationship between computerization and several measures of overall firm performance is examined. Sample firms consisted of 138 wholesalers in a single industry with annual sales between one million and over ten million dollars. Three performance comparisons are presented: users versus non-users of computers, three levels of usage, and class of computer usage. Results indicate that computerization is related to overall performance. Non-users tend to be small firms with about average overall performance. On the other hand, firms owning computers and making extensive use of them in a variety of ways tend to be either very high or low performers. Possible explanations for these bi-modal results are presented.

Keywords: Economic Performance, Computer Software Usage, Computer Ownership Status, Computers and Economic Performance, Wholesalers, Medical Supply Industry, Computers and Return on Assets, Computers and Return on Net Worth, Computer Time-Sharing

## 2. Previous Studies

One of the earliest articles on the evaluation of management information systems was by Mason and Mitroff [12]. They suggested that psychological type, class of problem, organizational context, and modes of presentation be investigated to study the performance of management information systems; but they never discussed how performance should be measured or related to these variables.

Using factor analysis and stepwise multiple regression, H.V. Lucas, Jr. [8] examined the relationship between performance and usage of information systems by ready-to-wear clothing salesmen and account executives. Use of the information system was found to be significantly helpful only to salesmen in uncertain, competitive environments; they would use the system to search for problems. For account executives, low performance was a predictor of the use of the system. Generally the relation between performance and use of the system was in the predicted direction, but relatively weak. Lucas suggested a study on the relative performance of users and non-users. He also suggested that use be related to personal decision style and situational variables, so that information systems should be flexible.

In a later article on the use of accounting information systems in banks, Lucas distinguished between uses in problem finding versus problem solving [9]. He found little significant explanation of variation in performance. Favorable attitudes toward the computer, feelings of participation in goal setting and feeling that compensation is dependent on goal achievement were positively related to the use of the management information system.

In a later study Lucas [10] introduced decision style (heuristic vs. analytic) and was surprised to find that heuristic decision makers were more likely to use information systems than analytical ones. Possibly analytic decision makers used their own forms of analysis. This study did not investigate computer implementation and its consequences.

Other attempts at evaluating performance of information systems have relied on management opinions, based on user surveys. Thus Neumann and Segev [13] state, “a universal measure like net effect on earnings cannot be applied yet”. They solicited subjective evaluations from 81 branch bank managers on 54 reports and related them to the performance of the branch. The correlation of bank performance ratings and computer evaluations was usually very low. An additional study of high performing branches showed little consensus on the evaluation of the usefulness of the different information systems reports.

In summary, the literature shows that relatively few studies of these effects have been made. Thus, the profitability of companies which do not use computerized facilities has not been compared to those who do. The effects of extensive versus relatively low level computer information system usage has not been studied. The effects of different types of computer usages has not been evaluated nor have the effects of owning, renting, or leasing computer facilities been investigated. Subject to the caveats, listed in the first paragraph, this study shows how the degree of computerization relates to company performance.

## 3. Design of the Study

The Study of effects of computerization among wholesaler institutions was chosen mainly because of the many ways in which these institutions can utilize computers. In an attempt to increase productivity, wholesalers are relying more and more on computers, not only to monitor inventory levels but also to determine product line profitability and to chart the buying history of major clients. Computer use is becoming feasible for even the smallest of wholesalers due to the availability of canned programs, the development of mini- and microcomputers, and the large supply of inexpensive used computers [5]. Because of the hazards in making financial performance comparisons among firms in different industries [2], this study concentrates on wholesalers in a single industry. While operating within a fairly similar competitive environment, firms in the medical supply industry vary considerably in size, computer adoption, and extent of computer usage. Excellent performance, operating information, and computer usage data are available in this industry from the annual operating and performance survey sponsored by the American Surgical Trade Association, the industry's trade association. As a result, we were able to obtain data for 90 percent of firms in this industry.

## 3.1. Industry Characteristics

The medical supply industry is a part of the total United States health care industry. It consists of firms primarily involved in the wholesale distribution of supplies for human health care. The dominant channel configuration is manufacturer – wholesaler – health care agency. Over 150 independent wholesalers, mostly operating on a local basis, provide local supplies capable of meeting emergency needs. Health care agencies which are the ultimate users of these medical supplies consist of hospitals, physicians, nursing homes, laboratories, and industrial facilities.

During the sixties and early seventies, industry wholesalers experienced exceptional levels of sales growth and profitability. Nevertheless, almost all of their independent wholesalers have been forced to re-evaluate and adjust their operations in response to several recent competitive forces that have squeezed profits. In particular, the independent medical supply wholesaler has faced three fairly pervasive industry competitive forces:

● Large manufacturers have established national salesforces calling directly on large health agencies and establishing a distinct brand position and identity with these customers. This relegates the wholesaler to performing a physical distribution function, thereby restricting their differentiation opportunities.

\- Large health agencies, especially hospitals (which represent the largest purchasers of medical supplies), are in a position to “call the shots”, knowing they can either get the product from one distributor or another. As a result, they have become price sensitive.

\- The lucrative business from large health agencies is becoming increasingly inaccessible to small independent wholesalers because of their inability to match the sophisticated computer assisted order placement, handling and delivery processes of the few, growing, national and regional wholesalers.

In an effort to operate profitably within the tighter margins that these competitive forces have established, a number of wholesalers have turned to the computer to service their customers better and to improve control of their operations. At the same time, a number of industry wholesalers have, so far, resisted the move to computerization, providing an opportunity for comparison of computerized and non-computerized firms.

## 3.2. Characteristics of Sample Firms

Sample firms in this study consist of the 138 wholesalers that responded to the annual financial and operating survey sponsored by the American Surgical Trade Association. This survey has been conducted annually for the past eight years, with a steady trend of increased participation to the present level of over 90 percent of the firms predominantly selling medical supplies [15]. Notable non-participants include American Hospital Supply, the only wholesaler operating on a national scale, and the six regional wholesalers in this industry. These firms have distinctly different operating characteristics from the sample firms and account for approximately 40% of industry sales. The 1979 survey was selected for analysis because it contained relevant computer usage information not in other editions.

## 3.3. Computerization Measures

As mentioned earlier, previous studies have concentrated on the effects of having or not having a computer. However, once a firm has decided to use a computer, it must decide which of the major types of computers or computer services it will purchase, as well as which software packages it should use. As a result, multiple measures of computerization were constructed.

## 3.3.1. Computer Ownership

There are at least two clear ownership alternatives available to business: ownership/leasing versus the use of time-sharing. Time-sharing is typically the initial venture of firms into the use of computer facilities; equipment maintenance and storage are minimal and all software is packaged, thus the firm often requires no programming capabilities. However, time-sharing packages may result in excessive limitations in data entry, retrieval, and analysis. By contrast, ownership or leasing of in-house computer facilities greatly increases management's ability to adapt to particular requirements. Among the sample firms, 40 percent owned or leased their facilities, 23 percent used time-sharing packages, while 27 percent did not use a computer at all. Ten percent of the wholesalers use both time-sharing and in-house computers.

## 3.3.3. Type of Software Capabilities

An examination of the different software capabilities regularly used by the sample firms indicated that these fell into three general categories: (1) the storage and computation of accounting related information; (2) the presentation of computer information to facilitate management control and decisionmaking; and (3) environmental links that allow for direct interaction with customer ordering activities or indirectly facilitate interaction with suppliers and customers. Although six combinations of the categories in Table 1 are possible, ranging from no computer capabilities to the regular use of all three capabilities, only four combinations were considered in order to have a sufficient number of firms for statistical analysis of each group. Twenty-seven percent of the firms did not have a computer capability, 7 percent used accounting capabilities only, 17 percent regularly used some of the accounting as well as management control capabilities, and 49 percent used some or all of the capabilities within each of the three categories.

## 3.4. Performance Measures

The measurement of firm performance is somewhat simplified since the private or closely held ownership of all sample firms eliminates the need to consider the earnings per share measure. These is no consensus on exactly how to measure their financial performance. The product portfolio BCG (Boston Consulting Group) and PIMS (Profit Impact of Marketing Strategy) [14] approaches, which are currently in so much vogue, assume that return on investment is the best measure of financial performance, despite its well documented deficiencies in dealing with the timing, duration, and risk differences among returns [16]. While shareholder wealth is conceptually the best measure of performance, workable operational definitions have not been developed [1]. As a result, multiple measures of financial performance were used in this study:

\- pretax return on assets: profit divided by total assets.

\- return on net worth: pretax profits divided by net worth.

\- pretax profits as a percent of sales: profits divided by total sales.

● average five-year growth: average sales growth between 1975 and 1979.

Two operating performance measures were obtained: pretax return on assets and pretax profits as a percent of sales. In addition, return on net worth is widely used in other empirical investigations of organization performance [14] and because firms typically have multiple objectives, the average five year growth rate between 1975 and 1979 was also investigated.

## 4. Results

Prior to analyzing financial performance, the relation among the three computer measures was analyzed. Chi-square analysis of the three decisions showed that, as expected, all three were significantly related to each other. Type of ownership and software capabilities are related. Firms owning their own computer facilities were more likely to use all three types of general computer capabilities, as well as a greater total number of individual computer capabilities ( $\chi^{2}=61.35$ , p=0.001). Firms with only time-sharing facilities were more likely to use fewer software packages and to use only those related to accounting procedures and management control ( $\chi^{2}=13.98$ , p=0.001). Although its influence on performance may vary, type of ownership is obviously related to computer decisions.

Because all performance measures are ratio data while each of the computerization measures is categorical, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was the logical choice for testing the relationship between performance and computerization. However, preliminary analysis of the performance measures indicated that ANOVA was not likely to pick up the differences in performance, because the performance data were not normally distributed. Specifically, firms that owned their own computers and/or made extensive use of their computer capacities tended towards a bi-model distribution on the various performance measures, while firms without a computer exhibited much less variation and were more closely distributed about their mean for the performance measures. Each of the four performance measures was divided into quartiles (lowest 25% of firms, second 25%, third 25%, and highest 25%), to test for statistically different performance distributions within the three computerization measures. As shown in Table 2, although no statistically significant differences were found for return on net worth and the five-year average growth rate, differences were found in pretax profits as a percent of sales and in return on assets.

## 4.4. High Performance Firms

The previous analysis indicates that extensive and appropriate use of computer capabilities is most likely to be associated with top quartile performance. Minimal use of computer facilities, either in terms of the number of uses or the type of usage, is least likely to be related to top overall financial performance. However, non-computerization does not appear to be related to poor performance and, indeed, there were a good number of non-computerized firms in the top-quartile of performance as measured by return on assets. If extensive computer usage is related to high performance through control of operating expenses, then how are these non-computerized firms able to achieve similar overall financial performance results?

To gain insight into the performance process of these topquartile firms, their return on assets ratio was separated into its two main components, pretax profits as a percent of sales and asset turnover. Pretax profits were further separated into gross margin as a percent of sales, which represents the difference between what the firm paid for its products and the prices charged its customers, and total operating expenses as a percent of sales. Gross margin tends to be especially critical to these firms because it represents the money available to the firm to cover expenses and to generate a profit. In addition, asset turnover was divided into net sales and total assets, which tend to be very fluid because inventory and account receivables accounting for nearly 90 percent of the typical firm's total asset base [2]. As a result, intelligent asset management has the potential for dramatic performance benefits, but emphasis on sales increases alone is not likely to result in higher asset turnover and overall performance.

However, a further breakdown of these two ratios into their main components shows drastically different routes are being taken to achieve similar high overall performance. While the non-computerized firms (Group I) are much smaller than firms in any of the three computerized groups, they are able to achieve a much larger gross margin percent, nearly seven points higher than firms owning their own computers (Group II). These higher gross margins most likely reflect the positioning of these small, highly profitable, non-computerized firms in profitable niches within the general industry. High gross margins may result from some degree of geographic isolation from competition, but more likely reflect astute buyer selection enabling these small firms to avoid direct competition with the larger wholesalers against whom they are at a price and cost disadvantage. Whether or not computerization would increase their profit margins cannot be totally answered here, but their substantially higher operating expenses, by comparison to firms with computer capacity, suggests that increased profit margins may be possible. It may be that management feels no immediate need for computerization because of the relatively comfortable financial performance levels currently being experienced. Or indeed, if they cater to a small number of customers and have relatively small sales they may not have as much need for computerized efficiency as their larger counterparts. It is worthy of note that despite the larger gross margins for the smaller non-computer group in the end they have 10–15% lower returns on assets than the computerized firms.

## 5. Conclusions

Although it is widely accepted that computers have the potential to reduce expenses and facilitate management decision-making, the performance experience of computerized firms has never been investigated using either a large sample of firms or objective measures of overall performance. In today's intensely competitive business environment, it is increasingly important for management to link all important decisions to bottom-line performance. As advances in technology make computers available to a wider number of businesses, even those usually thought of as being small, the issues of intelligent computer adoption and usage becomes increasingly relevant to the business community in general.

In this study the performance experiences of 138 wholesalers, of which 37 did not use any type of computer in their operations, were examined according to their ownership status (own/lease versus timesharing), the extensiveness of computer usage, and the type of computer usage. Though it is always difficult to establish causality from correlation data conclusively, a number of significant relationships were found:

1. Firms that own or lease their computer(s) are generally more likely to be top-quartile performers as measured by return on assets and pretax profits as a percent of sales. The computer ownership-high performance relationship is especially true for the larger and faster growing wholesalers.

2. Firms, that make extensive use of the computer (measured by number of types of applications for which the computer is used) tend to be either very high or very low performers, as measured by return on assets and pretax profits. The low performance firms tended to be rather small firms with above average operating expenses. It would therefore seem that computerization combined with extensive software utilization is most advantageous for larger firms for whom economies of scale may arise. Computers may significantly increase the total operating expenses of small firms, the potential for significant cost savings may be more limited in their situation.

3. How the computer is used was even more closely related to differences in overall performance than was type of ownership or number of uses. Thirty percent of the firms making use of all three types of software capabilities (accounting, management control, and environmental link) were in the highest quartile of return on assets and pretax profits. By comparison, only 15 percent of firms using the computer for accounting and management control were in the top-quartile of performance. On the other hand, the performance distribution for firms using all three types of software capabilities was bi-modal. Thirty-nine percent of those firms were in the lowest performance quartile. These results may be due to the recursive causal relationship between computerization and performance. As Lucas (5) pointed out, sales manager with the greatest problems were most likely to use the computer extensively to identify their problems. At the same time, very efficient managers would use the computer to its fullest extent to derive optimal economies of scale. (A more extensive study would be needed to determine if there is a saturation point for the profitable use of computer software. Certainly at some point extensive use would require the introduction of additional hardware.) This may also be the case with respect to the firms in this study and is supported by the lower relative operating expenses of the high profit firms.

While recognizing that it is important to replicate this study using different kinds of firms and industries, results of this study indicate that computerization is positively related to the overall performance of medium and larger size firms. Small firms should be cautious in estimating the potential for significant economies of scale from computerization, but should use information to facilitate management control and decision-making. Firms with existing computer capability should make more extensive use of its software capabilities as these are related to performance.
