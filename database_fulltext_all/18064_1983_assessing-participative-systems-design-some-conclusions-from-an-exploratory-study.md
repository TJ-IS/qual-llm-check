# Assessing Participative Systems Design: Some Conclusions from an Exploratory Study \*

R.A. Hirschheim

London School of Economics and Political Science, University of London, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

Participative systems design has been proposed by many as the best approach for information system/technology implementation. A study was undertaken to see if this supposition could be supported. The results suggest that participative design: (a) is a much more complicated phenomenon than the literature contends, (b) is not normally formally evaluated, (c) is almost universally praised by the participants, and (d) is, however, not normally used a second time.

Keywords: Participation, user involvement, participative systems design, socio-technical systems, participative design assessment.

Rudolf A. Hirschheim is currently lecturer in System Analysis at the London School of Economics (1980 – present). Previously, he has been a senior consultant with the National Computing Centre, a research associate at the Henley Management College and a lecturer in information systems at McMaster University and Wilfrid Laurier University in Canada. His primary research interests lie in new information technology and its implications on individuals, organisations and society.

He is the author or co-author of two recent books: Local Area Networks, and Office Automation, and a number of articles related to information systems.

## 1. Introduction

"There is hardly a study in the entire literature (on participation) which fails to demonstrate that satisfaction in work is enhanced or that other generally acknowledged beneficial consequences accrue from a genuine increase in workers' decision making power. Such consistency of findings, I submit, is rare in social research." [9]

Participation has, in the past, been advocated in a number of organisational settings: decision making, job design, etc. (c.f. [7]). More recently, participation has been discussed in the context of information systems implementation and the introduction of information technology into the firm.

Participative systems design on an intuitive level is appealing. It refers to the handing of responsibilities for design and means of introduction of a new system to that group of workers who must use the system. The arguments for this approach can be summarised as follows:

1. Participation allows the interests of the user to be protected. It is based on the belief that individuals should have a say in their destinies.

2. Participation allows individuals to redesign their jobs and working environments.

3. Activities are ultimately controlled by those who perform them; people who do not have a say in these decisions may choose not to abide by them. Participation thus facilitates compliance.

4. Participation acts as a motivation and leads to individuals identifying with the system. Thus it \* This research was funded by IBM UK Ltd.

leads to higher productivity and more efficiency.

5. Participation permits the various user skills and knowledge to be incorporated. This permits the system to be constructed to provide the best fit between the needs of the organisation and workers.

Yet participation does have its detractors. Adams [2] presents a case where users did participate in systems design but had no power to control the direction in which the design progressed. Eason [4] cites three disadvantages with involving users in the design process: (1) difficulties in developing a system specification understandable to the users, (2) potential delays caused in dealing with multiple groups, and (3) possible sub-optimal design because of the involvement of competing user groups.

Croisdale [13] asks how participation is possible when the number of potential participants are measured in 1000's scattered over a large number of locations. Mumford [36], one of the pioneers of participative methods, doubts whether they are possible where the objectives include a reduction in costs by making employees redundant.

Hedberg [20] asserts that some user participation is dysfunctional. He claims there are two such types: (1) "hostage" – when the participating user groups become shy and introspective because of the technical jargon used by the systems staff. (2) "indoctrination" – when the user or group is indoctrinated into the system group, providing little input. Hedberg thus claims, participation is not good enough, users must have influence; i.e. they must have power.

Lawrence [29] suggests that participation is difficult to accomplish. How do you force or teach someone to participate? It is not a skill possessed or understood by all. Meaningful participative systems design will be very challenging.

Keen and Gerson [24] present an argument against user participation from the systems' personnel viewpoint. They believe it leads to political problems, i.e., power struggles. And the systems staff are not equipped to handle conflicts. Therefore, systems staff prefer not to have users involved in the design process.

Krait [28] presents a provocative argument; he feels that this form of participation is manipulative – it is yet another management tool used to usurp further power from the workers.

Additionally, it has been argued that participative systems design: (a) is not cost/effective, (b) leads to poorly designed systems, and (c) does not fit naturally with the norms, value system and 'management style' of most of today's organisations.

Unfortunately, the disagreement for and against participative systems design is based at best on subjective interpretation of limited (and often contradictory) data. Recent empirical studies [23,25,40,41,46] suffer from a number of methodological difficulties and/or are inconclusive. Longitudinal studies are lacking, and because of this, a study was undertaken to address the value of participative systems design.

Moreover, according to Olson and Ives [41] user involvement (or participation) can be defined as "participation in the development process by a member or members of the larger user group". But note how this definition is very general and tends to belie the complexity inherent in the participative process. (This is an important point which will be explored in greater detail later.)

## 2. Research Method

It was felt that the most appropriate approach was to base the research around the knowledge of those individuals who had used participative design. The method involved the use of a modified case study approach. It is based on Mintzberg's [35] notion of 'direct research', where data is collected via interviews using a semi-structured questionnaire format on a variety of people in a number of organisations. The participating individuals were: a sample of users, the participation team, the DP manager, relevant systems analysts, the sponsor, any appropriate middle and/or senior managers, and any others who were willing and were thought to be able to provide relevant comments. Perceptual measures of success and/or value of participative design were elicited. Such data was supplemented by unobtrusive data collection (e.g., search of corporate records, memoranda, etc.) when and where possible. The research was conducted over a one year period: October 1981 to October 1982.

## 2.1. An Appraisal of the Literature

There is not a great deal of evaluative literature on participative systems design. That which does exist can be classified by the nature of its support/opposition of participation into two categories: intuitive/theoretical, and empirical. The former supports/opposes participation by way of 'appeal to reason', e.g., it allows the various skills and knowledge of the users to be incorporated. A review of the literature points up how poor intuitive arguments are. c.f. [1]. Thus, this study concentrated only on literature with empirical evidence.

Unfortunately, after carefully analysing this literature, little can definitely be said about the value of participative design. The majority show mixed or inconclusive results. What follows is a summary of the empirically based studies using the Ives and Olson [23] framework, followed by an appraisal of some major studies.

## 2.1.1. User Involvement and System Use

Proponents would probably argue that user involvement should have a positive correlation with systems use; that is, those who participate would more likely use the system. The empirical literature does not, however, support this. Studies performed by Fuerst [16], King and Rodriguez [25], Maish [34], Schewe [48], Swanson [52] and Zmud [56] all show no relationship. Alter [3] and Lonnstedt [31], on the other hand, do report a positive relationship.

It is not particularly clear what this shows. Often systems allow users little or no choice in their use of the system, i.e. they have no alternative. In these cases, system use would have little to do with amount of user participation.

None of the reported studies were sufficiently flexible to deal with this condition. It should also be noted that this variable was popular, since it was one of the few which could be quantitatively assessed.

## 2.1.2. User Involvement and User Attitude

It is assumed that if users participate in system design, they have a more favourable attitude toward the system. The available empirical literature, however, paints a confused picture. A number of studies report a positive relationship, c.f. [8,15,22,26,45,52]. Guthrie [19] and Sartore [47], however, report a negative correlation; and Olson and Ives [41] and Spence [51] found no correlation. Edstrom [15] noted that some user involvement caused users to have negative attitudes. Maish [34] found user involvement not to be related to attitudes toward the system rather, it was related to 'positive feelings toward information systems staff'. Lucas [32], on the other hand, found a positive correlation between user involvement and 'computer potential' but not with attitudes toward information systems staff.

The contradictory results make it difficult to say anything meaningful about the relationship between user participation and user attitudes. Moreover, it is not clear what the relationship means to an organisation even if it is true. A positive attitude towards a particular system does not necessarily mean the system will be more heavily used, is any better, will lead to more effective and/or efficient use, and so on.

## 2.1.3. User Involvement and System Quality

Some studies have attempted to measure the relationship between user involvement and system quality. The contention of the proponents would likely be that user involvement increases the quality of implemented systems. Unfortunately, the literature is rather limited and is not overly conclusive. Boland [10] and Gallagher [17] found a positive relationship between the two variables. Edstrom [15] also found a positive relationship but this was only in two out of six cases in his study. Powers and Dickson [44] and Vanlommel and De Brabander [53] reported no correlation between the two variables.

The limited number of studies makes it difficult to make any definitive statement regarding the relationship. Part of the problem, lies in the nature of the variable ‘system quality’. One study attempted to assess system quality using historical corporate records, most of the other studies used perceptual measures. The potential problems with such measures are self evident, but due to lack of alternatives, most studies used it. It is suspected that until a good measure is developed, the issue of system quality will remain moot.

King and Rodriguez [26], used a laboratory experiment with MBA students where they attempted to simulate user involvement by allowing one group to change system outputs. As the other groups could not change the outputs, this was felt to model a case where one group participated, while the others did not. Tests were then done to see if the 'involved group' made 'better' decisions (as judged by a group of business professors). The results proved negative which led King and Rodriguez to conclude that participation had no effect on the quality of decision making. This study is indicative of the laboratory experiments in the literature. As the methodological difficulties are self-evident, one is left wondering whether some other research approach may be more appropriate.

Laboratory experiments are not the only research vehicles which are problematic. Gallagher [17] concluded that user participation led to higher system quality because in his study those participatively designed systems produced reports which were assigned higher dollar values by the participants, even though the respondents expressed considerable difficulty in providing dollar estimates. Olson and Ives [41], concluded “the commonly held belief that user involvement contributes to more satisfied users receives almost no support in this study”. Yet in their research the authors did not permit the users to differentiate between satisfaction on participative and non-participative designed systems, i.e. only a user’s overall information systems satisfaction was measured. There was no comparison between user involvement on a particular system and satisfaction with that system, only overall satisfaction.

Robey and Farrow [45] provided another interesting example of a study whose results are difficult to interpret. The authors build up a rather impressive model termed a 'constructive conflict model for user involvement' which seeks to explain how user participation leads to conflict resolution.

Unfortunately, after a very elaborate empirical study the authors are at great pains to explain how the data support their model. This is manifest by their remark: “participation alone does little to resolve conflicts … participation is only effective if it allows users to exert influence toward both conflict generation and its constructive resolution.” Moreover, at the end of their study participation was shown to be directly negatively related to conflict resolution, but at the same time indirectly positively related to conflict resolution! Is ‘conflict resolution’ a meaningful variable, i.e. would it lead to more successful and/or better systems?

In summary, the empirically based evaluative literature is inconclusive. And the methodological flaws inherent in many of the studies lead one to be extremely sceptical. This is a point which has not escaped the attention of Ives and Olson [23]. The y state:

"User involvement has been weakly operationalised. Heavy reliance on perceptual self-report measures casts suspicion on study results. Scale reliability and validity have been almost universally ignored".

And they also note that there are no reported examples where user involvement has been observed and measured over time in a longitudinal study.

Interestingly, the literature on participative decision making from sociology and organisational theory compares favourably with the literature on participative systems design. Participation in decision making is much better established and the literature is far richer. Organisational theorists have performed considerable research on participation and concluded that contingency models are particularly useful in explaining when and where participation in decision making is valuable (c.f. [27]).

## 2.2. Research Results - The Empirical Study

It was realised, at the outset, that a considerable effort would be devoted to learning about participative systems design – for this reason the modified case study approach was adopted.

An interview guide was constructed (see Appendix) to elicit information about the nature of the participative systems design exercise(s) in each organisation. The questions were divided into three categories: background information, substantive information, and evaluation. Background information questions were used to obtain an understanding of the nature of the participative design. Substantive information questions were for the assessment of participative systems design. Evaluation questions were to obtain interviewee perceptual data on the value of participative design.

Respondents were given freedom to respond as they wished. Some interviewees answered the questions in an abbreviated fashion. Others, used each question as a springboard to other points which seemed related. Such impromptu revelations were encouraged. Notes were jotted down and used to write-up the interview in a more comprehensive fashion. No tape recordings were used and all material was treated as private. Organisation details were also kept in confidence.

Interviews usually lasted between one and three hours. An informal, rather than formal, atmosphere was attempted (as it was felt that more openness on the part of the interviewee would be achieved). When and where possible, a second meeting (to attempt to build up rapport with the participants) was encouraged.

In all, some sixty interviews were conducted in twenty organisations. The number and variety of interviewees varied from senior management in a number of organisations, to management services/data processing department managers, to systems analysts, to users and participative systems design participants and others. The study also interviewed the sponsor of the participative systems design exercise(s) although in a few organisations, this individual did not exist, could not be remembered, or was not available.

## 3. Findings

1. Participative systems design is a very complicated phenomena. This fact became clear when the study had to decide what organisations it was looking for. In attempting to find suitable organisations, the study faced the exceedingly difficult task of defining, or, at least, operationalising the concept 'participative systems design'. When users participate in systems design or technology introduction, the resulting system is not necessarily 'participatively developed' – all systems have some degree of user involvement. Thus, definitions of user involvement such as that previously offered [41] are much too broad to be effective. Participative systems design tends to connote an approach where the users must take the lead in the systems design process. Yet this also misses the mark.

It appears that the amount of participation is only a small element of what constitutes participative systems design. It differs from conventional design in two primary areas:

a) Content of participation. Participative systems design tends to broaden the scope of what is being designed or introduced. Instead of addressing only the technical characteristics it also tries to introduce social and job considerations. This is the so-called “socio-technical system ideal”.

b) Involvement. Participative systems seeks to broaden involvement in such a way that more users (or potential users) are involved. Mumford [36] has argued adamantly for a ‘consensus’ and ‘responsible’ form of participation which allows all the users to participate – or at least make their wishes known.

The clear separation of technical from social considerations is an extreme case: it is more a spectrum, with technical at one end and social at the other. Participative systems design seeks to balance these two. See Fig. 1.

It can be noted that participative systems design deals with both content or subject matter of concern and user involvement. All the above approaches require user involvement to some degree or another, but is the content (and perhaps context) of that involvement which differentiates non-participative from participative approaches. In fact, it is probably correct to state that participative systems design is something of a misnomer in that it is not equal to user involvement.

The conclusion that participative systems design differs from user involvement has a consequence on the user involvement literature. If the literature is scanned closely, it is noted that the content of user involvement was rarely specified and the suspicion is that the content was of a technical nature. If this is true, i.e. if the content of user participation is of a technical nature, then the value of participation, measured in terms of system quality, user attitudes, system use, etc., will be insignificant or indeterminate. This hypothesis does seem to be supported by the available empirical literature. However, even if this hypothesis is true, no assessment can be made of the validity of the alternative hypothesis: if the content of user participation is of a more social nature then the value of participation will be significant. This alternative hypothesis was the subject of the research.

2. Meaningful cross-sectional comparisons between organisations using participative systems design is problematic. Although a considerable number of organisations use participative systems design, meaningful comparisons are difficult. This is because:

a) organisations are at various stages of use of participative design.

b) organisations have used it on a wide variety of applications. One common aspect was that the system being designed (or the technology being introduced) was viewed as significant, i.e. participative design was not used for simple applications.

c) the organisations varied considerably: from large multi-national to small; from private to public. They had nothing in common, except they felt a socio-technical balance was vital for systems to be introduced successfully.

d) no common sponsor surfaced. The management services manager, dp manager, or some other manager were often cited; usually, the sponsor was reasonably senior. It usually was one person's idea; the ideas was generally introduced through informal means, e.g., reading about it in a journal or newspaper, word of mouth, informal contact, or attending a seminar.

e) actual user involvement varied considerably. Some had large design groups. Interaction between design group members and other users was reported to be high but this was one of the few commonalities across organisations. The level of users, the type of involvement, and the subject matter varied so much that it was often hard to make any meaningful statement about actual participation.

f) individuals who were involved in early exercises had long since left their organisations; thus it was difficult to obtain the desired data from these organisations. It was expressed by some that involvement gave these individuals new skills that made them desirable to other organisations, and led to their departure.

g) many of the organisations were working on their first system. It was therefore difficult to know how the exercise would end.

The difficulty in making meaningful comparisons was exacerbated by the following fact.

3. No evaluative work has been attempted on any of the participatively designed systems. It appears that the method is undertaken as an article of faith or assumed value, but no quantitative evidence was given to support this belief. No organisation had engaged in any formal post implementation assessment of the system's value. (This result may not be as surprising as it seems. Few systems analysis and design methodologies are ever really assessed by organisations.) There are at least four reasons why this might be so:

a) it is exceedingly difficult to actually assess the value of the system. And there is enough dissimilarity to make any comparison formidable.

b) the real costs of participative systems design are high in terms of time, effort, and resources. These are quantifiable and might easily swamp any tangible benefits, and if this is true, an organisation may fear than an assessment would shed an unfavourable light on the exercise.

c) an organisation may feel that it does not have the time to assess the value of participative design. Formal assessment is time and resource consuming and more informal means may be chosen.

d) some organisations may have felt that they did not have the expertise to evaluate it, and thus did not.

These reasons are not, of course, mutually exclusive nor exhaustive.

4. Participative systems design was praised almost universally by those who had been involved with it. Of the sixty interviews, virtually everyone had the highest regard for the approach. It was preferred over any other approach, particularly by users who felt it opened up the system design process to them. Most felt that the quality of the produced system was high, though such perceptual measures often exhibit a 'halo effect'.

Because of the suspected bias, more emphasis might be better placed on a potentially more neutral observer, e.g., a non-sponsoring data processing department manager. But attitudes toward participative systems design are not markedly different even when the observer is neutral – results are positive but with more caution.

In one organisation, there was strong disagreement about the success of a participatively designed and implemented system. The sponsor and one user who were interviewed, felt the system quality was high and that the whole exercise had been a success. One of the systems analysts termed it a 'disaster'. He felt the whole exercise was futile; it led to a system which was technically poor and had to be re-designed.

5. Organisations, in general, do not choose to use participative systems design a second time. In the light of the positive attitudes expressed by most respondents in the study this result appears surprising; one might expect that it would be further used. Not one organisation who had used ETHICS have used it a second time. (Some organisations have used Checklands' [11,12] 'Soft Methodology' more than once however). In fairness it must be stated that the majority of the organisations participating in the study are presently using ETHICS for the first time. Thus this result is not as damaging to the proponent as it may seem. It does not, however, explain why it has not been used again in organisations which had used it in the past. The following may be the explanation:

a) Organisations have not had a chance to use it a second time. Maybe they will use it in the future. Some respondents gave this reply, but it was not clear why they had not used it again.

b) Organisations have not found a second case suitable for it. This argument points to the fact that it may not always be appropriate. Precisely what constitutes a 'suitable case' could not be satisfactorily determined in the study; one respondent felt that participative systems design was necessary for those systems which were highly complex and could not be fully understood by systems analysts.

Those systems which were not very complex and could be understood by a systems analyst, probably could be designed without a participative approach. Support for this position comes from Edstrom [15] who noted the 'degree of structuredness' was related to the need for user involvement – the more unstructured the task the more involvement needed.

c) Certain key individuals may have vetoed it. This argument suggests that it is not universally favoured. If (a) there were enough who felt this way, or (b) they were in a position of authority, then participative design would not have been used a second time. There is reason to believe this may have been the case in one organisation – the senior systems analyst did not like the method, he felt the system was poorly designed, took a great deal of time and effort, and was the cause of numerous battles.

d) It may not be organisationally acceptable if it does not fit conventional organisational norms and/or ideology. Organisations have evolved in a Weberian fashion embracing the ideals of Taylorism. This has led to organisations that are highly bureaucratic and rational and not amenable to change. Organisations therefore are conservative, and change procedures so they differ minimally from existing methods. Lindblom [30] has referred to this process as 'muddling through'. Selznick [49] wrote about another process, 'cooptation', which organisations use if muddling through proves ineffective. This is "the process of absorbing of absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organisation as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence". Alternatively, an organisation can choose to do nothing. One of the respondents felt participative design, although a very valuable approach, was doomed to failure because it was "too foreign to most managers in organisations".

e) It increases the overall complexity of implementation. No one individual perceives the totality of the complexity, it is only later (looking at the complete picture) that the realisation of its complexity is apparent. Participative design, involving many more people on many more topics, increases the amount of communication necessary, exacerbating an already communication overloaded situation. Thus, it can take seemingly simple problems and turn them into major undertakings.

f) Participative systems design seems to need two key individuals – a sponsor and a facilitator. One may not have been available for a second usage. Because participative design is not a small undertaking and the resources which have to be committed to the process are usually felt to be considerable, the sponsor must have either a senior position in the organisation and/or command the respect of senior organisational management. Some participative approaches, e.g. ETHICS, require a facilitator to start the process, educate the participants, oversee the process, and be available to help in conflict resolution, etc. A number of respondents said that the first exercise had the involvement and support of an external facilitator. Once the exercise was finished the facilitator left and the gap has never been filled.

This list is not exhaustive. There are however other possible explanations based around: (a) its failure to be cost/effective, (b) that it discloses many informal aspects about an organisation (see [4,5,6]), and (c) that it potentially disturbs the power balance [21].

To summarise, the results of this study indicate a number of interesting aspects about participative systems design. Some of these may have been suspected by a number of people for quite some time, others are probably more surprising. For example, many people felt that it was more complicated than discussed in the literature. This is clearly supported. The lack of similarity on what participative design is used for, who sponsors it, who is involved, may have been unexpected. No organisation has formally evaluated the results of its use. None have used it a second time, in spite of the favourable attitudes of most participants.

## 4. Conclusions

Participative systems design, for all intuitive appeal still cannot be shown to be the solution to systems design and/or technology introduction. Much still needs to be done. The study has confirmed some suspicions but raised new ones; e.g., the empirical literature has been virtually totally unhelpful, much is contradictory, and woefully unsound in its research approach. And the focus of attention is on user involvement which this study has asserted is not the same as participative design. Thus, the inevitable conclusion is that the available literature offers little to our understanding of participative systems design.

The study has helped to increase our knowledge of participative design. It is far more complicated than the conventional literature suggests. The study has suggested that the name ‘participative design’ is a misnomer; it does not connote what it is.

There appears little similarity among organisations using participative design; when it is used, for what types of cases, who is involved, who is the sponsor, etc.

Organisations have not formally evaluated their use of participative design, though there is widespread positive feeling about it. Moreover, few organisations have ever used it a second time.

It is clear that our understanding of participative systems design is still fairly limited and elementary. Further research is needed to explore a number of issues which have been raised. The research provides some knowledge, albeit limited, about participative design.
