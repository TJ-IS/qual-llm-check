# The Incremental Benefits of Changes in the Timeliness of Information

Willis R. Greer, Jr.

Professor of Accounting, Department of Administrative Science, Naval Postgraduate School, Monterey, CA 93943, USA

and

Dean H. Kropp

Associate Professor of Business Administration, Dartmouth College, Hanover, NH 03755, USA

The managers of all purposeful organizations rely on historical information as an aid to decision making. Information enables managers to formulate expectations about the future. Less timely information is generally inferior because the manager's expectations will contain greater error; and the operating results obtained may therefore be less satisfactory. The timeliness of information often can be improved by incurring incremental systems costs. However, the degree to which such improvements will produce benefits is uncertain. This research uses modeling to explore the relationship between information timeliness and the benefits derived by implementing optimal decisions based upon the information. The most significant finding is that the shape of this relationship is dependent upon the scope of the action set available to the decision maker. If a continuous range of options is available (such as a pricing decision), then the relationship is linear. If only a few discrete options are present (as in linear programming) the relationship is more complex but describable. The decision maker is therefore found to be in a position to offer guidance to the systems designer which will facilitate enhancement of the benefit/cost characteristics of the organization's information resource.

Keywords: Information attributes, Information system design, Information timeliness, Benefit analysis, Marginal benefits, Decision support, Timeliness, Timeliness benefits, Timeliness decisions.

The underlying theory of equating marginal benefit to marginal cost is simple, but applications in this context are less so. One complication is that information has value only as and if it affects a decision. The extent to which potential value exists is therefore highly environmental (and often transcendental). Furthermore, a user's ability to describe the attribute/benefit relationship in terms which are authentically useful to the designer is often limited.

Under these circumstances, it is understandable that information systems design has become an evolutionary process. The use of intelligent heuristics substitutes for our inability to model the problems with some precision.

This paper concentrates on studying the incremental benefits associated with changes in the performance level of one attribute – timeliness. The underlying assumption is that the timeliness of information is at least partially controllable by incurring certain costs. Our objectives are; (1) to state what data may be useful to the heuristic designer of information systems in attempting to assess the inevitable tradeoffs, and (2) to sharpen the perceptions of both the user and the designer to facilitate more sharply focused dialogue.

## 2. Definitional Background

A user-specifier of information systems can completely describe a system in terms of the attributes possessed by the information it generates. Therefore, it is not surprising that much of the more practitioner-oriented systems literature has dealt with questions of attribute identification, attribute performance control and systems value enhancement through attribute control. Since the benefits obtain solely from use, one can regard all systems which produce information with identical attributes as providing identical benefits.

Numerous authors have identified timeliness as an information attribute [9,15,17,25]. An “appropriate level” of performance of the timeliness attribute is held to be of such importance in some systems that timeliness is identified as a major systems design objective [1,2,8,14,16,23]. Other authors [19,20,21,22] more properly emphasize that the objective of an information system is to support the decision maker. They point to timeliness as a critical attribute of information intended for this purpose, and then argue that the value of an information system can be enhanced by improving the performance level of timeliness. This paper, of course, follows the latter position.

Consider a series of operating periods, each of which ends in a decision moment. During these moments, information users make decisions affecting the organization's actions during the next operating period. Any changes in the costs of the ongoing operating process take place during the instant in time just after the decision, (i.e., at the start of the operating period). The progression is shown in Figure 1.

A decision maker at decision moment “0” is making a decision which irrevocably specifies organizational actions to be taken during “operating period 1”. Ideally, the decision maker should have precise knowledge of the economic relationships which will exist during “period 1” before the decision must be made. However, such perfect knowledge is usually unobtainable, so the decision maker must rely on a forecast.

In general, decision makers are assumed to select from the range of all possible actions the one they feel will maximize the satisfaction of the organization's objectives. For simplicity, assume profit maximization is the sole objective, so the decision maker seeks to elect that action which results in the highest profit.

Now an optimal action is usually predicated or conditioned on the anticipated value(s) of some variable(s) or parameter(s) on which the attainment of the objective depends. For example, if a decision maker is re-setting the selling price of a product, the cost of the materials necessary to produce the product is a relevant variable, and the decision (selling price) will be based (in part) on the decision maker's anticipation of its value.

Clearly, if the actual value of the conditioning variable turns out to be different from the anticipated one, then the decision (in retrospect) will not have been optimal. That is, the level of profit actually achieved will be lower than the maximum. The more accurate the forecast, the lower the opportunity cost.

The general theme we explore is: If historical information is reported to management on a more timely basis, the result will be less average forecasting error; therefore there will be a tangible reduction in opportunity cost. However, improvements in timeliness can usually be achieved only with higher processing cost; and, again, the information system designer must balance the cost of timeliness against the value of reduced opportunity costs.

The functional relationships among timeliness, opportunity cost and processing cost have not been explored in any formal way: our objective is to lay the conceptual foundation for this exploration. Thus we do not provide answers, but demonstrate a method for approaching the problem.

## 3. Initial Analysis

Suppose a decision is made by considering an operating cost. In its most general form, we can consider the decision to be a function of the cost. The decision maker produces a “best” decision, $D^{*}$ , based on the best available forecast of the cost, $\hat{C}$ . The decision maker’s output is therefore $D^{*}| \hat{C}$ .

The actual profit consequence of this decision for operating period 1 will be the result of the decision interacting with the actual cost, C. In general form, we have actual profit, $\pi$ , as a function of the decision and the actual cost:

$$
\pi = g (D ^ {*} | \hat {C}, C).
$$

Had the decision maker possessed perfect foresight, the decision would have depended on C instead of $\hat{C}$ , and optimum profit, $\pi^{*}$ , would have been achieved:

$$
\pi^ {*} = g (D ^ {*} | C, C).
$$

The difference between actual and optimum profit, $\pi^{*}-\pi$ , is sometimes referred to as the “opportunity cost variance”, see [4,18,24]. The opportunity cost variance is a measure of the potential benefit from improved decision making. The role that timely information plays in enhancing this benefit is to permit better forecasting of C, thereby reducing the difference between the forecast and actual values of cost, $|\hat{C}-C|$ .

Now suppose the movement of C follows a martingale process with period-to-period changes which are normally distributed with a known variance, $\sigma^{2}$ . For simplicity, assume there is no drift, so that the process has a mean shift of zero. Under these circumstances, the best forecast of C is the most recently measured value of C.

The decision maker (still at time 0) is making a decision for operating period one. The best information would be provided in a report conveying the actual value of C for the most recent operating period. Denote this information as having timeliness N = 1. The resulting forecasting error would be normally distributed with mean zero and standard deviation $\sigma$ .

But now suppose that the most timely information available to the decision maker at decision moment zero is for an operating period P periods earlier, so that $N = P + 1$ . Assuming errors are independent and identically distributed, the distribution of total forecasting error is still normal with a mean of zero, but with a standard deviation of $\sigma\sqrt{N}$ . The benefits deriving from the timeliness attribute can therefore be expressed by quantifying the expected value of the opportunity cost as a function of N,

$$
E (\pi^ {*} - \pi) = h (N).
$$

Our objective is to provide the system designer with insights into the nature of this function. Under what circumstances is the function increasing? Is it monotonic? Does its slope increase or decrease with N? What conditions lead to the conclusions?

One other definitional matter remains. Since the results of all timeliness analyses are specific to a particular problem, the opportunity cost itself may not be interesting - problems change with time. Therefore, the relative changes in expected benefits as a function of $N$ will become our focus. We will express results in terms of percentages of the opportunity cost found at some arbitrary limit in time. We established this limit at ten periods. Thus, the range of analysis will be restricted to $0 < N \leq 10$ , and the dependent variable for $h(n)$ will become

$$
\frac {E (\pi^ {*} - \pi)}{E (\pi^ {*} - \pi) _ {N = 1 0}} \times 1 0 0.
$$

## 4. Timeliness Research to Date

Several investigators have dealt with timeliness issues [5,10,11,12]. In [10], the authors examined the value of changes in the timeliness of demand information, but in a somewhat different context. They began with a pre-operating estimate of demand (or production volume). Part-way through the period, they introduced a correction to the original estimate. The timing of the correction formed their basis for a definition of “timeliness.”

While this work was valuable, it did not provide guidance for designers of information systems. Such systems typically report information used by preparers of forecasts rather than information used to make corrections to existing forecasts.

Hilton [7] addressed the question of cost information timeliness by using the same cost-volume-profit. in-process update scenario advanced in [10]. His conclusion, which was rather restrictive in its applicability, was that the value of information increased linearly with respect to timeliness. This finding implies that the incremental benefits of timeliness improvements are invariant with respect to N. However, Hilton is quick to point out the contextual narrowness leading to this conclusion.

## 5. Context Theory

Another relevant area of inquiry is termed "context theory." Merkhofer [13] developed one of the more useful formalizations in this field.

In particular, he introduced and defined the concept of “decision flexibility” as relating to the size of the choice set available to the user. He showed that information (in general) has its greatest potential value when a choice set is very large – when there is “undiminished flexibility.” The value of information tends to decline as decision flexibility diminishes – as the choice set decreases in size – until the value reaches zero when the decision choice set contains only one possible action.

These concepts can be used to define “continuous decision space” (undiminished flexibility, or an infinite choice set) and “discrete decision space” (a choice set containing a relatively small number of possible decision actions). The type of decision space turns out to have a profound impact on the incremental benefits of changes in information.

## 6. Decision Scenarios

Based on Merkhofer's work, we focused on two example decision settings. One can be characterized as set in continuous decision space – the other in discrete decision space. Then we examined the relative effects of changes in the timeliness of cost information. The example decisions are shown in Fig. 2.

To demonstrate how opportunity costs can be calculated, suppose the latest information report showed C = 50, so that the indicated decision was $Q^{*} = 5$ (or Price = 100). If the actual value of C turned out to be, say, 70, the actual profit would be.

$$
\pi = g (5, 7 0) = 5 0.
$$

Had the true value of C, 70, been known ex ante, the decision would have been $Q^{*}=4$ (or Price = 110), so profit would have been,

$$
\pi^ {*} = g (4, 7 0) = 6 0.
$$

Therefore, the opportunity cost would be 10.

Since the differences between expected and actual values of C are stochastic, the opportunity cost,

$$
g (D ^ {*} | C, C) - g (D ^ {*} | \hat {C}, C),
$$

is also stochastic. However, the opportunity cost does have a unique expected value for any given reporting lag, N, and any given standard deviation of the period-to-period change in C, $\sigma$ . This result will be explained later.

## 7. The Results

In the case of the pricing problem, it is possible to obtain results analytically. To begin, recall that,

$$
\sigma_ {i (i - 1)} = \sigma \sqrt {N}.
$$

The opportunity cost is (following from Fig. 3),

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \pi^ {*} - \pi & = (1 5 0 - C) \left(\frac {1 5 0 - C}{2 0}\right) - 1 0 \left(\frac {1 5 0 - C}{2 0}\right) ^ {2} \\ & - (1 5 0 - C) \left(\frac {1 5 0 - \hat {C}}{2 0}\right) + 1 0 \left(\frac {1 5 0 - \hat {C}}{2 0}\right) ^ {2}, \end{array}
$$

which simplifies to,

$$
\pi^ {*} - \pi = \frac {(\hat {C} - C) ^ {2}}{4 0}.
$$

It can be shown that the expected opportunity cost, as a function of N, is;

$$
E (\pi^ {*} - \pi) = \frac {N o ^ {2}}{4 0}.
$$

Examining this result, it is clear that the denominator is simply a constant which reflects the sensitivity of quantity demanded to price. This is marketplace-determined and (short of product changes) is external to the firm. The variance of the period-to-period movement in per-unit cost, $\sigma^{2}$ , is likely also to be externally determined and outside the realm of the decision maker's control. Assume it is also a constant. This means the only controllable parameter that influences the expected opportunity loss is N - the timeliness attribute.

A simple derivative shows that incremental changes in timeliness produce incremental changes in opportunity cost at a constant rate, $\sigma^{2}/40$ . Thus, regardless of the current level of the timeliness attribute, we can make equal reductions in opportunity cost by making incremental changes in the attribute.

In a real organizational setting there may be many operating processes and information with numerous degrees of timeliness. But these results indicate that, so long as decision space is continuous, a system analyst seeking timeliness improvements should feel free to consider any and all of them. The optimal selection would depend only on the systems cost of making the changes and on the parameters of the process.

Notice that while this particular cost function was linear, the same conclusion would apply with a quadratic cost function; introduction of a quadratic term in the cost equation would simply modify the quadratic term in the profit equation. The main requirements are that C, the stochastic cost, can only multiply a linear term, and that terms involving other powers of C can not appear. As the authors of [10] point out, the quadratic can serve as an excellent approximation of higher-order functions over a limited range. As a result, these findings may therefore be applicable to many (if not most) policy decisions in continuous decision space.

In the linear programming problem setting, we had to resort to simulation. These results are very different. In fact, the shape of the incremental benefit curve depends on the proximity of the last observed value of C to one which is critical to the decision (and on $\sigma$ ). To illustrate the nature of the results, we explore three cases: $\hat{C}=28$ , 33 and 38, all with $\sigma=3$ .

Consider first the outcome for the $\hat{C}=28$ case, as shown in Table 1. As N grows, the opportunity cost rises at an increasing rate. Thus, under these circumstances the incremental benefit associated with improvements in the timeliness of information is greater for older (relatively untimely) information than for more current (relatively timely) information.

This result is not too difficult to understand. The last observed cost lies 12 units below the first value (C=40) which is critical to the decision. This means the critical value lies four standard deviations from the last observed value. If N is small, it is extremely unlikely that a wrong decision will result; hence the opportunity cost is very low. Since $\sigma_{(\hat{C}-C)}=\sigma\sqrt{N}$ , the value of N would have to be at least four to place C as few as two standard deviations from a value which is critical to the decision. But as N grows further, the likelihood of a wrong decision also grows, and so does the opportunity cost – at an increasing rate.

In Table 2 the analysis is repated for $\hat{C} = 38$ . In this case the results look very different. True, the absolute amount of opportunity cost is higher, but this is to be expected because the wrong decision would be made much more frequently. Also, the over-increasing numbers in the "Percent of Maximum" column come as no surprise. However, the incremental benefit of timely information now declines as $N$ grows.

To understand this point, envision a situation in which the mean of the cost distribution is the same as the single critical value for a decision. No change in the variance of the distribution would impact the opportunity cost because the decision maker would always be wrong 50% of the time. When, as in the present case, the mean is close to this point (say less than one standard deviation away) the area “across the line” approaches 50% as N grows only slightly. The opportunity cost therefore soon stops growing; or at least grows at a much slower rate. This implies that higher incremental benefits are associated with improvements in the timeliness of information which is already relatively timely – just the reverse of the finding in the first case.

## 8. Conclusions and Implications

This research carries several implications for practicing designers of computerized information systems. It should be clear that in many cases improvements in timeliness are beneficial to decision processes in terms of value added. However, the incremental benefits must be weighed against the costs of making them.

The key to understanding how incremental benefits vary across a range of timeliness values relates to the decision flexibility available to the information user. For example, when the user has a great deal of flexibility, as in the continuous decision space pricing example, the benefits associated with improved timeliness appear to increase at a linear rate. That is, incremental timeliness improvements, for both highly current information and older information, appear to have invariant benefits.

When decision space is discrete, our linear programming example demonstrates that the relationship between timeliness and incremental benefits can become considerably more complex. To approach the problem intelligently, one must determine by how much an operating cost would have to change to affect a decision. It will probably be impossible to make precise measurements. However, based on the example, we offer the following suggestions as guidelines:

1. If the estimated operating cost lies within, say, one standard deviation of a critical value, timeliness improvements for relatively timely information would probably produce the greatest incremental benefit.

2. If the expected cost lies more than three standard deviations from a critical value, timeliness improvements for information which is currently produced in a very untimely manner would probably produce the greater benefits.

3. If the expected cost lies somewhere between these extremes, improvements in the timeliness of moderately current information hold the greatest potential for producing benefits.

Obviously these guidelines are strictly applicable only to the examples we studied. However, continued experience with such models should permit system designers to identify more easily the information having greatest potential for improving system performance.
