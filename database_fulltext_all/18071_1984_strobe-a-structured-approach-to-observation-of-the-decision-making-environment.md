# STROBE: A Structured Approach to Observation of the Decision-Making Environment

Kenneth E. Kendall

Department of Management, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lincoln, NE 68588-0400, USA

and

Julie E. Kendall

Department of Speech Communication, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lincoln, NE 68588-0329, USA

Information Analysts must be able to gather information and interpret it reliably to promote good organizational decision making. The Information Analyst has three ways of obtaining this information: (1) interviewing, (2) investigation of hard data, and (3) observation. Until recently, observation has only been accomplished subconsciously. This paper describes how STRuctured OB servation of the Environment (STROBE) was used to aid in determining the information requirements of a regional blood service organization. STROBE was developed from a methodology used in film criticism. It is conceptualized by drawing a parallel between the concrete and abstract elements in a film, and similar elements found in an organization. It is then applied to the blood service organization and examples are provided for both concrete and abstract elements. These examples demonstrate that structured observation provides information unobtainable through interviewing and investigation.

Keywords: Management information systems, information analyst, information gathering, observation, structured observation, mise-en-scene analysis, interviewing, investigation.

## 1. Introduction

Whereas interviewing and investigation have received ample attention [9,11,13,33], observation has been neglected. Authors encourage IAs to observe, but rarely describe how to do so. The only systematic observation framework specifically targeted to the IA is a guide to understanding body language, by Jenkins and Johnson [14]. This allows the IA to observe and interpret different channels of information during interviews. However, this paper goes beyond body language to include also interpretation of organizational surroundings, and shows how structured observation was used to analyze the information requirements of a Midwestern regional blood service organization. STRuctured OBservation of the Environment (STROBE) is reconceptualized from a type of film criticism referred to as mise-en-scene analysis. While another article [16] develops and justifies the complete observational framework, here we focus on providing in-depth illustrations of how the framework is applied in actual organizational situations. STROBE is grounded in the idea that many components in an organization's internal environment are capable of subtly influencing decision makers [12,15,26].

Briefly, STROBE, as borrowed from film criticism, calls for observing the influence of seven elements on organizational decision makers. The organizational elements are divided into three concrete and four abstract elements. When film critics apply mise-en-scene analysis they systematically examine selected shots in the film. Actors and their costumes, set decor, camera angle, lighting and editing, as well as other elements are all considered for their contributions to the intended meaning of the film. Nichols [25] when writing of mise-en-scene film analysis highlights the:

“Value and importance of a visual reading of film that takes careful note of (mise-en-scene) … since these elements may often after or reverse information communicated by speech or narrative code, sometimes called the plot.”

With STROBE, the organizational equivalents of these elements are observed by the IA in order to confirm, reverse, modify, or supplement information gained through more traditional methods, about decision maker's needs.

As in films, internal organizational elements provide important information – they may serve to alter or reverse what has been learned through the organizational “storyline” (interviews and hard data). Information collected through STROBE helps the IA in providing a truer fit between informational requirements of decision makers and the proposed system.

The IA must systematically observe the seven specific elements of Table 1, which illustrates the parallel between elements of mise-en-scene and those of STROBE. This analogy, and especially the one-to-one correspondence between filmic and organizational elements, is useful since it facilitates acceptance of the methodology as it applies to organizations. Rather than a mere checklist of items for observation, the seven elements represent a framework for systematic observation.

The interpretations made here are based on research done by others on the influence of each element. The purpose of STROBE is not to replicate these studies, but to integrate them into a single framework of use to the IA. A reliability and validity assessment of STROBE can be found in another of our articles $[17]$ .

## 2. Applying STROBE in a Regional Blood Center

The project described here helped ascertain informational needs of four primary decision makers in a large, generally well-managed Midwestern Red Cross regional blood service organization. Our previous experience in another large blood center was unstructured. In the example that follows, STROBE was used to confirm, reverse, modify, or supplement the “narrative” of the decision makers as evidenced in interviews and questionnaires.

The four primary decision makers (as depicted in the organization chart of Fig. 1) were: administrator of the blood center, administrative assistant, medical director (equal in rank to the administrator of the blood center) and chapter manager (the chief executive of the Red Cross regional organization). The directors of donor resources and blood collections were also considered indirectly.

## 2.1. Office Design, Space, and Location

Sets and background are analyzed by film critics in order to understand location, status of characters within them, and the period of time portrayed. Location of office in relation to others, size of office, types of equipment and furnishings can all be considered as equivalent to sets and background.

Within the blood service organization, two groups were directly dependent on one another. They were donor resources (recruiters) and blood collections (mostly nurses). Interviews and questionnaires revealed an overall attitude that neither department understood the information needs of the other, though an interface was crucial.

Donor resources were primarily concerned with getting information about potential blood donors. Collections needed much more explicit information about these people – health, weight, temperature, drugs used. With this information they then decide eligibility to give blood. Although the nurses were necessarily dependent on the donor recruiters for donors, friction was caused by the disparate informational requirements and standards of each department.

Observation of office location served to highlight and deepen our appreciation of the differences between the two departments and to support our final recommendations for formation of a liaison committee to share interdepartmental information. What became initially apparent was that the departments were separated physically by: (1) extensive travel requirements (nurses traveled for almost a week at a time), and (2) two flights of stairs (nurses were on the “main” floor, while the donor recruiters were situated in the “garden level” or basement).

Thus the differing office locations strengthened our impression that it was difficult for such highly differentiated departments to share vital information. Their physical setup confirmed their positions of subordinate and superior. Also, the difficulty of communication due to the nature of the nurses' jobs (setting up mobile blood donating sites throughout a four-state area) made it hard for them to meet with donor recruiters and share decision-making information.

Without this additional observation information, the interview and questionnaire data were like a puzzle with a piece missing. Both departments felt as if they had different objectives, and each felt that the other department did not understand their function.

Another example of observing office placement is found for the chapter manager. He maintained that the employees ought to visit him and share their problems. He stated, during interviews and group meetings with subordinates, that his “door was always open.” However, the organizational elements observed during this narrative almost completely reversed an open-door premise, as the office was two floors above those of the subordinates. It was tucked at the end of a narrow hallway, and difficult to reach. The open-door policy, proclaimed as the rule, was truly not the case. The office was “inaccessible.” Allen [1], Knapp [18], Pile [27] and Rogers and Agarwala-Rogers [29] all provide studies discussing the impact of office location on information exchange and organizational relationships.

The use of equipment within the office can also provide clues to the IA. The narrative code given by management indicated that because of its leadership as a large regional blood center serving over 100 hospitals, the center acquired the most up-to-date and sophisticated equipment. However, this was contradicted by observing the office of the regional medical director, where an old blood donating table was serving as a storage shelf for medical journals.

This apparent disparity was a clue to the IAs. Further investigation showed that the chapter manager was aware that non-blood functional areas (disaster, service to military families, etc.) perceived the blood-related areas as the best-treated of the entire Red Cross program. They reasoned that increased costs in blood service operations could be incorporated into the pricing structure for blood processing. Any increased costs in their areas would have to be covered through increased community fund raising. The chapter manager's policy was intended to equalize the status of service areas by holding down purchases of equipment for non-laboratory blood areas. This was later confirmed by directly questioning the chapter manager on this point. Korda [19] gives insights into interpreting the presence or absence of specific office equipment in organizational settings.

## 2.2. Office Lighting, Color, and Graphics

Film analysis of color and lighting is very close to the interpretation of those elements in organizational settings. Lighting and color are considered together because color is a property of light and both influence organizational members. The effects of lighting, corporate symbols, and graphics must all be observed. Birren [3,4], is one of the best sources on interpreting light and color in the environment and their influence on organizational members. Knapp [18] and Sharpe [34] also discuss the affect of lighting on human interaction.

The American National Red Cross has a commonly acknowledged image of conservatism and dependability. Interviews with decision makers indicated an interest in maintaining a stable environment while progressing at a steady pace. The blood center's environment was dominated by the display of the corporate Red Cross symbol. It was displayed in oil paintings, posters, and needlework hung on office walls, on paper coffee cups, nurses' uniforms, and staff automobiles.

Innovative decisions designed to take the blood program in new directions were subtly tempered by strong reminders of the past. In this illustration, STROBE confirmed the narrative code as revealed through interviews and the conservative mass media image of the American National Red Cross.

## 2.3. Clothing Worn by Decision Makers

Costumes worn in a film are an outer reflection of characters' personalities. Organizational clothing reflects self-image of employees and their functional roles; it furnishes clues about how decisionmaking roles are conceptualized and carried out.

Three of the four key decision makers in the organization dressed very similarly. All three were male, and wore variations of the business uniform (the suit and tie).

The most formal was the medical director, the least formal was his organizational peer, the administrator of the blood center, who wore sport coats. The chapter manager dressed more moderately than either. This similarity in dress was noted and reversed the idea (gained through interviews) that the three conceptualized their roles as decision makers very differently.

The chapter manager (according to others) was authoritarian and uninterested in democratic or participative decision making. If that were the case, his use and need for quantities of information would be much greater than an administrator, who conceived of administrative duties as participative. However, the chapter manager did not rigidly adhere to a formal type of dress. We observed that he was not as authoritarian or undemocratic and role-rigid as others stated.

The medical director dressed the most formally. His formality of dress was interpreted as representing a more rigid differentiation of roles. He explained that he visualized himself in an advising, rather than commanding, capacity. His job was to educate regional physicians in the proper use of blood and its components, and to oversee laboratory procedures. He expressed no desire to command the laboratory or nursing staff. Yet his clothing contradicted him. He clearly presented himself as an authority figure. People in Red Cross looked up to him and did not question his medical or administrative opinions. This alerted us to the fact that the medical director was interested in having more influence in decision making and having more information routed to him.

In contrast, the clothing of the administrator of the non-medical/technical aspects of the blood center confirmed his interviews: he also wore a suit and tie, but with casual variations (sport coats and slacks with a “western” appearance). He claimed to prize openness and good communication and to be interested in staff input to decisions. He maintained an easy-going, casual approach to management; observation of his clothing confirmed this.

The assistant administrator's clothing reflected a different decision making style. By wearing slacks and an unmatched blazer, she was more casual in dress than her superiors. The blazer was a sign of authority, but by wearing slacks, she showed she also identified with staff members. Observing this element, the IAs were faced with choosing which of the two positions (authoritative or participative)

more accurately characterized her needs.

Analysis of clothing helped provide insight on their information uses, needs, and requirements and improved the level of information that is usually dismissed as superficial or without impact or consequence to the IA. Organizational clothing is frequently associated with specific behaviors. See for example, Langner [20], Flugel [10], Leathers [21], and Molloy [23,24].

## 2.4. Abilities of Decision Makers

Film actors play assigned roles, which, through their lines, advance the story. In an organization, decision makers may be considered as actors. As actors may or may not possess ability to play a particular role, the organizational members may or may not be qualified decision makers.

One element that can be observed is a decision maker's ability to handle quantity and complexity of information. In the Red Cross, decision makers were found to have varying abilities. One index of quantity of information that served to illuminate abilities was the amount of paper on each decision maker's desk. Propst [28] discusses the necessity of maintaining a “generated work situation” of paper versus “the clean desk syndrome” and adds a photograph of Albert Einstein in his office surrounded by “meaningful clutter” to assure us that we are in good company.

The chapter manager had an extremely neat desk. Although he was to oversee numerous and diverse operations, his assistant's desk carried the bulk of paperwork. From observing his desk, it was necessary to reconsider some information gained in interviews. Some lower-level employees felt that all decisions had to be cleared with the chapter manager and that department heads were reticent to make decisions on their own. From observation of the quantity of paperwork on the chapter manager's desk it was possible to surmise that this was not the case. He preferred to delegate responsibility for decisions, hence the lack of paper on his desk.

The desks of the three blood administrators presented contrasts. The medical director had a desk piled high with paperwork. He continually requested more information (research reports, journals) and never complained of information overload. Additionally, he was interested in a new computerized blood inventory system which would inevitably result in more information to assimilate in decision making. Apparently, the medical director possessed the ability to use large quantities of information in decision making and exhibited the capability to use complex information.

The blood administrator fell somewhere in between the medical director and the chapter manager. His desk was cluttered with paperwork, yet it was information of a recent nature (in contrast to the medical director's journals, handbooks, etc.). The blood administrator had paperwork which seemed to be a relatively short-range, i.e., correspondence from national head-quarters or local organizations demanding fairly prompt action. He complained about excessive paperwork. His ability to deal with short and medium-range information in decision making seemed adequate.

The assistant administrator had a desk which was almost clear. The paperwork which remained on her desk had a short time-range, usually notes from daily phone conversations or correspondence from local or national organizations. This confirmed our interview data that she was new to administrative work. Although she was asked to prepare budgets and other reports months in advance, she dealt with information of a more immediate nature. Bariff and Lusk [2] and Schroder, Driver and Steufert [32] discuss complexity theory. This is a useful way to understand manager's problem formulation and information processing, as well as decision making. Burger [5] comments on the use of the desk as a workspace. DiSalvo [7] addresses information overload, while Rogers and Agarwala-Rogers [29] discuss use of accumulated materials.

## 2.5. Attention to Multiple Objectives

Film analysis examines the objects on which the director is focusing. Depth of field is the sharpness in front of and behind the plane of focus hence less depth of field emphasizes the importance of a single object or person. Objects compete for attention in a film, much as a number of conflicting objectives exist in the organization. Coping with multiple objectives simultaneously is analogous to greater depth of field, while concentrating on only one objective at a time is the equivalent of shallow depth of field.

Attention to multiple objectives can be observed in a number of ways. One way is to keep the important elements in mind while watching activities in a typical day. When attention is being paid to multiple objectives, time spent on each is distributed throughout the days and weeks. If only singular objectives are consistently addressed, the attention to objectives can be construed as narrow.

This was observed in the Red Cross case and informal comments served to confirm it. During a natural disaster, such as a flood, personnel from all departments are called to help. Normal objectives are eclipsed by attention paid to meeting new short-term objectives. But, it was observed that the blood center was often managed in this manner – management by crisis, such as attention to a single pressing objective (the need for a particular blood type) rather than balancing time spent among multiple objectives. One organizational member made the comment that long-range planning at the blood center would be welcome.

It thus became apparent that attention to multiple objectives was difficult because of the crisis-oriented nature of the blood industry. Administrators' information needs were not met. MacCrimmon [22] provides a useful overview of various methods for treating multiple objectives.

## 2.6. Cognitive Maps of Decision Makers

Camera angles supply the viewer with the director's intended perspective; they indicate superiority or inferiority of characters. The IA can conceptualize camera angle as the unique cognitive maps of organizational decision makers. Through observing reflections of a decision maker's cognitive map it is possible for the IA to understand how that person perceives the work environment.

During an interview with the blood administrator, we were repeatedly told that every staff member had input into planning decisions (e.g., budgetary decisions), yet staff members disagreed. When we requested that project information be passed to all staff members, distribution of memos stopped at department heads. The blood administrator's cognitive map of the communication network was clear. He assumed formal communications were passed down. In reality, general information was posted on bulletin boards and sometimes ignored. In this instance, observation of the blood administrator's cognitive map contradicted his narrative.

Another observation was made through use of an organization-wide survey of employee attitudes. Through the questionnaire, the chapter manager found that employees did not associate with people outside their department. In an effort to overcome this, he displayed pictures of employees from all functional areas on a bulletin board. However, the chapter manager's cognitive map was clear when the location of the bulletin board was observed: it was on the third floor where his office was located. The third floor was central to his cognitive map, however, most employee traffic was on other floors. Further discussion of organizational relationships as reflections of cognitive maps is found in Downs and Stea [8], and Sayles [30]. Cyert and March [6] also discuss organizational coalitions and their affect on members' perspectives.

## 2.7. Individual Versus Group Decision Makers

Film editing uses cuts or transitions from long shots to close-ups, etc. For the IA the number of actors in a shot corresponds to the number of people taking part in a decision. As editing creates smooth or uneven transitions, decision making may involve smooth or uneven interactive processes between individuals and groups.

It is important for the IA to be able to observe shifts in power between individuals and groups. Decision making an organizations is often a political process. With the ability to interpret how decisions are made and by whom, the IA is better able to predict information requirements.

In this organization, the task of scheduling bloodmobiles seemed to require a straightforward decision by donor recruiting staff responsible for recruiting a certain number of donors each week. However, it was possible to recognize that the decisions of where and when to schedule bloodmobiles were made by involving groups rather than individuals.

While we were observing a meeting for bloodmobile scheduling, the donor recruiter announced a month's tentative bloodmobile schedule. The process then began with nursing being highly interested in amending the schedule. Information that was not previously of interest became “crucial.” Donor recruiters are interested in the number of people who will donate blood at a particular site: the more people, the more successful the bloodmobile visit. The nurses in the collections department, however, feel that bloodmobile scheduling is not a donor resources department decision, since recruiters' objectives are too much at odds with theirs. The nurses have the following considerations: 1. If too many sites are scheduled in a week, nurses are away from the regional center for too many successive days; 2. If too many donors are recruited to one site, the assigned number of nurses will be overworked; 3. They are concerned with the eligibility of donors; and 4. They are concerned with the quality of blood donated.

These points were brought up by nurses during the bloodmobile scheduling meeting, but other departments had a stake in the decision. The coordinator of volunteers was concerned with the quality of volunteer help available at each site, because additional paid staff might be needed or the bloodmobile might need to be rescheduled to a time when the volunteer help would be available. The blood inventory manager also wanted to take part: she needed to be sure that the donors would match requests for blood types low in stock or outdating soon. Schein [31] provides other detailed observational methods which may aid the IA in following the dynamic shifts between individual and group decision making.

## 3. Conclusion

Our illustrations underscore the original purpose for developing STROBE. The IA can confirm, reverse, modify and/or supplement interview information and hard data, through this approach.

Its strengths are:

1. It is an unobtrusive method of gathering information.

2. It allows the IA to take a multiple method approach (together with interviewing and investigation) to ascertaining information needs.

3. It requires no additional executive or staff time.

4. There is no need for the IA to schedule additional visits.

5. It can be easily integrated with methods already in use.

Perhaps many of the observations could have been surmised without the use of STROBE, but the key factors may remain unusable; i.e., at the subconscious level. STROBE is a structured way to observe critical elements influencing decision makers in the organizational environment. It is a tool that allows the IA to exercise full judgment in interpreting its data, just as information collected in interviews and hard data is subject to expert interpretation.
