# Project Management: Have We Applied All That We Know?

James H. Waldrop

Hamilton Brothers Oil Company, P.O. Box 5870, 1600 Broadway, Denver, Colorado 80217, USA

Information systems projects are often successfully justified but fail in their development. This is usually caused by ineffective management of the project during development combined with an overemphasis on coding techniques. A proven management methodology effectively applied to every project addresses the unpredictability of new objectives, personnel reorganizations, and changing priorities. Key management components include: obtaining management commitment, planning the work, organizing the staff, setting project standards, guiding the team, communicating with general management, documenting decisions, motivating staff, negotiating changes, and training the user. Thorough application of these principles during each project, combined with tracking the actual performance versus the schedule, provides a quality data base of estimating criteria for a data processing organization. Experience indicates proper management applied to any project significantly increases the probability of success.

Keywords: Project Management, System Development, Change Negotiation, Communication, Standards, System Planning, Management Support, Staff Motivation, Project Organization, User Participation.

## 1. Introduction

Recently our management committee unveiled plans for a major systems enhancement project. The plans incorporated extensive work with abbreviated schedules. Project managers often find themselves a part of such a scenario. F. Warren McFarlan [3, p. 142] cites numerous instances of disastrous data processing projects emanating, in part, from this management environment. His analysis over the past ten years identifies three serious deficiencies that contribute to these disasters: lack of understanding that different projects require different managerial approaches is the prime problem. We hypothesize that our problem is more fundamental; as managers, we often select the appropriate project management approach but fail to apply the process. It is only by effective management that a project can successfully adapt to the unpredictability of new objectives, personnel re-organization, and changing priorities.

## 2. What Can We Do For Our Management?

To answer this question, we describe our project management philosophy at Hamilton Brothers Oil Company, an independent oil and gas exploration and production firm. This philosophy has been used successfully on several projects, one requiring over 35 man-years of effort.

We were confronted with a critical business problem: dramatic expansion within the industry requiring quantum leaps in qualitative and quantitative information. The solution to this problem was considered to be the development of a new Financial Reporting System (FRS) encompassing all the major accounting and financial functions of the organization – a system that would ultimately contain over 300 online and batch programs.

To large companies possessing sizeable data processing organizations, this project would be only slightly more than business-as-usual. But to small or medium companies, this exercise allows both the corporate management and the data processing staff to become intimately familiar with the trauma of “future shock”. Set against the backdrop of a very limited data processing staff (three people), this opportunity manifested itself through two classical challenges: a tight development schedule and corporate politics. This scenario was surely a recipe for disaster.

A tight development schedule is often the result of two errors: general management's lack of appreciation for the scope of the effort and the data processing staff's occasionally ineffective and often overly optimistic estimating process. The realm of corporate politics further exacerbates these inherent problems; it offers a further opportunity for the project team to succumb to constant user demands for system change requests. It is this combination of stringent development schedules and shifting demands that has contributed to the negative reputation “enjoyed” by the data processing community.

## 3. Project Management Philosophy

What actions should be taken to effect a successful project? What are the results from their application during the development life cycle of actual projects? Prior to embarking upon our first project, we chose these ten principles as the cornerstone of our project management philosophy.

## 3.1. Obtain Top Management Commitment

Top management support is the foundation of all project management actions. The greater the scope of the project, the more critical this facet is to its ultimate success.

The top manager for a project is usually the individual who has ultimate authority for spending the funds for its development and is usually the primary proponent of the system. Normally, he or she occupies a position high enough in the organization to arbitrate all interorganizational issues.

Once the top manager is identified, the project manager must convince this person of the system's benefits, demonstrate the importance of active participation in the project, and suggest specific management actions within the development plan. This is an ideal situation to apply the technique of managing your manager. The top manager must have a selected but limited number of high visibility actions that others within the company can easily understand. Providing such input offers the top manager an opportunity to control the size of the project (through budgetary constraints) and to aid in project direction.

Top management commitment for a project should be maintained from project inception through completion. The senior manager must communicate his support throughout the company; if not, mixed signals will be perceived by the user support staff, who are often faced with supporting many different corporate objectives. Top management, must be constantly aware of changing corporate priorities and communicate the relative priority of the project. Our senior vice president responsible for top management decisions met with the project team at key points to reaffirm his support and the value of the FRS project to the company. This involvement boosted the team members' morale in the project's early stages.

## 3.2. Plan the Work

It is paramount that the project leader with the assistance of the development team prepare a preliminary system plan. This must incorporate the scope of the project, key milestones, financial and staffing budget, and deliverable products. Most projects require phased development; the current phase should be planned in detail. We use a standard approach when preparing project plans. There are five separate phases: concept definition, general design, detail design, programming and testing, and implementation. In detail task planning, the project team identifies tasks that have a deliverable product at their completion. Products may be a design document in one phase or tested code in another. Structuring the detail plan in this manner permits the use of the binary approach to milestone evaluation. A task is either complete (deliverable product in hand) or it is incomplete (deliverable product not in hand). This avoids the “ninety percent complete” syndrome that plagues many projects. The planning exercise is initiated prior to the start of each phase.

Task estimation is a critical planning function.

The obvious benefit derived from this estimation approach is the speed with which a rough estimate can be prepared for general management. By performing a detail estimate of the ensuing phase, all remaining phases can be approximated using a relative technique.

An iterative review and approval process should be conducted between the project leader and general management during each phase to further refine the plan. The project leader must seize this opportunity also to clarify the expectations of general management. This will minimize last minute surprises brought to the attention of senior management as key milestones are achieved. I refer to this process as expectation shaping. Management reactions to the perceived progress (or lack of it) are based on the results of this exercise. It is key to the perception of success or failure developed by the end user at the project's conclusion.

The results of the planning process should be documented in a task plan. Fig. 2 contains an example of a manually prepared form used by the FRS project team. Its key features include identification of each task, responsible individual, planned and actual start/completion dates, and a brief status indicator.

## 3.3. Organize the Project Staff

The underlying success factor for all projects is the acquisition of qualified, dedicated people. The ideal project environment includes members from the user organization with the key user serving as the project manager. Choice of such a person usually results in a higher level of credibility within the user arena than a data processing individual. V. Perez [5, p. 144] describes a very common condition in most development environments. He states, “[users] are waiting for their systems analysts to find their information requirements; this produces a comfortable condition, for they are always in a position to criticize any result they disfavor." This approach demonstrates user involvement and requires them to develop ownership in the project's success.

When this level of user involvement cannot be achieved, the concept of a project sponsor from the user area should be used. This technique does, however, increase the communication problems within the management of a project and also diminishes the total effectiveness of the project management function.

The team members must have technical and interpersonal skills. A particularly beneficial trait is the ability to adapt to change. Projects requiring longer than six months are susceptible to the “moving target” syndrome caused by changing system requirements. Projects of a year or more in length are even further complicated by employee turnover.

The project leader must be adept at structuring the project team to maximize the skills of the team members while simultaneously challenging each. This is, of course, no small undertaking. Each project member must have a clear understanding of his or her direct responsibilities and be provided the resources to achieve assigned objectives. Since most organizations lack the luxury of exactly the right skill mix for a given project, the technique employed by professional sports teams provides an excellent alternative. Dr. Stephen Ruth [6, p. 24] suggests forming project teams with a “cadre or nucleus of five or six first-round draft picks or seasoned all-pros.” This technique was beneficial to the success of the FRS project. The nucleus of the project team contained four or five individuals who possessed managerial experience but not necessarily the best application background. Conversely, some of the junior team members had application experience but lagged in project management skills. The synergism created by this environment offered challenges to everyone. The company received a quality product while most individuals enhanced their career objectives.

The efficiency of the project organization depends significantly on the allocation of work within the organization structure. Clear-cut, functional boundaries must be established within the project. These functional areas normally allow the project leader to define teams within the overall project and establish associated team leaders. This technique balances the work, reduces the overall communication required (assuming proper standards have been developed), and properly addresses management span-of-control.

## 3.4. Set Project Standards

The principle concern is not the specific type of standards, but rather the assurance that standards are formulated early in the project. Certainly standards must be established for the technical facets of the project. Examples of these include file and program naming practices, report format structuring, and technical performance criteria such as response time thresholds. Equally important. however, is the development of documentation standards. These include: document formats, correspondence identification codes, and personnel time accounting codes for tracking the actual time spent. Standards facilitate communication within the project team by establishing a mutual understanding of how common functions are performed.

## 3.5. Guide the Project Team

Effective project managers take an active, central role in the development of a project. The tendency to assume a passive or administrative role must be avoided at all costs. The project manager must maintain consistent and regular interaction with general management and provide clear, concise, and consistent leadership to the team members. All issues that arise must be directly addressed, no matter how difficult to resolve. The quality of these actions will directly determine the ultimate success of the project.

A vital function of the project manager is monitoring progress. He or she must frequently evaluate each team member's progress toward their milestones. When a negative variance occurs, corrective action must be applied immediately. This may require overtime, replacing people, or altering the schedule.

The project manager must have constant communication and feedback from the user and general management regarding the project's direction. We use the user committee approach to provide the necessary checks and balances on progress. A committee consists of up to ten but typically five or six key managers, one from each affected functional area. The primary system user serves as chairman.

The committee is charged with reviewing progress and addressing interorganizational issues. To facilitate this process, the project manager uses a tracking tool known as an issue log. Fig. 3 contains an example of this. Its key features include a problem identification number, a brief description, the specific individual assigned to resolve the issue, the status, and the action date. The issue log forces immediate attention on the problem. Also, it ensures that issues are highlighted and do not “fall through the crack.”

The issue log provides general management with a barometer of the user involvement and commitment. It contributes significantly to the expectation shaping process. For example, a key issue such as the structure of the chart of accounts, a responsibility of the controller, may continue to remain unresolved. A review of the issue log indicates the impact of its deferral or failure to resolve it.

## 3.6. Communicate Regularly with General Management

The project manager has the obligation to initiate and maintain constant written and oral communication with the end user and general management. Dave Mollen of IBM [4, p. 195] references a study that suggests data processing productivity can be increased by nearly 400% through effective application of this principle. The phased development approach provides convenient predetermined opportunities for the management team to ask the question: “Will this project ever work at all?” This question was posed at numerous times in the FRS project life cycle by the senior vice president responsible for developing and using the system.

Management communication should take several forms. Brief summary status reports serve as a constant reminder of the project's progress. Regular meetings with the sponsoring management preclude surprises occurring at key points. They also provide opportunities for further expectation shaping. Effective and timely management communication provides the appropriate opportunity to redirect current efforts or acquire additional unplanned resources. It is a good time to concisely demonstrate quality progress and market the benefits of the system.

For projects greater than six elapsed months, interim demonstrations serve to reinforce management's confidence in the quality and direction of the work. As selected pieces of the system are unit tested (e.g., an online input or inquiry screen), the project manager should arrange for the general management and the user committee to review the progress. This seemingly insignificant event provides an excellent vehicle for recognizing the team's performance while further marketing the project.

## 3.7. Document All Decisions

More people interacting on more issues create a geometrically increasing number of communication channels. Since most decisions are based on previous conclusions, it is imperative that all decisions be documented and distributed to all affected parties. The documentation should include alternatives considered, benefits of each approach, and the decision reached on each key issue.

This technique served two valuable purposes during the development of FRS. New team members were oriented to the project through a review of this background material. And valuable time was saved by not re-addressing critical decisions. A further long-term benefit is achieved for the organization by providing a repository of information available for future projects.

## 3.8. Motivate the Team Members

Successful project managers direct their project teams rather than just participate in them. Opportunities provided to team members to participate in new experiences may offer motivational rewards. Association of junior members with more senior individuals provides excellent learning opportunities especially within an environment of goodwill. This approach can be optimized when individuals with complementing skills and personalities are matched.

Overlaying each of these facets is the need to promote identification of each individual with his or her role. This aspect should be constantly nurtured through proper recognition of individual accomplishments. Each participant must acquire a feeling of “ownership” or pride of authorship. It is the project manager's direct responsibility, through team leaders, to instill this spirit throughout the project team.

Numerous studies have been conducted on the psyche of the data processing professional. Research by Couger and Zawacki [1, p. 116] analyzes the motivation of the data processing individual. This statistically demonstrated that data processing professionals have the lowest social need strength (SNS) when compared to more than 500 professions. Paradoxically, they have the highest growth need strength (GNS). They have the lowest need for social interaction with their fellow workers but have the highest need for growth in their job to be happy in their work. Their high ratings in the motivation potential score (MPS) category indicated that they see their chosen career as the vehicle to achieve growth without much social interaction.

Since communication is critical to the success of any project, team members must be provided opportunities to expand their SNS within the purview of a project. Rotating responsibilities for preparing meeting minutes provides a need to interact with other team members. Assigning specific user related tasks (such as requirement definitions) to each individual also provides the pathway for technical team members to interact directly with non-technical personnel. This approach builds a rapport internal to the project as well as builds confidence in interpersonal skills that are so critical as careers advance.

A similar study by Charles K. Woodruff [7, p. 137] identifies the implication of these characteristics. Individual managers endeavor to be consistent and equitable in their treatment of staff members. However, he states, “to ignore individual differences and rigidly adhere to a practice of identical treatment for all organization members can be detrimental to organization performance. It is not axiomatic that identical treatment equates to equitable treatment for each individual.” The data processing manager must consider the individual differences of his staff as measured by personality needs.

Good senior data processing professionals will constantly challenge junior staff members with questions such as, “What would you do if you were faced with this problem?” One should avoid the tendency to solve a problem directly without providing the junior member a chance to address it. This mentor concept assists in the brainstorming exercise for a current project and quickly builds skill growth.

## 3.9. Negotiate System Changes

The data processing field fails to support change negotiation. This causes project overruns. Dave Mollen of IBM [4, p. 195] describes such overruns as “actually the costs, in time and money, that resulted from the difference between the information the end user provided the data processing department in the system proposal stage, and what the end user demanded of data processing prior to considering the project complete.” To reduce the risk of overrun and to accentuate the expectation shaping process, the project leader must not accept any change without a compensating modification in the project plan. This means changes occur only after appropriate staffing and schedule impacts are assessed by the sponsoring user manager. If such care is not taken, false scheduling assumptions are made by project members or key users.

## 3.10. Train The User

A new data processing system without the appropriate training for the end user is like an airplane without flight training. That is, there is potential usage but only with the proper knowledge. Training should incorporate not only the “how to” but also the “why do we” perspectives of a system.

Training should be presented in users terms; the user members of the project team should concentrate their efforts on this while the technical team members perform the system development function. Too often end users are only trained to perform a specific task within a broader functioning system. Failure to provide a perspective on the interrelationships of each functional area normally leads to sub-optimal use.

## 4. Conclusion

Our application of these tenets to a project instead of fitting the project to the management approach has reduced the risk of project failure while increasing the quality of the end product. It has widened the perceptions of our user management. They now incorporate non-coding oriented tasks within the total scope of a data processing project. This expanded understanding of the total effort required to develop a system has improved the quality the development environment experienced on subsequent projects. When applied to the Financial Reporting System, they also resulted in a 15% cost reduction from a previous average of \$8 per line of developed code.

A project management methodology is a means to an end not an end itself. It is a necessary but insufficient condition to achieve a successful implementation. There is no substitute for the performance of people. As managers in the information systems arena, we must focus on key results produced from critical tasks while applying these principles during the system development life cycle. We must be constantly aware of the 1513 Machiavellian maxim that

“...there is nothing more difficult to plan, more doubtful of success, nor more dangerous to manage than the creation of a new system...”
