# A Descriptive Model of Success for Computer-Based Information Systems

P.J.S. Bruwer

Department of Computer Science and Post Graduate Management School, Potchefstroom University for CHE, Potchefstroom 2520, South Africa

This article presents a model of the success of computer-based information systems. We hypothesize that the following variables influence the success of the system: involvement of managers in computerization projects: higher-level managerial support; the technical quality of the system; personal factors; the attitude of managers towards EDP personnel and the attitude of managers towards the computerized system. The research finds that the manager's attitudes to computerization are related to all but the last two.

Certain classes of variables are difficult to relate to the success of the system (for example, personal factors). If the evidence for the model is considered strong enough to warrant action, the organization should concentrate on several key variables: high quality of the system; good management support, and good training and management involvement.

The results of our research should provide better understanding of the variables associated with the success of a computer-based system.

Keywords: Management Information Systems, Success of Implementation, System Analysis and Design, Post Implementation Evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

In the last two decades, a large number of automated information systems have been implemented by private and public organizations. From the literature, it is evident that quite a number of these systems have failed (see Lucas [7]), not necessarily because of poor technical quality, but because of certain other aspects, which have been ignored.

Post implementation evaluation of computer-based information systems is of vital importance for improving the operation of current systems and in the design of new applications. The management of an organization is faced with three major activities in which this plays an important part. The first is in the acquisition of equipment, including upgrading components or the acquisition of completely new hardware and software systems. This activity is only carried out periodically, and in fact is often forced by technological change. The second is in improving the operation of production systems. Increased loading of the system often necessitates additional equipment, it is therefore important, particularly during difficult economic periods, to be sure that the existing system is operating efficiently. The third is in the design of new applications; this is extremely important, both to the computer staff and users of the system. Here we focus on the second activity-specifically on the key variables influencing the success of existing systems.

## 2. The Model

Fig. 1 presents a descriptive model of success of an information system. The key variable in the model is success: How is it defined operationally? In past studies, success has been measured by indicators such as actual use, intended use, attitude, etc. [10], but a cost/benefit study is one of the best ways to measure it. Obviously, the benefits from a computer-based system should exceed the costs of developing and running it. Unfortunately, assessing the benefits has been difficult, particularly for systems supporting sophisticated decisions; i.e., beyond the routine processing of transactions. We are faced with questions like: What are the benefits of strategic planning? and: How can the results of strategic planning be evaluated against performance without it?

Because of this difficulty, some other indicator of success is needed. The most appealing, from a measurement standpoint, is system use. However, there are instances where a high level of system use is not a sign of success, and when the use of computer-based system is involuntary, this criteria cannot be applied.

The third criteria used in this study is user satisfaction. Use and satisfaction are relative, that is, they are measured on a continuum, not a binary scale. In this study the users are lower, middle, and top managers of an organization.

## 3. Hypotheses

A number of hypotheses may be derived from the proposed model. Empirical data were used to test them. The key variable in the model is success (S). It is hypothesized to be a function of the attitude of the managers towards computerization (A), technical quality of the system (C), management support (E), management involvement (H), personal factors (G) and the attitude of management towards computer personnel (W).

$$
S = f (A, C, E, H, G, W).\tag{1}
$$

The six hypotheses are numbered to correspond with the numbers on the arrows in Fig. 1.

1. The success of the computer-based system is related to the attitude of the managers. A negative attitude towards computerization from top level management will have significant influence on middle and lower level managers. This could lead to a negative attitude by the clerical users of the system. A system which is not accepted and actively supported by management could not be expected to be a success.

2. The success of a computer-based system is related to the technical quality of the system. A computer-based system must be of sufficient quality so that the output is valid and useful. Quality here is considered to be a user-oriented criteria rather than one due to technical elegance. A high quality system has a pleasant user interface, is available and reliable, etc.

Information systems designers have even been guilty of developing input forms which are “impossible” to complete!

Many systems produce incomprehensible output, which discourages use. The logic of the system is also important. Technical quality must be evaluated based on importance to users and not on technical elegance. Unfortunately, technical quality is a necessity but not a sufficient condition for the success of a computer-based information system.

3. The success of the system is related to management support. Support by management is expected to influence attitudes and this directly influence system use. If upper level managers actively use the output of a system, subordinates will probably follow their lead.

4. The success of the system is related to personal factors. The “condition” of an individual influences his or her feelings for a system. In this study, three personal factors were used: the age of the manager, the number of years of service in the organization, and the level of education.

A study by Mumford and Banks [9] found that users with different age and sex respond differently to new systems. In this study it was expected that the older individual (with long experience in the organization) would be less positive towards change (computerization) than a younger employee. Lucas [4] summed up his research studies by saying: “The clearest finding that emerges from these studies confirms the earlier predictions that the older and less educated member of the organization is most likely to resist a computer-based system.”

5. Management involvement in the design, development, and implementation of a computer-based system is related to favourable management attitudes and thus the success of the system. Researchers in past studies frequently proposed user involvement in the design of a computer-based system as a way to improve the quality of the system and the satisfaction of the user with the system $[3,6,11]$ . This, of course, is as applicable to the managers as users of the system. Lucas $[5]$ suggests: “Creative systems design emphasizes the role of the user in designing systems; we shall suggest that the user should actually design the system himself.”

6. The success of a computer-based system is related to management attitude towards computer personnel. In large organizations, it is often the managers and not the low-level users who are in contact with computer personnel. The assumption made here is that a favourable attitude of managers towards computer personnel would lead to greater success of the systems.

## 4. User Attitudes

We also hypothesize (see Fig. 1) that the attitude of the manager (as a user of the system) is a function of the technical quality of the system, management involvement, the support of higher level management and personal factors.

$$
A = f (C, H, E, G).\tag{2}
$$

Four hypotheses follow from this.

7. The attitude of the manager towards computerization is related to the technical quality of a computer-based system. A system must be of sufficient quality for output to be used. A poor quality system may discourage the use of the system and furthers unfavourable attitudes.

8. Higher level management support affects lower managers attitudes. The management of any organization has significant influence on subordinates. The attitudes of middle and lower level managers may be influenced positively if the systems are supported by higher level management.

9. Management involvement in computerization efforts is related to favourable management attitudes. User and management involvement in the development of a new system could result in the improvement of the quality of the system. Computer-based systems often fail because users and managers in user departments feel that the system has been forced upon them. The active involvement of users and managers in the development of the system would result in a feeling that the new system is their own. This would lead to favourable attitudes towards computerization.

10. Personal factors are related to the attitude of the manager towards computerization. In this study it was expected that the older manager with long experience in the organization would be more negative towards computerization.

## 5. The Field Study

In order to determine the relationships between the variables in the model, data were collected from managers who are users of computerized systems in a very large organization with about 140 computerized systems used by about 1200 clerical staff and 114 managers.

A questionnaire was designed, and the full population of managers were involved in the investigation. Most of the questions in the questionnaire could be answered on a 7-point scale [1]. The purpose of these questions was to quantify the variables in the model. A number of variables were used to measure a main variable (for example the technical quality of a computerized system). With the aid of BMDP statistical computer programs [2], correlation and regression methods were used to investigate linear relationships between the variables in the model. The data were found to support the existence of the relationships as hypothesized in all ten cases.

## 8. Conclusion

The relationships shown in the model of Fig. 1 have been found to be sufficiently strong to seriously consider them when investigating poor usage of existing computer-based systems or when planning the implementation of new ones.

When computer-based information systems fail in an organization, it is most often assumed to be a technical problem. In our research, it was shown that other important factors exist and contribute to the success or failure of a computer-based information system.
