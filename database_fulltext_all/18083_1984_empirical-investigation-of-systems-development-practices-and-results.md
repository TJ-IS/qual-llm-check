# Empirical Investigation of Systems Development Practices and Results

A. Milton Jenkins

560 Business Building, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN 47405, USA

Justus D. Naumann

University of Minnesota, Management Information Systems Research Center, School of Management, 93 Blegen Hall, 269 19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

and

James C. Wetherbe

University of Minnesota, Management Information Systems Research Center, School of Management, 93 Blegen Hall, 269 19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

A survey was conducted of 72 information system development projects in 23 major U.S. corporations. The characteristics of the organizations, their system development methodologies, and the information system projects were reviewed. The study focused on the early phases of systems development, especially systems analysis and systems development planning. Early plans and budgets were evaluated with respect to the results of the completed projects. An analysis of the development of these projects provides a summary description of systems development practice as performed in these leading organizations. Included with development practices, the study measured or estimated such aspects of the projects as user satisfaction and development time and cost overruns. The results provide useful insight into system development methodology, cost and time estimating, variables affecting user satisfaction, and the need to iteratively determine user requirements.

Keywords: Development Project, System Development Practice, Development Results, System Development Methodology, Systems Analysis, System Development Planning, User Satisfaction, Development Time, Cost Overrun, User Requirements, Project Management.

This article focuses on the characteristics of information system projects undertaken in organizations. ## 2. Background

## 2.1. Organizations Participating in the Study

Twenty-three organizations located in three geographical areas – Minneapolis-St. Paul, Houston, and Indianapolis – participated in this study. A total of 72 systems development projects with a maximum of 6 projects per organization were included in the study.

The participating organizations were all large. The industries represented included publishing, government, education, hard and soft goods manufacturing, financial, insurance, computer service, distribution, construction, energy production, and utilities. In some instances, participating organizations were subsidiaries or divisions of large organizations. In general, the study was based upon organizations that are broadly representative of large, U.S. organizations.

Computing hardware budgets (annual) of the companies contributing to this study averaged \$5.6 million, with the lowest just under \$500,000, and the largest over \$43 million. Systems development staff budgets ranged from \$325,000 to over \$10 million, and averaged \$2.7 million. Systems development staff sizes, including managers, analysts, programmers, and supporting specialists, ranged from 11 to 330, and averaged 94. On the average, 43% of development personnel were classified as “primarily programming,” with the remainder classified as doing “primarily systems analysis and design.” Companies identified an average of 12 people as “project leaders” or “project managers;” the maximum number of project leaders was 40 in one firm.

Participating organizations had completed an average of 190 projects in the preceding year (1980). The number of projects completed per organization ranged from 3 to 830.

## 2.2. Data Collection Procedure

Participation began with a brief interview of a systems development manager, who provided background information on the organization and identified projects and project leaders for detailed interviews and study. Projects were selected by the systems development manager together with one of the senior researchers. Projects were candidates for selection if they were “typical projects,” good or bad, that had been completed in the last two years, for which the individual who had been project leader during the time of initial analysis was available, and for which cost estimates and actual costs were available.

Systems development managers were informed of the descriptive intent of the study, but not all the details, during this project selection stage. The projects included are quite typical of the sort of development efforts undertaken by the participating organizations.

Interviews with project leaders ranged from 45 minutes to two hours; the interviewer used a 50 item interview guide as a structure for questioning and to record replies. The interview guide included a “Systems Development Methodology Worksheet” designed to capture estimates of cost, effort, and time for each phase or stage of development. In some cases, this worksheet was completed by the respondent after the interview, because detailed data was not at hand.

## 3. Characteristics of Projects Studied

## 3.1. Cost and Person-Days

Distribution and averages for dollars and effort in person-days expended on the projects studied are provided in Figs. 1a and 1b. As depicted in Figure 1a, project costs ranged from \$1,687 to \$1,200,000. Median project size was \$103,000. Fig. 1b shows that the person-days required for the projects ranged from 6 to 7,224 person-days, although most (73%) took less than 1,000 days. Cost and effort were closely related ( $r = 0.84$ ) but not identical. In 34 cases, reported project costs included expenditures other than personnel (such as special hardware, training, etc.). Some projects lasted only one month from start to finish, one took five and one-half years. Project duration averaged 10.5 months (see Fig. 1c).

## 3.2. Relative Size

Respondents were asked to classify projects relative to other projects completed by their organization during the preceding two years. Of the 72 projects studied, 24 were considered relatively large, 30 were medium, and 18 were small.

Their criteria for relative size were also sought. The results of that analysis is provided in Fig. 2. On average, projects with total costs greater than \$220,000, efforts greater than 700 person-days, or durations longer than 12 months were considered to be large. Projects with total costs less than \$40,000, efforts less than 130 person-days, or durations less than 4 months were considered to be small.

The relationship between relative project size and the actual size of the project under study was significant (r = 0.53) at the 99% percent level, indicating that the project leaders' classifications were consistent for the projects studied.

## 3.3. Number and Types of Users

The numbers of users at three organizational levels - operations, middle, and top management

– were recorded for each project. Fig. 3 shows the distribution of users. One system had more than 2,000 users; others had only one user.

Most projects (80%) were intended to serve multiple user organizations – divisions, departments, subsidiaries, branches, etc. The range of multiple user organizations was from 1 to 100, with a mean of 9 user organizations per project. In almost all cases, the multiple user organizations had identical or similar interactions with the system being developed. That is, multiple uses consisted mainly of similar types of users or organizations, rather than several different types of organizational units using the same system for different purposes.

This implies that systems are still based upon organizational structure rather than the recommended functional basis. (Note that these projects were all started prior to 1980.)

## 3.4. Technology Orientation

Just over half (55%) of the systems used generalized database management software. The remaining projects were developed without such technology.

Slightly less than one half (45%) of the systems were online systems or at least had online components. The remaining projects were batch systems. There was no strong relationship between systems that used database and systems that were at least partially online, that is, batch-only systems were just as likely to use database management as online systems.

The majority of systems were developed with COBOL as their only or primary language. None used Assembly as a primary language, although 15% listed an Assembler as a secondary language. Slightly under one half (48%) indicated the use of some generalized software in the project. (Because of the way this question was asked, some of the generalized software used may have been the DBMS software previously mentioned.)

## 3.5. Type of Systems

Most projects (54%) were new systems, but a sizeable proportion were redesigns (33%) and enhancements (11%). Fig. 4 shows this distribution. Small projects were mostly new systems (58%) or redesigns (42%); large projects were new systems (50%), redesigns (22%), or enhancements (33%). The relationship between type of project and project size was not statistically significant. There was some relationship (not statistically significant) between type of project and the estimate of user satisfaction. That is, users were estimated to be slightly more satisfied with enhancements to existing systems than with completely new systems.

## 4. The Development Process

## 4.1. Systems Development Methodologies

A significant element in the systems development process is the Systems Development Methodology (SDM) employed. Data on the SDM employed its uses, and its usefulness was gathered for each project in this study.

Sixty of the project leaders interviewed reported at least one SDM in use; three reported the availability of both a commercial and an in-house SDM. Of the 63 SDMs reported, 26 were commercial (meaning acquired from a vendor, not simply adopted from another installation) and 37 were considered “home-grown.” Of the commercial SDMs, 76% had been in use two years or less while 62% of the home-grown SDMs had been used for at least five years. In many cases where a commercial SDM was in use, a previously used home-grown system had been or was being supplanted. Project leaders indicated that commercial SDMs were modified 81% of the time.

4.2. Use of SDM: Scheduler vs. Project Management

Project leaders were asked whether or not they viewed the SDM as primarily a scheduler or project management aid (in contrast with SDMs that include specific methods, techniques, practices, standards, or forms). Of the 54 project leaders who responded to this question, 44% said yes. 43% of the commercial SDM users and 50% of the in-house SDM users felt that their SDM was primarily a project management aid.

The scheduling or project management features were used in just 39% of the projects studied; in the remaining 61%, no formal project management system was employed.

## 4.3. Value of SDMs

Project leaders were asked to rate their SDM, on a scale of 1 (no value) to 10 (extremely valuable), in terms of its value for the project under study. Fig. 5 shows their responses, which ranged from 2 to 10, with an average of 7. We also compared the type of SDM used, commercial versus in-house, with their rating. There was no significant difference.

Discovering user needs through interviews dominates the scene (35% plus 13%), but over one fourth (26%) began their analysis by presenting a possible design, either as a paper model (11% plus 11%) or as a prototype (4%).

On two thirds of the projects, the initial information requirements analysis effort had to be repeated; only half of the project leaders (56%) had planned to include an iteration of the initial analysis effort. These figures tend to confirm the folk wisdom that systems analysis must typically be repeated. They also indicate that this idea has not been fully incorporated into systems development project planning.

By a large majority (78%), the project leaders reported that additional requirements were discovered after approval of the systems requirements statement. Only 37% of these new requirements were treated as new development requests; in the remainder, new requirements were simply incorporated into the project at the time they were discovered.

## 4.5. Reasons for Late Discovery of Requirements

The interviewers also attempted to uncover the reasons underlying the late discovery of additional requirements. Project leaders were given two broad categories – “requirements missed during analysis,” and “changes in the users’ functions or environment." Project leaders were asked for a percentage of the requirements that were missed in each of these categories.

The respondents were surprisingly self-critical; 65% reported that some percentage of requirements were missed due to faulty or incomplete analysis, while only 39% reported missed requirements due to real changes in the users' function or environment.

There was no discernable relationship between the type of SDM used and the discovery of additional requirements.

## 5. Measure of Project Outcome

Systems development project failure is often attributed to incomplete or incorrect requirements determination. One measure, then, of correct and complete requirements determination is the successful implementation of the developed system. Note that there are certainly other possible causes of systems development failure – poor designs, hardware and software selection errors, poor coding, testing, or integration practices, poor implementation strategies, and uncontrollable changes in the user's environment. However, in this study the emphasis was on the relationship between the approach to information requirements determination and the successful or unsuccessful implementation of a system.

Three measures of implementation success have been suggested: (1) the degree of user satisfaction with the implemented system, (2) being “on-time”, and (3) being “on-budget.” These implementation measures were derived by comparing actual implementation costs and calendar times with those of the first formal schedule and budget made for each project.

## 5.1. User Satisfaction

User satisfaction was estimated by the project manager, using an instrument designed to measure user satisfaction with management information systems [6]. This is clearly a measure of perceived user satisfaction, and does not directly indicate the attitudes and opinions of users of the systems that were studied. Ideally, measures of satisfaction with an information system should include input from all major users of a system. Unfortunately this was not economically and operationally possible in such an exploratory study, therefore, the project manager's perception was used as an estimate of user satisfaction.

The 25 question instrument used/employed provides a composite rating of user satisfaction and one “global satisfaction” question: “Overall satisfaction with this system is best described as: very satisfied to very unsatisfied,” on a seven-point scale.

Median project manager ratings on the global satisfaction question averaged 6. The distribution of global satisfaction ratings is shown in Figure 8. While 72% of the users were rated as satisfied or very satisfied, users of seven projects were reported to be not satisfied (ratings of 2 or 3) with their systems.

## 5.2. Cost and Effort

Two measures of cost over-run were made as part of the study: dollars and person-days. In many cases, records had not been kept for initial estimates made by project leaders (or, in some cases, by analysts, managers, etc., who preceded the project leader on the project). In such cases, project leaders relied on memory or estimated what the initial cost and effort estimates had been. In other cases, only one measure (dollars or person-days) was available; where only one was available, the appropriate transformation was made to estimate the missing cost. The study bases cost and effort plans and performance statistics on the estimates made prior to information requirements determination compared to actual dollar and manpower expenditures.

Actual project costs ranged from an under-run (or over-estimate) of 57% to a five-fold over-run (525%). The median cost over-run was one third (33.5%), mean over-run for all projects studied was 66.7%. Median is the more appropriate measure, since several of the projects with very high cost over-runs were changed during development – skewing this distribution (Figure 10a). On average, then, actual project cost was one third greater than the first estimate of project cost – which was made before any detailed systems analysis was begun.

Effort, measured in person-days, has a similar distribution (Figure 10b); average (median) effort over-run was 36%. In many cases, either or both estimates and actual costs were accumulated in one of these measures, and transformed into the other at current labor rates. Where both were reported, these two outcome measures do substantially correspond, indicating accurate reporting of original estimates and actual costs.

The third outcome measure was schedule overruns. Relative to the initially estimated completion date, the projects studied were on average 22% late. Only 4 projects were completed early, while 14 projects took more than twice as long to complete as was initially scheduled (Figure 10c). In some cases, external factors (e.g., reduced funding levels) contributed to schedule over-runs – rather than poor estimates or systems development performance.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

The results of this explanatory study on information system development are primarily descriptive. They indicate that:

\- Projects that are considered to be “typical” include new development, redesign, and even minor modifications, and range from a few thousand to a few million dollars.

\- Problems such as cost and schedule over-runs seem to be uniformly distributed among large, medium and small projects.

● A high proportion of the systems that are being developed are intended to serve multiple users and user organizations.

● A majority of new systems being developed by large organizations are online and use database technology.

\- Approximately half of the systems development projects undertaken in large organizations are new systems, not just enhancements or modifications of existing systems.

\- Most large organizations have a formalized systems development methodology (SDM), either purchased or internally developed.

\- Many organizations that have had and used an initially developed SDM have recently acquired and are beginning to use a commercial SDM.

\- In many cases (about half of the projects studied), the SDM is used only as an estimating tool and project management aid.

\- There is no apparent difference between purchased or home grown SDMs in terms of perceived value (function and utility).

● Typical systems development projects are most often initiated by a formal systems study request.

\- The initial systems analysis effort is predominantly conducted using individual interviews.

\- Documenting old systems, and group interviews are the next most frequently used systems analysis techniques.

\- The majority of information system projects require one or more iterations of systems analysis before requirements are completely and correctly determined. The cause is most often failure to completely identify user requirements (rather than changes in user requirements).

\- Project manager perception of users satisfaction indicates that users are generally satisfied with their systems.

\- Most projects are completed significantly over budget, require more effort than estimated, and are completed late.

On a prescriptive note, a few preliminary and guarded recommendations can be made. The use of a formalized systems development methodology is perceived as positive and well-advised. It does not seem to matter whether the SDM was internally developed or purchased.

It appears to be necessary explicitly to recognize the iterative nature of systems development processes, and to build into the development process the capability to offer users at least one iteration as their requirements are being defined and developed.

Projects cost and personnel requirements estimating techniques definitely need to be improved. A good place to start is to compare actual performance to initial estimates, as we did in this study. Even if original estimate data has not been recorded, a systematic effort to recapture or reconstruct past estimates can provide useful measures of estimating practices. At the same time, since estimates are often inaccurate, it may be advisable to prepare organizations psychologically for significant deviations from estimates.
