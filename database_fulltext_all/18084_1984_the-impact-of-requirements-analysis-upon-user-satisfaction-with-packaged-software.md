# The Impact of Requirements Analysis upon User Satisfaction with Packaged Software

R.H. Edmundson and D.R. Jeffery

The University of New South Wales, P.O. Box 1, Kensington, 2033, N.S.W., Australia

This article explores the relationship between carrying out requirements analysis activities and ultimate satisfaction with the software acquired. The requirements analysis activities performed by a sample of twelve organizations which acquired a particular general accounting package are evaluated. The satisfaction with the package is determined and analysis performed to establish the relationships between requirements analysis and user satisfaction. It is determined that for the particular population sampled there is little relationship. Possible reasons for this counter-intuitive finding are advanced.

Keywords: Information systems, requirements analysis, software packages, implementation

## 1. Introduction

With the falling cost of in-house computers there has been a trend for companies to implement their first computerized system on their own machines through the use of purchased business software packages. This is at variance with the older process of using a computer service bureau for some time until the scope of the required facilities and the expertise of the users could support a dedicated computer [13]. The result is that many companies introduce computerized systems without benefit of the learning process provided by a bureau environment.

In the professional literature, the normal assumption is that satisfactory requirements definition is a prerequisite for successful implementation $[5]$ , but there is little evidence that this view has been tested. Typically, the first step taken in development, after a request for a new system is received, is to produce a document defining the scope of that system and its objectives $[17]$ . This document forms the basis of the phased development of the system. There have been several attempts to improve the application of this approach $[16,17]$ . However, the phased development of systems based upon a formal statement of scope and user requirements remains the core of conventional wisdom.

The application of this wisdom to the acquisition of small business systems appears to have the difficulty that the company may not have the requisite skills. Furthermore, the company may believe that a study is not required since the developer of the package has carried out a general requirements analysis.

The major purpose of this paper is to report on a preliminary investigation of the relationship between the performance of requirements analysis activities and satisfaction with a purchased accounting software package.

More specifically, our concern is to determine whether the phased approach (with detailed requirements analysis used for the development of in-house systems) is necessarily the best, or even a suitable approach, when it comes to the purchase of packaged accounting software. As this is an area which has not been researched, it was felt that a small study, in which many potentially problematic variables were held constant, would most likely produce findings which would set the direction for subsequent investigation.

## 2. Prior Research

There is a strong undercurrent in the literature that points to failure in requirements definition as a cause of total or partial failure in the implementation of information systems. However, much of the literature is not directed at empirical evaluation of the impact of requirements analysis but is predicated on the view that this is an area of fundamental importance. Once this view is established, an attempt is made to explain the shortcomings in requirements analysis, and often to then propose a methodology to overcome these problems $[2,11,14,16]$ . These works emphasize the need for user involvement and good communications between the user and the designer as a means of improving the requirements definition activity. However the link between requirements definition and good design is not tested. Furthermore, these writers, and others $[1,12]$ were concerned with Management Information Systems as opposed to transactions based systems and it is arguable that the connection between requirements analysis and user satisfaction is clearer in these circumstances than it might otherwise be. Most importantly, however, previous researchers have not been concerned with the purchase and use of packaged accounting software and it is this particular environment which is under study here.

In one paper [15], the opinion was expressed that failure to define requirements leads to lack of control over costs, missed schedules, and expensive system patching. There is no evidence proffered to support this view, despite a generally rigorous approach. It appears that the combined experience of the writers, coupled with the frequent restatement of the opinion has resulted in the adoption of the view that good systems are dependent upon good requirements definition. It is difficult to imagine how this would not be the case, particularly concerning developed software. There is some indirect support for this in the light of the effort that commercial concerns and government agencies have been prepared to put into improving requirements definition $[3,4,6,7,10,17]$ .

More recently Ginzberg [9] examined MIS applications for key factors in implementation. The approach taken was to survey 35 users of information systems for an assessment of implementation activities and their overall satisfaction with their MIS. Linear discriminant analysis was performed on the data obtained. The stepwise option produced three factors central to successful MIS implementation. The first factor is commitment to the particular project, and the second is commitment to change in general. These two factors appear to be concerned with organizational and individual characteristics. Ginzberg is of the opinion that commitment to change may be more applicable to MIS projects than to other information systems. The third factor is the extent of project definition and planning. This factor covers definition of needs, the impact of the project, key factor analysis as well as the planning of the project effort. There is obviously some considerable overlap between this factor and the conventional view of requirements analysis activities. The Ginzberg research differs from the current exercise in two major respects. The first difference lies in the subject matter, Ginzberg considered custom-made MIS projects while the current research considers a packaged, transaction-driven system. The second difference lies in the methodology adopted. Ginzberg relied upon user evaluation of the activities. The present exercise was carried out by a single interviewer evaluating the activities. it is expected that this second difference will bring a high degree of consistency to activity assessment permitting a smaller sample to generate results of acceptable significance.

## 3. A Framework for Analysis

## 3.1. Delimiting the Problem

The problems that might be encountered in implementing a small business computer system are diverse. In order to set reasonable boundaries upon this investigation a limited set of the possible problems was considered. The full set of problems can be divided into:

a) Technically Related,

b) Application Related and

c) Commercial problems.

Technical Problems. These problems relate mainly to run-time difficulties. They vary in degree and are evidenced for example by system crashes and excessive run times. The major causes of such problems are related to hardware capacity, software design, and hardware or software bugs.

Application Related Problems. These problems vary in degree from severe to mild and are evidenced by output that is inappropriate or insufficient for the organization's needs. The problems could be compounded with technical difficulties.

Commercial Difficulties. These conditions overlay the other problems. They relate to the effectiveness of support for the user and concern matters such as the technical competence of the vendors, the contractual conditions attaching to the supply of the system and the relationship between the hardware and software suppliers. These difficulties might be evidenced by problems encountered in overcoming technical or application related problems.

## 3.2. The Scope of the Investigation

As far as possible this investigation is limited to application related problems. In order to reduce the impact of the other problems, the implementation of a relatively mature hardware and software package was examined. This reduced the number and variety of technical difficulties encountered, except to the extent that modifications to the original package had been carried out. The hardware can be expected to be relatively fault free. Further, the operation of the software on the particular hardware would have been established over time.

In order to avoid variability in the commercial infra-structure, only those installations supplied as turn-key systems by one major supplier were examined. The users were therefore faced with identical conditions with respect to the supplier's technical competence, capacity, and, perhaps to a lesser extent, the contractual arrangements. In particular the possible difficulties arising from separate suppliers of software and hardware were eliminated. These restrictions reduced the possible sample size but provided considerable advantages with respect to the internal validity of the study.

## 4. Research Methodology

## 4.1. The Approach to Measurement

The investigation was conducted by means of interviews in an attempt to provide a more reliable assessment of the requirements definition than would be generated by questionnaire alone. The assessment of requirements analysis was made by the one interviewer. This reduces measurement problems, though it would not necessarily eliminate them, as there is a slight possibility that the interviewer's scale may have changed over time and with the increasing exposure to the investigation.

The assessment of both the extent of requirements definition and the user perception of the success of the system was carried out using summated rating scales. The assessment of satisfaction with the system was checked against replies to open-ended questions in an attempt to increase consistency across the sample.

In their examination of user evaluation of the man/machine interface, Dzida, Herda and Itzfeltd [8] were able to determine seven factors that contributed to perception of quality. At least five of these were statistically reliable and valid. The study concluded that the categories of identified factors provided, for the first time, an empirical model for assessing user-perceived quality. The question of user satisfaction with the information services of a system is somewhat more complex than that of evaluating the man/machine interface, and thus the tool developed by them is not directly applicable in the current exercise. However, an approach close to that described by Dzida et al was adopted.

## 4.2. Research Hypotheses

The major hypothesis tested in this investigation is:

H1: Overall satisfaction with an implemented small business system package is not significantly related to the performance of requirements analysis activities.

This hypothesis is operationalized by investigating the extent to which the variation in overall satisfaction expressed by the respondents is explained by variation in the performance of a set of requirements analysis activities.

Because there is no opportunity to test this hypothesis in a laboratory setting in which all variables except the independent variables are held constant, some additional tests are performed upon variables other than those indicated as measures of requirements analysis. In particular the following tests on moderating variables were investigated:

HM1: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the management level of the respondent.

HM2: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the type of activity in which the company is engaged.

HM3: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the length of time that the package has been installed on the site.

HM4: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the use of external consultants.

HM5: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the number of vendors approached for tenders.

HM6: Overall satisfaction expressed is not significantly related to the number of detailed proposals considered.

Table 1 contains the group of independent variables included in the analysis of test hypothesis H1. Each variable was measured on a scale of one to seven, where one indicates a virtual absence of the activity. RA1 measures the quality of the documentation, RA2 the extent to which the boundaries of the new system were defined, RA3 the adequacy of the description of the old system, RA4 the extent to which the key success factors of the accounting side of the organization were determined, RA5 the extent of the work carried out in determining output form requirements, RA6 the extent to which consideration was given to resources available and needed, and RA7 the adequacy of the work in specifying the new system's functional requirements. It was felt that these seven factors encompass the major requirements activities normally carried out.

## 4.3. Data Collection

Of 16 organizations found to be using the selected software package two refused to take part in the research. Additionally, one would not permit the interviewer access to the documentation and one had suffered a complete staff turnover since the acquisition of the system and no longer had the required documentation. Exclusion of these (because their evaluation was not possible) left a sample of twelve organizations from which the data was collected.

## 5. Analysis

Analysis of data was carried out using the SPSS package to perform regression and crosstabulation upon the variables. Table 6 shows a summary of the user satisfaction rankings and the corresponding average extent of requirements analysis activity for all seven variables RA1 to RA7. This shows that the users who were most satisfied with the package, on average carried out the least requirements analysis related activities.

The comparison of satisfaction ranking with average requirements analysis activity reveals no simple relationship. Table 7 shows regression statistics of satisfaction against each of the requirements analysis activities.

It can be seen from this table that no single requirements analysis variable can be used to explain the variation in overall satisfaction, with the possible exception of RA5 (definition of draft output forms) which explains 22% of variation in satisfaction. Similarly, if the average total requirements activity is regressed against satisfaction an $r^{2}$ of 0.238 is obtained. It should also be noted that the sign of the regression coefficients is negative in all cases, implying that the greater the effort in requirements analysis, the less satisfied the user. Further discussion of these findings is left until consideration is given to possible confounding variables.

## 6. Discussion of the Results

In a study such as this it is important that the software product being studied be held constant. Otherwise too many immeasurable variables that might impact satisfaction would enter into the problem. For example, different hardware and software characteristics would most likely contribute significantly to overall satisfaction with a system, but it would be impossible to measure the hardware and software differences so that this contribution could be assessed separately from the requirements analysis activities. For that reason our study has used only one hardware and software environment. This reduces the possible sample size markedly with the consequent effects on the statistical analyses that can be carried out. However, the study captured data from all suitable users of the product in Sydney, Australia, and there is no observable reason to suspect that the sample is not representative of other organizations using the product or other organizations using similar packaged accounting software.

The statistical analysis shows that the completion of requirements analysis activities does not result in greater user satisfaction with the system. Three possible reasons for this are:

1. the activity of requirements analysis is counter productive;

2. requirements analysis for the purchase of packaged software is counter productive;

3. requirements analysis for the purchase of packaged accounting software is counter productive.

The first of these cannot be supported by the study and in the light of information systems practice must probably be rejected. There is also no sound reason to suspect that when purchasing packaged software in general, it would not be important to establish the requirements for that software so that a sound selection can be made. It would be ridiculous to suggest that the best approach to hardware/software selection would be to select without establishing the requirements. What then is different about the environment investigated in this study?

The situation is one in which the package was required to satisfy a fairly standard range of accounting functions such as accounts receivable, accounts payable, general ledger, etc. In this environment, many packages would be able to satisfy the needs of smaller organizations, as investigated here, and it may well be that when the functional requirements of the package are fairly standard, thorough requirements analysis is not a suitable means of ensuring user satisfaction with the purchased software. It is likely (and weakly supported by the data) that the effect of carrying out a requirements study would be to increase the user's awareness of desirable system characteristics; this may reduce satisfaction when the system is a package. This is likely to be the case given the usual difficulty in modifying packaged software to meet changing needs.

The important point to be made from this research is that the gaining of user satisfaction with systems is not simply a matter of defining the requirements before a system is provided. The determinants of user satisfaction are more complex than this simple model, and are likely to include factors such as the user's understanding of the requirements, the flexibility of the software, the nature of the system, the nature of the organization, the manner in which the software was developed, and the extent of requirements analysis activity. In addition to these factors, there is the major issue of the impact of activities (such as requirements analysis) upon the end-user expectations. Many of the system development processes are concerned with the selection between alternatives. The consequences of this continuing selection process is the firming of expectations in the minds of the system participants. If the environment is one in which little real choice exists (e.g. software which is difficult to modify) then the presentation of options (such as in a requirements definition phase), may only serve to increase expectations which cannot be satisfied. Further research is needed to investigate the prerequisites for user satisfaction with computer based systems.

## 7. Summary and Conclusions

In social science research of this nature there is extreme difficulty in controlling the experiment for alternative hypotheses. However, it is not possible to simulate in the laboratory the events and conditions encountered in the field. Therefore there is no opportunity to conduct a true experiment in which only the independent variables are manipulated. In these circumstances the internal validity of the research is inevitably under threat. On the other hand, the external validity, or potential for generalization, is usually enhanced. This is a result of there being less abstraction from reality than is often present in the true experiment. Given that the major threat to the validity of experiments of this design is the lack of control for alternative hypotheses, an attempt has been made to anticipate those alternatives as far as possible. To achieve this, data was collected on variables that are perceived to carry the greatest potential for confounding the results. These may then be treated as independent variables and alternative hypotheses tested.

This approach was adopted in the current exercise. The limitations of this technique are that important variables may have been overlooked, and that relationships between variables missed. With this in mind, it is necessary for results to be confirmed before they can be generalized with confidence. This particularly holds true when the results of the initial research produces unusual results.

In so far as this exercise produced counter-intuitive results, it can only be said that there are indications that in the particular population sampled, requirements analysis activities are not strongly linked with overall satisfaction with the software package acquired, and the major hypothesis cannot be rejected. Thus the usual call for thorough requirements definition can only be a part of the necessary prerequisites for user satisfaction. Many other factors are likely to be important.

This research has indicated an anomaly that does exist, albeit in a rather limited population. It is necessary to conduct further research to relax the limitations of the result obtained here. Triangulation studies might reveal more subtle factors to be considered, such as the effect of the capacity of the organisation to adapt their operations to suit the design of the package.

The importance of this research is that it points to the need for exhaustive analysis of the role of requirements analysis in the acquisition of packaged software. The intuitive assumption that good requirements analysis is a condition precedent for successful implementation of packaged software has been shown to be doubtful.
