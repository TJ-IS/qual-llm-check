# The Relevance of Microcomputers to Health Improvement in Developing Countries

Aziz El-Kholy

Secretary General, Board of Health, Ministry of Health, Cairo, Egypt

and

Salah Mandil

Director, Division of Information Systems Support, World Health Organization, Geneva, Switzerland

The views expressed in this paper are those of the authors and are based on observations, findings and conclusions drawn from a joint WHO-Egypt study in support of that part of the 5-year health plan (1979–82) of the Ministry of Health of Egypt which dealt with the strengthening of health information systems for the management of health resources. The joint study included the actual pilot operation in two Egyptian provinces – Fayoum and South Cairo – of microcomputer-supported health data collection, validation, processing, printing and distribution, from and to users.

Sections 2 and 3 briefly discuss how current and foreseen developments in the medical approaches to many of the major health problems in the developing countries appear to coincide with tremendous breakthroughs in the information technology (Section 6), which in turn contribute to alleviating many of the impediments to the two-way process of information collection, processing, printing and distribution. Section 7 briefly discusses the loaded techno-political issue of micro-informatics technology transfer, and how (Section 8) an international effort could assist in this respect.

Keywords: Health Management, Strategies, Medical Informatics, Microcomputers, Design Requisites, Appropriate Technology, Technology Transfer, International Cooperation.

Dr. Aziz El-Kholy studied in France, and MPH from the School of Public Health, and in California. He started his career as an Epidemiologist and then successively as Director of three major Disease Control Programmes. He was then Secretary General of the Board of Health, Egypt. He is at present the Chairman of the Egyptian General Organization for Biological Products and Vaccines.

## 1. Introduction

It was once remarked that the fire of progress is lit by inspiration, fuelled by information (informatics) and sustained by hope and hard work.

The Alma Ata declaration of “Health for All by the Year 2000” has been endorsed by all the slowly developing countries. Considered, by many, as only a slogan to induce a moral commitment to the enhancement of human rights, it appears today as a genial foresight of the technical advances in the medical field – and related supporting fields – and as a tool for speeding up the socio-economic development of the Third World.

It is our contention, based on recent experience in Egypt, that the use of microprocessor-based health information systems, as an integral part of health services activities in the developing world could, if seriously pursued, be the most important single factor which would bring this ambitious goal of “health for all” closer to the realm of reality. The capability and characteristics of microprocessors make them compatible with the conditions prevailing in the third World and, appropriately used, could ensure the timeliness and quality of usable health information. By itself, the microcomputing gadgetry cannot eliminate health problems. But, for many well-understood health problems, today's microprocessing capabilities make it possible to introduce significant and effective improvements in the traditional approach to addressing such problems.

## 2. Health Problems and Traditional Approaches

The basic health problems of the majority of the population of the slowly developing countries have been identified for a long time. A plethora of infectious and nutritional diseases leads to a high frequency of morbidity, premature deaths and crippling disabilities. Almost 30 percent of these populations are children under 15 years. Although infancy and childhood represent only a fraction of the life span, they influence the course of adult life and the life of future generations, and inhibit attempts to control population growth.

Until two decades ago, there were hardly any affordable effective medical interventions to solve these basic health problems. Health related interventions, such as sanitation, proper nutrition, and behavioural changes were attempted to promote health in these countries, but without success because they require heavy capital investment and long term programmes.

It is not surprising, therefore, that most health activities in the developing world are rarely target oriented. They embrace, without discrimination, almost every health problem area, ignoring some main strands in the historical development of medical intervention.

The adoption of intervention activities in the areas of diagnosis, case management, therapy and health education without adequate evidence of effectiveness and without confirmation of such effectiveness in the local environment, and within the available local resources, has lead to what A.L.F. Cochrane has called, with perspicacity “the nicest possible type of inflation.”

Parallel to and as a consequence of such development, health data and information has evolved around activities rather than objectives. The quantity of data generated is unduly large. The entering, processing and analysing of the entire nation's data are usually done centrally either because adequate facilities and expertise at intermediary levels are lacking or because of autocratic attitudes to health leadership. Consequently, the workload at the central level has always led to such long delays that the information produced was of no practical use in health planning, health management or even in the field of health education and training.

## 3. Current and Foreseen Developments in the Field of Medicine and Public Health

Today, direct medical interventions are available for the prevention of much of the burden of illnesses and deaths of the young in the developing countries.

Vaccines are available to prevent many childhood diseases, such as tetanus, diphtheria, polio, measles and tuberculosis. Oral rehydration is effective in preventing premature deaths due to diarrhoeal diseases which are responsible for some 40–60 percent of infant deaths in the Third World. Effective treatment of schistosmiasis is available with safe, new and inexpensive oral drugs. Simple and reliable diagnostic techniques are increasingly available and allow for better identification of epidemic situations and for improved case management.

Developments in pharmacology, immunology, vaccine production and chemotherapy will probably add in the near future many more effective medical interventions against other poverty associated diseases. There are already good prospects that the development of new anti-secretory and anti-emetic drugs, and that of vaccines against rotavirus and some toxins of the enterotoxigenic E. coli, will allow the prevention of many diarrhoeal diseases and better case management. Pneumococcal, meningococcal and Haemophilus type b vaccines are already in field trials and may prove effective in preventing bacterial meningitis and some lower respiratory infections in infants and children. There are also good prospects that vaccines will be developed against the respiratory syncitial virus and parainfluenza C virus, which represent major causes of death in infants. Vaccines against hepatitis B virus and perhaps virus A, will also be available soon for field trials. There is a high probability that more of such effective medical interventions will be available in the forthcoming decade.

For the first time in history, the medical sciences, as a specialized technology, can be used effectively to prevent morbidity which is responsible for the vast state of collective ill health in the developing world and also to enhance attempts to control their growth. The challenge to the medical profession today, in the slowly developing world, is to use medical technology to promote some aspects of the health of the population in an environment that still induces a high risk of morbidity and mortality, while interfacing effectively with the national socio-economic development.

## 4. Health Informatimon Systems: Key Prerequisites to Their Design

The successful community-wide application of available effective medical technology to improve the health of the impoverished population throughout the Third World will require the development of adequate national health information systems as a fundamental and integral element of any national health services. Timely adequate health information is necessary for monitoring proper implementation of health services programmes, their progress and cost-effectiveness. Such information is also essential for health planning and for the development of relevant and appropriate educational curricula and training programmes.

Some general principles adopted by the WHO Inter-Regional Consultation on National Health Information Systems in Costa Rica, 1979 (paper ISS/78/13 Rev. 4), recognize the necessity to develop the information system incrementally, in order to gear information needs to specific, explicit and detailed health services objectives within a long range plan and design. To be able to specify data and information requirements and consequently the information system that deals with these, it is essential (a) that these health objectives be amenable to tangible identification and measurements, (b) that the medical intervention related to such objectives are proven to be effective, and (c) that their costs are within available resources.

## 5. Health Information: Some Consideratimons Affecting Implementation

In order to begin to design a successful national health information system, it is essential to comply with at least the three conditions listed above. Its successful implementation however requires the adequate and timely collection, storage, processing, analysis and interpretation of data and the generation of information, its publication and dissemination to users, as well as a comparable process for the resulting feedback information. The timeliness of adequate information is a necessary condition for its use. The amount of data at the processing level and the remoteness of information for its users and recipients are critical factors.

Collection of data occurs usually at local small health centres representing the peripheral units of the national health services infrastructure. The workload at this level (the gathering of the minimum requirements of data, generated by and collected from a set of selected effective health services programmes), will be moderate and amenable to hand manipulation. However, at this level the need, expertise and facilities for processing, analysis, interpretation, publication and exchange of information is usually not available and may not be cost effective. The transportation of raw data gathered at local levels to the national level leads to a massive accumulation of data with long delays, sometimes in terms of years, for its assembly, processing and exchange when only manual skill is involved. Given such a volume of data, the only solution has been the use of centrally-placed, large and expensive computers. In a large country with poor communications facilities, such a bottom-to-top approach for the assembly of data and a top-to-bottom approach for the exchange of information, would probably be unduly delayed, thus defeating the purpose of the collection of the data, which must be timely for use in decision making.

An alternative approach is to assemble, process, analyse and interpret data and publish and exchange information, at and from, an intermediate point in the cycling process. This point would be in between the central national focal point and the local peripheral units of the health care delivery “hierarchy.” Such an intermediate point would have to be selected through some “optimal” method that depends on geography, population size and available or readily-trainable personnel.

It is difficult to estimate the size of population which would generate meaningful information in terms of morbidity, disability and mortality while making effective use of available resources. It would seem that for many developing countries 200,000 population would be a reasonable size, since that could generate a manageable volume of data on medically preventable diseases of low case fatality or disability rates. A detailed study of such optimal population size should be made for each national situation. However, with even an optimal population size, the amount of data generated may not be amenable to hand sorting and processing. Expertise at that level is normally scarce for both data analysis and its interpretation; publication and duplication facilities would also be rare.

The geographical area of a 200,000 population will vary from one part to another in the same country. The distance between the intermediate focal point (for data processing and distribution of information) and that of the local peripheral health units will determine the ways and means of the timely assembly of data and exchange of information between generator and recipient of data.

It seems therefore that the minimum requirements for information about selected health service programmes and medical interventions can only be satisfied with the support of appropriate facilities for computer-processing, publication and communication.

## 6. The Role of Microcomputers

The assembly, publication and distribution of health information will require a vast network of communications, extensive computerization and publication facilities. But such facilities (because of cost and complicated logistics) will probably not be available in most developing countries in the near future, and certainly not if they are to be developed for the specific purpose of Health Information Systems.

The second best alternative, if not the only one at present, is the use of the newer and more appropriate microcomputer technology for the intermediate level of optimal population and geographic working size. It can be made compatible with the environmental, technical and logistic needs of most developing countries. The cost of such technology is already within the available resources.

At present, a microcomputer of 32,000 to 64,000 characters of memory can process and compute nearly all of the health indicators needed for the affordable and medically preventable health service programmes covering 200,000 populations. The secondary storage and printing facilities are equally easily provided. Such microcomputer configurations are less sensitive to environmental conditions than mainframes, and need no expensive special-floor or air-conditioned rooms. It requires a desktop, an electrical connection and a reasonably clean office environment. Its operation is simple and requires far less expertise than a traditional computer. The whole configuration will cost in the range of US \$5000 and less. The time required for processing the health data and printing the information outputs will not use more than 10 working days (6 hours per day) per month leaving as many working days for the gathering of data and the distribution of information up and down the hierarchy.

The use of microcomputer technology on the intermediate level not only solves the problems of processing the large volume of data and of providing timely information, it also enlarges the base of users, enhancing the probability of a successful transfer of technology. It disciplines and trains people on planning, logistics and objective measurements and allows for the large diffusion of health information among medical and health-related personnel. It actually addresses the primary health care level, where assistance is necessary and can be most effective.

The use of microcomputer technology therefore appears as an imperative supportive prerequisite for the successful implementation of target-oriented health programmes and for upgrading people participation and for decentralization of the development effort.

## 7. Problems of Microcomputer Technology Transfer

The critics of the transfer of computer technology to the Third World are usually concerned with (a) the lack of expertise in developing the appropriate applications and software, (b) the cost of operation, (c) the difficulties of maintenance, and (d) the effects on labour employment.

These views are unjustified. Traditional computer technology, which technically and resourcewise is more demanding than microprocessor technology, is being used successfully in many developing countries. It is presently used in the field of air transportation, banking establishments, security and armed forces, biomedical researches and in the diagnosis and monitoring of diseases.

It is important to distinguish between the operation of sophisticated equipment from the development of technologies underlying their creation and production. People in developing countries operate electrical, mechanical and electronic equipment. They fly sophisticated aircraft, use cars, brain scanners, operate oil refineries, weather stations, hydro-electric and various industrial plants. This does not imply that they have been very active in the development of any of the technologies underlying any of this equipment, but it demonstrates their ability to operate relatively sophisticated equipment notwithstanding some waste. In fact, they do, and there is ample evidence that they provide the critical mass of people amenable to proper training for the use and operation of microcomputers and their effective servicing.

The problems with the transfer of technology are, however, manifold. One important problem is that the commercial competition in the industry is producing a very wide diversity of hardware components, with similar basic characteristics, but with minor differences preventing the universal use of a single software entity or program for a specific application. This type of diversity and variety in hardware and software creates a trade dependence which could lead to abuses and exploitation. A good balance must be sought between the competitiveness that promotes creativity and technical excellence and the trade dependence that leads to abuses and exploitation. This problem is also badly felt in the developed countries but its economic, not-to-mention other consequences, are somehow being absorbed and the call for standards is not accompanied by legislation and laws binding commercial companies.

Hardware and software modifications to allow input and output in native languages is a relatively simple process but unduly priced or simply not available in the developing world. The cost of a unit is bound to tremendous manipulation by producers and distributors. System analysis and applications software development are also still unduly high priced.

These and comparable other problems are surmountable and have been overcome in certain pilot examples; the problems are not however enough to hinder or slow a full exploitation of the opportunities raised by microcomputer uses in the health sector.

## 8. Need for an International Effort

The WHO is already engaged in supporting its Member States in the identification of health objectives related to effective medical interventions, in the development of appropriate general and specific health indicators and in the development of concepts and guidelines for related health information systems support.

It would seem that the time has come for WHO to be more involved and take initiatives, with its Member States, in the study of the logistics and requisites for implementing such information systems and in developing institutionalized expertise in “the state of the art” of the microcomputer technology. In general terms, the needs of developing countries for technical support in the field of health informatics could include, as far as the subject of this paper is concerned:

1. Determination, by simulation studies, of the need for the use of microcomputer technology (a) for the timely handling of the volumes of data generated from selected, effective health services programmes on a scale sufficient to be of assistance to decision makers in monitoring the activities related to the objective of these programmes and (b) identifying further needs for the implementation of new target oriented activities. This could lead inter alia to the determination of an optimal population and geographical size needed to generate the minimum data that will render meaningful and useful information when processed and analysed, and which will be compatible with low cost microcomputer capabilities. Such optimal size would depend on at least three major types of variables:

(a) The health status of the population concerned and the health services programmes actually in operation, plus the nature and amount of data needed to establish the evaluation indicators of the performance of the health services;

(b) The facilities needed for computerization,

printing and distribution of such information;

(c) The resources available in terms of funds and trained or trainable personnel and the availability of electricity, servicing and transportation facilities.

The determination of such optimal size working units will determine the proper level at which adequate technical support is wanted and can be used. It could also lead to new strategic and tactical approaches for health development in the developing countries.

2. Developing wareness among the health leaders of the developing world, of the potential for the use of microcomputer systems in the promotion of the health of their countries.

3. Assisting Member States in selecting the right hardware and in testing the practicability and ease of operation of proposed systems.

4. assisting in negotiating, with industry, the charges for their services, including the adaptation of a range of hardware and software components to local needs, e.g. character generation in native languages; more robust battery-powered devices.

5. Initiating a research programme which could lead ultimately to a flexible and versatile package software and low cost tailored hardware for mass use in the developing countries, for the implementation of health activities relating to Primary Health Care and Health for All by the Year 2000.

The Division of Informatimon Systems Support and several other divisions and technical programmes, of the World Health Organization, are presently collaborating with several institutions in developed and developing countries to study the usefulness, modus operandi and costs of using microcomputers in the health sector, particularly for health statistics, health literature and the administrative management of health activities.
