# Office Automation in British Rail – On Delivering Value to End Users \*

Michael G. Ferrand and Neil Fairhead \*\*
British Railways Board, 222 Marylebone Road, London NW1 6JJ, UK

The development of a strategy for office automation within a large organisation is described. The process begins with the analysis of the existing office workers', some 25,000 staff. This analysis was used to identify potential benefits and target areas for office technology. The paper then describes the concepts used to think through the application of office automation. These were combined with the Corporation's business strategy to produce the strategy for office automation. This strategy was based on using office automation to help organisational change, particularly in the three key areas identified by the Corporation's top management. Also described in the article is an approach to identifying information requirements and separately, some comments on the training and education required.

Keywords: Office Automation, Strategy, Information Systems, Information Analysis, Organisational Analysis, Development Methodologies, Training, Systems Development, Word Processing, Organisational Development.

\*\* N. Fairhead is employed at: N. Fairhead is employed at Digital Equipment Co. Ltd, PO Box 131, Reading, England.

## 1. Precis

The managing director of a major computer manufacturer recently said “The electronics industry improves the price performance of chips tenfold every five years. The computer industry takes about seven years to deliver the same performance improvement, but how long does it take your DP department to deliver a tenfold increase in value to its users?”

The problem is no longer the supply of technology but the delivery of real value to end users. This paper describes the case experiences in developing a strategy for office automation in a large European corporation, using information technology now available.

## 2. Background

British Rail is a state owned corporation with over 161,000 railway employees and a turnover of nearly \$4 billion. It is under heavy pressure to reduce costs.

In many ways, a railway is a fertile field for the application of information technology. The organisation is large and covers practically the entire British mainland. Despite its size it is remarkably homogeneous with a closely linked production process. In few other industries can a machine failure in the north east cause a shortage of productive facilities in the south west within a few hours. The product is perishable and there is no buffer stock between the production line and the consumer. There is also a high proportion of repeat purchases, indeed commuters sample the product five days a week. There is no fooling a British Rail customer. It is deliver or else. And to an unrecognised extent BR does deliver; approximately 16,000 trains run every day, and 14,000 arrive within 5 minutes of their schedules.

The results of these production process characteristics is a tremendous demand for information, both by the operators (the production department) and the customers. To meet this demand there is a range of communications systems including a private telephone system whose lines go everywhere that railway tracks do; a private telex network, and several online computing systems, including a production recording system which tracks every train. However, enormous volumes of information are still communicated on paper.

Two years ago, a programme for installing word processing was under way. At the corporate HQ and the main offices of the five Regions, shared resource systems were being installed in the main typing pools, often replacing manual typewriters. However, in truth, these merely mechanised the existing systems. At that time, the Internal Management Consulting Department, which was responsible for the word processing programme (but not data processing nor telecommunications) started to plan the next stage – office automation. This paper describes the work done under the Director, Internal Consultancy Services, (now Director, Management Services).

## 3. The Office Automation Project

The objective of the planning exercise was to identify where and how to use office automation, to estimate the value and costs, and to develop a programme for its implementation. The definition of office automation used is:

"Office Automation is the application of information systems technology to increase the organisation's productivity by improving the effectiveness of the managerial, professional, para-professional, and clerical personnel who currently work within the office."

## 3.1. Stage 1 - Detailed Analysis and Evaluation

The starting point was a detailed analysis of the managerial, professional, and clerical work undertaken in the corporation. A major project was undertaken by the internal consulting group into the activities of the corporate and regional headquarters departments. The workload of 23,000 staff was analysed, with the object of reducing overhead costs.

Firstly the workload of the departments was divided into two categories:

1. Mandatory work; is essential to the continued operations of the corporation; or

2. Discretionary work; not essential but undertaken because management believe their value outweighs their cost, i.e., the activities are at management's discretion.

Next a detailed analysis of the discretionary activities was undertaken. The objective was the reduction of overhead cost through the elimination, simplification or merging of discretionary work. For each department, the discretionary work was divided into main activities and then into sub-activities. Each activity was identified, described (including the staff required) and costed by a small team. Each team, which analysed one department, consisted of one consultant, one member of the department from the corporate HQ, and one member of the department from a Regional HQ. The activities were then ranked with departmental managers' agreement. This process forced line managers' to prioritize their departments' tasks.

Following this, the team identified those activities duplicated elsewhere in the department and those activities which could be merged and simplified. Further, they challenged departmental managers to justify the value of continuing the activities which the managers had themselves ranked low in importance.

For each department, the end result was a list of activities and sub-activities divided into three parts. The first category contained those activities and sub-activities which both the departmental managers and the investigation teams agreed were still justified. The middle contained the activities where the departmental managers and teams disagreed, usually the teams recommended cuts while the responsible managers believed that the value of the activities outweighed the costs. These were referred to senior management for decision. The last identified those activities and sub-activities which both managers and teams agreed needed cutting back or even stopping.

The end results of this exercise were three fold. Firstly the administrative workload was cut by over 10%, with minimal loss of value. Secondly, a tremendous amount of management training had taken place. In a large bureaucratic organisation it is not always possible to ensure that all managers concentrate on the basics of value and cost!

Finally, we had gathered a large base of information on the majority of the white collar work in the organisation.

## 3.2. Stage 2 - Identifying IT Opportunities and Values

The next stage was to use this information to identify the value of information technology. With the assistance of Urwick-Nexos (now Eosys), we developed a set of characteristics which described work that is likely to benefit from information technology, as follows:

1. The same or very similar work is undertaken by four or more staff working in the same group.

2. The same sort of work is done elsewhere.

3. Proven technology already exists but is not currently used on this activity by this group.

4. The work depends on information and

(i) can be defined by a series of procedures/ rules

(ii) the information needed to undertake the work can be reasonably pre-specified.

5. The information handled is for/from another automated system such as a word processor or computer.

The investigative teams then rated every activity against these characteristics, showing high, medium, or low potential for productivity improvement through the application of information technology. The result was that 38% of the work (measured by staff employed) was in the high potential category, 30% in the medium, and 32% in low. We then had to estimate what this meant in real terms. Because of the difficulty in measuring white collar productivity we used range estimates with an upper and lower bound of the productivity improvements attainable as shown in Table 2.

We were also very conservative. For example, typing pools fall into the high potential category. When implementing word processing in these pools we usually exceeded a 75% productivity improvement. However, typing is perhaps the only white collar work where the productivity can be so easily measured. Hence we chose to use an estimate of only 25% as the upper bound high productivity improvement in calculating the benefits. This recognized the political realities of the organization, and its ability to absorb change.

The last step in this analytical process was to estimate the total potential benefits from installing information technology in the main managerial, professional and regional offices. Firstly the number of staff at the corporate and divisional offices was reduced by one-third to reflect the impact of the discretionary cost analysis and other initiatives leaving a total of 16,000 staff. These staff were allocated between the high, medium, and low potential categories, and the resulting staff reductions calculated as shown in Table 3. From this direct cost savings could be calculated to give the potential benefits.

The benefits convinced us to try ever harder to boost investment in office automation. The analysis also highlighted the main target areas which could show high return on investment. What it did not show is how to use office automation, what overall strategy is needed, what guidelines and policies are best, and what organization will be effective. To answer these questions we had to build a conceptual foundation as well.

## 3.3. The Conceptual Foundation

Any large organisation has a wide collection of component parts and, in particular, a large number of offices. British Rail has nearly four hundred local management units across the entire country. Each unit may have several hundred staff, and is led by a manager with a small support staff. At the same time, there are also corporate support groups, for example, there are two legal offices, one in Glasgow for Scottish Law, and the other in London for all other legal advice.

We had to impose a structure on this diversity. Looking at offices as the potential “customers” for office automation, we had to segment the market. The criteria used were the size of the office, the variety of the work done in the office, and the number of similar offices in the corporation. This is shown diagrammatically in Fig. 1. One more factor was required to complete the structure, namely the level within the organisation. We used the now traditional breakdown of strategic, control, and operations. A simple pattern was immediately apparent.

At the operational level, offices are small, multifunctional, and numerous. For example, there are over 100 traffic area managers responsible for the provision of services in discrete contiguous geographic areas. They are responsible for selling tickets, running trains, and managing up to 1800 staff, with the assistance of relatively few clerical staff.

Hence the Area Manager and his support staff are using information on a wide range of subjects including receipts; train operating data (e.g., the planned and actual train times); personnel records; time and attendance records; and budgets.

At the control level, we found a different pattern. Here offices tend to be large, single purpose and with several but not many replications. Examples are: accounting offices, drawing and design offices, purchasing sections, typing pools, and data input centres. The staff involved are performing very similar work, usually in parallel, and they are concerned with changing the degree of diversity involved. For example, accounting offices are often involved in producing summaries from information provided by a number of similar sources (e.g., the cash receipts from stations).

At the strategic level, the situation superficially resembles the operational level with small offices dealing with varied subjects (e.g., the corporate planners). However, there are three significant differences. Firstly there is little replication – there is only one Chief Executive, one Director of Personnel, or one Board Secretariat. Secondly, much of the information needed comes from outside the organisation, and thirdly, there is a high degree of uncertainty involved.

This overall pattern of office type is matched by a similar pattern of information within them, as shown in Fig. 2. Considering only the upward flow, information starts at a large number of operational points within the local management units, each of which creates information on a number of subjects, e.g., at a booking office, cash receipts for finance, tickets sold for marketing, staff hours worked for payroll, and staff details for personnel. This information is sorted by function and flows to the control level for aggregation and analysis; the results of this may flow back together at the strategic or senior executive level. In this way, conceptually, we built up a model of the organisation in order to identify any underlying patterns on which to base a strategy for the use of office automation.

## 3.4. Distinguishing data from information

However, further conceptualisation was needed in identifying how information is of value to the organisation. It is crucial to distinguish between data and information. Data are facts, with a cost: information acquires value by the use of those data.

Consequently the value of information can only be understood with reference to the customers' use.

Use is related to decisions. Information is required to recognise that a decision to change must be made. Information is used to determine how to make the decision. Finally, information is needed to implement the decision.

The value of the information is related to the possible outcomes of the decision. Where there is a wide range in the value of possible outcomes, then information which increases the probability of a high value outcome is itself valuable. On the other hand, where there is little difference in the value between the various options available, then the value of the information is negligible.

The decisions which managers make fall into two categories. Most managers have a set of key decisions, relatively small in number; they set the main patterns for control. However, key decisions often require large numbers of minor decisions. These are “primary” and “secondary” decisions; they are the independent and dependent variables of the user requirements.

To give an example, consider a train running ten minutes late. It passes a recording point where its time and location (data) are logged. The time is compared with the plan, i.e. the timetable, and the delay (data) calculated. However, a decision maker is informed that the train is ten minutes late. He decides to hold a connecting train to enable passengers on the delayed train (now information) to catch their connection. This is done, and the value obtains from the improved service received by the passengers who catch the “held” train.

The key decision is to hold the train. However there are a number of secondary decisions. The signalling system must be informed, and the signals set to hold the train. The train may occupy a platform for longer than planned, possibly leading to a re-arrangement of platforms used by later or arriving trains. In turn, the station staff must be informed; they inform passengers, etc..

A crucial task is the identification of all secondary decisions resulting from each primary decision, to ensure that the necessary information is available at the right place and time. A methodology called “event tracking” has been developed for this. It is described briefly in the Appendix 2.

## 3.5. The Resulting Strategy

Strategy is a living flexible framework that must change. The following paragraphs are an example: a situation in British Rail; that has already developed further.

The strategy selected was the result of influence of several factors. The analysis described above showed the value of office automation to British Rail. The questions were where to start and how to apply office automation. The answers were provided by other work being developed within British Rail including,

Firstly,

1. Productivity indicators, recently developed by the Director, Consultancy Services, predicted insupportable deterioration in both Corporate Headquarters and Engineering administration costs;

2. British Rail had just adopted a corporate strategy to push towards “sector” or market oriented management structure; and

3. Agreement had just been reached to eliminate a whole tier of the line management of the railway, in order to achieve a drastic reduction in staff numbers and a more responsive organisation.

These three factors led to an overall corporate strategy for the future management and administration of British Rail. This, in turn, gave direction to the office automation strategy. As in most large organisations, the development of the office automation strategy required an iterative, consensus seeking approach. However, through the twists and turns of discussion, the goal remained simple – responsibility was to be devolved as far down the management chain as possible, and office automation was to be used to help that process.

BR strategy for the use of office automation had two main parts. Firstly the identification and description of the major application areas, and secondly the infrastructure required. Three key areas were selected as those with highest priority: the line management of the railway, the administration of the engineering departments, and the corporate head office. The objectives in each case were improved performance and reduced costs. To some extent, the opportunity areas have already been overtaken by the demand by senior management for radical change. One of the largest projects is aimed at achieving the savings from the earlier study.

Two key infrastructure requirements have also been identified. One is a telecommunications network capable of meeting the growing requirements. Because of the requirements of the signalling system and the availability of the necessary rights of way, British Rail has its own telecommunications system. Planning for this network is obviously ongoing, and the latest plan will include office automation. Appendix 1 shows the qualitative requirement. With the assistance of the consultants, initial quantitative estimates have been made. The second infrastructure requirement is management education. Like any other major resource, management can only be reshaped with time and effort. Many managers have only limited experience with information technology, and that is usually with large systems, based on mainframes, and supported by a central group.

Before they can make the most of an end user oriented technology they will need additional skills and knowledge.

## 4. Initial Results and General Conclusions

A number of conclusions have been drawn from the work to date. They are largely concerned with how to use office automation.

1. Office automation should be tightly linked to organisational change.

While office automation can be used to make immediate staff savings, this is often fractionnated; a micro-processor goes in here and one clerk comes out etc.. This “death of 1000 cuts” approach produces the maximum pain with minimum savings. It is far better to use office automation to facilitate the process of organisational change.

We would rather design a new organisation for the company, division, departments, etc., making it as small as feasible. Then make it productive through investment in systems. Information systems must be linked to the organisational objectives – but how? By building the organisation and its supporting systems in tandem, this problem can be resolved.

British Rail is presently a four “tier” organisation. There is the corporate headquarters at the top, supported by five Regions each in turn divided into approximately five Divisions, and they in their turn, into approximately five Areas. (This is the railway management and excludes supporting functions, such as engineering groups). The organisational target is to move to a three tier organisation, re-assigning most of the current roles and responsibilities of the Divisional level down to the Area level.

Several projects have been started on developing the contribution which can be made by information technology. In particular, there are projects designed at gaining experience in using office automation at each of the three key levels. At the same time, organisational studies are underway designing the new organisational structure, and the resulting requirements for information.

## 2. Standards are very important.

Often standards are seen as reducing flexibility. However, in office automation the converse is true: standard tools lead to flexibility in use. Obviously the standards must be wide-ranging, covering hardware, operating systems, telecommunications, software development methods, data base management systems, and selected end user applications and facilities. Standardised applications lead to data which is not only compatible but also comparable. This is a crucial factor in a large organisation, which is aggregating data across a number of sources.

Mandatory standards are necessary for a system to work at all, e.g. standards for telecommunications. These are usually technical standards. Discretionary standards are implemented at management's discretion. These are valuable rather than necessary. The selection of a standard range of workstations is valuable because of reduced purchase, maintenance, and staff training costs. However, discretionary standards are often emotionally charged. They are seen as the imposition of a staff group's decisions on line managers.

3. A major educational effort is required. Managers at all levels of the organisation need to ask themselves “Given the capabilities that office automation is delivering, how can I organise my work and department to gain greater effectiveness?” Managers will need education in systems thinking. They will also need motivation to accept that the status quo is not sufficient, and that improvement is required because competitors may move faster.

Training is also required by end users; the value obtained from a given investment in office automation is very closely related to the competence and confidence of the end user. The inherent flexibility leads to much more dependence on the user to control the system. To maximise the benefits the end user should obtain almost immediate value from the system and yet also be introduced to the full capabilities of advanced stages (later).

The office automation support team should be selected largely from the rank of end users or the organisation and methods analysts rather than traditional data processing staff. It is usually quicker to train the former along the lines shown in Appendix 1 than to re-orient the latter.

4. The prime criterion for equipment should be the effectiveness of the end user.

This is not a technically based criterion. It is criterion related to the delivery of value to end users. It is not an exact criterion; it will require judgements about value, and probability of success. Finally, it should even the balance between reduced cost and added value as justification for investment. Traditional investment evaluation has rated cost reduction, usually staff reduction, as “hard” and added value as “soft” benefits. This has biased investment towards the lowest cost solution to single applications. As a result the potential for adding function to existing systems has been stunted. For example, dumb terminals have been selected where intelligent terminals could have provided the basis for the local processing of locally relevant data. Looking at the overall effectiveness of end users, who are usually involved in several different tasks, will highlight the value of other potential uses.

## 5. The Next Step

Within British Rail, we have the implementation of presently usable office automation, mainly text processing and personal computing; and simultaneously, there is the development work for the next stage. Pilot projects need to be completed and evaluated. These include trials of equipment, facilities, and the ability to develop systems rapidly. The evaluation criteria must include as many measures of end user productivity as possible. One particularly important trial presently underway is the use of rapid prototyping systems. The present long systems development cycle is a major weakness in the application of office automation. Managerial, administrative, and professional work is too subject to change and inherent uncertainty for traditional pre-specification of all the detailed requirements.

It is this intrinsic incompatibility between development method and user characteristics that causes so much dissatisfaction with data processing. As office automation penetrates further into the imprecise world of managers, we must have development methods to match.

Suppliers have further work to do to enable office automation to be used successfully. They must devote more attention to developing the techniques and methods that help their customers use the technology. In data processing, the major suppliers made enormous efforts to develop methodologies to assist their customers in implanting computer systems with high level languages and the development of structured development techniques as examples.

To date, the major vendors of office automation have not provided the same degree of assistance in the area of portable methodologies for planning and implementing office automation.

To give just one example, consider the financial justification of office automation. This is, all too often, an act of faith by senior managers. There are two crucial elements. Firstly how do we predict and then measure improvements in the effectiveness of white collar staff. Is increased effectiveness of a draughtsman more drawings per annum or better designs which lead to fewer modifications and hence decrease the need for more drawings? Put simplistically, we can (intuitively) see the answer; but where are the techniques to evaluate and put a value on the alternatives?

The second element concerns evaluating proposed investments in systems where the majority of applications will be developed by end users over time. We need to estimate a project worth which reflects the life cycle costs and probable benefits from the different potential applications which end users may develop. However, further work and experience is required before reliable estimates of those benefits can be made.

This again is an area for the major vendors. The impact of office automation on the cost side of the profit and loss account is clear. The benefits must be equally clear.

Office automation is not about hardware and software, it is about delivered value to the end users. It is the responsibility of vendors, support teams and corporate managers to ensure that the maximum value is obtained.

## Appendix 1

## Office Automation Training

Training of the support team is fundamental to the success of an office automation programme. Beginning with staff who are basically users or O&M professionals with some computer literacy, the training must include the following elements.

## 1. Inter-personal Skills

There is a basic requirement to give the support team training in inter-personal skills to enable them to help end users who are facing considerable uncertainty.

## 2. Management-of-Change

Any office automation support team will be small in numbers compared with the end users. One way of leveraging their impact is through the provision and use of a formalised management-of-change methodology. Also, it will help to gain user confidence by demonstrating the professionalism of the support team.

## 3. Conceptual Information Skills

Both the support team and the user management will need training in information concepts. This should start at the concepts of data, information, and decisions, and then move into the more practical questions of what information is required and why. The support team will also need training in data analysis, flow charting, and overall systems design.

## 4. General Hardware and Software Knowledge

All members of the support team will need a reasonable understanding of the basic elements and how they are assembled. They will also need an overview of the hardware and software available (the standard range and any non-standard items that may be allowed to meet peculiar needs.)

## 5. Specific System Skills

When equipment and software has been selected either to form a corporate standard or for a particular major project, the support team will need training on that equipment; but suppliers' training tends to concentrate on how to operate equipment, and this is not necessarily the same as using the system. The support team need time to develop the procedures and practices that most closely suit the requirements.

## 6. Professional Techniques

Techniques required include project planning and control, networking, budgetary management, documentation methods and standards, and group and meeting management techniques.

## 7. Team Building

With a small support team covering a wide range of applications and usually out in the field alongside users, sharing of experience is an essential part of the training programme. Team building exercises should be set up to ensure rapid sharing of good ideas. Such exercises maintain the support teams morale, build an experience base and teach support team members how to help their end users team build.

## Event Tracking Method in Outline

## Select events:

-Interview senior management to identify several ‘events’ or decisions which are important to their work (e.g. producing fare schedule adjustments).

-Establish with them further information about each event to assess broadly whether they are:

## -of high importance to British Rail

(e.g. affect large capital sums and are repeated in many locations).

-likely to benefit from information technology (e.g., if improving the speed or accuracy of information would be valuable).

-Ask person interviewed to produce short list from the events identified where they believe further investigation of potential applications would be worthwhile.

## Track contributions to the event:

\- First check if there is an obvious way in which specific equipment could be used to reduce the cost of the event or increase its value to British Rail. If so, collect information to confirm benefits, costs and volumes.

\- If no solution is obvious, subdivide event into a series of separate ‘contributions; which are necessary for the event.

\- Asking standard questions, establish which contributions are worthy of further investigation on the basis of whether they have a large influence on the cost/value of the event and are likely to benefit from further technology. (As a by-product this may show ‘contributions’ which could be bypassed.)

\- Continue reviewing to check if there is an obvious good application for new equipment; if no such good application appears, break down into smaller contributions until either an application is produced or a decision made to abandon this event. At each stage, collect standard information on the contribution in increasing detail.

## Advantage of Event Tracking Method

## Speed and simplicity

The modular (top down) approach - starting with an important ‘event’ -avoids the need for massive studies.

## Directed at 'high value' work

The selection of important ‘events’ avoids the risk of proposals to automate task which may subsequently be scrapped. It also emphasises the possibilities for more effective use of all resources; (not just personnel).

## Robust to changes in organisational structure

Tracking events and the contributions necessary for the event to take place relates the studies to the information flows, rather than to the current organisational structure.

## Common approach

This is helpful to the development of expertise and provision of comparable data from studies in different locations.
