# The Evolution of Intellectual Technologies: Applying Product Life-Cycle Models to MIS Implementation

Philip J. Pyburn

Boston University School of Management,

704 Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02215, USA

and

Kathleen Foley Curley
Northeastern University College of Business Administration,
Boston, MA 02115, USA

This article develops the theoretical construct of an “intellectual technology” (as distinct from traditional industrial technologies) as a way to explain the difficulties organizations have in implementing new computer-based applications. Building on the notions of a product and process life-cycle, this concept is used to suggest that non-traditional approaches to the implementation of computer-based applications may be necessary. The “intellectual technology” concept implies that organizations must learn to apply new applications in their particular environment, and that this learning explains the difficulty many firms have in implementing Office Automation, CAD/CAM, Decision Support Systems, and the like.

Keywords: DSS, Office Automation, Implementation, Intellectual Technologies, Product Life Cycle.

## 1. Introduction

Yet user organizations still seem to experience a high level of uncertainty regarding their particular use of the hardware in these new application areas and the benefits that this use will provide.

The authors therefore pose the following questions:

1. Why is there a high degree of uncertainty in many organizations regarding the application of major new computer-based systems?

2. Should this uncertainty be measured (and incorporated) as a parameter in models of user and producer behavior. In other words, should be unit of analysis include the product, the process used to make it, and its application and use?

In an attempt to answer these questions for one application area (word processing), we considered whether this technology was an innovative new product or simply an advance on a relatively mature product; i.e., a “mature” technology (an extension of the typewriter), or a radical new mechanism for accomplishing office work? On the one hand, it seems that the product is essentially mature, in that the electronics and keyboard design (on which word processing is based) is reasonably standardized. On the other hand, there seems to be a great deal of uncertainty regarding how this technology will change work patterns in the firms we studied – normally the evidence of an emerging new product.

To help us understand how the new information technologies fit within the context of the traditional models of product evolution, we undertook a comparative case study of 24 different companies [18]. We also surveyed an additional 21 companies on their acquisition and use of office automation, decision support, and CAD/CAM applications [5,19]. These companies represent a wide variety of manufacturing and service organizations, ranging from a \$50 million regional firm to a \$2.5 billion multinational. The research was conducted over a three-year period between 1980 and 1983.

These studies caused us to reevaluate our understanding of the way new technologies evolve over time, and what this means for the utilization of computer-based applications. The concept of an “intellectual technology” and its unique evolutionary path is the result of that reevaluation, and is the subject of this article.

## 2. Evolutionary Models of Technology Development

In our review of the way the firms in the study managed major new computer-based applications, we found it helpful to consider existing models which describe how technologies and products evolve. An understanding of this evolution is helpful, of course, because it provides insight into the future uses of a particular technology, based on the evolution of technologies that have come before. Two well-known models of technology evolution are the Product and Process Life-Cycle Models, and with traditional technologies such as the automobile and the airplane they have proven to be powerful tools for understanding the evolution of the product and industry [1].

The Product Life-Cycle model suggests that the early stages of a technology's development are characterized by uncertainty, both at the marketing and the technical levels, as the technology undergoes the transformation from an idea to a commercial venture. The interaction between producer and buyer defines the required dimensions of the product.

The transition from this early stage to an adolescent period of rapid growth is brought about by the introduction of at least one stable design which reduces the technical and marketing uncertainty and allows for volume production of the product. This transition might also be called the “take-off” stage, with new competitors jumping in to take advantage of increasing demand. The mature stage is characterized by replacement buying rather than new purchases. Because competition in this stage is based mostly on price, producers increasingly standardize the technology to reduce their costs.

A complementary concept is the notion of a “Technology” or “Process” Life-Cycle. Abernathy and Utterback [1] found that there were certain patterns of innovation which could be discerned over time within a given product technology. They showed how both the production process and the organization structure of a producer change as it matures from a small technology-based firm to a major high-volume producer. Hayes and Wheelwright [10], linking these two views of evolution, suggested that sound corporate strategy requires a recognition of this process with coordination of marketing and manufacturing policies appropriate to the stage of development.

Recognizing that evolutionary models provided useful guidelines for policy formation, Adeboye [2] developed a classification system as a tool for determining the evolutionary stage of a technology. He reasoned that if an accurate scale could be developed, then it would be possible to determine the life-cycle stage of any product/process segment. Once this was known, the models could define actions which would be most likely to succeed. Since Abernathy and others had identified “uncertainty” as a key characteristic of the early stage, Adeboye developed classification variables which would measure the degree of uncertainty associated with the following parameters: (1) Product Characteristics, (2) Manufacturing Dimension, (3) Marketing Dimension, and (4) Firm Characteristics. He assumed that a high degree of uncertainty regarding these items would indicate a technology in its early stage of development.

## 3. Incorporating User Uncertainty

In testing Adeboye's classification system to determine the evolutionary stage of word processors, it seemed that there were certain features, such as the micro-processor and video display, which were highly standardized, suggesting a mature product. On the other hand, uncertainty seemed to exist in the implementation and effective use of the technology, even in firms with extensive experience in data processing.

Of course, this notion of uncertainty has been recognized and addressed within the MIS literature for some time. Nolan, for example, proposes that organizations go through a series of identifiable stages in learning how to implement information systems $[16,17]$ . What is implied, but not stated explicitly, is that “maturity” in data processing occurs when users are fairly certain about the way in which they will use computer technology. This suggests that organizations must learn how to apply the technology, and that this learning can only occur through experience.

The user's learning process is not explicitly addressed by the Product/Process Life-Cycle models, however, even though it is this learning which apparently drives the evolution of information technologies. For example, Gorry and Scott-Morton [7] suggested a way of understanding organizational use of management information systems by categorizing managerial activity as a function of decision-making. They proposed a process where information systems, initially used to support structured tasks, evolved toward the support of tasks with less and less structure. They state that “as we improve our understanding of a particular decision, we can make it more structured and allow the system to take care of it, freeing the manager for other tasks.” In other words, they expect managers to learn to structure tasks by utilizing decision-support systems. Similarly, Meador and Ness [15], Keen [13], Radnor et al. [20] and Alter [3] all allude to the ongoing nature of user learning as part of the information systems implementation process. Keen notes that “the life-cycle effect is one of the few clearly supported and generalizable conclusions from implementation research”.

Many cases are also documented in the DSS literature. For example, Joyce and Oliver [12] describe how a planning group changed its way of defining and managing projects as a result of its use of a DSS. Ginzberg [6] postulates that changes in the manager's approach to (and even definition of) the necessary tasks are intrinsic to successful DSS adoption. Haseman [9] notes that the real problem in implementing GPLAN (a planning support DSS) was not in teaching the users how the system worked, but rather in getting them to make the role change from programmer/analyst to decision maker.

## 4. Intellectual Technologies

With most industrial technologies the physical design of a particular device determines, to a large extent, how the device will be used. For examples, a milling machine has only one real purpose – to cut, grind and shape various parts. The output of the machine can certainly be quite varied, but the use of the machine is pretty much defined by its original design. If a customer wants a machine which can both form the part and drill holes in it, then a vendor engineer must design a completely new machine.

Intellectual Technologies, on the other hand, vary greatly in use even though they are all based on essentially the same computer components. Because the computer is programmable, a device such as a personal computer can be a word processor for one user, a decision support system for another user, and a dumb terminal for a third user. Thus, the application of the machine can be defined (and redefined) by the purchaser or the vendor. The device purchased as “fast typewriter” to perform word processing can be used for many other purposes – but only if the user learns how to define, create, and utilize the machine in these new areas. Unlike industrial technologies, where utilization is constrained by physical design, Intellectual Technologies are limited only by the imagination of the user.

The demarcation between these technologies is imprecise, of course, and the attributes differ in emphasis and degree rather than being absolutes. Important differences do seem to exist, however, in the following dimensions:

1. Intellectual Technologies are flexible and malleable. The computer can be programmed in virtually unlimited ways, and people are continually learning and revising the methods by which they use the technology to solve various problems. This continuing and evolving learning process is not characteristic of industrial technologies, where the use of a device is more clearly defined within a narrow range of applicability.

2. The markets for Intellectual Technologies are best defined and understood by looking at the use of the product rather than at the product itself. The components of a home computer, a word processor, a small business data processor, and an executive work station are quite similar – it is the application of the components that defines the market. In other words, the market is more clearly defined by software than by hardware, and these may be at very different points on the product life-cycle. Management models which focus on the physical product and the related manufacturing process as the unit of analysis (i.e. the Product/Process Life-Cycle) overlook this important dimension of Intellectual Technologies. We need a theoretical model which will enable us to view the evolution of the hardware as separate from the evolution of its use.

3. For Intellectual Technologies, the concept of a product's evolution is different from that of an industrial product. For the latter, evolution depends on (1) the underlying technology, and (2) the packaging of that technology as a "product." For the former, evolution depends on these two factors plus the stage of user learning. There is a vast difference in the level of uncertainty of an executive who uses a spread sheet package three or four hours per week, and one who uses the same hardware to work at home, communicate with subordinates, and access outside data bases'– and this uncertainty must be considered part of the product's evolution. The notion of a "mature" product (in the sense of reduced uncertainty) is thus much different for Intellectual Technologies than for industrial technologies.

## 5. The Intellectual Technology Life-Cycle

Because of these differences, we think it makes sense to extend the two-dimensional Product/Process model to include the evolutionary nature of user learning and address the fact that the same technology can be applied in vastly different ways. As Fig. 1 suggests, for Intellectual Technologies the evolution of the physical product and its application are separate and are driven by different forces. By recognizing the third dimension of user learning, the model makes the difference between technological evolution and organizational evolution clearer.

The traditional concepts of “early”, “transitional” and “mature” are thus not points in time, but rather the degree of user sophistication within the general application area. Even as the technology evolves to higher levels, its use must be learned by each new set of users. Therefore, it is the user’s learning curve, much more than the producer’s, which paces evolution.

In Fig. 1, for example, the intellectual technology (word processing) is relatively “mature” at $T_{x}$ . Yet the accompanying users’ learning curves suggest that different organizations might be at different stages in their understanding and implementation of systems in this particular application area. In one organization a word processor might simply be used to do repetitive typing, while in another it might be supporting a wide range of text and data processing activities.

Moreover, in our comparative case studies of word processing and DSS, there was some evidence that experience with data processing applications was not particularly helpful in mastering the effective use of these Intellectual Technologies. Twenty years of experience with transaction processing and management reporting systems did not seem to give organizations an advantage in new application areas over firms whose first attempts were word processing or DSS. Therefore, while technological evolution may make computers in general easier to use, there will be a range of use because of different rates of organizational learning. In essence, evolution of the physical technology reduces the amount and difficulty of training, but the need for ongoing learning (about possible new uses) remains.

## 6. Conclusions

For user organizations, recognition of these learning notions implies that planning must consider (1) the status of the user vis-a-vis this application area, (2) the product/process stage of the technology, and (3) the likely pattern of user development. Thus, unlike industrial technologies, which are more likely to be successful when behavior is modified to the fit the machine, Intellectual Technologies are more likely to be successful when the user is clearly in the driver's seat. The planning problem is therefore to encourage the organizational learning process that must take place with each application/user combination.

And it is this need for ongoing learning in each application area that explains the MIS Life-Cycle phenomenon described by Nolan $[16,17]$ and others $[14]$ . Many practitioners resists the notions suggested by such models, especially the difficulties of trial-and-error implementations, because they feel it should be possible to learn from past experience. The problem, of course, is that such experience was often with a different user group in a different application setting. Thus, even if the technology did not change, each new group must learn how to apply the technology to address its particular problems. Until the results of this learning process become “general knowledge” within the organization, experimentation (and the mistakes that go with it) is probably essential.

In some sense, the question of why each new application area is as difficult to implement as previous ones implies that the organization has failed to learn from experience. We would suggest, however, that these difficulties are inevitable. To avoid the difficulties and uncertainties inherent in the early stages of the life-cycle, one must either have a group of users experienced in the new application area, or wait until the application area is so widely used that a “conventional wisdom” has emerged. For most organizations the first solution is infeasible while the latter is unacceptable; they cannot afford to be far behind their competitors in applying productivity-enhancing tools.

Thus, while there is a certain “inevitability” about these ideas, the more successful firms in our study had two factors in common: they were prepared to experiment and they were explicit in their management of ongoing user learning. With personal-computer-based DSS, for example, they were willing to give users access without extensive justification, but they were careful to provide initial training and to monitor utilization. As exciting new applications were discovered, they could then be shared throughout the firm, and when poor uses were being made of the technology, these could be discouraged.
