# A Preliminary Investigation of Information Systems Team Structures

Kathy Brittain White

University of North Carolina Business Information Systems
Greensboro, North Carolina 27412, USA

Productivity in the information age is widely perceived to be a major organizational problem. One strategy for enhancing organizational productivity has been the use of task teams. Assignment to task teams is usually based on individual technical expertise, individual availability, and/or positional politics rather than on the effectiveness of the team members in the specific organizational situation. This paper investigations characteristics of team members and then examines the effect of these characteristics and the requirements of the organizational task on team effectiveness.

The findings indicate that the perceptual types of team members and the task structure impacts team effectiveness. It lends evidence that heterogeneity of perceptual types is best for solving unstructured tasks whereas heterogeneity can be counter-productive when solving structured tasks. It also suggests that one team might not be appropriate for all stages of a project. As the structure of the tasks change, the optimum team composition might also change.

Keywords: Cognitive style, Team design, Information systems development, People productivity.

## 1. Introduction

Strategies to enhance human productivity have been the focus of many recent research studies $[2,5,6,9,24,28,29]$ . The idea of a “task team” has resulted from studies to increase organizational productivity and provide better implementation of complex information systems. They are utilized in such projects as systems development and implementation, strategic planning, and office automation and are formed to initialize decision making, innovative thinking and productivity $[4,14,20,30,31,34,35,36,37]$ . Task team membership usually reflects technical expertise and availability of the individual, and/or positional politics, rather than focusing on the effectiveness of the team members in the specific task situation.

Research is indicating that optimum team composition in one task situation is not necessarily best for another $[1,8,10,36]$ . In fact, the major impact on productivity and effectiveness of the team seems to be situational $[36]$ . Little work has been performed to determine the situational components that affect team effectiveness $[3,27,32]$ . This paper examines characteristics of team members and then examines the effects of these characteristics and the requirements of the organizational activity on team effectiveness.

## 2. Characteristics of Team Members

Behavioral research has added credibility to the process of explaining individual perceptual differences. One of the primary contributors to such theories was the Swiss psychologist, Dr. Carl Jung [13]. Jung's theory accounts for human differences that other frameworks leave to random variation; yet, the theory has the merit of unusual simplicity. Briefly, the theory is based on the assumption that much apparently random variation in human behavior is actually quite orderly and consistent and due to the differences in the way individuals gather and evaluate information about their environment. Jung combines these differences into four basic types [12] as follows:

1. The Intuition-Thinking (NT) type is the one who observes and inputs data from a holistic or system type of framework, seeing things, perhaps, not as they are but as they can be. The output or evaluation of these possibilities is judged in accordance with some formal rules and tend to be objective or impersonal.

2. The Intuition-Feeling (NF) type will observe input data in the same way as the NT, but it will be judged in a personal or value-laden manner. This personality is subjective and does not follow formal rules of logic.

3. The Sensation-Thinking (ST) type is one who sees information as concrete facts, turning the specific facts into a formal solution according to some well-defined set of rules. This person wishes to work on specific, clear problems and will probably have a low tolerance for ambiguity.

4. The Sensation-Feeling (SF) type also prefers to observe concrete facts apart from the total picture but is less formal in evaluation of the data. This person does not apply the facts formally, but utilizes instead a subjective, value-laden assessment.

Jung's typology emphasizes only each type's major strengths and weaknesses without considering any one as superior to another [17].

Mason and Mitroff relate the Jungian scales specifically to information systems and discuss the heterogeneity of each type:

“Each of these types has a different concept of information and this is important for MIS design. What is information for one type is definitely not information for another” [20].

## 3. Team Composition

The perceptual type of each individual team member influences the information that is gathered and evaluated from the environment. Research has shown the more similar the perceptual types of team members, the more harmonious team relationships [8]. Since much of any team's early activities are devoted to establishing inter-group relationships, it would indicate the more homogeneous individual team members, the less time this early effort would require. But other researchers have important reservations as to task productivity of homogeneous teams. Mitroff and Kilmann [22] discuss the impact of team homogeneity on task solutions:

"If the extreme homogeneity of each group is a blessing in that it reinforces the natural strengths and similar tendencies of each individual in the group, then the extreme homogeneity is also a danger in that it magnifies the weaknesses (i.e. the one-sidedness) of perceptions of the individuals."

Myers [25] also discusses the composition of teams and indicates that homogeneity can reduce task productivity. She states,

"If the group is composed of very different types, agreement will be harder to reach than if the group was homogeneous but the decision will be more broadly based and thoroughly considered, and in less danger of turning out badly for some unperceived reason [26]."

Such findings indicate that homogeneous teams are characterized by freedom from conflict but possibly heterogeneous teams are more productive.

## 4. Organizational Problems

An organization faces a variety of problems that vary considerably in degree of complexity. One framework for identifying the differing complexities is used by decision theorists when they refer to a problem as structured or unstructured [23]. Briefly, a structured problem is one which is well defined: the key variables, such as the various states of the environment, possible actions, possible outcomes, and utility of outcomes, are known. The unstructured problem is one which cannot be clearly defined [16,33]. One or more of the variables is either unknown or cannot be determined with any degree of confidence. So decision making in organizations must deal with problems that run the gamut from simple to complex and beyond that to the ambiguous [17]. Specifically, it is expected that with problems characterized by a high degree of structure, the team will be able to gather and evaluate information with little difficulty. This notion is supported by a number of research studies [7,11,27]. For problems with a low degree of structure, it is exceedingly difficult for the group to gather and evaluate all pertinent information [17]. In these tasks, the team structure must be able to manage this complexity and perceive and evaluate all relevant information. Very little work in information systems has been directed at the impact team composition (either homogeneous/heterogeneous) has on task success.

## 5. Research

## 5.1. Setting and Method

Two task situations were examined. The first was a field study to investigate two project teams in an unstructured system development task. The second was a structured programming task involving six teams. Members of a programming class were participants in the second task.

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) was used to determine the perceptual type of team members. Each team was then classified as to homogeneity or heterogeneity based on these results. Although the MBTI is not the only instrument available to determine perceptual types, it was chosen in this study because of the number of business studies utilizing this instrument $[14,15,18,19,21,22,23]$ .

An in-depth account of the interview information and data analysis was previously published [37,38]. Project team one, with a void of two perceptual types (SF and NF) was classified as homogeneous. Project team two, with all four MBTI types represented was classified as heterogeneous. It would seem that in such an unstructured situation as the systems development activities assigned these two project teams, it is exceedingly difficult to have all relevant information evaluated. When the group is homogeneous in its information-evaluation orientation (as with Project Team One), overall performance suffers. Project Team One showed a narrower perceptual viewpoint (as indicated by interviewees) and this is attributed to their one-sided perceptual nature. Project team two had a broader perceptual viewpoint (as stated by the interviewees) and this is attributed to their diverse perceptual orientations. These findings also support research of Kaiser and Bostrom [14] which showed that a project team with all four MBTI types represented was successful while a project team void of two perceptual types was unsuccessful.

Although many factors cannot be controlled in a field study, evidence is offered that indicates heterogeneous teams are more successful than homogeneous teams in unstructured organizational tasks. Specifically, the conclusion is that the heterogeneity of combining all four perceptual types on a project team is “optimum” in an unstructured organizational situation.

## 5.3. Structured Situation

To further clarify the variables affecting team performance, both heterogeneous and homogeneous teams were studied in a structured situation. Members of a university programming class were assigned into homogeneous/ heterogeneous team combinations. A timed programming task which met the definition of a structured problem was assigned. The problem was composed of materials that has been previously taught, all students had successfully solved similar problems. The time factor made it essential that problem definition be completed quickly to enable a team to finish the task. Solutions were considered on two dimensions: completeness and correctness. Three teams were assembled with all four MBTI types included, three teams were assembled with a homogeneous information-gathering orientation (sensing) and a void of other types. No attention was given to individual personalities; teams were assembled solely on their measured MBTI types.

The surprising results were that the heterogeneous teams were unable to complete the task within the given time-frame although individual team members had previously completed similar tasks in the given time successfully. The group evaluations completed by each team member upon finishing the task were particularly enlightening. The individuals included on the heterogeneous teams indicated that a consensus of the problem definition was not reached in time to complete the assigned task. The group evaluations completed for the homogeneous groups indicated consensus was reached quickly and the task rapidly finished.

In the structured task, the heterogeneity of information-gathering types seemed to hinder group process and undermine their overall effectiveness. Further evidence of these findings is offered in research by Aamodt and Kimbrough [1]. They found that heterogeneous teams were not as successful as homogeneous teams in completing a structured debugging tasks. In such structured task situations, it is possible that homogeneous groups are able to work together to complete the task quickly. Heterogeneous groups may have to deal with the tension created by their different perceptual viewpoints and be unable to rapidly complete the task.

The finding that heterogeneous perceptual types were unable to complete the structured programming task was serendipitous. Controls were not imposed and there was little attempt to observe the phenomenon because it was unexpected. Better controls and other samples might make these findings more conclusive. However, this study preliminarily indicates that in an organizational task situation where the information is finite and limited, homogeneous team compositions may be the most productive.

## 6. Discussion

This study offers preliminary evidence that the task structure determines the effectiveness of team composition. It lends evidence that heterogeneous teams are “optimum” when solving unstructured tasks while homogeneous teams are “optimum” when solving structured tasks. The subject matter has important implications for information systems productivity. It is presented in the hope that other researchers will expand these findings to determine the most productive team compositions for a number of information system’s tasks.
