# A Systems Life Cycle for Office Automation Projects

Donald P. Ballou

State University of New York at Albany, School of Business, 1400 Washington Avenue, Albany, New York 12222, USA

and

Sung W. Kim

Nam Kwang Engineering and Construction Company, 222 Bridge Plaza South, Fort Lee, New Jersey 07024, USA

As office automation activities become more complex and interrelated, the need for generalized, systematic planning and control procedures increases. For data processing projects, systems life cycles have proven to be a highly effective planning and control mechanisms. This paper presents an analogous life cycle for office automation projects. It incorporates those unique characteristics of the office environment that preclude use of existing data processing life cycles. The paper concludes with an illustrative application of the office automation life cycle.

Keywords: Office Automation, Electronic Office, Systems Life Cycle, Project Management, Office Environment, Systems Analysis and Design.

## 1. Introduction

1.1. A Framework for Office Automation

Office automation is a complex activity that involves technological issues, behavioral constraints, organizational considerations, measurement and control strategies and implementation procedures $[4,7]$ . Systematic planning and control techniques are required for the management of office automation projects. For data processing systems the concept of the project life cycle has proven to be a highly beneficial project management device, and an analogous framework would be valuable for office automation projects. However, significant differences between these two environments preclude direct application to office projects of traditional data processing life cycles. This paper presents a life cycle designed to meet planning and control requirements for office automation projects.

We assume that automated offices will evolve through a series of relatively small steps. Zisman's [11] description for this evolution is based on Nolan's [5] stages of growth for DP systems: initiation, expansion, formalization and maturity. Our life cycle applies to projects in any of the first three stages. However, the emphasis placed on the various phases will depend on the stage of growth appropriate for the organization.

## 1.2. Data Processing Life Cycles

Many different DP life cycles have been proposed. For example, Davis [2] developed one based on the decision making process as postulated by Simon [8]. More recently, DeMarco [3], incorporating the concepts of structured analysis and design as propounded by Yourdon [10], presented a structured systems life cycle. For our purposes the life cycle developed by Biggs et al. [1] of Touche Ross is valuable, as it has a project management planning and control orientation. This cycle utilizes four phases with 19 steps or subphases as outlined in Table 1. Although the percentage of total project effort devoted to each phase is project dependent, typically the systems development phase is most significant in this regard and the initial planning phase is least so.

1.3. Differences in DP and OA Environments Affecting Life Cycles

It is likely that a number of existing DP life cycles have served as the basis for system analysis and design efforts in office environments. However, modifications are required for them, as such environments and the nature of the tasks being automated require that some aspects of traditional life cycles vary in importance while other activities must be added or dropped. Factors that dictate such changes are summarized in Table 2 and are examined in more detail.

Interaction with Technology. The beneficiaries of office technologies must interface directly with them: secretaries operate word processors, professionals utilize multifunction work stations, and managers use electronic mail facilities. Thus office systems must possess a very high level of “user friendliness”: the operator and end user are generally one and the same. In contrast, the end user or beneficiary of data processing, transaction-oriented systems is shielded from most of the technological aspects; he or she does not load and run the programs, make modifications to them, or even enter the data.

Nature of Projects. Most data processing projects deal with systems that can be classified as "structured" in the Keen and Scott-Morton [6] sense. Data processing requirements and data formats must be determined in advance; making changes later is expensive. Office activities are less structured and more varied. A manager preparing a report may identify useful but unanticipated sources of information as the report is being written. Secretaries are involved with a host of diverse activities, and even the typing assignments are highly heterogeneous.

Basic Unit of Information Processed. Much office activity can be viewed as the creating, processing, storing, duplicating, and distributing of documents or messages. Hence, in a generic sense the basic unit for the office is the document, which is highly variable and unstructured. By contrast the basic unit for data processing is the data record, an item that is much more structured and much less variable.

Role of Communication. One of the significant characteristics of office environments is the prevalence of interpersonal communication, both formal and informal. The interactions implied by these communications play a major part in making the office a satisfactory place of employment. Any changes induced by OA activities may be very damaging. In contrast, interpersonal communications usually are not disrupted by data processing activities.

Technological Assessment. The data processing industry has matured sufficiently so that many computer professionals can evaluate vendor offerings and make appropriate selections. Since office automation is in an earlier growth stage, there are fewer people capable of assessing vendor claims, especially as they pertain to organizational requirements and constraints. Also the rate of introduction of new products is higher in the office field than with data processing. (Many recently introduced “DP” products have significant OA implications).

Implementation Strategies. Data processing has had a long enough track record so that management is aware of both the capabilities and deficiencies of the MIS department. Management's relative lack of experience with OA projects makes it important that those responsible for OA activities establish their credibility (Uhlig, et al. [9]). An effective way to do this is by identifying and developing small scale “pilot” or “prototype” projects that offer the greatest likelihood of success (Zisman [12]). (Any new technology should be introduced in this manner.) However, it is desirable to avoid developing a series of unrelated pilots, for even if they are all successful, they cannot achieve synergistic benefits. Thus it is important to “fit” the pilot into a larger plan.

## 2. Office Automation Life Cycle

In this section we present a life cycle designed to assist the planning and control of office automation activities. Any such life cycle must explicitly address behavioral factors. For credibility purposes it must provide for an assessment of the likelihood of success. It should encourage on-going technological evaluation. It should plan for user training and develop implementation procedures. And it must require that document and message flows be identified and analyzed.

## 2.1. Initial Assessment and Commitment Phase

The primary purpose of this phase is to determine if a proposed office automation project meets a perceived organizational or managerial need and if so, to make a preliminary judgement as to whether the personnel affected, the organizational climate, and the current state of office automation technology are all such that there is a strong likelihood that the project can be successfully carried out and implemented. If both these conditions are met, then appropriate higher level management should be asked to support a more detailed study of the proposed project. This phase should require relatively little in the way of time and resources.

Comparison with DP Life Cycle. For data processing projects these activities are either given (e.g., management requests the MIS department to generate certain reports) or unnecessary (most transaction-oriented DP projects do not have significant behavioral implications). Thus for DP projects these activities do not warrant a separate phase. The primary purpose of Phase I as proposed by Biggs et al. is to estimate roughly costs and benefits so that management can decide whether or not to conduct the feasibility study. The feasibility study itself is a more detailed evaluation of the proposed project. On the other hand, since OA credibility is less well established, OA implementors must be concerned with obtaining management support and with the likelihood of ultimate project success.

## 2.2. Feasibility Analysis Phase

A more detailed evaluation of the proposed project is conducted in this phase. A systematic study from a behavioral perspective of the personnel who would be most affected by the project should be undertaken. This information assists in modelling the user interface and helps determine the degree to which office procedures could be modified. Also it is valuable in planning the implementation phase. Information on resistance to change should be obtained. Attitudes of management and office personnel regarding technology should be solicited. This information is needed to design alternative solutions that appear satisfactory to present employees. Information should also be generated on the organizational climate and its receptivity to change, in particular to office automation. Is top management in general supportive of office automation or at best indifferent? Are those managers affected by the project likely to be cooperative or uncooperative?

A detailed study must be undertaken to identify available office technologies, their capabilities and characteristics, and their purchase or lease costs. This information is needed for a preliminary cost-benefit analysis. At this stage many of the benefits would be of a subjective nature. For example, the project under consideration could be the first phase of a long term, more extensive office automation plan. Or it could build on and interact with existing office automation activities. The cost information together with a listing of benefits, both subjective and tangible, would be presented to management, who must then decide whether to continue with the analysis phase.

Comparison with DP LIfe Cycle. This phase is somewhat analogous to the traditional DP feasibility study (Phase I). As is the case with DP studies, these activities build on and extend information generated previously. However, the emphasis is much more organizational and behavioral than is traditionally the case. Also, since OA projects are more likely to be of a pilot nature, the growth potential needs to be evaluated, something that usually is not an issue with DP projects.

## 2.3. Analysis and Requirements Phase

During this phase, current office activities and responsibilities are identified and analyzed. Office procedure in general and document flows in particular need to be studied. The word “document” is used in a generic sense and includes letters, memos, form letters, reports, contracts, proposals and budgets. It is important to identify both perceived and actual priorities regarding levels of service for each category of document. Then the document flows should be traced. The originating form (handwritten, typed, dictated, OCR, etc.) should be determined by document type. Information should be gathered on the quantities of each kind of document, the distribution of lengths and typing times, the number of revisions and, in general, data of value in assessing current office performance vis-a-vis desired or required service levels. Next current duplication, storage and retrieval procedures and requirements should be assayed. Lastly the distribution pattern for the documents should be determined. Only by studying the sequence of input, processing, storage, reproduction (reprographics), and distribution for all document categories can procedures be rationalized and technologies be introduced fruitfully.

Similar analyses should be applied to all other office activities. In particular the responsibilities, tasks, and activities of the professional and managerial workers supported by the office must be studied. At this point the available technologies should be reviewed in the light of all the available information. Analysis of current activities and problems, insights into possible improvements in procedures and work flows, and updated information on suitable technologies should be used in conjunction with management's needs as identified in the Initial Assessment and Commitment Phase to design alternative solutions. Sufficient information would be available to determine cost figures and performance characteristics for each alternative. Usually each option would specify not only the technology but also the vendor who could supply it. The alternatives would be presented to management for selection of the most attractive option.

Comparison with DP Life Cycle. This phase comprises activities comparable to the analysis and design stages of DP life cycles (Phases II and III). For both types of projects the analysis activities have a fact finding orientation. However, frequent, new product offerings require that the technological assessment be reviewed, something which usually is not needed with DP projects. The major difference between the two is that the design phase, which for DP projects can consume 60% or more of total project effort, is significantly reduced. Once requirements for an OA project are known, vendor offerings are evaluated to determine what best meets the specified needs. Rather than designing a system that meets specifications, as is usually done with DP projects, a system is put together using existing (vendor) components that satisfies the identified needs as much as possible.

## 2.4. Implementation Phase

Following vendor selection, implementation plans are developed. These plans provide for: training and educational activities; feedback from users to vendors and those responsible for the project; continual monitoring of user performance and attitudes; timing, phasing and sequencing of the various components; fallback positions (in case unforeseen circumstances require modifications to the original plan) and identification of benchmarks. The educational activities should commence before any technology is introduced and should continue until the users are comfortable and familiar with the system. It is extremely important that this activity be done well, and thus considerable thought should be given to it. It is desirable to establish channels so that users can easily communicate reactions and concerns. Those charged with implementation cannot assume that users will spontaneously volunteer information regarding the project. Procedures need to be established to monitor and oversee the project to detect problems as soon as possible to facilitate their solution.

Plans must be made regarding how normal office activities will be carried out during periods of disruption caused by office automation projects. If office performance is excessively degraded, it may be necessary to activate fall back procedures or alternatives. In essence this limits the damage if things do not work out, managerial support for the project was predicated on the assumption that certain goals would be achieved. It is important that the project team devise procedures and benchmarks for establishing progress toward attainment of the agreed-upon goals.

Some time after the project has been fully implemented, an assessment or post audit of the project should be performed. Several benefits accrue from doing this, one being that the office automation manager and team acquire additional knowledge regarding what is effective and what is not. Essentially they should learn from both past mistakes and past successes.

Comparison with DP Life Cycle. For data processing projects the implementation phase, although important, is perhaps the most straight-forward component of the life cycle. However, for OA projects these activities are crucial, for at this stage users begin to interact on a continuing basis with technologies which are new to them. If this activity is not handled properly, the project will fail. Thus for office systems considerably more attention should be directed toward the planning and control of the implementation phase than is the case for DP systems. If the other phases of the DP life cycle have been handled correctly, the user will be receiving what he or she expects.

## 3. Application of Life Cycle to an Office Automation Scenario

In this section an office automation scenario is presented to illustrate use of the life cycle. The aspect of this scenario which makes it an OA rather than a DP project is that documents rather than data files are accessed and transferred. The situation involves a major medical center associated with a medial school, many of whose faculty hold joint appointments with the Center. These physicians teach, conduct research, and treat patients. Often the treatments administered and procedures followed by these physicians are state-ofthe-art and involve recently introduced drugs, equipment, and technology. The physicians need to evaluate and analyze treatments and procedures and often the results have a bearing on their on-going research activities.

The medical center stores patient records in a central repository which facilitates records management. However, accessing these records is time-consuming and a major hindrance to the research activities and treatment evaluations performed by the medical school physicians. Also, when the records are being used by the physicians, they are not available for other purposes, such as emergency treatment or billing.

The office automation project team has responsibility for initiating, implementing, and administering OA activities. Members of the team felt that office automation procedures could expedite the document flows and increase their availability. The team proposed storing these records on hard disks controlled and accessed by shared logic word processing systems. Physicians participating in the program would then have work stations in their offices; they or their assistants would use these terminals to access patient records and update them instead of the traditional (paper) records. Since sketches cannot be put into word processors and since hardcopy backup is required, the physicians would still draw any required figures on the special backup paper records. Later the secretary would transfer text entered from the word processor by the physician to the backup record; this would be stored at the site of the shared logic equipment, where it would be more readily accessible to the physician. Records management personnel would be able to access patient records stored on the disks electronically, and the hardcopy records would be available if required.

The OA team felt that a successful pilot could be expanded and would result ultimately in a system of significant value. However, since no OA project of this complexity had been undertaken, project planning and control procedures such as are found in project life cycles were deemed necessary.

## 3.1. Initial Assessment and Commitment Phase

Informal discussions and brain-storming sessions generated the basic ideas. Several of the more computer-oriented physicians felt that such a plan under the right circumstances could be implemented and would improve their effectiveness considerably. The bulk of funding for equipment for a pilot project was available from grants awarded to medical school faculty. Encouraged, the OA team set up a meeting with the Dean of the medical school and senior medical center administrators. Although they expressed concerns, they agreed the idea had potential. They promised to provide strong support provided further study confirmed its feasibility.

## 3.2. Feasibility Analysis Phase

The key to success was to identify physicians whose research was being hindered by delays in getting patient records and who either had interacted personally with computer systems (they were willing to use a key board) or who showed an interest in technology and its applications. Since medical center and medical school personnel were accustomed to change, the OA team determined that the organizational climate was supportive. The initial technological assessment identified vendors of shared logic systems with “user friendly” interfaces and the ability to communicate with the Center’s mainframes. This furnished information on equipment and communication costs.

Another kind of cost was the potential incompatibility of the new system with the original equipment, procedures and records. The primary benefit was expected to be increased effectiveness of the medical school faculty in their research and evaluation activities. Also increased sharing of experiences would be facilitated. The growth potential was deemed to be considerable provided the pilot was successful. Information generated by this phase together with the analyses of it were presented to the ad hoc management committee, which gave its OK to continue the project.

## 3.3. Analysis and Requirements Phase

The OA team gathered information on the activities of the medical school faculty (type and extent of teaching activities, nature of research activities, patient load, relationship between clinical and research responsibilities) and on the supporting office structure (number and types of support personnel, existing equipment and user attitudes toward it). Not surprisingly this analysis disclosed a wide diversity of situations and environments. A subset of the medical school faculty, selected on the basis of the information generated, was analyzed in more depth. In particular, the flow of patient records was studied in detail. Information was gathered on volume of flows, frequency of record access, purpose of record access (patient visit or analysis) and length of time before records were returned to records management. These physicians were questioned regarding their perception of the value of computer-based technologies and in particular their reaction to key aspects of the proposed systems, such as entry of patient information from a terminal. The degree of previous interaction of the support personnel (such as secretaries) with technology was studied. In particular, experiences with WP equipment were noted. Attitudes of the records management personnel toward the proposed project were assayed and their needs identified. Suggestions were solicited. Previously studied shared logic systems were reexamined in the context of a better awareness of needs and requirements. Recent vendor offerings were also considered. Three vendors were selected for more intensive evaluation. This evaluation was concerned in part with document handling capabilities. Also these vendors were asked to demonstrate the ability of their systems to communicate and interact with the Center's mainframes. Specifically, the equipment had to demonstrate the ability to transfer files, a capability requested by records management. On the basis of these tests, two vendors, A and B, were selected for further consideration. Four potential user groups (each with three to six physicians) were identified. For two groups A was the designated vendor; B was chosen for the other two. The selections were made to maximize compatibility with existing equipment and procedures.

Working with each vendor the OA team developed the procedures to be followed by the physicians, their secretaries and personnel from records management in handling the documents. For example, the procedures a physician had to follow to identify and access patient records were developed. The alternatives were presented to the ad hoc management committee.

The ad hoc management committee's decision was based in part on the apparent respectivity of the user group but also on the long-term implications of vendor selection. The committee stated that if the pilot was successful, then the project should be expanded. The vendor chosen was the one the committee felt offered the most complete line of compatible equipment, which would facilitate future interaction among the shared logic systems.

## 3.4. Implementation Phase

The OA team developed an implementation plan. This included tentative dates for equipment introduction and training, identification of the physicians to be trained first (initially 2 of the 5) and determination of the fraction of records to be processed initially by the new system. Also, the OA team decided that both the old and new systems should operate in tandem for the first month. The secretaries were selected who would transfer information from the WP system to the paper records. they also had the responsibility of writing billing and insurance information to a file in the WP to be accessed by records management. Training of these various personnel commenced before equipment was installed and continued both formally and informally afterwards. For the initial period the supervisors of the personnel involved agreed to release them from many of their normal activities. The OA team met regularly with those affected on both an individual and group basis. At these times concerns were solicited and problems identified. The user manuals, prepared after selection of the vendor and the pilot site, were revised in response to user inputs. The other three physicians were brought into the system after those initially trained become familiar with the procedures. The learning period for the former was shorter, as they received assistance from both the OA team and the physicians already trained. At various points in time, both positive and negative reactions were recorded. After one year a more in-depth evaluation was conducted. Based on the generally enthusiastic responses, the program was expanded.

## 4. Use of the Office Automation Life Cycle

The life cycle given here should not be followed rigidly but rather should be adapted and modified to meet the needs of a specific project and environment. For example, organizations experienced with office automation techniques and procedures would downplay or even eliminate activities related to organizational receptiveness. However such firms should be especially sensitive to the analysis components of the third phase, as projects for such organizations would tend to be more extensive and more disruptive of the organization. Also, the more advanced companies would have a better awareness and understanding of the current technology and thus could spend less time on initial technological assessment.

On the other hand organizations just beginning to undertake office automation activities should be especially sensitive to the tasks that comprise the initial assessment and commitment phase. But since these projects are relatively straight forward, the analysis activities would not be conducted in as much depth.

Although office automation and data processing have much in common, there are differences in their respective environments. Nevertheless it is natural to use concepts from data processing as the starting point for the development of office automation procedures. That has been the approach used in this paper.
