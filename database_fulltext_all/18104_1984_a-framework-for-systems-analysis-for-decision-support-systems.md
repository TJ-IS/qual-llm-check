# A Framework for Systems Analysis for Decision Support Systems

Harish C. Bahl and Raymond G. Hunt \*
School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo,
207 Crosby Hall, Buffalo, NY 14214, USA

Comprehensive and elaborate systems analysis techniques have been developed in the past of routine and operational information systems. Developing support systems for organizational decision-making requires new tools and methodologies. We present a new framework for data collection and decision analysis which is useful for developing decision support systems. This task analysis methodology encompasses (1) event analysis, (2) participant analysis, and (3) decision content analysis. With a proper coding manual, it provides a framework for collecting relevant and detailed information required for decision support design and implementation. Further research is suggested for application and evaluation of the methodology in real-life DSS environments.

Keywords: Decision support systems, systems analysis, decision making.

## 1. Introduction

Traditionally, data processing in complex organizations has been concerned with information flows for routine and operational activities. Recent work on decision support systems (DSS), however, concentrates on assisting organizational decision-making by providing data collection, information retrieval, and processing capabilities for nonroutine decisions: i.e., for problem-solving [6]. But, if DSS are to accomplish their intended purpose of helping managers with unprogrammed decisions, then new tools and methodologies are needed for developing and implementing them.

Little attention has been paid to the systems analysis phase, even though it has been recognized as a pivotal part of the DSS development process. Traditional systems analysis tools and techniques [2], involving the study of information flows for operational activities of organizations, are not well suited to the analysis of the less routine decisions which consume much of a manager's time. This paper proposed a methodology for the analysis of these complex decisions. It is an organization-theoretic approach to DSS design, and its use presumes close collaboration between a DSS developer and the affected manager(s).

## 2. A Methodology for Systems Analysis

Traditional as well as the new structured approaches [9] for systems analysis concentrate only on operational systems. These output-focused techniques depend upon a systems analyst querying a user (decision-maker) for the types of reports or information ostensibly useful to the user, and then identifying and collecting the details of input data and data flows so that the desired reports are produced. The decision process or direct decision aids which are basic to a decision support system are ordinarily no more than casually considered in such methodology.

The new method we propose for systems analysis is based on concepts and techniques previously developed for the comparative study of decision-making in complex organizations. Although developed in the context of a particular theory of organizational behavior, the descriptive methodology is almost “theory-neutral.” It is divided into three procedural segments – (1) Event Analysis, (2) Participant Analysis, and (3) Decision Content Analysis. A detailed presentation of the decision-analytic methodology is found in [5] and, of the underlying theory, in [4]. $^{1}$ It is outlined here.

## 3. Event Analysis

Complex decisions are flows of overt and covert activity. “Events” are the identifiable (and inferable) activities in a decision-making process; they are the basic units of decision analysis – the “bounded” elements that are content analyzed to describe a “decision.”

The interesting types of unprogrammed decisions commonly cover time spans of a few hours to a few months. In making them, any number of more or less distinct activities (discovering symptoms, searching for information, holding meetings, appointing task forces, etc.) can be observed. In a decision-making cycle, identification of an event-structure is an obvious first step for either its scientific study or the design of support systems for it.

Events are not rigorously defined, but are generally recognizable. (As in many coding tasks, there is an element of judgment here.) Events tend to involve a unitary activity engaged in by a unitary set of actors for a limited period of time in a single physical location.

By “unitary activity” we mean that there is usually a single problem or activity which dominates the attention of the actors, or there is a single object of their behavior. Examples of unitary activities are entering data into a computer, writing a report, discussing information on some subject with a colleague, making a phone call, voting on a resolution.

The unitary set of one or more actors engaging in an event may vary in minor ways, as when certain actors come late to a meeting or leave early, yet remain essentially constant throughout its duration. And the activity which describes an event tends to be uninterrupted by lapses of time, such as those that occur between one working day and the next. Taking a “coffee break” would not ordinarily constitute such a lapse. Finally, the activity which is the content of an “event” tends to be concentrated in a single physical location; for example: a person’s work station, a laboratory, someone’s office.

Obviously, then, definition of an event is somewhat elastic. Depending on one's purposes, quite microscopic specifications might be sought or one might be well-satisfied with broader and looser ones. In any case the events may be classified into two broad categories: endogenous events, which comprise the decision-making process itself, and exogenous events, which comprise the relevant context in which the decision process occurs.

Endogenous events, involving activities directly related to decisions, may consists of several kinds of activities carried out by “actors” both inside and outside an organization. They include preparation for a decision, such as collecting information, discussing, critiquing, or evaluating information, reporting information or the results of information analysis to decision-makers, attempting to create a choice opportunity, or trying to influence decision-makers. Making a decision is itself an endogenous event.

Meanwhile, exogenous events involve activities that are not part of the decision-making process itself but are considered to be a relevant part of the environment in which decision-making occurs (or are a by-product of the process). These events are “external” to the process because the actors are not playing a role directly related to the making of the “decision-in-question.” For instance, an event is exogenous if it involves a participant (such as a colleague, DSS designer, or someone else) who is only describing the process or state of the system to some nonparticipant in the decision-making process. An announcement of an MIS in another part of an organization or of a restaffing of an EDP department might be changes in a manager’s decision-making content that might be coded as exogenous events; as might the introduction of a new line of personal computers.

Decision events may be functionally classified, for example, as “starter, instrumental, or terminal” events and then “mapped” or charted (as in Fig. 1) as a summary device for post hoc description of a decision’s empirical structure.

Starter events (S) activate or set in motion a cycle of decision-making activity. Such an event might be a request that a personnel manager recommend an individual for a specified job. Another might be a report that sales have declined in the preceding quarter. Plainly the status of an event as a “starter” (or anything else) depends on its context; for example, on whether anything follows the sales report.

Terminal events (T) stand out somehow in a context of organization activity as notable markers of the decision process. They signal the culmination of a series of events or they serve as a final marker, an end point, of an ongoing activity. For example, completion of a search and review of candidates for a job would be a terminal event, even though the decision may not have been made which candidate to select. That selection, however, would also be a terminal event (a special one we label T\*). Procedurally in coding or analysis, one ordinarily notes terminals (subject perhaps to latter amendment) and then works backwards, as it were, to identify the associated starter and instrumental events.

Instrumental events (I) are the activities that link starters and terminal events. They are goal-directed activities. The search for and review of candidates mentioned above are instrumental events.

In Fig. 1, which is an illustrative abstraction of an empirical case of complex decision making, events are numbered 1–14 and dated along the lower margin. Each one is coded as a “starter” (S), and “instrumental” (I), or a “terminal” (T) event. (T/S is a terminal event which starts a succeeding phase of the decision process and a T\* is the end of the decision-under-analysis, as in the case of Event no. 14, or the status quo at the time of onset of the decision task, as in the case of Event no. 1.) Normally a decision map would array specific actors along the left margin to that events are associated with their primary and secondary participants. These are omitted here, except that events coded above the horizontal line through the chart all occurred within the bundaries of the organization and those below the line were outside it.

## 4. Participant Analysis

Participant analysis means specifying the actors involved in a decision and their relevant properties, keeping in mind that “relevance” is subject to case-by-case definition. One may identify any number of characteristics of the participants in a decision-making process, such as their organizational position, their partisanship regarding a particular issue (i.e., in favor, opposed, or indifferent to it), their role in decision-making (e.g., whether a decision-maker or supporting character), etc. The “power positions” of the participants (i.e., how much influence any one of them has over a decision that might be supported) may be and generally are directly relevant to the study of decisions, as may be their motivations and expertise. In addition, one can further study (and plan) the actual and/or anticipated interaction amongst the participants. Obviously an analyst/designer needs to choose the participant characteristics to use, basing the choice on the purpose of the analysis and the feasibility of obtaining information.

This part of a decision analysis begins with exhaustively listing the cast of all characters who participate (or are expected to participate) in a decision under analysis. This listing may be winnowed or segregated into major, minor, and incidental participants and may be amended either because of new information or because changes are made in the decision structure. Thus, the listing will define the “roster of actors” for a decision. A wide variety of categories for classifying actors organizationally and functionally may be useful to explain their role and relationships in the decision process (e.g., whether an actor is the decision maker or not, or has a “stake” in the decision, whether an actor controls resources relevant to the decision, etc.)

## 5. Decision Content Analysis

The content analysis step is key. Our method constitutes an outline of a micro-level model of the decision process. We seek to identify the various factors which define and influence a manager's behavior.

Decision-making includes four distinct but ordered elements:

1. A definition of the situation (i.e., a decision "model").

2. A set of alternatives.

3. The selection of the choice (a cognitive or mental process).

4. An overt action.

Codes for modeling decision-making are still preliminary and suggestive. These and their application procedures change as experience in their use and the purpose of the analysis varies. Later, other analysts can tailor them to their special requirements and interests. We now, summarily, lay out a full analytic agenda; complete coverage of this takes considerable time and resources – unfortunately these may not always be available.

## 5.1. A Strategy for Analyzing Decisions

“Decisions” denominate a range of happenings (events) from the start to end of a process. They are divisible (temporally) into three periods:

. A pre-decision period - itself divided into immediate and remote phases;

. A decision period - a series of events, beginning with a presentation of choice and ending with a selection; and

. A post-decision period - implementation activity, though not confined to that - divisible into a short- and a long term.

We wish to specify a category system and procedure for identifying and describing decision processes. In a time-relative flow chart, Fig. 2 summarizes a scheme for doing this. The “drivers” of the decision process are on the figure’s central axis, starting with an “Evocation” (of choice) and ending with output/outcome. The several stages of decision-making are depicted as linked by a continuing micro-level process of cognitive modeling (definitions of the situation) moderated by changing states of a “decider actor” and of the “system state.”

## 5.2. Tactics for Analyzing Decisions: Coding Decision Content

The content coding scheme is an array of categories which divides the decision-making process, by period, into sets of “facets” and “variables.” The facets are rubrics and major constructs which denominate basic features of decision-making and its moderating context. Within these facets, variables are specific measurements – indicators of properties of the major constructs.

Procedurally, the coding and analysis of decisions (events) moves in a sequence of six basic steps over the components. The six steps are now discussed.

(1) Specifying the Decision-maker and (2) The Presentation of Choice. These steps are essentially concurrent. They involve identifying some initial (“starter”) event for a decision. This defines the onset. The content of this event states the (actual or intended) form of the decision – how it is or may be seen by the decision-maker, the apparent alternatives, etc.; and it defines the subject, topic, or domain of the decision. The choices may be well defined at the outset (e.g., whether or not to buy a building site in a certain city), or not-so-well defined (e.g., whether or not to develop a DSS).

(3) Pre-decision Analysis. The Pre-decision Analysis seeks an explanation or specification of the "presentation of choice". Generally speaking we target two features of the Pre-decision Period for description. The first is the "State of the System" (ex ante), and the second is the "Evocation of Choice". Within each of these broad categories certain indicators are coded (see Fig. 3).

(A) The State of the System we divide into a subset of “general” considerations and a subset of considerations regarding the system’s “capacity for decision.” The general state of the system refers to descriptive properties of a focal organization (FO) – the host for a decision-under-analysis – and its environment. The state of this system can be described in many ways depending on one’s purposes. Some properties of interest to a typical systems analyst might be the organizational “design” (size, differentiation, etc.), modes of operation (specialization, centralization, etc.), and technology (unit, batch, continuous process, etc.). Features of an organization’s social and material environments can be similarly coded (e.g., as hostile, friendly, etc.). As with other analytic categories, detailed explication should be provided in a coding manual, and detailed instructions for their application to raw data should be written in the manual.

The capacity for decision of a system depends on:

(a) The availability of resources to the FO (the existence of a resource and its “slack,” property [future availability] including cognitive resources [knowledge and conceptual frameworks], social-emotional resources [motivation and commitment], and material resources [money, time, personnel, equipment, etc.]), and

(b) The preparation for the task. This is the FO's general readiness (i.e., the degree to which it is formally organized to make decisions, or sensitized to the need) and its preparation for managing specific kinds of decisions.

(B) The second major feature of the Pre-decision Period, which is targeted for analysis, is the

Evocation of Choice. This refers to the input condition and mechanisms through which the need or opportunity for choice is evoked. They give rise to output in the form of a “presentation of choice,” which, as noted earlier, marks the onset of the Decision Period. The “evocation process” is divided into recognition and diagnosis.

Recognition is a sensing or “signal detection” process, It may result from systematic scanning or “intelligence” gathering or from some other circumstances. It involves “cues,” which may be singular or multiple, clear or vague, and intense or weak. Cues, in turn, have “sources,” which may be internal or external to the FO, singular or multiple, and public or private. Cues also have receptors, which are the scanning systems and sensing mechanisms via which cues become input to the organization (although not necessarily to the decision-maker). Here the operative question is: who (organizationally) first detects the signal? It is the decision-maker or some specialized agent(s) of the decision-maker (e.g., a research and evaluation unit) or is it some other agent?

Diagnosis is an interpretive sense-making process between recognition of cues and presentation of choice. It includes procedures which may be brief or lengthy, formal or informal, and mediated or unmediated (i.e. interpretation may involve the decision-maker or be done by others). Diagnosis also involves an outcome (interpretation), which is the result of diagnosis: it may be analytical or nonanalytical (a specification of decision alternatives, scenarios, optimal interpretations, particular implications of choice, etc., or a characterization of a situation). Finally, there will be diagnostic outcomes, which may be evaluative or nonevaluative (diagnosis may include a ranking of possible choices with or without recommendation).

(4) Decision Analysis. A Decision Period begins with a “presentation of choice.” This is the conceptual (definitional) output of the evocation process. The Decision Period ends with “selection” of a choice. In between these two is an array of decision processes and factors that “drive” them. These processes involve search, evaluation, and possible change in a decision-maker’s conception of the decision task. We see as many as seven major facets for analysis of this major phase; they are outlined in Fig. 4.

Since we assume choice by a decision-maker, and hence coloration of it by characteristics of the decision-maker, it follows that description of those characteristics may sometimes be useful to an understanding of decision-making. We shall say no more about this here, but the interested reader may consult Hunt et al. [5] on the point.

The Presentation of Choice is essentially a definition of a situation - a conceptual or cognitive model of the decision task. In fact, this model specifies the decision task. It has at least nine features.

(a) Form: either “reactive” (or problemistic), in which orientation is to the present time, or “proactive” (or opportunistic) which is future oriented;

(b) Organizational locus, which, after Thompson [8], may consist of the technical core of the organization, its managerial, or its institutional subsystems (obviously other categories are possible);

(c) Linkage or coupling with other organizational loci or other activities/interests in a decision-set. Such linkages may be evaluated for their extent (the number of loci, functions, activities, or interests affected), intensity (the significance of the effects), and specificity (whether the effects are generalized or particular in nature);

(d) Substance; made up of policy (strategy) or procedure (tactics/operations) [1];

(e) Task; consisting of transfer, development, relocation, or palliation-maintenance varieties, where, respectively, search (for choices) focusses on an existing repertoire of alternatives, or on designing new responses to situations, or on finding new settings wherein to use an existing repertoire, or is oriented to preserving a status quo.

Taken together the above five features of the “presentation of choice” (along with any indications of partisanship) define the decision-maker’s “motivations” in the decision task – what the decision-maker is trying to do with and what and whom. The remaining four features of the “presentation of choice” are:

(f) Familiarity of the subject (of the decision) to the decision-maker;

(g) Time for the decision (it may be definite or indefinite, short or long);

(h) Importance (high or low to the organization or decision-maker; where “urgency” is the product of time and importance); and

(i) Range or constraint of choice, which involves the flexibility available in making a decision on a subject at issue. Constraint is essentially the sum of separate constraints that arise from: conceptual or cognitive sources (particular beliefs about a subject); organizational sources (structural limits on discretion, rules, authority structures, etc.); operational or technical sources relating to abilities to perform and to translate a choice into action (given the resources); political-social sources in the environment; interpersonal sources (informal bonds of obligation, friendship, etc.); and economic sources (money, time, skill, personnel).

Given an initial presentation, definition, conceptualization, or model of a decision task, certain Processes of Choice ensue. These consists of assessments of the initial presentation of choice, leading perhaps to redefinitions of that model, and to actual selection of a choice.

In discussing these, we presume choice behavior under conditions of some latency between “presentations of choice” and “selection” – conditions where a “decision period” is plainly manifest. Obviously, decision periods can be extremely short, and then some of the processes might not be apparent or analytic steps be needed. Such a case would exist when the outcome is in accord with a predefined model that includes performance specifications that can be implemented immediately or even reflexively (with no problematic results). Such a case corresponds to “programmed selection” [7]. The cases of decision-making we consider here, however, are not of the programmed variety. They involve both ambiguity and latency of selection (i.e. deliberative, semi- or possibly pseudo-deliberative selection). Hence they correspond to what may be properly termed cases of “problem solving” [3].

We suggest that progress though the processes of evocation, diagnosis, and presentation of choice will yield a preliminary or baseline model of a decision situation. Prior to selection, however, this model will be subject to redefinition by the “decider.” This Redefinition is the third facet; it may involve any of the following:

(a) Planning; the development of prescriptions for performance;

(b) Searching, the collection of information either from the environment de novo or memory;

(c) Reviewing/analyzing; any processing of information (collating, coding, organizing, etc.); and

(d) Evaluating; the application of normative standards to the decision situation (i.e., the "issue" and its context), the choices, the methods for implementing these choices (or for further development), and the criteria for judging choices.

Procedurally, any or all of the preceding redefinitional activities may be:

(a) Goal-directed or diffuse; they may be organized around specific objectives in means-ends fashion, or unorganized;

(b) Programmed or ad hoc; following previously specified routines or activities chosen with particular reference to the instant decision;

(c) Varied or unvaried; flexible or rigidly followed;

(d) Extensive or intensive; emphasis on breadth of scope or depth of treatment (or a combination);

(e) Empirical or nonempirical;

(f) Fast-paced or slow-paced;

(g) Analytical or nonanalytical; the degree of information processing involved - coding, reduction, etc.;

(h) Specialized or unspecialized; the skill resources involved.

The many activities that may require redefinition of decision models are subject to control or management. We distinguish between management of activity by members of the focal organization (i.e., internal control), and activity by members that cross the organization's boundaries (external control).

We may ask whether “internal control” techniques are centralized or decentralized and participative or nonparticipative. Together these variables refer to the source and point of application of control. We can consider whether control is close or loose, and whether it is planned or unplanned.

By “external control” we mean efforts by an organization to manage its environment. One may ask, for instance, whether the organization is open or closed to information from outside and about the kinds of relations it seeks to establish with outside interests (co-optive, negotiatory, etc.).

We come now to the actual Selection Process, beginning with specification of the alternatives for choice and continuing (through the elapsed time) to selecting among them.

(a) The “structure of alternatives” for selection, refers to the specific menu of available choices. The items on these menus vary in number; they also vary in differentiation or mutual distinctiveness; specificity of resolution; source or whether the alternatives originate inside or outside the organization; and novelty or the extent to which the menu includes unfamiliar choices.

(b) Given a set of alternatives, “evaluation criteria” for selection are needed. Standards for choice may vary in their number, form, and variety (economic, technical, social-political, ethical). In addition, the “quality” that is required of a decision may vary between high and low.

(c) Next, the “mechanism” of selection has three aspects, one of which is cognitive; i.e., choosing among alternatives may be essentially conscious (deliberative, reflective, heuristic, etc), automatic (programmed, reflexive, etc.), serendipitous (accidental, coincidental), or even implicit (when changes of behavior are apparent but without manifest indication of an intervening selection process). Selection mechanisms also have a social aspect; they may be solitary or collective-narrow (selection by top managers, command staff, etc.), or collective-broad, and/or collective-nomial (where others are included symbolically – by considering their beliefs, preferences, interests, etc.). And selection has a “motivational” aspect, coded as whether incentives and disincentives (to choose) are present or absent.

(d) "Selection" is a mental event that has behavioral overtones. It is an outcome of the decision task modeling process. Since it is necessary to provide for the null case of non-selection, one may simply add a filter to any code for the process of non-selection.

If selection is made, it is innovative or routine (a variation of existing practice)? It is explicit or not explicit? And, is it noncontingent or does it depend on some particular state of the system?

(e) "Commitment" to a selection by a decision-maker may be high or low and public or private; and, finally,

(f) “Latency” of selection is the time interval (elapsed time) between evocation and selection.

Selection is performed by a human decisionmaker within a particular organizational setting. It may or may not require ratification, endorsement, or support from another actor. Selection may require authorization, or it may not be required but still be desirable. Thus, authorization of selection may be required by regulation/statute, and/or by policy, and/or by courtesy. These kinds of authorization are not mutually exclusive.

If a requirement for authorization exists, it may be satisfied by procedures which are formal or informal. And the results may be that authorization is granted conditionally or unconditionally or not.

After a selection is made, whether or not it is authorized, but before it is implemented, it may be disseminated. Dissemination of Decisions may be confined to the organization or broadcast to a wider audience. "Internal" dissemination may be confined to no more than a small coterie of individuals, but if there is further dissemination, it may be intended or not (leaked), limited or wide, and by word-of-mouth or by publication.

Dissemination of decisions may also be “external” – this may also be intended or not, and limited or wide.

Processes of selection, authorization, and dissemination may include preparation for implementation of a decision. We may analyze preparation for implementation of decisions (note, this is not literal implementation) as present or absent. If present, it may be explicit or implicit, detailed or not, contingent or noncontingent, and political or non-political.

(5) Post-decision Analysis: Selection and (6) Implementation. Analysis of the Post-decision Period includes consideration of any overt implementation of the cognitive selection; this is the culmination of the operation.

If Implementation is full or partial, one may ask what is the elapsed time between selection and implementation, or is the latency long or short.

Finally, we come to the question of Impact. Attention to impact is important, especially in the normative case of DSS design. We have not coded the details of this (although one might), but we have tried to evaluate whether the impact of a decision was (or is intended to be) “transformational” or “conservational;” i.e., whether its effect was mainly one producing change in the system, or conserving a status quo.

Moreover, regardless of whether the impact of a decision was conservational or transformational, one may ask as well whether its effect was internal or external, and whether it was direct or indirect in effect?

## 6. Conclusion

Decision support systems are devices for formalizing unprogrammed managerial choices. Designing them is a difficult job that involves finding answers to important questions such as:

\- What general and particular weaknesses (if any) have been shown or can be inferred to exist in the decision-making process?

● What specific decision-making activities can be helped with a computer system?

\- Which, if any, aspects of the decisions are amenable to operations research (OR) or quantitative model-buildup and use?

\- Is there any lack of information which would help improve decision-making activities? How might this be arranged?

\- Where is there an overabundance of data which could use information processing aid?

\- What type(s) of DSS (batch/on-line, file-based/DBMS, fixed-format/query, etc.) would fit the apparent needs of the decision-maker? Would more than one type of support be needed for different aspects of the decision task?

\- Do any of the participants require more education and training? Is there a need for research on group interaction processes? Or for organizational redesigns?

\- What organizational and/or interpersonal impediments exist? What strategic considerations are there relative to DSS implementation?

Virtually by definition, effective DSS design depends upon answering questions like these. We have outlined a broad interrogatory framework for conducting such task analysis, i.e., for specifying and organizing data relevant to complex managerial decisions. It is a framework expressly intended to serve as both a model for after-the-fact description of empirical decisions and a structure around which to plan and formulate programs for supporting or reconfiguring organizational decision activity.

Plainly the methodology we have proposed is an ambitiously detailed as well as formal one. But there is no responsible alternative. Inattention to the complexities of information flows, contextual details of managerial operations, and cognitive-behavioral dimensions of unprogrammed organizational decision processes accounts for too many (if not most) DSS failures.

At the same time, however, the task analysis method is realistic: adaptable as well as comprehensive. A user can use only parts of it for limited purposes and may tailor it to his particular needs. Obviously it would help if the level of abstraction of the model were reduced by writing a coding manual specifying analytic targets and giving general instructions for collecting information about them.

To this point the framework has had insufficient use to allow this. It has been used in a study of complex decisions in public organizations for which it was developed [4]. We are currently planning its further application to these cases and expect to report on it in due course. In addition, we are preparing to field test the method among DSS users in a commercial banking environment. Other applications are needed both to further specify the method and to evaluate its utility.

It still is not possible to gauge fully the practical value of the task analysis method either for after-the-fact analysis of specific decisions or as a basis for designing effective DSS. Plainly we think its value is high; but the issues can only be settled empirically by application and assessment of the method.
