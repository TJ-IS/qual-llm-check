# Attitudes Toward Telecommuting: Implications for Work-At-Home Programs

Gerardine DeSanctis

University of Minnesota, Management Sciences Department, 271
19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455, USA;
Teleph.: (612) 373-5211

Telecommuting, or working at home through use of telecommunications systems, is receiving increasing attention in the popular literature. MIS managers interested in improving the productivity and job satisfaction of their technical personnel are beginning to consider work alternatives made possible by declining costs of data communications and the influx of microcomputers into homes and offices. Though interest is high, there is little material available which can serve to guide organizations when implementing remote work programs. The research discussed here examines the attitudes of 51 managers and 129 programmers toward working at home. Survey data were collected from 3 divisions in 2 locations of a computer services firm which had experimented with work at home. Results suggest more interest in telecommuting among programmers than project managers; surprisingly, the two groups tend to identify the same benefits and problems. In general, programmers express support for any alternative which would add variability and autonomy to their work, whether it is a change in work location or simply a change in scheduling. The implications of these findings are discussed.

Keywords: Keywords: telecommuting, MIS personnel, remote work, work scheduling, attitudes, work alternatives

## 1. Introduction

Self-employed professionals, consultants, and contract workers have worked in their homes for years, but only recently has attention been given to the idea of having permanent employees perform their jobs at home on a regular basis. Several trends have contributed to interest in work at home:

● Pressure within organizations to find ways to improve worker productivity and attract and retain technically competent employees;

\- Social concern for improvement in the quality of work life, including emphasis on worker needs for independence, leisure time, and flexibility in the work place;

● Political-economic concern for conservation of energy resources required to move people to and from work.

There is speculation among organizational researchers and futurists that pressures toward remote work will be greater in the future. These pressures will be associated with the following trends:

\- Progressively lower telecommunications costs to move information and higher energy costs to move labor and other resources;

\- Increasing numbers of dual-career households with home and childcare responsibilities shared by both adults;

\- Increasing presence of low-cost computers and telecommunications equipment in homes with fast, reliable communication between an individual and the work place.

An employee may work at home anywhere from part-time (one or two days per week) to full-time, rarely making a trip to the office. This paper considers full-time work done at home three or four days per week and in the office on remaining days. Based largely on anecdotal evidence, several authors have identified benefits and problems of home work [3,4,7,11,12]. These are summarized in Table 1.

Few companies have conducted formal experiments with home working; an even smaller number have committed themselves to the idea by establishing permanent policies or programs. Where trial programs have been conducted, they generally have been informal, involving small numbers of employees with little attempt to measure project outcomes. Only two empirical studies have been reported [9,10], and these have relied on samples which were small (N = 40 and N = 9 respectively) and heterogeneous with respect to job task (including data entry clerks, course development analysts, technical managers, etc.). Research on the effects of telecommuting on individuals and organizations is difficult to conduct, because organizations are generally reluctant to experiment with the concept. Telecommuting is a costly undertaking, even on a pilot basis, and there is uncertainty as to how to implement it. At this point, all work in the area must be considered exploratory.

Telecommuting is of particular interest in the management information systems area for several reasons. First, it has been argued that in bad economic times businesses turn to their MIS departments for improvements in productivity [2]. MIS departments, in turn, look to their personnel for ways to increase output. Telecommuting has been viewed as a means to improve productivity. Working at home implies less travel time and more flexibility for work and leisure activities, resulting in less stress on the individual and more time available for effective work [4,5].

Second, several studies have found that systems personnel have higher needs for autonomy and achievement than other workers, such as accountants and engineers [15,16]. The improved flexibility in work hours and less direct supervision associated with home work might give the individual a greater feeling of autonomy.

Third, MIS departments are concerned with attracting and retaining high quality personnel in an era when they are difficult to recruit and expensive to replace. If working at home results in higher job satisfaction, then this alternative may be desirable for workers who are highly valued by the organization.

Finally, the possibility of flexible hours may result in greater off-hour use of computer resources. The individual programmer who can avoid queuing and other delays associated with heavily-used systems could become more efficient.

Whether or not MIS departments can experience any of the above benefits cannot be determined until pilot programs are established and evaluated. Meanwhile, several pre-implementation issues can be examined, such as the extent to which MIS personnel are interested in working at home; whether or not programming and other systems tasks can be performed remotely; and what benefits and problems can be expected from telecommuting.

The purpose of the study was to provide initial insight into several pre-implementation issues by studying the attitudes of MIS personnel toward telecommuting. Since systems personnel and their managers have been shown to have different psychological needs [6], the viewpoints of these two groups were considered separately.

In the study, the following hypotheses were tested:

H1: As a group, programmers are more interested in working at home than managers.

H2: Programmers are more likely than managers to receive their work as lending itself to being performed remotely.

H3: Employees who support the telecommuting concept tend to support other nonconventional programs, such as (a) flextime and (b) working in a suburban satellite facility.

In addition to testing the above hypotheses the following questions were explored:

Q1: Do managers and programmers differ in their views of the benefits and problems of working at home?

Q1: Do managers and programmers differ in their views of the benefits and problems of working at home?

Q2: Are the expected benefits and problems of telecommuting similar to those of flextime?

Q3: Are attitudes toward telecommuting a function of the demographic characteristics of the employee, such as age, sex, educational level, or number of children at home?

Q4: Are attitudes toward telecommuting a function of how far employees commute, or their method of transportation?

Q5: Do attitudes depend on job characteristics, such as responsibility for the supervision of others or the need to interact with coworkers?

Q6: Are attitudes toward telecommuting related to the individual's status, as measured by salary grade or length of stay in the company?

## 3. Method

51 managers and 129 programmer/analysts from 3 divisions in 2 locations of a computer services firm were surveyed regarding their attitudes toward telecommuting. The firm has a traditional work scheduling policy for all employees: 8 hours per day, 5 days per week. Any change to this schedule is arranged on an informal basis, between individual managers and their subordinates. Prior to administering this survey, a small pilot program had been conducted in which one programmer/analyst worked in her home 3 days per week. None of the respondents in this study had worked at home themselves, but they were familiar with the concept.

The survey contained 33 items. Initial questions related to background characteristics of the respondent (age, sex, number of children at home, job experience, salary grade, etc.). The employee was then presented with three hypothetical changes: work at home, work in a suburban satellite facility, and flextime. In each case the respondent was asked to compare the alternative with the current work schedule and indicate their preference. Next they were asked if their work was adaptable to being done at home or on a flextime schedule. Finally, they were asked their views regarding potential benefits and problems of flextime and telecommuting both for themselves and the organization.

H3: For both flextime and a satellite facility a significant chi square was observed (see Table 3). Employees who selected telecommuting over traditional office work also preferred flextime and a satellite location.

Q1: In open-ended questions, managers and programmers were asked to identify benefits and problems of telecommuting for themselves and for the organization. Their responses were categorized and counted for frequency of occurrence. Perhaps because of their greater interest, programmers were more likely to list benefits than managers (see Table 4). Conversely, managers were more likely to mention problems. The two groups identified the same advantages and disadvantages, however. Telecommuting was seen as promoting better morale and productivity, and both groups recognized the difficulties of managing remote workers and coordinating communications. However, easier childcare, better recruiting and reduced turnover, and problems of career development were not mentioned by either group, contrary to popular writing on the topic.

Q2: In the literature, many of the potential advantages attributed to telecommuting are also espoused by flextime advocates (improved job satisfaction, reduced transportation costs, etc.). Table 5 lists the perceived advantages and disadvantages of flextime. As with telecommuting, a change to flextime was expected to improve productivity but result in communication and management problems. Perhaps because of the more significant change in scheduling, better use of computer resources was mentioned for flextime but not for work at home; and while telecommuting was expected to impact worker morale, flextime was not. As with telecommuting, programmers were more likely to list benefits while managers mentioned problems.

Q3: Using 7 categories of age, 5 categories of education, and 4 categories of number of children at home, chi square analyses revealed no significant relationships between these variables and attitude toward telecommuting. However, preference to work at home was related to sex (chi square = 3.51, alpha = 0.05), with a greater proportion of women (74%) favoring the alternative than men (59%).

Q4: The organization used in this study was located in a large metropolitan area. On the average, employees commuted 13.1 miles (21 km) to work, with a range of 0.5 to 40 miles (0.8 to 64.4 km). Three transportation variables were examined: commuting distance, method of transportation, and availability of public transportation near one's home. None of these was meaningfully related to respondents' attitudes.

Q5: Preference for telecommuting was significantly related to supervisory responsibility (chi square = 4.22, alpha = 0.04). A smaller proportion of those with supervisory duties (53%) favored the home work option than those without such duties (69%). Also, the more frequently the respondents had to interact with others to perform their jobs, the less likely they were to prefer telecommuting; but this relationship was not significant. Interest in telecommuting was not a function of the tendency to take work home at night (chi square = 0.09, alpha = 0.95) but was related to the need to come into the office during offhours to deal with emergencies or work overtime (chi square = 5.80, alpha = 0.05).

Q6: Chi square analyses revealed that persons in lower salary grades and those with less seniority were more likely to prefer the home work option. These relationships were not, however, significant.

## 5. Discussion

In their study of the telecommunications-transportation tradeoff, Nilles et al. [8] concluded that perceptions play a significant role in the success of telecommunications systems. Through survey research, they demonstrated that prior attitudes towards cable television, radio, and touchtone telephones affected subsequent use of these systems. They go on to conjecture that attitudes toward telecommunications technologies in the home, including electronic banking, video conferencing, printed messaging, and job-related work via terminals will meaningfully impact the way in which these technologies are used. The current study attempted to be more careful in examining the expectations of organizational members toward working at home.

Results suggest that the decision to work at home is primarily related to a person's work position and job characteristics. Programmer/analysts, whose work in the office is closely tied to computer technology, can easily envision how their work might be done remotely. On the other hand, those with supervisory duties are more resistant to the idea. Because management is not embracing the concept, we might expect that telecommuting will not become widely practiced by full-time organization employees in the near future, even though the technology is already available.

It is interesting that most demographic and transportation variables were not related to attitudes toward telecommuting. The fact that more women than men, given the option, may choose to telecommute may imply that companies should be sensitive to possible discrimination problems if they make such programs available. The fact that reduced travel time was not seen as a valuable outcome of working at home suggests that organizations may not want to solicit telecommuters based on ease of commuting as a main objective. With regard to objectives, it appears that organizational members will be looking for effects on morale and productivity; effects on recruiting, turnover, and transportation are not as apparent to people. Lack of social interaction was not identified as a major problem with working at home.

This is consistent with research showing a low level of interest in associations with people among DP professionals and managers [1].

As in other areas of MIS, the success or nonsuccess of telecommuting will be a function of how it is implemented. Expectations and other behavioral factors will be critical to outcomes of telecommuting programs. Expectations, as reflected in attitudes toward telecommuting, are related to characteristics of the potential telecommuter (e.g., sex) and the nature of the work being performed (e.g., amount of offhour time required). These variables, in turn, influence how major implementation issues are handled. The decision to place programmers in the home requires, among other things, interest on the part of programmers and support for the idea by management. Given that the program has some objective, such as improving programmers' job satisfaction, telecommuting should be considered in comparison to other work scheduling or location alternatives. The findings of this study indicate that the expected outcomes of telecommuting are similar to those of flextime. Programmers are interested in home work and feel that it will result in 'improved morale'. However, they are also interested in flextime and working in a satellite facility, – and perhaps any alternative which would be thought to reduce work interruptions and increase productivity. Given a desire to improve job satisfaction or productivity, telecommuting is only one alternative. Research needs to be performed on how organizations can select the most appropriate alternative.

The attitudes of organizational members have implications for training and problem-avoidance during implementation. Sensitivity on the part of programmers to the problems of remote work suggests that they may be cooperative and work with management to deal with performance measurement, communication problems, and other issues during implementation. Both managers and programmers emphasized the problems of communication and resource availability when working at home. The implication is that organizations should give special attention to developing methods for dealing with such problems when implementing a telecommuting program. For example, electronic messaging or computerized access to systems manuals might be made available to home workers. The expectation of bad working conditions while at home implies the need to develop suggestions for dealing with this problem when preparing workers for the move from office to home. Lack of mention of remaining technically competent and assuring career development while at home suggests that this potential problem be brought out to home workers. Special training seminars which deal with such issues as: 'How to discipline oneself while working at home' and 'How to maintain competence and visibility in the organization while at home' may be in order. Such training has not been provided in the documented cases of telecommuting.

In summary, this study provides some initial insight into the attitudes of systems personnel toward home work. The results may be useful to organizations when planning pilot programs. The relationship between worker expectations and telecommuting success remains to be determined through experience and research.
