# The Impact of DSS on Organizational Communication \*

G. Larry Sanders

Management Science and Systems, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, New York 14214, USA

James F. Courtney and Stephen L. Loy
College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, Texas 79409, USA

There is some controversy in the MIS literature concerning the potential impact of computer systems on interpersonal communication in organizations. Generally, MIS researchers have found that effective communication between users and designers is an important factor in determining user satisfaction and MIS success.

Recently, however, Naylor [8] has argued that decision support systems (DSS) may isolate managers from interaction with others. This is in marked contrast to the contentions of Wagner [16] and Huber [6] that DSS may lead to more effective managerial communication.

We would argue that DSS encourage communication for several reasons: adaptive DSS development requires continued manager-analyst interaction; DSS-based decisions often require groups of managers; and there is a greater need for human information processing in a DSS environment.

This paper reports the results of a study of the relationships between DSS usage and organizational communications. It was found that users in three different categories (managers, financial or planning analysts (FPAs), and “others”) felt that DSS usage encouraged communication. Also, significant positive relationships were found between increased communication and overall satisfaction with the DSS for all three groups. Finally, for managers and FPAs, significant positive relationships were found between increased communication and satisfaction with the DSS in decision-making activities.

Keywords: DSS, Communication, Organization

## 1. Introduction

Recent experiments (see [20] and [12]) confirm that successful MIS implementation, as measured by user satisfaction, depends upon several factors: top management support, user involvement, DSS technical quality, organizational context, task structure, and individual differences.

A great deal of the MIS implementation literature has dealt with the importance of user involvement during the development process, especially user-designer communication. For example, Zmud [20] states that “...user involvement in MIS design has consistently been observed to be positively associated with user satisfaction.” He cites six studies supporting this contention. Moreover, interpersonal communication is included within several of the success factors cited above, including top management support and organizational context, in addition to user involvement.

Interpersonal communication may be even more important in DSS (as contrasted with MIS) development and use for at least four reasons: (1) DSS development is often interactive and adaptive in nature, requiring a high degree of manager-builder interaction; (2) DSS use may be through an intermediary or “information transfer specialist” (ITS) [17], thereby requiring manager-ITS communication; (3) decisions based on DSS-usage are seldom made in isolation, but usually involve interaction with other managers; and (4) there is a greater need for information processing and communication in environments characterized by a high level of uncertainty [9].

Several researchers have noted the importance of open, honest communication in reaching an acceptance consensus during the decision-making process $[16,6,10]$ . Also, Tushman and Nadler $[15]$ , Ginzberg $[3]$ , and Hackathorn and Keen $[5]$ have suggested that task-interdependence is an important variable in the DSS development and use process.

Recently, Naylor [8] has argued that DSS technology may require managers to spend all of their time at a computer terminal, thereby isolating them and reducing the level of organizational communication. Whistler [19] was the first researcher to address this issue in the context of EDP systems. He found that, for managers, there was no change in the level of interpersonal communication with the passage of time after the installation of an EDP system; in contradistinction for non-managerial personnel he did find a decrease in interpersonal communication. However, he felt that, in the long run, computer usage may reduce face-to-face communication throughout the organization. Mintzberg [7] has taken a stronger stand and warns about the pitfalls implicit in installing an MIS. He contends that MIS create a structure which is contrary to the managerial nature of utilizing informal verbal channels for gathering information.

The basic contention of this paper is that DSS, by their very nature, encourage organizational communication, and ultimately lead to a high degree of user satisfaction. We hypothesize this phenomenon because (1) manager-builder communication is encouraged through the adaptive development process; (2) manager-manager communication is enhanced by a more analytical approach to decision-making; and (3) the DSS modeling approach provides more timely and more relevant information than otherwise might be available. These propositions regarding communications in a DSS development/use environment are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Our contentions have been tested with data from a field survey of DSS users. Subsequent sections of this paper discuss the methodology used in the study, results of the research tests, and implications for DSS development, implementation, and research.

## 2. Methodology

Execucom Systems Corporation permitted use of its IFPS (Interactive Financial Planning System) client base for this study. IFPS provides a set of capabilities to quickly and easily build specific DSS applications [14]. It has been used to develop DSS to assist in strategic planning, annual planning, profit planning, budgeting, financial analysis, project analysis and, in general, those areas concerned with financial decision-making.

Execucom maintains a mailing list with a management contact and a technical contact in each of its client organizations. The responsibilities of the technical contact are related to the technical aspects of IFPS - such as maintenance, new updates, and system performance. The management contacts were concerned with the interface between the user and IFPS in model-building and decision-making.

An initial letter of introduction was sent to the management contacts in the US and Canada asking them and their organizations to participate in the study. In addition, a one-page questionnaire was included for gathering organizational information and to obtain the names of four IFPS users. A follow-up request was sent to management contacts who did not respond to the first letter.

Approximately three weeks later, a questionnaire packet was sent to the management contacts of the organizations willing to participate in the study. Each packet contained four IFPS user questionnaires and a letter of introduction to each user. A follow-up questionnaire was sent, through the management contact, to those users who did not respond to the initial request. We recognize that there are limitations in the methodology used in this study. First is the possibility that the Hawthorne effect may be influencing the subject's responses to the survey instrument. Secondly, the sample was not random, since subjects were selected by the management contact. Because of these considerations and the fact that the study was cross-sectional, we cannot say that the results definitely demonstrate cause and effect relationships. These difficulties are in general a function of the difficulty of conducting research in organizational settings and to a greater or lesser degree affect all such studies.

Out of approximately 400 installations, 124 organizations agreed to participate in the study. A total of 378 IFPS users returned the instrument. Table 1 breaks down the respondents by their organizational role. For purposes of data analysis, the subjects were categorized as managers, financial and planning analysts, and “others.” The rationale for considering managers as a separate category relates to DSS being primarily targeted for managerial decision-making. Financial analysts and planning analysts (FPAs) were placed in a separate category because IFPS was originally developed for that particular audience. The remaining individuals were primarily data processing or MS/OR analysts. While it would have been desirable to examine responses by managerial level, the number of respondents in upper management positions was not sufficiently large for meaningful analyses.

In this study, a two-dimensional perceptual measure of DSS success was used in evaluating the system. Based on the work of Welsch [18], Ginzberg [4], and Schultz and Slevin [13] the dimensions identified were: decision-making satisfaction (DECM) and overall satisfaction (OSAT). The first factor DECM (see Table 2) can be referred to as an indicator of improved decision-making. An example of one of the seven items from the scale would be: "Utilization of IFPS has enabled me to make better decisions." The second dimension (OSAT) underscores the user's perceived overall satisfaction with the system. The six questions in the scale relate to how the system, in general, assists the user in his or her job. For example, "IFPS is extremely useful." (See Sanders [11] for the scale development methodology and an overview of MIS/DSS success.) As noted earlier, it was our contention that if DSS use does indeed lead to increased communication, better models will be developed and the models will ultimately bring about increased overall satisfaction and decision-making satisfaction. The following item was used to determine the level to which DSS facilitates communication: "IFPS encourage communication with others in the organization." The DSS success measures and the above statement were scaled using a five-point Likert Scale.

## 4. Results

As noted earlier it was our contention that managerial personnel, FPAs, and “others” would have considerably different viewpoints of DSS usage and development, and different perspectives on communication. The results for the three groups are therefore presented separately.

## 4.1. Managers

A majority of the managers (116) indicated that they worked with others to develop IFPS models, while only 16 answered that they did not. When asked to respond to the question concerning the ability of IFPS to encourage communication, 42 percent of the managers agree or strongly agreed with the statement (Table 3), only 13 percent disagreed. Correlation analysis conducted on the two measures of DSS success (decision making satisfaction and overall satisfaction) and the ability of the DSS to promote communication revealed significant results. The correlation between “encourages communication” and decision-making satisfaction was 0.39 (Table 4), and between the overall satisfaction measure and the communication variable was 0.40. As previously indicated, several researchers have conjectured that DSS may be used to encourage communication.

From these results it is apparent that when the system is perceived to increase communication, there is evidence to suggest that the system will be more successful. A moderate relationship was also found between the length of time managers had been using the DSS and the level to which they perceived the system encouraged communication (r = 0.19, significant at the 0.05 level). This contrasts with Whistler's assertion concerning the long run decrease in communications for EDP. There is preliminary contradictory evidence for DSS.

## 4.2. Financial / Planning Analysts

A majority of the FPAs indicated that they work with others in developing IFPS models (85 percent). The percentage of FPAs who agree or strongly agreed that IFPS encourages communication was 48.1 percent (whereas 14.1 percent disagreed).

Correlation analysis between the two measures of success (decision making satisfaction and overall satisfaction) and the level to which the system encouraged communication were significant (r = 0.35 and r = 0.43 respectively). The outcomes for the FPAs are strikingly similar to the results for managers.

Finally, a small positive relationship was found between the length of time that the FPAs had been using the DSS and the level to which respondents indicated that DSS encourages communication (r = 0.11). Though the relationship was not statistically significant, it was not in the direction that Whistler hypothesized.

## 4.3. Others

This final category was comprised of individuals who were accountants, MS/OR analysts, staff support specialists, programmer analysts, etc. Approximately 91 percent of this group worked with other individuals to develop IFPS models. A large contingency of this group (55 percent) reported that IFPS encourages communication with others whereas only 13.5 percent disagreed with the statement concerning the impact of the system on communication.

The correlation between “encourages communication” and overall satisfaction was 0.37, closely paralleling the findings for managers and FPAs. However, the relationship between the communication variable and decision-making satisfaction was negligible. This result may reflect the type of tasks and responsibilities of the individuals in this category. Rather than using IFPS in a decision-making context these individuals assist decision-makers in model development.

The correlation between the length of time that respondents in the “other” category had been using the DSS and the level to which they perceived the system to encourage communication was positive (r = 0.14) but not statistically significant.

## 5. Discussion

As noted earlier, there are some limitations to the methodology used in this study. However, as a first step, the findings provide insight into an important topic. There has been some controversy regarding the impact of computer-based technology on organizational communication. The number of individuals indicating that the system increased communication (179) was substantially larger than the number who disagreed (51). A relationship was detected between the level of overall satisfaction with the system and the degree to which respondents indicated that the system increased communication. For managers and FPAs, increased communication due to the system also led to greater decision-making satisfaction.

These findings provide encouragement for DSS researchers. Whistler's findings regarding EDP systems appeared to support the contention that automation leads to decreased levels of interaction. There was some alarm that DSS would also reduce communication and tend to isolate managers. Our findings, however, appear to discount that notion in the context of DSS. This study has given some support to the assertion that DSS will bring about increased interaction within the organization and ultimately result in higher satisfaction with decision support systems and with organizational decisions.

## 6. Future Research

Investigation should be extended to other types of computer-based systems. The system investigated in this study was of a decidedly dissimilar nature than the EDP system used in Whistler's research. We expect that systems for organizational control and for processing transactions would have a markedly different impact on formal and informal communication channels than DSS. We therefore suggest that future communications research be conducted within the context of transaction processing systems, organizational control systems, and micro-based systems as well as the DSS environment.

The research design of choice would involve a pre- and post-installation examination of communication patterns to determine if any change had occurred. Emphasis could be placed on identifying the type of communication (formal versus informal) and the actual network structure resulting from system intervention. Insight might also be gained on developing strategies for redesigning communication networks to reflect procedural and human demands of new systems.

Another area for potential investigation would be the impact of computer-based systems on lateral relation mechanisms. The creation of lateral relation mechanisms (such as liaison roles, task forces, and linking managerial roles) has been used to promote communication and enhance problem-solving between organizational departments. The main purpose of lateral relations mechanisms is to reduce the number of decisions referred upward for their resolution [2]. A question then arises as to the appropriateness of a given form of lateral relations for a particular type of system and organizational context.

Finally, the relationship between communication and satisfaction is certainly more complex than the simple model used in this study, and further research into complexities seems definitely justified. It may, for example, be possible to capitalize on the DSS use to satisfaction relationship to yield increases in job satisfaction, productivity, and quality of work life for both managerial and nonmanagerial personnel.
