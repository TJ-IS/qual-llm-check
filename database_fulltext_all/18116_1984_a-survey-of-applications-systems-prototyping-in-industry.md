# A Survey of Applications Systems Prototyping in Industry

Gernot B. Längle, Robert L. Leitheiser and Justus D. Naumann
Management Information Systems Research Center,
School of Management, University of Minnesota,
93 Blegen Hall, 269 19th Avenue South, Minneapolis,
Minnesota 55455, U.S.A.

Managers of information systems development are faced with a substantial backlog of development projects. Traditional approaches to developing systems can not keep pace with new application demand. New methods must be found to develop systems. This study investigates prototyping, an approach that promises order-of-magnitude reductions in the time and effort required to implement satisfactory information systems.

A mail survey was used to collect information about systems development and prototyping in Fortune 500 firms. Data was collected on methodologies, tools, and practices from the Directors of Information Systems. The findings suggest that a large number of organizations are prototyping and consider it successful. Generic high level prototyping tools are rated and the reasons for replacing prototypes with conventionally developed systems are explored.

Keywords: Prototyping, Evolutionary Design, Broad-boarding, System Development, Heuristic Design, System Design.

## 1. Introduction

Computer based information systems are a vital part of a successful organization's operations and management. Unfortunately, the development of these systems has not kept up with the explosive growth in their demand. Even when the needed systems are developed, they often do not meet the requirements of their users. New approaches must be found to increase the number of applications developed and to improve their quality.

But the gains made by just these methods and tools have not closed the gap between organizations' needs for computer information systems and developers' abilities to produce them.

James Martin [10], argues that this gap cannot be eliminated by approaches that “only” double or triple applications development productivity. Instead, he calls for the use of tools and methods that achieve at least order-of-magnitude productivity gains.

One approach that promises these order-of-magnitude reductions in time and effort is prototyping. In prototyping, an intentionally incomplete system is quickly implemented and then evolved into a final version by the close cooperation of the user and builder [12]. While prototyping is only now coming into widespread use, some organizations have been using it successfully for over a decade [1,11,7]. Their experiences suggest that prototyping can lead to satisfied users with systems developed in days or weeks rather than months or years. Little empirical research has been done, however, to determine the extent to which organizations are doing prototyping and how successful they have been.

To begin to get an understanding of how organizations are using this new approach, this study examined the systems development methods of major U.S. corporations. A mail survey was used to collect data on the use of prototyping, systems development methodologies, high level development tools, and other important systems development activities from the directors of Information Systems Development in Fortune 500 firms.

The results of this survey are important to information systems managers who are considering or are already using prototyping in their systems development programs. They are also important to researchers who need background information on the prototyping phenomenon or who are searching for research opportunities in this area.

## 2. Background

Traditional systems development methodologies attempt to control the process of building information systems. They divide the system building process into phases and stages. This division facilitates project status control and allows frequent reviews by both development and user management. Unfortunately, these methods are not effective in dealing with (1) users who are unable to express their requirements (at least not until they can interact with a system [6]) and (2) a high rate of change in the environment. The traditional solutions to these problems are to (1) require formal user approval of written requirements specifications, and (2) prevent consideration of requirements changes during the development life cycle. Both of these responses exist in “traditional” approaches.

A well-managed systems development project that uses a traditional approach should result in a system that meets the formal requirements specification. Unfortunately, the specification often does not contain the users' actual needs because users could not state them or they changed during the long development process. In contrast, less restrictive approaches such as prototyping address the issues of requirements recognition and environmental change by (1) drastically reducing the time between recognition and implementation of at least a partial system, and (2) evolving designs through iterations of system usage and modification. Other approaches that attempt to meet these goals include “heuristic development” [3], “infological simulation” [2], “middle-out design” [8], and “evolutionary design” [9].

For the purposes of this survey, “prototyping” was defined as: “producing quickly built, purposely incomplete systems”. This definition includes all approaches that bypass the rigorous application of reviews, controls, specification freezes, etc., in favor of “systems that exhibit the essential features of later systems” [12, p. 30]. This definition is not as rigorous as some found elsewhere [e.g., 12].

## 3. Study Design

This study attempts to describe the current practices and attitudes of systems development managers in the largest U.S. industrial organizations. In particular, it examines the use of traditional systems development methodologies and prototyping. Fortune 500 firms (1981) were selected for the survey. A multi-part questionnaire (Appendix A) was mailed to the “The Director of Information Systems Development”. An accompanying letter further described the characteristics of desired respondents as managers who have “…responsibility for systems development…” and who “…know about current information systems as well as about the methods used to develop them”.

The issues addressed by the survey were:

\- To what degree are organizations using “prototyping”?

\- What tools are perceived as necessary for applications prototyping?

\- Is there widespread dissatisfaction with traditional systems development methodologies?

● Why is prototyping used?

\- Has prototyping been successful?

● What are the perceived benefits of prototyping?

● Under what conditions are prototypes replaced?

The questionnaires were distributed in August, 1981, and responses were tabulated and analyzed in 1982. The response rate, 14 percent, was low, but expected, given that the names of targeted managers were not available for addressing the survey forms. The size of the mailing (500) helped insure that enough data were available to perform the analyses. This response rate was adequate for the exploratory purposes of this study. Further investigation into specific issues will require better representation of the target population.

## 4. Prototyping Subgroups

Three kinds of respondents were expected: (1) those who prototype, (2) those who plan to prototype, and (3) those who don't use or plan to prototype. The answer to question number 20 was used to place respondents into one of these subgroups. The following definitions will be used.

\- Prototyper – applies to those organizations who have used a prototyping approach to develop applications systems.

\- Plan to – applies to those organizations that have specific plans to try prototyping but have not yet done so.

\- No – applies to those organizations where prototyping has not been used and there are no plans to try it.

Of the 78 usable responses returned, 33 percent (26) said that they had developed applications using prototyping (the “prototyper” subgroup), 21 percent (16) said that they were planning to do applications prototyping (the “plan to” subgroup), and 46 percent (36) said that they had neither done nor planned to do any prototyping (the “no” subgroup).

## 5. Characteristics of Respondents

Respondent background and organizational data were collected and analyzed to provide a context for the systems development findings.

## 5.1. Individual Respondents

The survey was targeted at managers who were responsible for application development in Fortune 500 firms. The sample obtained was primarily from this group. All 78 respondents were affiliated with information systems organizations. Ninety-one percent were managers or directors, 5 percent were vice-presidents, and only 4 percent were analysts. The respondents' experience level reflected their positions. They had spent a mean of 10.8 years with their current company and 15.9 years in the Information Systems field. Most belonged to at least one professional society.

## 5.2. The Organizations

The organizational context for the respondents' replies varied by firm and individual. Forty-five percent (35) claimed to represent their whole firm. An almost equal number, 41 percent (32), represented just the corporate head-quarters where the questionnaire was sent. The remaining 14 percent (11) represented a division or group (Fig. 1). While there were differences among the prototyping subgroups, none was found to be statistically significant (using a t-test and alpha = 0.10).

Respondents reported the industries that their firms were competing in. A breakout of firms by their primary industry is given in Fig. 2. Eleven firms were considered so diversified that they could not be assigned a major industry classification. No statistical relationship was found between principal industry and prototyping subgroups.

The final organizational characteristic examined is the existence and completeness of a corporate MIS plan. Only three respondents reported that they did not have a formal MIS plan.

## 6. Systems Development

Respondents were asked to describe the resources they used for systems development. Data were collected on organizations, methods, and tools.

## 6.1. The Development Organization

The number of people engaged in developing systems varied widely among firms but most (62 percent) reported a staff of less than 50. A few very large development organizations (staff greater than 200) skewed the distribution (mean = 75, median = 30). There was some statistical evidence (alpha = 0.10) that smaller development organizations were less likely to have prototyped or to have planned to prototype.

Firms located their systems analysts in one or more of the following units: user organizations (21 percent), centralized DP organization (88 percent), and divisional DP organization (4 percent). There were no differences among subgroups on this dimension. Primary responsibility for determining information requirements fell on users (17 percent of respondents), managers/directors (34 percent), analysts (64 percent), and steering committees (1 percent). While all of the prototyping subgroups listed analysts and managers/directors above users, prototypers listed them more frequently than non-prototypers (alpha = 0.05) or “plan to” prototypers (alpha = 0.10).

The existence of a central systems development standards group within the IS Department was also examined. Fifty-five percent of all respondents had such a central standards group and firms that prototyped were more likely to have one (alpha = 0.10) than those who did not.

## 6.2. Systems Development Methods

Commercial systems development methodologies were used by 29 percent of the respondents, while 45 percent had developed and used their own. Fifteen different commercial methodologies were listed. Differences between subgroups were not statistically significant, but they suggested that prototypers and those who planned to prototype were more inclined to use an SDM. Overall, in-house methodologies tended to have been in use slightly longer (mean 3.9 years) than commercial methodologies (3.3 years).

Respondents were asked to rate their satisfaction with commercial and in-house methodologies along four dimensions: (1) Information requirements produced, (2) Documentation produced, (3) Control of projects, and (4) User acceptance of methodology. Only on the documentation dimension was there a statistical difference (alpha = 0.05). The average rating for commercial methodologies was higher than that for in-house methodologies.

In comparing subgroup ratings for commercial methodologies, only one dimension produced significant differences. Respondents who don't prototype rated the “Project Control” dimension of their methodology higher than those who prototype (alpha = 0.05) or plan to prototype (alpha = 0.05). This observation suggests that in firms where the negative control aspects of formal methodologies have not been felt, there may be little incentive to try prototyping. For in-house methodologies, prototypers were more satisfied with their documentation (alpha = 0.05) and less satisfied with their user acceptance of systems (alpha = 0.10) than were non-prototypers. This suggests that the desire to improve user acceptance of systems may be an important motivator for trying prototyping. More research is needed, however, before this can be stated with confidence.

When respondents were asked to list other approaches that they had considered for developing systems, 54 percent of the prototypers and 71 percent of the planning to prototype firms contributed approaches. This compares to only 36 percent of the non-prototypers. Thirty different approaches were listed ranging from methods (prototyping) to hardware (IBM System 38).

Fifty-four of the respondents reported using nineteen different database management systems (DBMS); the remaining respondents reported that they were not using a DBMS. Firms that had prototyped or planned to prototype were somewhat more likely to have DBMSs - 76 percent versus 63 percent. When database management systems were further categorized as either programmer-oriented (host language based) or non-programmer oriented (query language based), no significant differences could be found.

## 7. Prototyping

The second half of the questionnaire examined current and planned prototyping activities. It was completed by the 26 respondents who had prototyped and the 16 who planned to do so. There were large differences in experience among those that had prototyped. One respondent reported that prototyping had been in use in his firm for 10 years. The median time, however, was 2 years. In terms of the number of prototypes developed, the median was three but one organization reported 50 completed projects. This experience is examined in the following sections.

## 7.1. Prototyping Tools

A set of advanced tools is needed to build application prototypes successfully. For this study a list of tool categories was compiled from the literature $[10,14,12]$ . Each respondent was asked to rate the usefulness of each category on a five-point scale where “1” was useless and “5” was useful. Table 1 shows the median ratings for each class of tool. The ratings indicate that very high level programming languages were perceived as most useful. This rating is followed closely by interactive updating facilities and high level query languages. Access to existing data files was rated higher than database management systems. We interpret this result to imply that prototyped systems must be integrated with existing databases and files. Respondents could write in tools and utilities that were not on the compiled list. They included recovery and reorganization utilities, relational databases, and program generators.

## 7.2. Prototype Development

A key feature of prototyping is the rapid development of applications. With high level tools, the “first-cut” can be developed “overnight” [11,12]. Fig. 4 shows the time required for first-cut and for completed systems. The median time required for the builder to respond with a first-cut prototype was reported to be one month. The final system was completed in a median time of two months. One respondent claimed to have done a first-cut prototype in 1 hour; another reported a finished prototype in 4 days.

"Heuristic development" [3] suggests that prototyping be done for the requirements determination phase of a traditional development life cycle. An alternative view is that the decision to replace a prototype should be based on a economic analysis of ongoing operating costs and the cost of developing and operating the replacement system [12].

To shed some light on this issue, respondents were asked about their replacement of prototypes. Fifty-five percent reported that they had replaced at least one system with a conventionally developed version. When asked about reasons for this replacement, the response “Difficulties with interfacing other applications” was rated the highest (median of 3.83 on a 5-point scale). This compares with 3.50 for “Maintenance” and 3.00 for “Efficiency". This suggests that prototyping might hinder the sharing of data and processes to an extent that can justify a change to a conventionally developed system. More study is needed to understand the specific causes of this replacement.

## 7.3. Prototyping Outcomes

An important measure of the success of any systems development effort is user satisfaction $[13]$ . Since prototyping requires close cooperation between users and system builders, the questionnaire solicited the respondents' (MIS managers) opinion on both user and builder satisfaction. Table 2 shows the results of these estimates. Both users and builders were thought to be more satisfied with prototyped systems than with traditionally developed systems.

Respondents were asked for reasons why they were using the prototyping approach. These reasons were grouped into major categories. Fig. 5 shows the four most important classes. “Reduced development time” was mentioned most frequently, followed by “increased user involvement and satisfaction”.

## 8. Conclusions and Recommendations

The literature has many descriptions of prototyping and examples of its successes [see 7 for an annotated bibliography]. The authors, however, know of no study that has examined the practice of prototyping across many organizations. This research was an attempt to do just that. Its purposes were (1) to obtain a broad view of how a large segment of industry was dealing with the rising demand for information systems and (2) to point to areas where more research is needed. Some of the more interesting findings are summarized below.

## 8.1. Level of Prototyping Activity

An important question that the study sought to answer is: “How much prototyping is going on?” It found that a majority of the participating Fortune 500 firms either have used or are planning to use prototyping. While the specific percentage should be viewed cautiously (“prototypers” would be more likely to respond than “non-prototypers”), there is strong evidence that prototyping is becoming an important systems development activity. With the increasing availability of the high level tools necessary for prototyping, this activity could only have increased since the survey was taken.

## 8.2. Organizations that Prototype

Another important question the study sought to answer is whether there were any differences between firms that prototype and those that do not. While many organizational variables were examined, only a few produced significant differences. One area examined was the completeness of MIS plans. Almost all organizations had formal MIS plans. The results suggest that firms that prototype have more complete plans. The evidence is by no means conclusive, but it does seem reasonable that managers who take planning seriously would be concerned over the rapidly growing demand for computing and their inability to keep up with it. Perhaps they would even investigate non-traditional ways to do systems development, i.e. prototyping.

Another area examined was the existence of a central standards group. The presence of this group indicates a concern for compatible, maintainable, and economical applications systems. The group's existence could be viewed as detrimental to the use of prototyping. Instead, the survey found that firms that prototyped or planned to prototype had a greater likelihood of having a standards group. It may be that organizations that are willing to respond to the perceived need for development coordination may be willing to respond to other perceived needs, like improved turn-around times for applications. It may also be that with such a group in place, firms do not feel threatened by the loss of control due to not using formal development life cycles.

Finally, the formal systems development methodologies used by the firms were investigated.

Respondents who prototyped or plan to prototype rated their commercial SDM lower on the “Project control” dimension than those who did not prototype. This lower rating may be due to a recognition of the negative aspects of rigid project control and of the need for greater development flexibility. In comparing in-house methodologies, prototypers tended to be more satisfied with their documentation but less satisfied with their level of user acceptance than non-prototypers. Since prototyping gives up formal documentation to improve user satisfaction, these results suggest motivations for trying the new approach.

## 8.3. Tools for Prototyping

The importance of database management systems to successful prototyping is widely accepted $[11,12]$ . This survey found that other tools may be considered even more important. “Very high level programming languages” and “high level query languages” were rated higher by the MIS managers. A possible explanation for this is that most organizations had “host-language oriented” database management systems. These DBMS require builders to write host language code to build applications. This slows down the design iteration and increases the expense of developing prototypes. To make prototyping truly effective, “4th generation languages” $[10]$ are critical.

Access to existing data files was also highly rated. This response indicates that firms using prototyping recognize the need for ready access to previously stored data. Other tools rated as important were generalized input and edit, update, and report software. These facilities are usually included in very high level languages.

Graphics output, while not rated as highly as the other tools, was considered more important by those who had prototyped than by those who only planned to. It may be that the value of graphics output becomes more apparent with prototyping experience.

## 8.4. Prototype Replacement

A widely accepted view is that systems developed with generalized software tools will need to be replaced by more efficient programs developed with conventional life cycle methodology. These findings suggest that other reasons are more important. Perhaps organizations give up operating efficiency to make better use of their scarce personnel resources.

The most highly rated reason for replacement of prototyped systems was “interfacing difficulties”. This response matches the finding of a high “need for access to other data”. To the extent that prototyped systems are difficult to integrate (data and processes) with other systems, they are candidates for replacement.

The overall proportion of systems that have been replaced cannot be reported because the number of prototyped systems covered by the survey is not known. Some indication of the replacement rate comes from the finding that 45 percent of the firms have not replaced any prototype systems with conventional ones. It is not known whether this level is constant or merely a reflection of the “newness” of the prototyping approach.

## 8.5. Summary

The results of this survey indicate that very experienced systems development organizations are

As experience is gained with applications prototyping new and improved tools will be developed. These advances should further reduce the cost of the resources necessary for this approach. Even more organizations will then take advantage of the order-of-magnitude productivity improvement that is available from prototyping.

As in any exploratory study of this kind, more questions have been raised than answered. When is prototyping appropriate? When should replacement take place? Are some ways of prototyping more appropriate than others? Many issues remain for researchers and managers. This study does demonstrate that prototyping is an important systems development option. It shows that the means to do it exist in most large organizations. With increasing demand for information systems and changing user requirements, the use of of prototyping can only rise.
