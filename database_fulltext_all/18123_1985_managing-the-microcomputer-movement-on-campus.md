# Managing the Microcomputer Movement on Campus

Barry Render

Department of Decision Sciences, George Mason University 4400 University Drive, Fairfax, Virginia 22030, USA (Tel. 703-323-2758

and

Ralph M. Stair, Jr.

Department of Management Florida State University, Tallahassee, Florida 32306 USA (Tel. 904-644-5505)

The microcomputer revolution is beginning to have a significant impact on colleges throughout the U.S. and Canada. The proliferation of microcomputer labs, found in 90 percent of 210 schools participating in this study, affects faculty, curricula, students, and the administrators who must manage them. This article examines the current and projected hardware and software needs of colleges. It also addresses the agenda of how to manage and control the expensive and rapidly expanding microcomputer resource.

Keywords: Microcomputers, microcomputer laboratories, colleges, selection of microcomputer hardware, microsoft software, staffing micro laboratories, equipment replacement, laboratory control procedures, software protection.

The latest of his five textbooks deals with microcomputer software for Management Science and Operations Management.

Barry Render is Associate Professor and Chairman of the Department of Decision Sciences at George Mason University. He holds a Ph.D. in Quantitative Analysis from the University of Cincinnati. He is currently researching the impact of microcomputers on managers in the U.S. and in developing countries, and is also working with psychologists in addressing the issue of cyberphobia in the management ranks.

## 1. Introduction

![](/api/attachments/5C2U6X4E/fulltext/images/8c26e7937854e7d367dd7c39df844c6139cb678f6c9e96f75b497069c3646553.jpg)

The worldwide microcomputer revolution that is impacting on government and industry is also having a tremendous effect on colleges and universities. Employers not only want computer literature graduates in general, but micro literate ones specifically. A recent survey in the Washington, D.C. area shows that business wants graduates who can enter prepared to do spreadsheet analyses, set up micro-oriented data bases, and use word processing.

Colleges across the country are responding to such pressure in a dramatic way, with a complete rethinking of the traditional data processing education [1-10]. They are also making a quick move away from the mainframe with new, powerful microcomputer laboratories.

This article presents the results of a broad study of colleges and universities throughout the U.S. and Canada. The sample for the research involves 210 schools that responded to a late 1984 questionnaire (shown as Appendix A) sent to 600 institutions that offer degrees in the field of Business Administration. The 35 percent response rate indicates a high level of concern and interest on the part of administrators who are responsible for managing the microcomputer movement and their campuses.

Ralph M. Stair is Professor of Management at Florida State University and author of over a dozen textbooks in the computer and management science areas. He holds a Ph.D. Operations Management from the University of Oregon. This current research centers on the role of microcomputers on small businesses in Florida.

The study reveals that only 10 percent of the colleges today have no micro laboratory. Forty percent have a lab just for the business school, 40 percent have a university-wide micro center, and 10 percent have both. The 1984 range in size and equipment is enormous, from labs with a handful of micros to labs with 150 or more machines. The real growth, however, is scheduled to take place between now and 1989, with a large number of schools planning on placing several hundred micros in use.

This growth means big business for both hardware and software vendors, and a big change in the way colleges go about the business of education. This article gives an overview of what schools are doing today in the hardware and software spheres, and what they plan on doing in the future. It also describes how colleges are managing the microcomputer revolution and the staffing, control, and equipment issues that accompany it.

## 2. The Hardware Situation

The hardware leader on campus is, not surprisingly, the IBM P.C. Sixty-five percent of all colleges with micro labs included IBM equipment in their late 1984 inventory. Table 1 provides a profile of the hardware distribution. The average college with a micro lab (188 of them in our survey) had 30.7 computers. Apple, an early leader in the educational market, is a distant second to IBM in terms of all three categories depicted: customer installation base, average installation size, and largest surveyed lab. Radio Shack's TRS models, also early entrants, are in third place and losing ground.

But the college marketplace is a fluid environment, open to all vendors who can cater to cost-conscious and volume customers. The DEC Rainbow, Victor 9000, Sperry PC, and Televideo PC, for example, have all gained footholds in the campus world.

Tapping the market means knowing what the consumer wants, and our study gleaned useful information as to how colleges evaluate the micros they are going to purchase. Computer administrators on campus were asked to rate the importance of a variety of factors in selecting microcomputers for their labs.

The most important concern in the minds of college decision makers is software availability for the micro being considered. Fully 78 percent of the respondents rated software “very important”. Cost, more a factor at larger, state institutions, placed second with 49.5 percent rating with “very important.” Memory capacity was a close third. The good news for Apple-IBM competitors is that brand name was ranked a distant fourth, indicating that colleges are perhaps willing to buy from a lessor known vendor if the machine is priced well and software is abundant. The difficulty these challengers face is that educational software publishers tend to concentrate their limited developmental effort on the proven IBM-Apple (or compatible) markets.

The five least important factors rated were available peripheral equipment, mainframe linkage, processor bit size (and hence speed), compatibility with faculty equipment, and dependence on the central computer center. We also found that larger, more research-oriented universities rated the ability to link to the mainframe much more strongly than did smaller colleges. Likewise, the larger institutions were more sensitive to purchasing computers compatible with those owned by the faculty than were smaller schools.

## 4. Hardware and Software Growth

The proper words to describe microcomputer hardware and software growth in colleges in the next few years are “very strong”. As mentioned earlier, we found that the number of micros in an average college lab in late 1984 was 30.7. This is projected to increase into an average size of 34.9 in 1985, 49.7 in 1986, and 75.0 by 1989. The largest single lab in our study, in 1984, had 180 micros. Several colleges projected they would have 250 machines in 1985, and one school claimed a planned lab of 900 micros by 1989.

Yet we should point out that, even four years from now, there are 20 colleges (out of 210 surveyed) that are not planning to install any microcomputers, but instead plan to retain their mainframe orientation. Whether they can hold out or not is not the issue to much as the fact that some resistance to the whole concept of forcing and rushing into micros still remains. This is an understandable belief that is likely shared by a number of corporate and governmental data processing center directors.

Just as strong as hardware growth are short-terms plans for new software procurement. Here are the figures: 51.4% of all schools plan to purchase more spreadsheet software in the next 1–2 years; 61.4% will buy data base software; 51.4% word processing software; 67.6% accounting packages; 56.2% management science software; and 69.0% statistical programs.

## 5. Managing the New Micros on Campus

The issue of managing the new microcomputer resource has implications that affect many levels of university administration—from the President down to Deans to D.P. Center Directors to professors—all of whom must assume responsibility for addressing a whole new agenda of problems. In addition to deciding how the micros should be selected, other questions must be answered. Who and how many people should staff the labs? How should obsolete equipment be replaced? How should maintenance and control of this expensive resource be handled? And what can be done to protect costly and easily stolen software?

## 5.1. Staffing Issues

Since the schools in our study have university-wide microcomputer centers, labs just for the Business School, or both, we found a variety of staffing systems present. In 34% of these labs, the director reports to the Business School Dean, 12% to the Assistant Dean of the School of Business, and 9% to a designated faculty member. In 18 percent of the colleges, the micro lab head reports to the Director of University Computing, and in the remaining 27% to a variety of other university officials.

Levels of staffing also revealed a broad range. Only 50 percent of the labs had a full-time Director and only 14 percent had an Assistant Director. Eight percent of the labs employed programmers (the mean number on those staffs was 1.87 programmers). Most labs run on the fuel of student workers – over 85 percent use them, with an average of 4.8 students in each lab. Only 8 percent of the labs have full-time consultants, and only 13 percent have full-time clerical staff.

## 5.2. The Issue of Replacing Equipment

Setting policies to deal with computer obsolescence, maintenance, and software control are new and difficult issues that schools are only beginning to resolve. Some micros purchased in the late 1970's are technologically obsolete already and unable to run many of the powerful new software packages such as Lotus and d Base.

We found that over 54 percent of the colleges with micro labs had no established means to provide funding for equipment replacement. Thirty-three percent do not set up funds each year to replace equipment, 19 percent charge students using the facility a lab fee, 3 percent depend on grant monies, and 8 percent employ a variety of other approaches. Some schools use more than one of these replacement policies (so the numbers above total to more than 100%).

## 5.3. Maintenance and Control Procedures

Colleges were found to maintain and control their microcomputer hardware with four main procedures: 48 percent retain formal maintenance agreements with vendors or service organizations; 36 percent have formal procedures that outline the use of all computer equipment; 29 percent have insurance that covers the loss, theft or damage of computer equipment (although larger colleges tended to avoid this policy); 18 percent use an on-campus self maintenance policy; 15 percent have a variety of other approaches; and only 4 percent have no maintenance policy or procedures. Again, of course, many schools use more than one of these policies.

## 5.4. Software Protection

Perhaps the most perplexing issue faced in college microcomputer labs is that of software. The temptation to students, faculty and lab directors to make illegal copies of expensive software is great – and the temptation to students to keep useful disks that belong to the university is equally strong. Nevertheless, just as colleges eventually recognized the legal ramifications of photocopying misuse, they are quickly becoming aware of the dangers of disk copying.

To protect software, we found that 64 percent of all labs maintain one checkout point where software is kept. Sixty percent back up all software used by students and faculty. In 56 percent of the labs students and faculty must check out software as they would a book on reserve in the library. At 18 percent of the schools, students and faculty are required to sign a form indicating that they understand the rules and vendor agreements applying to their lab. Only 9 percent of all colleges have no formal procedures of any kind.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

This first major study of the microcomputer movement on campus provides some important figures. Not only are microcomputers spreading throughout 90 percent of the colleges surveyed, but they are spreading quickly and expending large sums of money in the process. The policy at a lot more than one college during this micro-fashionable period is to approve purchase of any microsoft package requested by a faculty member for examination. Entire curricula are being redesigned in schools of business administration to emphasize the increasing new role of the microcomputer, and to take advantage of the powerful software now at student/professor fingertips.

Managing this technological change is another challenge on campus, especially as the traditional, well-understood mainframe environment makes room for the new demands of microcomputer hardware and software. How to grope with new policies and procedures for controlling, protecting, staffing, and maintaining the labs requires broader knowledge about options and costs. This study is intended as a first step in understanding the microcomputer movement and in learning how to manage it.
