# An IS\* Case: The Closed Loop Scenario

Stephanie S. Barrett

Department of Decision Sciences, The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

Organizations are exploring the potential of inter-organizational information systems (IS\*s) in greater numbers. Generally the incentives are cost reduction and/or productivity improvement. Gaining these potential benefits requires cooperation within the information exchange path. Potential barriers, however, are the recognition of an information processing problem and the readiness to accept automated solutions.

Keywords: Inter-organizational Systems, IS Strategy.

Stephanie Barrett is an Assistant Professor of Decision Sciences at the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton School. In addition to continuing projects in Inter-organizational Systems she is working on research in computer-based education and user training.

## 1. Introduction

All organizations have some internal information processing systems be they manual or electronic. Inter-organizational Information Systems (IS\*s), however, are those data communication/data processing systems linking independent organizations so that electronic information processing resources may be shared. The most typical of these is the telecommunication-oriented order processing system enabling direct customer-supplier CPU interaction.

IS\* is a generic label permitting the discussion of all inter-organizational configurations regardless of their form, purpose, number of participants, degree of complexity or physical implementation. Shareability of resources implies that there is a consistency of form, purpose and/or information content in data exchanges between sharing partners, i.e., IS\* participants. Consequently, wherever such consistencies exist there is the potential to develop both the procedural and transmission standardization necessary for building an IS\*.

Shareable resources in the IS\* context include data, databases, hardware, software, transmission facilities, rules, procedures and expertise. Although organizations are not dismantling their internal systems, they are certainly examining the potential benefits of IS\* participation in greater numbers. At the present time, operational IS\*s or prototypes are being used in a wide range of industrial settings. Drug wholesaling [4], Grocery manufacturing [5], Insurance claims processing, Travel Reservation and Automated Clearing Houses for financial transactions are just a few examples. Some of these systems were developed following a long tradition of cooperation through intra-industry organizations. All were precipitated, in part, by recognition of the potential benefits of such cooperation.

\- Cost Reductions: An example of direct reduction is decreased error (re)processing. Indirect reductions may be more subtle such as by decreasing the amount of search time for a buyer.
- Cost Displacement: The most common cost displacements occur through downloading data entry tasks or utilizing sophisticated software developed by another IS\* participant.

\- Productivity Improvements: By generally improving the age of information, IS\*s may allow improvement of inventory handling, delivery routing, discount taking and other related tasks.

\- Product / Market Strategy: The firm maintaining or building an IS\* may gain advantages vis-a-vis its completion through on-going channel analysis and the formalization of participant relationships. [2,3]

Two information processing strategies would appear to support these outcomes. The first is moving the site of electronic data capture as close as possible to the data source. The second is moving the site of information retrieval as close as possible to the user/manager/decision-maker. Neither of these is a particularly novel strategy within the context of a single firm. However, within a multi-firm context, both represent a significant change of attitudes with respect to data “ownership” and processing control.

Depictable as a closed loop, the extreme application of these strategies is shown in Fig. 1. In the closed loop scenario, initial electronic data capture occurs at the very end of a production or distribution chain. For consumer goods this would be at the retail outlet. Subsequent nodes would process the upflow into information for specific analyses or decisions as necessary and add data. The loop, when closed, ensures that upstream participants have access to two types of data – the data representing decisions made at a downstream node (e.g. orders) and the data on which those decisions were made.

The implication, of course, is that all members of a product creation/distribution chain must be participants in a common IS\*. That no such system exists is not surprising. High application complexity and low participant infrastructure compatibility represent virtually prohibitive development conditions on such a large scale.

Existing and prospective technology, such as gateways, would make such a task feasible on a smaller scale, but not necessarily cost effective. Cost effectiveness criteria demand that benefits accrue to each organizational participant in proportion to its participation costs. However, some participation costs may be less tangible than six labor months of programming. Consequently, even technical feasibility can not guarantee clearly participation incentives/disincentives for an individual firm.

This paper examines the IS\* participation strategies and incentives that exist in a well-defined potential loop. Specifically examined are the decision criteria at each node in the loop and the feasibility of closing it.

## 2. The General Retail Environment

Retail outlets would be the optimal first-stage electronic data capture source for producers. Producers must determine how much to produce and where to distribute. These decisions are necessarily dependent on current sales data (orders), projected orders as derived from historical data, and projected demand as derived from marketing studies. The most immediately useful dataset would be a real-time, aggregate of downstream product movement. This would be especially true for makers of perishable goods or those with a short shelf life. Aggregates, even on a small scale, cannot be created without capture of the primary unit, i.e., the movement of a specific product from Node A of Fig. 1 to the purchaser.

The distinction being made here is between upstream accounting data, USALEs (upstream sales), and downstream product movement data, DSALEs (downstream sales), at all nodes in the chain. Each purchase, whether by a consumer or value-adding firm, is the outcome of a decision process. The outcome is represented to the upstream partner by an 'order'. The upstream firm may infer that orders represent actual sales volume, eg. why would a wholesaler purchase what cannot be sold? However, significant differences, based, for example, on inventory holding patterns or price change expectations, are possible. These factors are embodied in the independent management decision functions of Fig. 1. and may lead to a poor correlation between expected and actual sales volume at the upstream node. More efficient producer activity should be possible with timely USALES AND DSALES data over the complete set of nodes. Moreover, while it is obvious that the management decision function used at Point A is critical to the entire chain, it may not be assumed that it is the most efficient of functions. This aspect will be discussed more fully in another section.

There are two possible IS\* processing options that would make DSALES available to the upstream partner of a retailer. In the first, the upstream partner would provide all application processing (inventory management reports, EOQ modeling, etc.). By providing inventory management services, the upstream partner has immediate and complete access to product movement data. This method would be effective, however, only where the upstream, IS\* processing partner is responsible for either the majority of items carried by the retailer or transmission routing between the retailer and it's other suppliers. Without this constraint the management reports would not be sufficiently comprehensive to compensate the retailer for task compliance, e.g., adopting the upstream partner's rules and procedures with respect to inventory management. This method is used very effectively by the large drug wholesalers who may provide up to 95% of a particular retailer's product line (5).

The second option is for the retailer to process management information locally, invoke its own management decision function with respect to creating USALES and transmit both datasets, USALES and DSALES, upstream voluntarily. This is the only possible option, if the retailer node is configured as part of a star net. The balance of this paper will focus exclusively on the second type of IS\* building as it would apply to the vast majority of retail operations.

## 3. The Specific Retail Environment

To study the potential of a voluntarily closed IS\* loop, it was necessary to define a fairly discrete, short distribution chain. Alcoholic beverage was chosen for several reasons. It is a highly regulated product and the regulations affect distribution in such a way that the number of potential outlets is manageable. It is peripherally related to the grocery industry – mainly U.S. states allow alcoholic beverage sales in grocery stores – which was built the Uniform Communications Systems (UCS) to facilitate IS\* development between suppliers/manufacturers. Some alcoholic beverage bottlers have already shown readiness for participation in UCS by use of the Universal Product Code.

In addition to a strict specification of the product, a single state was chosen. New Jersey ranked 12th in alcoholic beverage consumption per capita in 1982 [7] and under its law alcoholic beverage pricing and product availability data are controlled. Wholesalers are required to fix and submit price data to the state monthly. In addition, the price data is submitted for publication to a private organization which subsequently distributes it to subscribing retailers. As published, pricing data is available in either book or computer tape format.

Intuitively, a retailer able to process the electronic version of the “book” should be able to process the backflow electronically. Backflows are orders based on local management decision functions applied to locally collected sales data. The backflow is labeled USALES on Fig. 1. Similarly, a wholesaler able to process the order inflow (DSALEs of Fig. 1), should be able to process local backflows to the Bottlers. There should be no physical barrier to transmitting (passing on) inflowing USALES datasets. It is assumed that both of these backflows would be processed on a more timely basis than traditional manual preparation would allow.

Increased timeliness is not the only by-product or incentive in such a system. Consistency checking may be equally important to firms further back in the chain. Shorter delivery turnaround, lower price, or other more product-oriented benefit, may be of greater importance to firms at the leading edge. Achieving either set of benefits requires both the cooperation of upstream partners. AND the capacity to utilize the raw data. In the absence of electronic information processing capability, no discussion is IS\* benefits may take place. The next section discusses the state of electronic information processing in the target industry.

## 5. The State of the Art

Computing systems meeting all necessary technical criteria are currently available to retail alcoholic beverage stores in New Jersey. At least three in-state firms can provide, at varying prices, cash register-type terminals with multifunction CRT I/O capability, secure cash drawers and industry specific software for applications. Among these packages are accounting, inventory control and sales reporting/summarization. In addition, all three can provide networking/communications capability. For the most part, these systems are menu-driven and allow integrated data entry and access from both the front (register) and back (accounting/management) offices.

Ease of use is always an important characteristic at the retail level. Neither clerks nor managers/owners would necessarily have any prior training in computer technology, be able to perform adaptive programming or recognize performance errors. Reliability is also, then, a primary feature. All of the systems available from New Jersey vendors exhibited these characteristics to an acceptable degree in on-site demonstration.

Although all of the systems available provide similar functionality, there are significant, sometimes innovative, differences. One vendor, for example, copies the computer-taped price list to video tape and permits its customers to input locally from a VCR. The timing of inventory updates also varies from continuous to daily. Regardless of internal, algorithmic differences, all of these systems are intended as stand-alone retail support. Any networking capability currently in use is in support of multiple registered at a single location.

Of the nearly 2,000 retail distribution licensees (as of June 1984) in New Jersey, more than 80% are also grocery outlets. Eliminating these from study because of their more diverse inventory management and ordering needs, there are up to 300 primarily alcoholic beverage sites within the state. However, slightly less than 50 systems had been installed as of July 1984. This does not represent a significant installed base when the potential cost of IS\* application development is considered. The greatest expected change this year is for a 100% increase in installations. This would still represent an installed base of less than a third of the possible locations. Moreover, while two of the hardware set-ups are compatible, the third is not.

Interviews with a broad spectrum of retailers, wholesalers and computer system vendors in the state were conducted. The respondents revealed their reasons, pro and con, for system installation where applicable and their view of IS\* development potential. One significant result is that no respondent, in any group, indicated an IS\* rationale in support of installing an internal computing system. All internal system building was justified on local (node level) efficiency criteria.

In all cases of system installation, retailers cited the benefit of computer assistance with inventory management and reporting in support of the product ordering task. Many alcoholic beverage retailers have a significant problem with overstocking and emotional buying behavior when dealing with the traditional order modality, i.e. a direct visit from the wholesalers representative. Those with installed systems, however, have been able to cut in-store inventory by 15–25%. It is a simple matter to retrieve the on-hand quantity for a specific item, while the salesperson is present, if necessary, to determine whether a purchase should be made. Many of the larger stores no longer buy from the wholesaler's representatives at all. Aside from decreasing the retailer's average investment in inventory, the systems have also assisted in cutting the average purchase order value. Since the price is fixed to all retailers during a given month, use of a computer-assisted ‘price search’ guarantees lowest-cost ordering.

Another major reason given for system installation is that larger, multi-register stores and multi-store operations with a single owner would otherwise have to rely on clerks to do price labeling. All of the available computer-based systems print shelf labels. Store owners claim that the error rate for clerk priced goods is high and has the potential to significantly effect net profits. By having items priced and/or discounts computed by the system at check-out time, these errors are eliminated. Moreover, greater control over the register operation provides an added measure of security for absentee owners.

In the majority of cases where a pre-sale presentation was made but the retailer declined to install, cost was cited as the major issue. System costs range from a single unit with application software at \$10,000 to nearly \$30,000 for multiple register systems. The vendors themselves use yearly grosses in the range of \$250,000–500,000 to justify installation. Grosses of this magnitude are rare in the small, single store operation where, unfortunately, the inventory/bying management assistance is most often needed. Both wholesalers and computer system vendors noted a significant, relatively recent, change in the number of larger, multi-store operations. The implication is that efficiency of size, manifested as the ability to provide discount pricing, is already changing the retail environment.

While the trend to larger, discount retail operations is increasing, smaller retailers have banded together to form buying cooperatives. At least one vendor has targeted the co-op market with an IS\*-like system. With this system, each individual store would have a relatively inexpensive (less than \$1,000) on-site terminal connectable via modem to a micro-computer at the co-op warehouse. The micro-computer would contain all functionality for lowest-cost purchasing inclusive of the quantity discount available to the co-op as a whole. None of the co-ops has yet to install the system, however, because no real assistance with the inventory or in-store pricing would be provided.

Of the four largest wholesalers in the state, only two showed interest in the potential for IS\* development. Although they all have internal computing systems and, in come cases, operate sophisticated delivery routing and inventory control software, none have expertise with networking technology. No electronic links between firms currently exist.

The wholesalers do, however, recognize the desire for better inventory management at the retail level. More importantly, they recognize that inflated or otherwise erroneous consumer behavior statistics filtering upward, in the form of sub-optimal order patterns, create uncertainties in their own buying decisions. Both of the interested wholesalers are currently negotiating with third parties (computing service/product vendors) on ways to make lower cost, industry specific systems available to their customers. This represents a significant change of attitude.

The few alcoholic beverage bottlers located within the state tend to be small in terms of both sales and product line. As a consequence, their data processing sophistication is low, tending toward no electronic assistance at all. This is in marked contrast to industry leaders who have computerized most facets (production and distribution scheduling, inventory, accounting, etc.) of their primary business. At least one nationally significant bottler is interested in “closing the loop” between itself and the retail seller of its products.

## 6. IS\* Loop Building Potential

The most crucial constraint on IS\* building within the New Jersey alcoholic beverage industry is apparently neither the technology nor the desire of the possible upstream participants. In fact, the retailers would be willing to use a network for ordering, if it were available at no additional cost (current installation) or minimal cost (as part of a new installation).

Retailer cooperation, however, even under the most sophisticated IS\* scenario is limited to their order data (USALES). They are not willing to supply DSALES under any circumstances, even if directly paid for the information. USALES, in the IS\* context, is demonstrably related to the common pricing database regardless of the independent purchasing decisions. DSALES, however, is unique to a given retail operation in all respects. Moreover, the optimal information content (common data items with standardized descriptors) of the DSALES dataset is inconsistent across the participants. Not all bottlers are using UPS labeling, for example. Additionally, while bottlers (and wholesalers) would like to know the selling price at retail, retailers are disinclined to provide such numbers because of discounting. Also, if retailers are holding back on orders in expectation of price decreases, they would not want bottlers or wholesalers to see brisk sales volume.

Even if the retailer could be persuaded to provide DSALES, perhaps in expectation of lower wholesalers costs, there would still be the need for standardization (a single purchase order format, for example) and timing procedures. Developing such standards would require the agreement of all potential participants. High levels of cooperation have not been historically evident, although national organizations such as the Distiller Spirits Council, National Beer Wholesalers Association and National Soft Drink Association provide available and willing forums. All of these organizations have been involved, in varying degrees, with UPC adoption and may cooperate in examining the potential of UCS (grocery system) adoption in their industry.

Wholesalers participation cannot be guaranteed under the closed loop scenario despite the potential for good standards and their existing infrastructure. The wholesaler, as the middle node, would be in a mediating position with respect to bottlers and retailers. On a purely informational basis, the mid-point of the closed loop seems strategically beneficial. However, if a uniform set of standards were in place, there would be some incentive for the large, multi-store retailer to order directly from bottlers. The degree of danger implicit in that incentive would depend, in part, on state regulations.

At the present time, it would appear that there is a low potential for the development of an IS\* (of the form shown in Fig. 1) in the examined environment. There is, and will continue to be for the foreseeable future, an insufficient electronic information processing infrastructure. There is no identifiable organization demonstrating a leadership role, and there is little incentive for the retail data capture site to formalize message interchanging relationships.

## 7. Conclusions

Despite the unique regulatory aspects of the alcoholic beverage industry, its environment, in terms of informational relationships and infrastructures, is quite typical. The distribution chain is similar for such very diverse products as meat and carpeting. Conceptually, IS\* analysis supercedes product, in the normal sense, to focus on the informational elements and groupings (size parameters, etc.) most common within and across specific industries. The closed loop scenario, therefore, may be assessed for applicability within any distribution chain. Additionally, the geographic specifications of this study were highly artificial. The only real system-oriented geographic factors are data communication and participant coordinate costs.

It is evident that the IS\* participation decision must be preceded by far more environmental analysis than any other information system building decision. There are business policy decisions, competitive postures and inter-organizational relationship factors that may inhibit or promote the development of an inter-organizational system. None of these can be assessed independently. Moreover, increasing the number of organizations, with different cost/benefit structures and participation goals, would tend to increase participation decision complexity far more than the already high technological complexity would indicate.

The potential richness of the IS\* environment lies in the vast number of possible configurations and resource distributions. Management teams charged with investigating the possibility of the closed loop scenario, or any other IS\* participation central to the firm's primary business practice, would require a wide range of skills. Specialists in data communication and data processing would not necessarily have all the required economic analysis skills. Similarly, corporate planners and strategists may have a poor understanding of the capabilities and constraints embodied in an IS\* infrastructure.
