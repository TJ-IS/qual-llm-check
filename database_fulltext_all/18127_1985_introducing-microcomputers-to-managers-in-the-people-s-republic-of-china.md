# Introducing Microcomputers to Managers in the People's Republic of China

Daniel L. Orne and William A. Wallace

School of Management, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, NY 12180-3590, USA; tel.: (518) 266-6405

Having set up the first microcomputer laboratory in China, the authors provided 178 Chinese professionals with coursework, lectures, and workshops designed to increase their understanding of the potential for using microcomputers in their organizations. This paper assesses the impact of those educational experiences on the Chinese students' perceptions of how they could apply the PC to their organizations, what uses they would make of a PC, what services a PC would require, and how soon they expected to be using (to adopt) a PC. Analysis of appropriate data takes into account such factors as differences in the content of the educational experiences and differences in the amount of time the students spent actually using the PCs. Analysis indicates that the educational experiences did significantly increase the Chinese students' perceptions of possible applications and uses of PCs in their organizations and provides evidence that similar educational experiences could be most effective in introducing microcomputer technology in countries such as China.

Keywords: Microcomputers, Microcomputer Laboratory, Microcomputer Education, Educational Experience, Personal Computers, China

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, the People's Republic of China has begun to make Western-style economic reforms. One example of these reforms is a “responsibility system” under which enterprises are given the opportunity to select their own products and find their own customers as long as they stay within production quotas and prices set by the state. Each individual enterprise can then keep a portion of its “profit” to improve its plant and equipment and to distribute bonuses.

Such changes are encouraging Chinese managers to look for ways to improve productivity, and one option they are exploring is the introduction of information technology such as microcomputers. We accepted an invitation in 1983 to establish the first microcomputer laboratory in China and to introduce a select group of Chinese – middle-level managers, bureaucrats, and educators – to the uses of microcomputers in management. We were part of a six-month, graduate-level management education program at the National Center for Industrial Science and Technology Management Development at the Dalian Institute of Technology (DIT), Dalian, China. The program can be described as a condensed Master of Business Administration (MBA) program. Its graduates are expected to return to their sponsoring organizations and lead others in implementing new technology and management methods.

A typical six-month program starts with a four-to-six-week review of basic mathematics and economics taught by Chinese instructors. Then the American faculty presents courses in production, accounting, and organizational behavior. The program concludes with elective courses, playing a management game, and lectures by senior American executives. The composition of the staff supporting the program is roughly 10–15 American faculty, five to eight executives from U.S. industry and government, and approximately 100 Chinese faculty, translators, and administrators.

Our role in the program came toward its end, when we offered an eight-week elective course and provided participants with training and experience in using the microcomputer lab. This paper examines the impact of the educational experiences we provided on the participants' perceptions of microcomputers and their potential role in the management of Chinese enterprises.

## 2. The Educational Experiences

Toward the end of their six-month program, the participants were able to choose one of two elective courses. The course we taught was titled “Management Information and Decision Support Systems (MI/DSS)”. The other elective was titled “Management of Technology.” We will refer to the students in that course as non-MI/DSS students.

Computer facilities available were a small CDC computer owned by the Center and the microcomputer lab we set up. By American standards, the lab was rather spartan. It consisted of 20 personal computers, loaned to the Center by International Business Machines (IBM), which were installed on benches in a classroom. It could hold a maximum of 60 students with three to a workstation.

The lab was open from five to eight hours a day for use by the students. The Center's CDC personnel, as well as Chinese faculty in information systems from DIT and other universities, acted as consultants. They attended our computer-related lectures and helped prepare programs for the CDC.

## 2.1. Coursework

The MI/DSS course covered human and organizational information processing needs, fundamentals of database management, and the management of information resources. Although our students did have a four-week course in mathematics and computing taught by Chinese instructors, they had not used the Center's CDC computer nor had they been instructed in management science techniques. Therefore, we lectured on forecasting, inventory models, the transportation method, and PERT.

In gauging the amount of material that could be covered, we had to allow for sequential translation into Chinese. We found that we required two hours to cover an amount equivalent to an hour's lecture in a typical U.S. classroom. Although we encouraged questions, our students' cultural norm was not to interrupt the lecturer. When they did have questions, they would wait to ask them during lecture breaks or during small, informal "bull sessions" held one or two evenings a week.

## 2.2. Computer Experience

We gave two computer-based lectures to both the MI/DSS and non-MI/DSS students. One lecture was on microcomputer technology, and the other was on PERT. We chose to lecture on PERT because it is a tool used in research and development (R&D) management, and we felt it would be of interest to everyone. All students had two micro-lab workshops that covered the basic operation of the micros; how to access, use, and exit from the PERT program; and how to access, use, and exit from Multiplan (a spreadsheet program).

Students in the MI/DSS course were asked to do a simple exercise in both forecasting and queueing on the CDC before using the microcomputer facility for exercises on PERT, inventory models (EOQ), and the transportation method. All students were asked to do a PERT exercise and to practice with Multiplan, either on their own or in small groups of two or three. The programs on both the CDC and the PC's were menu-driven and did not require any previous computer expertise. However, they were in English.

## 2.3. The Management Game

All students were involved in a management game experience. They were divided into 20 teams to play a planning-and-control-oriented game run on the CDC. Although a relatively simple game, it did require a fair amount of financial analysis. Forecasts, projections, and balance-sheet projections were needed in order to maintain an adequate cash flow.

Just prior to playing the game, each of the 20 teams was given the opportunity to send two representatives to a more in-depth workshop on Multiplan and a forecasting program. Our goal was that the representatives would be able to help their teams use the Multiplan and forecasting programs in playing the game. Although the workshop was well attended and well received, we have no evidence that any of the teams actually used either of the programs in playing the game.

## 2.4. Summary

In summary, the educational experiences were:
1. The MI/DSS students had lectures on forecasting and queueing, each followed by an exercise on the CDC. Both the lectures and the exercise were completed before the introduction of the microcomputers.

2. All students had lectures on microcomputer technology and a description of the PERT program. Then the students were divided into three groups and each group was given two 1.5-hour micro-lab workshops. The first workshop was on using the PC's and the PERT program; the second workshop was on using Multiplan.

3. The MI/DSS students were given two additional lectures on inventory and the transportation method and corresponding exercises using the PC's.

4. Two representatives from each of 20 teams attended a more in-depth workshop on Multiplan and a forecasting program. Most but not all of the representatives were MI/DSS students.

## 3. The Study Methodology

## 3.1. Collecting the Data

To assess the impact of the educational experiences we provided, we designed two questionnaires. They were translated into Chinese by one translator, and the responses were translated into English by a different translator.

The first questionnaire was administered to all students after the lectures on microcomputer technology and the PERT technique but before the first PC workshop. The second questionnaire was administered after the students had taken all the workshops but before they had completed the management game.

Of a total population of 178 students, we included in the sample the 71 students who completed only the first questionnaire and the 62 who completed both questionnaires. We did not include the four students who completed only the second questionnaire or the 47 who did not complete either questionnaire. However, we used data from the class roster to compare the sample group to the total population and could find no significant differences between them.

In addition, we asked the students to hand in the results of their work on all the computer exercises. Thus the MI/DSS students were asked to hand in five exercises (Forecasting, Queueing, PERT, Transportation, Inventory), and the non-MI/DSS students were asked to hand in one exercise (PERT). The Center's staff reviewed the exercises and recorded one of the following for each exercise: Not Handed In, Handed In But Not Correct, or Handed In and Correct.

We obtained data on how often each student used the microcomputer lab by asking them to sign in each time they entered the lab.

From the class roster and from questions on the first questionnaire, we also obtained data on each student's age, proficiency in English, prior experience with computers, organization size and type, and position in the organization.

## 3.2. Questions for Analysis

We planned to assess the value of the educational experience by analyzing the students' responses on four questions that appeared on both questionnaires. The questions dealt with perceived applications, uses, and service requirements of microcomputers and with when students felt they would be using a personal computer.

## 3.3. Classifications for Analysis

We established four groupings for analyzing the data, based on differences in educational experiences. They were:

1. MI/DSS versus non-MI/DSS. The MI/DSS students had more computer-oriented lectures and more exercises on the PC's. They also did exercises on the CDC. Students from both courses were brought together for the lectures and workshops on microcomputer technology and PERT. They all played the management game in small teams that were independent of the MI/DSS and non-MI/DSS classifications. It is important to note at the outset that the students self selected the course they would take. Therefore, the non-MI/DSS group does not represent a control group. Rather, it represents a roughly similar group that had fewer educational activities concerning computer technology.

2. MI/DSS Only. High Lab Usage versus Low Lab Usage. All participants within the MI/DSS group essentially had the same lectures and exercises. Thus, on one level they all had the same educational experience. However, they did differ on how many times they visited the microcomputer laboratory. In separating this group into high lab users (four or more times) and low lab users (0 to three times), we assumed that the difference in lab use created a difference in educational experience.

3. Non-MI/DSS Only. High Lab Usage versus Low Lab Usage. Our rationale for making this classification was similar to the foregoing. In this case, we defined high lab usage as two or more times and low lab usage as 0 or one time. These definitions reflect the fewer computer exercises this group was asked to do.

4. MI/DSS Only. High, Medium, and Low Exercise Performance. To make this classification, we looked at the results of the students' exercises on the CDC and on the microcomputers. We assumed that differences in exercise performance either created differences in the educational experience or were the result of differences in the educational experience. Each student received a total score for exercise performance based on individual evaluations for each of five exercises. The exercises were evaluated by the Center's computer staff and faculty. They gave no points if an exercise was not handed in, one point if an exercise was handed in but was not correct, and two points if an exercise was handed in and was done correctly. Thus, total scores on exercise performance could range from 0 to 10. We divided the students into the following three groups based on their total scores: High Exercise Performance (10, 9, 8), Medium (7, 6, 5) and Low (4 and under).

## 4. Assessing the Impact of the Educational Experiences

Using the groupings we had established, we compared student's responses to those questions designed to indicate their perceptions of what areas of application a personal computer would have for them, what uses they would make of a PC, what services a PC would require in their organization, and when they expected to be using (to adopt) a PC. We analyzed the responses on the first questionnaire, administered before most but not all of the educational experiences, and then compared them with the responses on the second questionnaire, given after the coursework, lectures, and PC workshops.

## 4.1. MI/DSS versus non-MI/DSS Educational Experiences

In this sample there were 60 MI/DSS students and 73 non-MI/DSS students. We found these groups to differ on the following:

1. Organization/Position Type. The MI/DSS group had a slightly higher percentage of educators and a slightly lower percentage of government managers. Neither difference should be considered statistically significant.

2. Knowledge of English. The MI/DSS group had slightly more knowledge of English. This may be partially a result of this group's higher percentage of educators, who tended to be more proficient in English.

3. Organizational Size and Growth Rate. The non-MI/DSS group seemed to come from significantly more stable organizations. This may be associated with the higher percentage of government managers in this group.

4. Production Process. The MI/DSS managers were slightly more likely to have production processes that were assembly lines or continuous systems and slightly less likely to have a job-shop environment.

No significant differences were noted on any other organizational or individual characteristics.

We expected that the MI/DSS students, who had self-selected a more intensive course in information technology, would perceive more applications for microcomputers initially and would show a greater effect from their educational experience. We found that the MI/DSS students did indicate a slightly greater percentage of applications in the first questionnaire (37% for the MI/DSS students versus 33% for the non-MI/DSS students). Both groups significantly increased their perceptions of applications between the first and second questionnaires. The MI/DSS group went from 37% to 46% and the non-MI/DSS group went from 33% to 39%. For the MI/DSS group, the greatest increase in applications was for production scheduling and project management. The non-MI/DSS group had the greatest increase in budgeting and project management.

In regard to perceptions of uses of the PC, again we expected that the MI/DSS students would perceive more uses initially and would show the greater effect from their educational experience. This was strongly supported by the data. The MI/DSS group indicated a significantly higher percentage of uses initially, and both groups significantly increased their perceptions of uses between the first and second questionnaires. The MI/DSS group went from 44% to 65%, and the non-MI/DSS group went from 36% to 49%.

With regard to service requirements, we presumed that the MI/DSS group was more knowledgeable to begin with and would show a greater effect from their educational experience. However, while the MI/DSS group did indicate a slightly greater percentage of perceived service requirements initially, neither group significantly changed their perceptions of service requirements between the first and second questionnaires.

We also anticipated that the MI/DSS group would expect to use a microcomputer sooner, since they seemed to have more interest in information systems. However, there were no significant differences between the two groups' perceptions initially, and neither group's expectations about adoption changed as a result of their educational experiences. We speculate that they may have little personal control over when such technology is adopted by their organizations.

## 4.2. Educational Experiences of the MI/DSS High-Lab-Usage versus Low-Lab-Usage Groups

There were 30 students in each of the MI/DSS lab usage groups. We found that these groups differed significantly on:

1. Age. The students in the high-lab-usage group were slightly older.

2. English Reading Proficiency. The students in the high-lab-usage group were more likely to indicate that they could read simple technical material in English.

3. Prior Computer Use. The students in the high-lab-usage group were more likely to indicate that they had used computers before participating in the program at the center.

We expected that the students who used the micro-lab more often would be more interested in computers and would gain more knowledge of them. Therefore, we expected their initial perceptions of applications, uses, and service requirements to be higher and we expected that they would be more likely to adopt PC's sooner. The actual results were quite different. Before the educational experiences, the high lab users perceived significantly fewer applications than did the low lab users. After the educational experiences, they still identified slightly fewer applications, although their percentage of change was greater. Both groups did increase their perceptions of applications between the first and second questionnaires.

Both groups significantly increased their perceptions of computer uses between the first and second questionnaires, from 44% to 65% for the high-lab-usage group and from 45% to 65% for the low-lab-usage group. Neither group, however, changed its perceptions of service requirements or its adoption expectations between the two questionnaires.

## 4.3. Educational Experiences of the Non-MI/DSS High-Lab-Usage versus Low-Lab-Usage Groups

There were 49 students in the non-MI/DSS high-lab-usage group and 24 students in the low-lab-usage group. We found these groups to differ on the following background characteristics:

1. Organization / Position Type. The high-lab-usage group was slightly more likely to be industrial managers and slightly less likely to be from educational organizations.

2. Access to Computers. The high-lab-usage group was slightly more likely to come from organizations that did not have a computer.

3. Organizational Size. The high-lab-usage group tended to come from slightly smaller organizations.

No significant differences were noted on any individual characteristics.

On both the first and second questionnaires, the high-lab-usage group identified more applications than did the low-lab-usage group, but neither group significantly changed the level of perceived applications between the two questionnaires.

Both groups increased the level of perceived uses between the two questionnaires, but the increase for the low-lab-usage group was not significant. Similarly, the high lab users identified more service requirements initially and increased their perceived level of service requirements after their educational experience; the low lab users did not. The educational experience appeared to have no effect on either group's perceptions of when they would begin to use microcomputers in their organizations.

## 4.4. Educational Experiences of the MI/DSS Exercise versus Performance Groups

The number of students in the high, medium, and low exercise performance groups was 25, 20, and 15, respectively. Because of the small size of the groups, differences in individual and organizational characteristics would have had to be especially large for them to be statistically significant. None were.

We expected that the students who had a higher exercise performance level would be more interested in computers and would gain more knowledge of computers from their educational experiences. As expected, on both questionnaires the high performance group indicated more applications than did the medium performance group, which in turn indicated more applications than did the low performance group. The high performance group went from 44% on the first questionnaire to 57% on the second questionnaire, the medium performance group went from 35% to 44%, and the low performance group went from 32% to 29%. Thus, the higher the performance, the greater the change.

That result did not hold true for the other questions, however. In perceptions of computer uses, for example, all three groups significantly increased their levels of perceived uses between the first and second questionnaires – demonstrating a significant educational effect – but there was no significant difference between the perceptions of the three groups on either the first or the second questionnaire. On the first questionnaire, their responses were 46%, 42%, and 45%; on the second questionnaire, their responses were 66%, 66%, and 62%.

Similarly, although all three groups increased their number of perceived service requirements between the two questionnaires, none of the changes were statistically significant. Contrary to our expectations, those in the high performance group did not indicate significantly more service requirements initially or show a greater change in their perceptions after their educational experience. None of the groups showed significant differences in their initial perceptions of when they would use microcomputers, and none of groups changed their perceptions after their educational experiences.

## 5. Conclusions and Discussion

From our data, we concluded that the educational activities we provided did increase our students' perceptions of possible applications and uses of the microcomputer in their enterprises. Increases in perceptions of uses were generally greater than were increases in perceptions of applications. As we had anticipated, the MI/DSS students, who had more computer-oriented lectures and computer exercises, in general made greater increases in their perceptions of microcomputer applications and uses as a result of their educational experiences than did the non-MI/DSS students. Contrary to our expectations, however, spending more time in the micro-lab or completing computer exercises accurately did not uniformly result in greater increases in perceptions about the micros.

Our educational activities appeared to have little or no significant impact on any of the groups in terms of increasing their perceptions of service requirements or of when they would adopt microcomputer technology in their own organizations. We believe that the present lack of any delivery system for computer technology in China contributed to this result. We also believe that state involvement with enterprises in China significantly alters the amount of control individual managers have in making decisions about when their organizations will adopt microcomputers. Thus, their perceptions are not necessarily governed by their own desire to adopt the technology.

In fact, our students were highly interested in and exited by the PC's, from the day they arrived until our final session. The Chinese faculty and computer personnel who worked with use also were highly interested, and they grasped the technical information very readily. However, they lacked an appreciation of the managerial decisions and tasks where microcomputers can be of help. It is in that area that we believe educational experiences such as the ones we provided can be most effective in introducing microcomputer technology in countries such as China.
