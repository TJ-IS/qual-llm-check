# Impact of Design Methods on Decision Support Systems Success: An Empirical Assessment

Mo A. Mahmood and Jeanette N. Medewitz
School of Business Administration, University of Missouri-St.
Louis, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA; tel.: (314)553-6268 or 6291

Many of the Decision Support Systems (DSS) developed and implemented in the last decade or so, have not been used or are only used for mundane report generation rather than for direct support of strategic planning. These failures can be attributed to the fact that existing DSS design methods (representation-based, evolutive, and adaptive) have never been evaluated with respect to their success. Thus, the designer had no way of knowing what design method to employ in developing an effective DSS.

This paper investigated the relationship between the selection of a DSS design method and its ultimate success. DSS success was measured through DSS usage, user satisfaction, and user attitudes and perceptions criteria. The data utilized was collected from individuals responsible for designing and evaluating DSS, specifically the managers, intermediaries, and designers. In terms of the DSS usage and user satisfaction criteria, all three groups rated the evolutive design method superior, followed by the representation-based and adaptive methods. Using the DSS attitudes and perceptions criteria, the designer group rated the evolutive method best, followed by the representation-based, and adaptive methods. The ratings by the managers and intermediaries were rather mixed. Each design method did better in some success criteria than in others.

Keywords: DSS Design Methodologies, DSS Success Criteria, Successful DSS Implementations.

## 2. Background

The literature on DSS design is not very extensive. A literature search disclosed only three existing DSS design methods: (1) Carlson's [8,42] representation-based, (2) Courbon's [9,10] evolutive, and (3) Keen's [25] adaptive.

## 2.1. Representation-Based Design Method

Carlson [8, p. 4] proposed the representation-based design method to “help reduce the differences between the requirements of decision making and decision makers, and the capabilities of the DSS.” Thereafter, Sprague and Carlson [42] refined the method and put forth specific examples of its applications. The method is especially suited for the environment in which a decision maker can not specify or can only inadequately specify the requirements of a decision in advance. The representation-based design method consists of four components: representations, operations, memory aids, and control aids.

Representations provide the circumstances surrounding a decision situation, and parameters that can be used to translate DSS outputs and operations. Representations are supplied in terms of Simon's [40] well-known intelligence, design, and choice decision activities that can be used as a vehicle to conceptualize and communicate the decision situation.

Operations provide an opportunity to analyze and manipulate the representations associated with the intelligence, design, and choice activities. Conversely, these activities can also be used to identify operations for a DSS. An operation may represent complex decision aids such as forecasting, simulation, and sensitivity analysis, and may be used in more than one decision activity.

Memory aids help a decision maker employ representations and operations on representations. Memory aids may include a database, workspaces, triggers, and profiles, among others $[8,42]$ . A database reminds the decision maker that relevant data pertaining to a decision may be available. Workspaces, short-term memory aids, allow storing of representations and operations on representations. Triggers prompt the decision maker that certain operations may have to be executed. Profiles store different default options that are available in manipulating the DSS. Such options may be general or user-specific.

Control aids allow a decision maker, based on his or her knowledge and experience, to formalize a decision-making process using representations, operations, and memory aids. Control aids may include: (a) user-DSS interaction facilities, (b) user manuals and user training facilities, (c) procedure construction facilities to combine multiple operations on one or more representations and to add new operations, and (d) editing facilities to change the results of operations and any DSS default functions.

## 2.2. Evolutive Design Method

Courbon et al. [10] suggested the evolutive approach as “a methodology based on progressive design of a DSS, going through multiple as-short-as possible cycles, in which successive versions of the system under consideration are utilized by the end-user” [10, p. 2]. Thereafter, Courbon and Bourgeois [9] put forth the concept of the information system designer as a “nurturing agent” rather than a “change agent” in designing a DSS. Ness [35] also contributed toward this method by introducing the concept of “cyclical process.” The evolutive method is based on four steps: (a) identify an important subproblem; (b) quickly develop a small but usable system to assist the decision maker; (c) refine, expand, and modify the system; and (d) evaluate the system constantly.

The selected subproblem should be of high interest to the decision maker, even though the interest may be of a temporary nature. The subproblem should also have the demonstrative need for computer-based support. The objective is to provide an immediate relief to the problem, thereby establishing the decision maker's confidence in the system and setting up a good working relationship between the designer and the decision maker.

The objective of the second step is to provide the decision maker a system that he or she can employ quickly. This does not imply that one disregards the system development stages (analysis - design - implementation - utilization - evaluation), rather the emphasis is on minimizing the time required to go through these stages. Because of the pressing need, the developed DSS may not have elaborate capabilities. Nevertheless, the emphasis should be on urgency, since that would allow the decision maker to still remember his or her initial involvement in the design process and thus help relate to the DSS.

In the third step, the initially developed system should be revised and enhanced using short repetitive cycles, passing through all system development stages. This allows the designer to take advantage of the full extent of the decision maker's needs, which may not be well-developed before the utilization phase. The process allows the designer and the decision maker to share the responsibility of designing and enhancing the DSS. The decision maker plays a greater role in the utilization and evaluation stages, whereas the designer plays a greater role in the design and implementation stages.

In the final step, the system is evaluated constantly, at the end of each cycle, to ensure it satisfies the decision maker's requirements. This step also acts as a control mechanism for the evolutive method. If the DSS does not satisfy the decision maker's requirements, renewed efforts are made to improve the system. If improvement is not feasible, the system can be scratched without putting forth too much effort. Thus, this constant evaluation technique ensures that the cost in developing the system remains in proportion to its worth.

## 2.3. Adaptive Design Approach

Keen [25] introduced the adaptive design method. Many articles on DSS design have discussed this approach. Ness [35] calls it a middle-out approach which involves a “cyclical process of generalizing (bottom up) and specifying (top-down) at each stage of the problem solving process.” Bennett [6] put forth examples of the method. Sprague and Carlson [42] provide summaries.

The adaptive method, like the evolution method, combines the five system development stages. The entire design process passes through the various stages rapidly and repetitively to arrive at a satisfactory DSS. The key role players include the user (decision maker), the builder (designer), and the system itself. If the user can not directly participate, then an intermediary works as a go-between between the user and the designer. The design framework is described in terms of the system-user, the builder-user, and the builder-system “adaptive links” based on interactions among the key role players.

The system-user link emphasizes managerial learning either directly or indirectly through an intermediary. It is argued that through the utilization of the DSS capabilities, the decision maker comprehends the decision situation better, and therefore can be more specific with regard to what he or she needs from the system. In turn, the system “learns” more about the decision maker’s requirements, and thus can be more effective in assisting him or her. This exchange significantly improves the chance of satisfactorily resolving the problem.

The builder-user link stresses the fact that the designer should be responsive to the decision maker's changing needs. This is only possible through effective communication between the designer and the decision maker. Through this communication, the designer learns about the decision maker's needs. It is imperative that the decision maker, not the designer, leads the design process.

The builder-system link emphasizes that the designer be able to add new functions and capabilities to the DSS to satisfy the decision maker's changing requirements. The dynamic nature of managerial decision making, coupled with the decision maker's and designer's fast comprehension of the problem within the DSS environment, places significant demand on the designer's ability to modify and expand the system rapidly. This is only possible if a flexible system architecture exists which allows for fast change and expansion of the DSS.

## 3. Research Methodology

In order to examine the impact of DSS design methods on DSS success and after reviewing relevant literature on CBIS success, a questionnaire was constructed. It consisted of four sections. The first three contained criteria relevant to DSS usage, user satisfaction, and user attitudes and perceptions [Appendix]. The fourth included criteria used to gather background information about the subjects. Some of the criteria were written as positive statements, others as converse statements to minimize response biases (e.g., agreeing with each statement).

The level of a subject's attitude relative to any criteria was measured on a seven interval Likert-type scale, ranging from negative to positive, denoted by the adverbial qualifiers: disagree strongly, disagree, disagree somewhat, undecided, agree somewhat, agree, and agree strongly. Positive statements yielded high response scores in their raw form X (e.g., strongly agree = 7), while converse statements produced a score which was inverted by 8-X (e.g., strongly agree = 8-7 = 1).

## 3.1. Research Variables

The independent variable was the method used to design the DSS (representation-based, evolutive, or adaptive). Three categories of dependent variables were investigated: (a) DSS usage, (b) user satisfaction, and (c) user attitudes and perceptions.

DSS usage was measured in terms of extent of DSS usage, ease of usage, usefulness of DSS output reports, helpfulness of DSS as a learning facility, ability of DSS in extending range of the decision making process, improving efficiency of the decision making process, producing better types of output reports, and estimating different decision making parameters.

DSS user satisfaction was measured in terms of quality of derived solutions, helpfulness in increasing the confidence in decisions, user information satisfaction, accuracy of DSS reports, user favorable impression of the DSS, DSS ability in evaluating decision making alternatives, increasing communication between involved parties, and enhancing overall quality of decisions.

DSS user attitudes and perceptions were measured in terms of DSS ability to answer critical questions, involve users in the design process, contribute toward change in user specifications, decrease conflict among involved parties, increase user understanding of the DSS, enhance user influence in the design process, reinforce the level of user commitment, and help control and manage the design process.

## 3.2. Subjects

The subjects for the study were drawn from the evening graduate student population of a Midwestern university school of business administration. A total of forty-eight subjects (24 Master of Business Administration (MBA) and 24 Management Information Systems (MIS) students) from two graduate courses participated in the project. On average, the MBA students had 1.3 years of experience while the MIS students had 3 years of experience in designing and developing CBIS.

The selection of the subject population was considered appropriate for a number of reasons. First, researchers such as Lusk and Kersnick [31], Smith [41], and Remus [37] used undergraduate students, while Lucas and Neilson [30], and Benbasat and Schroeder [5] utilized graduate students. Second, a field study of this sort would be extremely difficult, since scarcely enough DSS are presently in operation [21,45]. Furthermore, it was felt that the training and experience of graduate MBA and MIS students provided them with the skill necessary to develop and evaluate DSS while gaining valuable experience. Also, the subjects were motivated, since the experiment was assigned as a formal course project.

## 3.3. Experimental Procedures

Sixteen groups were formed, each consisting of at least one MBA student and one MIS student.

The third member of the group was either an MBA or MIS student, depending on their availability. The MBA student played the role of a manager, the MIS student posed as a designer, and the third member acted as an intermediary. Each group was then randomly assigned a DSS design method.

Over a period of two weeks, all subjects were given a complete overview of a DSS and its components, followed by an overall explanation of the three design methods. Relevant literature describing the various phases of the different DSS design methods was then distributed and discussed with each respective group (i.e., representation-based materials were given to the representation-based groups). Through homework assignments, it was assured that each group had a good understanding of their assigned DSS design method.

Next, an overview of the Interactive Financial Planning System (IFPS) and its usage was presented to all groups, since IFPS is considered one of the best DSS generators. Over a one-week period, documentation papers demonstrating specific examples of IFPS usage were distributed. Through homework assignments, it was assured that all members had a working knowledge and specifically, the designers possessed a good understanding of the IFPS capabilities.

Each group was then assigned a case. The managers were told that the purpose of the project was to provide them with an opportunity to make strategic planning decisions in a specific case and to discover how a DSS works to support their decision making process. The designers were advised that the project objective was to involve them in DSS development under a simulated real-world environment. The intermediaries were informed that the project purpose was to expose them to the world of decision making and DSS design.

## 3.4. Post-experimental Procedures

After the designing and decision making process were completed, each group was required to submit a paper detailing their experience. The managers were to document all decisions, the basis for those decisions, and any problem they may have encountered in the decision making process; also, they were to elaborate on the help and cooperation they received from the other two members.

The designers were to list all steps and work undertaken in designing the system, and to express their satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the others' participation, including any communication problems. The intermediaries were to document all supports provided to the designers and managers, and to indicate any problems encountered in the communication process.

After the project was completed, post-experimental questionnaires were administered to determine the impact of design methods on DSS success, especially in the following areas:

a. manager and intermediary subjects use of the DSS in analyzing the case and arriving at appropriate decisions.

b. manager, designer, and intermediary subjects evaluation and satisfaction with the systems designed.

c. manager, designer, and intermediary subjects attitudes and perceptions of different DSS design methods.

## 4. Analyses

Respondents were asked to rate their agreement or disagreement with statements describing different criteria of DSS success. A seven interval Likert-type scale was used to record the nature of agreement or disagreement.

## 4.2. Kruskal-Wallis Test of Respondent Ratings

In the next step of the analyses, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to investigate whether there is any significant difference between the opinions of the managers, designers, and intermediaries in terms of DSS usage, user satisfaction, and user attitudes and perceptions criteria. The Kruskal-Wallis test, the nonparametric alternative to the parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA), was especially used since (a) it does not require the assumption of normality and homogeneity of variance, (b) the sample size was relatively small, and (c) the nature of data collected was ordinal.

Results are presented in Table 3. For the DSS usage criteria group (part A), only one of the eight variables (extent of DSS usage) was statistically significant (0.003 level). This means that the managers, designers, and intermediaries differed in their opinions on the extent of DSS usage. It is interesting to note that the designers rated the extent of DSS usage highest. This might be expected since they were partial to the system they designed. On the other hand, it is not at all obvious why the managers rated the same criterion far higher than the intermediaries. It seems likely that both would have similar opinions since they were directly or indirectly involved in the usage of the DSS.

The managers and designers rated ease of DSS usage, provides useful output reports, extends range of the decision making process, and improves efficiency of the decision making process, much higher than the intermediaries. On the other hand, the managers and intermediaries rated the criterion – DSS facilities learning – higher than the designers, while the designers and intermediaries rated the criterion – produces better types of output reports – higher than the managers. Each group rated similarly the capabilities of the DSS as a tool for estimating different decision making parameters. Overall, the managers and designers rated most of the DSS usage criteria higher than the intermediaries.

## 4.3. Respondent Ratings of DSS User Attitudes and Perceptions Criteria

Since the managers, designers, and intermediaries differed considerably in their opinions on attitudes and perceptions criteria, it was decided to investigate these further. Table 4 shows the means and within group ranks of DSS user perceptions and attitudes criteria by different groups.

All groups agreed on DSS being able to answer critical questions, increase user understanding of the DSS, and facilitate ease of control and management of the design process. Whereas, the within group rankings of the criterion – contribute towards change in user specifications – were similar, the intermediaries agreed with the idea that the DSS contributes towards the change, while the managers and designers were indifferent.

Interestingly, even though within group rankings were different, all groups agreed that the DSS involves users in the design process, decreases conflict among involved parties, and increases user influence in the design process. In addition, the managers and intermediaries were in close agreement on the criterion - the DSS increase level of user commitment, whereas the designers were close to being indifferent.

## 4.4. Respondent Ratings of Design Methods

Since no significant difference in group opinions were found on DSS usage, and user satisfaction criteria, they were pooled to investigate the difference between the three design methods (e.g., representation-based, adaptive, and evolutive). The Kruskal-Wallis test, once again, was used for the analysis (Table 5).

For DSS usage criteria, the respondents differed significantly (0.007 level) on only one variable. Specifically, the respondents had more confidence in DSS ability to estimate different decision making parameters when the DSS was designed by the evolutive method. This was followed closely by the representation-based, with the adaptive a distant third.

For the rest of the variables, the evolutive method consistently outscored the representation-based and the adaptive methods (5 out of 7 cases, with 1 tie). The representation-based method, on the other hand, consistently scored higher than the adaptive method (6 out of 7 cases). The adaptive method fared better only when it came to providing the DSS as a learning facility.

For DSS user satisfaction criteria, the respondents differed in opinions (0.009 level) with respect to favorable impression of the DSS. The DSS designed by the evolutive method was preferred, followed by the representation-based and adaptive methods.

## 5. Discussion

Interpretations of the research findings have to be qualified on two counts. First, the sample size was relatively small. Second, the alternative design methods were compared in a less than realistic setting.

The results suggest that the success of a DSS can be related to the selection of a design method. Overall, the findings favored the evolutive method over the other two. In terms of DSS usage and user satisfaction criteria, the respondents consistently rated the evolutive method as first, with the representation-based method second, and the adaptive method third.

In terms of DSS attitudes and perceptions criteria, the designers rated the evolutive method as the best, followed by the representation-based and adaptive methods. The ratings by the managers and intermediaries were mixed. Each design method fared better in some success criteria than in others.

Some of the research findings were unexpected (e.g., respondents rated DSS usage criteria higher than user satisfaction, and user attitudes and perceptions criteria). Normally, one would expect respondents to rate user satisfaction criteria higher than DSS usage, and user attitudes and perceptions criteria, because of respondents' direct involvement in the design process. The importance of such involvement in CBIS design has consistently been observed to be positively associated with user satisfaction with a CBIS [11,14,24,28,32,43].

It is interesting to note that no significant difference was found between the ratings of the managers, designers, and intermediaries in most of the DSS usage and user satisfaction criteria. Normally, one would expect some sort of difference between the individual group opinions because of their distinct identities.

The poor ratings of DSS success criteria by the intermediaries vis-à-vis the managers and designers were not expected. Generally, one would anticipate similar ratings by the managers and intermediaries since they were influenced by each other, and also since they used the same DSS in the decision making process. Contrary to normal expectation, the managers and designers consistently rated DSS success criteria higher than the intermediaries. Since the designers were directly involved in the design process, it can be argued that they were biased and thus rated the success criteria higher than the intermediaries. However, it is not obvious why the managers would rate the success criteria higher than the intermediaries. Our conjecture is that, since the intermediaries were not directly involved in the design and decision making process, their level of satisfaction was lower than the other two groups.

Last but not least, the dismal performance of the adaptive method against the evolutive and representation-based methods was not expected. One would normally expect a better performance by the adaptive design method, since it has been accepted and used by numerous MIS practitioners and researchers $[1,20,25,33]$ . Our inference is that, since the evolutive method is based on the prototype approach, it outperformed the non-prototype oriented representation-based method. Although this is clearly in tune with findings by other researchers $[2,7,12,13,19,34,46]$ , it still does not explain why the representation-based method fared better than the adaptive method, which is also based on the prototype approach.

## 6. Conclusions and Implications

The present research related DSS design methods to DSS success as measured by DSS usage, user satisfaction, and user attitudes and perceptions criteria. The results should help alleviate some of the problems faced by the DSS managers, designers, and intermediaries in developing and evaluating DSS. Specifically, the research should aid in the selection of a DSS design method, given a set of DSS success criteria.

Even though the research made good progress toward ensuring DSS success, further research is needed. The following are suggested:

1. Perform reliability and validity tests on the DSS success criteria to ensure they are accurate measures.

2. Perform the research under a field setting, if possible.

3. Increase the sample size, and reevaluate the research using some parametric tests (e.g., analysis of variance, factor analysis, discriminant analysis).
