# Information Systems Research Centers: An Initial Survey

Donald L. Amoroso, Houston H. Carr,

Paul H. Cheney and Robert Mann

Department of Management, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

The concept of the Information Systems Research Center (ISRC) is not new, yet its purpose, structure and activities are not universally agreed upon. This paper compares ten centers in the U.S.A. as to their objectives, organizational structure, curriculum, current research areas, and activities. The ISRCs are then examined in an attempt to assess benefits and problems.

Keywords: MIS University Based Research Centers, MIS Curriculums and Research Activities.

The volatility of the information systems field mandates changes in the technology profile of corporate structures and the management of the information resource. There are growing needs to establish and maintain lines of communication among the academic, private, and public sectors. The academic community needs ties to the industrial world to understand this change and to become part of the change process via their information systems research activity. Thus, there is little doubt as to the need for academic research in the information systems field. These industrial linkages will benefit both academics and practitioners alike (e.g., sharing solutions to common problems, creating more relevant information systems curricula, etc.).

The Information Systems Research Center (ISRC) is a relatively new phenomenon within Colleges of Business Administration. In order to define and profile existing university-oriented ISRCs information was collected from several primary sources including ISRC annual reports, ISRC working papers, letters, and telephone conversations with ISRC directors. Ten U.S. ISRCs have been identified in Table 1 which meet the following criteria:

\- center is located and administrated by the university's College or School of Business Administration

● academic research interests of the center concentrate on business and public enterprise applications

\- center facilitates formal links between the practitioner community and university faculty, students, and staff

● center has a multi-sponsor, multi-project scope.

## 2. The Environmental Context

Before examining the composition of the Information Systems Research Center, the ISRC should be observed within its environmental context, which includes the interrelationships between (1) the university environment, (2) the corporate environment, (3) the public sector, and (4) the ISRC. Fig. 1 depicts these relationships.

The university and the organizations depicted in Fig. 1 have a mutual need for research interaction if solutions to information systems problems are to be found. Support to carry out research projects may be limited to scarce university funds, a Federal grant, or a single corporation's allocation. The result is often a single person's thoughts, a single setting, applied to a single problem, and supported by a single sponsor [5]. Thus the “interaction” relationship between the university researcher and industry may well be weak or non-existent. Contacts are often made on a piece-meal basis by the researcher at professional meetings, through consulting, or simply by chance. In addition, the organizations involved are not necessarily the most appropriate; this could create a suboptimal research environment. The need for an ISRC is clear. In its simplest form, it provides support for the university researcher and at the same time supports activities intended for organizations. The case for ISRC's is made even stronger given the dynamic nature of the information systems field.

Dickson and Rockart describe two different types of ISRCs based on the center's focus: (1) multi-sponsor, multi-project centers with either a national or regional emphasis, and (2) dedicated centers which focus upon specific research problems and may have a single or multiple sponsors.

## 3. Objectives

After an examination of annual reports and statements of purpose, eight objectives for ISRCs were identified. (See Table 2.) All of the centers had as an objective, to perform MIS research in organizations. An ISRC's objectives determine its scope as either national or regional and shape the relationships between the center, the university and organizational sponsors. These relationships thus determine the nature of ISRC research and activities.

## 4. Curriculum

The university curriculum is also linked to the goals and objectives of the ISRC. The emphasis in university M.B.A. programs with an MIS concentration is to prepare graduates for IS positions such as systems analyst, project leader, or MIS manager. The objective of MIS Ph.D. programs is to produce educators and researchers in the field. Class projects can, in some cases, be tailored to the research interests of the ISRC. Theses and dissertation topics can be developed through interaction with corporate sponsors. A comparison of available curricula at schools having ISRC's is presented in Table 3.

## 5. ISRC Organization Structure

The ISRC is located within the College or School of Business Administration; thus there are four distinct groups of participants: faculty, students, sponsors, and research center staff. Faculty members are the most active participants; several centers stated that MIS faculty devoted half their time to ISRC research. Five centers noted that non-MIS faculty were also actively involved in ISRC research projects.

In addition, there is significant student involvement. Graduate students, under the direction of faculty, are often involved in the data collection and analysis phases of projects. Class projects involve undergraduate and graduate students, who interact with organizational personnel through the ISRC.

![](/api/attachments/NJRQKBVZ/fulltext/images/93d55811a836a6d50373b13998abc6d1aa15d30549fbe13d01a1c3fb50a0cfb3.jpg)  
Fig. 2. UCLA ISRC Organizational Chart (from [12]).

Sponsors (or associates as they are sometimes called) include a limited number of organizations that agree with the center's objectives, goals, and philosophy. Sponsors help fund the ISRC and provide assistance in defining and investigating specific research issues. Centers seek a fixed annual contribution from as little as \$5000 [9,11] to as much as \$500000 [1]. Often ISRCs will stipulate requirements from sponsors including: (1) appoint a representative to the ISRC board, (2) serve as a site for ongoing research, (3) suggest pilot project, (4) send employees to take part in research projects, (5) meet with graduate students during career nights, and (6) provide financial support (scholarships, assistantships, and research stipends) [4,5,9,12].

Center personnel normally include a director, research associates, IS professionals, and an administrative staff. The director is usually a senior faculty member who devotes at least half-time to the center [4,5,7,9]. The assistant director is often employed as a corporate liaison or research supervisor. (Larger centers, such as MIT and UCLA, have an associate director.) Research associates investigate specific research topics, such as: decision support systems, database issues, and expert systems. IS professionals on leave from their organization may also devote time to a research project of mutual interest to the ISRC and the sponsor organization. The administrative staff is responsible for secretarial support, accounting, office administration, and conference administration. A typical organizational structure is shown in Fig. 2.

## 6. ISRC Research

Associate organizations suggest areas where research is needed. Two factors have a major impact on the type of research: faculty interest and availability of funding. Projects involving graduate students are directly associated with the MIS education program [5]. Research work may depend on the objectives and size of the ISRC; MIT for example is very diverse, focusing on a wide variety of topics, due mainly to its size and national scope. The University of Minnesota's MISRC had as a primary 1983 goal the generation of research in all strategic areas of information systems. A comparison of ISRC topics is presented in Table 4.

## 8. ISRC Benefits

The benefits of the ISRC must be tangible and visible; sponsors must see how ISRC activities fulfill their organizational needs, while university researchers need research ideas, financial support, and cooperation. Five ISRC benefits were identified.

1. Concentration of resources. This allows for highly visible, large scale projects. Resources include financial support for faculty, students, administrative personnel, research sites, research subjects, and corporate staff. This is known as the “critical mass” phenomenon [5].

2. University connection to the “real world”. Much of the research has an applied focus. Many of the research ideas come from the sponsors. The sponsors can also call on a research team to work on a specific problem of current interest and importance to their organization [7].

3. Affect the university's educational program. Sponsors make suggestions regarding needed IS skills. Actual case studies are used in the classroom, and students often gather data at organizational sites as part of research efforts [5,7].

4. The IS area benefits from exploration and investigation. The research performed is current, vital, and important to the field at large and increases the reputation of the ISRC, making it easier to fund activities and research projects [5,6,7].

5. ISRC can support other adjunct activities. These can include funding visiting faculty, hosting conferences, and printing relevant publications (e.g. MIS Quarterly [9]). The support of new activities often requires start-up capital and therefore can be risky.

## 9.ISRC Problems

A general set of four operating problems was identified, with some controls or solutions. These problems depend on: (1) the type of research conducted, (2) faculty commitment requirements, (3) resource requirements, and (4) the level of funding.

ISRC Problem number 1. The research projects are often a result of the sponsor's needs and available funds. This may mean that the feelings of the ISRC researcher are not considered. Even worse, the sponsored projects may not coincide with the objectives of the ISRC.

Control for number 1. A matching of ISRC, faculty researchers, and organizational needs must take place. Unless survival is in question, this requires saying “No” to some “opportunities”.

ISRC Problem number 2. The ISRC requires a significant time commitment of one or more faculty members. The creation of a successful and stable ISRC will take three to five years, as academics are heavily involved with other responsibilities (their teaching, research, and service) and they have limited time for administration and other ISRC duties.

Control for number 2. A balanced program of reduced teaching loads, and other incentives has been found to be necessary in order to persuade faculty to take on sponsor-related research projects [6].

ISRC Problem number 3. The operation of an ISRC is extremely resource intensive. It involves coordinating faculty, prioritizing funded projects, determining the research focus, organizing seminars, and maintaining a liaison with sponsors.

Control for number 3. It is important to recognize that the creation and operation of an ISRC will require substantial resources in terms of money and time if it is to succeed. University administrators, the MIS faculty, and corporate sponsors must make these commitments if an ISRC is to succeed.

ISRC Problem number 4. ISRC funding must be maintained. This involves base level and short-term funds. For example, the University of Minnesota's MISRC expressed concern regarding the "peaks and valleys" in the demand for projects. This tended to produce an uneven cash flow and, in turn, affected the center's planning and management [5,9].

Control for number 4. The base level of funding depends on the services or activities provided by the ISRC to the corporation sponsors. Dickson and Rockart recommend that the ISRC provide a fixed package of outputs rather than making individual promises [5]. The ISRC projects must also produce results that are generalizable. Therefore, attention must be directed toward the appropriate types and levels of activity, based upon the demand for services, and the need to support generalizable research projects.

## 10. Conclusions

The number of ISRCs in the United States appears small in comparison with the number of recognized MIS-teaching institutions. This paper has described the existing centers and presented problems that explain, to some extent, the limited quantity. While the requirement for significant resources does seem to explain the hesitation to create these centers, it is probably the time and efforts required of the participants that is the more limiting factor. It has been estimated that a center requires the concentrated effort of a senior faculty member for more than a year to just establish the ISRC [5]. This infers that such an academic must revert to the status of a champion to effect the establishment of the ISRC. It goes without saying that additional significant efforts are also required to insure its continued existence. However, when this task is undertaken and carried out with vigor, the results have significant positive consequences. MIT's CISR and the University of Minnesota's MISRC are well established and highly recognized as centers of excellence.

For institutions viewing the creation of an ISRC with interest, Fig. 3 lists seven steps that must be addressed. This figure shows that there are considerations in addition to the center's budget, i.e., the research plan and a set of deliverables. The view should be taken that the ISRC and the other elements identified in Fig. 1 are a system. With this in mind, the outcome has a higher chance of success as each of the components of a system are considered (i.e., elements, interactions, goals, environment, etc.).
