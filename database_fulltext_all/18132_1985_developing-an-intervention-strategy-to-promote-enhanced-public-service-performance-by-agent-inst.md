# Developing an Intervention Strategy to Promote Enhanced Public Service Performance by Agent Institutions

Martin S. Putterill

Urban Research Unit, The University of Auckland, Private Bag, Auckland, New Zealand

Value for money-performance in the public sector has been under close scrutiny in many countries. Natural consequences of this pressure have been attempts to strengthen the managerial focus of information systems and to introduce new information processing technology. Rapidly changing social, technological and economic environments make it difficult for any single public institution to work out which way to go, while actually implementing changes often calls for more human resources than are available within that organisation.

This paper concentrates on those situations in which autonomous agencies act on behalf of a central authority in the delivery of a specific public service. Examples from many countries of this form of delegated authority occur in housing, education, roading and social welfare. Because in the final analysis, the responsibility for the service given and resources used rests at the centre, it is appropriate that the central body should play a part in promoting enhanced performance by its agents.

This review examines the ways a central authority might most effectively intervene in bringing about changes to the administrative, technical or information systems of its agents in relation to a delegated service activity. Extensive field work focussing especially on pavement maintenance management by territorial local governments in New Zealand, forms the background to the discussion and gives substance to the conclusions and recommendations.

Keywords: Performance Enhancement, Public Policy, Management Information Systems, Pavement Maintenance Management, Local Government, New Zealand.

## 1. Introduction

At first sight, the idea of a central authority promoting change among its subordinate organisations seems unexceptional. This is after all what government is about, the familiar legislative process through which new objectives are defined and requirements prescribed. This approach is relatively effective in getting institutions into line in the first place but does not seem quite as good in creating a climate in which the search for excellence and enhanced performance is ongoing. Given the unknowable nature of future events, it is understandable that legislators should avoid defining performance expectations too precisely, relying on administrators to find ways within a framework of law, to achieve the best value for money under prevailing conditions.

Once again, however, it is easier to write down in an agency agreement a set of 'do's and dont'ts' than to give expression to the hope that the agent institution will always be striving to do a better job or deliver a service more cost effectively.

In addition to any difficulty which dealing with one autonomous unit may present, the kind of situation commonly experienced by administrators involves many agent institutions, a compounding of the problem. Sometimes hundreds of agencies of different sizes with widely diverse political, economic and social characteristics are used to provide a public service on a regional or national scale.

The central authority which recognises the diversity of its agents, respects their autonomy and wishes to promote a positive climate of enhancement, needs to give careful thought to arrangements between the parties particularly the balance between incentives and regulations.

Prominent in most arrangements between central authority and local agencies is funding, often on a significant scale, never as generous as the agency feels it should be, but always sufficient to give the provider a keen interest in the prevailing quality of the recipient's financial management. The scale of cash transfers is not the only reason for the central government institution's concern about management performance.

Services in the categories mentioned above have high social and economic impact apart from any special political importance in the platform of a government of the day. For each of these reasons any central authority wishing to initiate an upgrading process, would be well advised to investigate in depth before setting down the terms of any changes in administrative or managerial activity.

After all, as most realists will quickly point out, unless this package of arrangements is interpreted as being genuinely beneficial to the agent, it will be greeted at best by adherence to the new form but with a singular absence of new energy and extra endeavour. The reasons are not hard to understand. On the one hand as most public bodies have to cope with a wide range of administrative responsibilities which compete for scarce resources, new requirements are resisted as a matter of course. On the other hand, enhancement strategies generally include improved monitoring and performance measurement arrangements which are construed locally as rods destined to beat already publicly flayed backs.

Although these are valid reasons for a resistance to change, there are also positive forces at work, none the least of which is a desire by local government managers to receive wider recognition as competent professionals, willingly accountable to public and peers alike. If agency attitudes are positive, the initial reaction of 'why should we' may soon give way to 'how can we'. If this does happen, the central authority promoting the notion of enhancement, has to be able to provide sensible answers without delay. These must cover technical and administrative matters concerning planning and control, as well as information requirements and the technology for data processing.

The management information support issue is not simply a matter of authority and agents agreeing on requirements and proceeding into the system design phase. At any stage along the way one or more agencies may suddenly feel threatened by too zealous an impetus to investigate and revise its systems. Some may consider the focus on a single service exaggerated and dysfunctional in the context of overall tasks. The level of the agency's staffing capability may be generally lower than the promoting authority would consider appropriate. Local political attitudes may harden against what is deemed to be interference by central authority. Agreeing on what systems are needed may be a hard task, but setting the stage in which individual agencies begin to make changes, calls for very careful selection of strategy both in relation to design and implementation.

The authority promoting enhancement is faced with some difficulties in justifying the need for changes. The strongest claim for tighter control being required would be based on a comparative study of costs and other performance measures. Unexplained differences between agencies could point to waste or other inefficiency. However, if the information systems of different agencies are not soundly based or well focussed, existing information is unreliable for isolating poor performance. Furthermore unless some standardised framework and coding structure exists, there is no easy way to restate in a common format previously published accounting statements for what is likely to be some hundreds of agencies.

Crudely stated the summary argument put up by agents against the use of comparative analysis to justify the need for improvement is ‘we aren’t alike and anyway the figures are wrong.’ Unless the central authority refutes this sentiment, impetus towards enhancement will soon fade. Alternatively if the authority resolves to break this impasse, it must first take a large degree of responsibility for devising reliable information systems and then establish conditions in which agents will be active in seeking better performance.

If positive involvement by the central body is chosen as the preferred course of action, the others being injunction or political pressure, what is then needed is a sound platform from which can be developed a design and implementation strategy.

Building this platform calls for a well planned investigation which when concerned with a major national or regional service involves considerable effort and judgement to ensure that this effort is constructive.

It seems pragmatic and sensible that an investigation for the purpose of designing and implementing an enhanced service delivery system should concentrate on a workable set of key ‘subjects’. These should reflect a balanced approach and give an initial assurance that there will be an adequate basis from which a strategy can be planned. Working with a short list should not be interpreted as putting blinkers on the investigators, but simply as a way of economising on data collection and sharpening the focus of the study.

As a starting point, a profile of agencies should be developed from sample data on size, range of functions and other descriptive parameters. This data might be called Basic Description (BD). One of its uses would be to identify peer agencies, e.g., those with an approximately similar range and scale of tasks for whom it might be expected that operating costs should not differ significantly.

Among a large group of public sector agencies there may be considerable differences in Organisational Structure (OS) which each has adopted as a consequence of some earlier or current local political reality. The ways in which the agency is constituted and managed could have implications both for its ability to accommodate change as well as its willingness to work towards new levels of achievement in terms of value for money.

A third part of the investigation should set about measuring costs and performance parameters for the selected area of public service. A summary title of this section could be Performance Data (PD). By linking this data to peer grouping information referred to above, it is possible to understand the impact of size of task on costs but also establish the pattern of cost differences between agencies of the same size.

Another key element of the investigation would be concerned with data processing. Information for each agency sampled should at least cover data processing and storage volumes, reporting workload, equipment and staffing, as well as estimates of spare capacity. Analysis of this information should establish where each agency is on the pathway of technology and how well placed agencies might be to respond to new data processing responsibilities or systems changes. Logically, this stage might be called Information Processing (IP).

The main concern on the part of the central authority is the prevailing performance level in each agency for a specific service. Accordingly the closest attention should be given during the investigation to planning, control and administrative aspects of that service. This crucial section of the study might be given the summary title of Technical Content (TC). Because of the possibility that individual agencies may have evolved new successful approaches which could serve as models for future widespread use, keen attention should also be given to identifying innovative methods and especially effective operations.

As the investigators become familiar with prevailing patterns of activity, an excellent opportunity is created to identify Conflicts Deficiencies and Anomalies (CDA). Under this rather broad heading would be grouped those counter-productive reactions which pervade many administrative activities. Examples would include strife about resource requirements, inadequate information flows to decision makers or the misinterpretation of regulations or procedures.

The six subject headings described above – 'Basic Description', 'Organisational Structure', 'Performance Data', 'Information Processing', 'Technical Content', and 'Conflicts, Deficiencies and Anomalies' are the charter set of aspects which an investigation program into current agency activities should incorporate. Each would be amplified in response to the specific characteristics of the public service and environment under review. A well designed and administered mail survey should produce much useful information but supplementary intensive field visiting is important especially in relation to the last two items of the set.

Two further topics conclude this outline of the scope of an initial investigation. Both require that the investigators' attention move from the agency level at which the service is delivered to one which is global, concentrating on developments and methods outside the environment under review. The two subjects for study are Trends in Information Processing (TI) and Technical Developments (TD). Both aim to develop a sense of what level of technology and methods should be incorporated in any modernisation process. A review of developments in other countries in the provision of the selected service makes good sense as insurance that suboptimal parochial recommendations for change are not promoted. Literature search, conference attendance and selected visiting to centres of excellence are ways in which the global assessment of trends can be made.

However appropriate may be the designation ‘key’ to these eight subject headings, many readers may not yet be satisfied that these are a sound basis for setting up a major investigation. Some more substantial justification may be needed as for example demonstration of the relevance of these topics to policy formation upon which the success of enhancement efforts is predicated. Linking such overriding questions and the investigative effort not only dispels fears that the scope of data gathering is incomplete, but anticipating the policy issues helps to focus data analysis and minimises research effort.

In bringing together the issues policy should address, it is useful to make distinction between design and implementation. The logical sequence for taking decisions would then be, “what changes are needed and why?”, followed by ‘how should such changes be put into effect?’. ## 3. Pavement Maintenance Management Systems

In many countries, there is considerable concern about the condition of pavements. Commenting on the experience of the World Bank, Morris [8] stated that “against the present outlook for slow real economic growth in the eighties, roading administrations across nations are facing a widening gap between needed and available funds for road construction and maintenance. The problem is becoming increasingly serious as the extensive road networks built over the past decades, together with their bridges and structures, are now aging."

In the context of road networks, maintenance has a broad meaning covering factors such as “highway safety, preservation of capital investment in the highway system, operational characteristics of the highway and aesthetic qualities of the facility” [9, p. 7]. Compounding the wide scope of responsibilities undertaken by pavement maintenance managers, is the factor of time. Pavement construction standards may aim at a 20-year operating life but many factors including maintenance strategy govern the attainment of this target. To maximize benefits to all parties including road users, engineering evaluation considerations must be combined with budgetary control procedures. A significant volume of information is needed to support this type of management system [10] and meet the many and varied planning and control data requirements for a typical pavement network [11].

A conceptual outline showing the building blocks of a pavement management system appears in Figure 2. An alternative analysis of such system focussing on the overriding aims of effectiveness, efficiency, economy and accountability is given in diagrammatic form in Figure 11. There is general agreement that pavement maintenance performance can be enhanced by a systematic approach along the lines shown in Figure 2 and that “there is sufficient technology to implement improved pavement management systems now” [11, p. 3]. Nonetheless there is a long way to go before these approaches are in widespread use particularly at the local authority level.

## 4. Recent Developments in Local Government Management Systems in New Zealand

Territorial local government in New Zealand has traditionally directed most attention to financial systems. Managerial accounting, with its focus on the provision of information for planning and control, has not been highly developed by TLA's in general. Consequently, this field is well behind the level of sophistication of private sector situations of similar scale.

In recent years, greater emphasis has been given to management in the public sector, with criteria for good management [12] being openly discussed and tacitly endorsed, though not necessarily actioned by individual TLA's.

There may not have been a rush to introduce new managerial procedures and make information systems changes to meet these requirements. Yet a political and organizational platform now exists, making the introduction of new systems for management in local government somewhat easier than before. Part of the impetus has come from the Controller and Auditor General whose statement of criteria $[12, Section 3]$ for good management in local government included the following:

\- “All activities should be subject to control systems which:

\- set performance standards in physical and/or financial terms;

\- measure actual performance against standards;

\- report variations promptly to managers."

\- “Financial information systems should primarily be designed for reporting to management although they should be flexible enough to provide for prompt preparation of the statements required by Local Authority Accounting Regulations.”

In the context of efforts being made to enhance pavement maintenance management, the existence of statements of this kind is an umbrella for anyone in local government wanting to implement a system of the type outlined in Section 3 above.

Another development has been taking place with considerable potential to enhance information systems in New Zealand local government. The past three years have seen the formation of the Local Authority Computer Services Advisory Group (LACSAG) aiming to strengthen inter-authority technical cooperation and facilitate the exchange of information. $^{1}$ Although essentially a forum for discussion and member education, LACSAG could in the future exercise considerable influence on the formation of standards for computing in local government and on the direction of hardware and software used.

A third development affecting the use of computers in local government and the capacity of TLA's to respond to specific requirements such as the enhancement of pavement maintenance management systems, is the formation among TLA's of computer user groups. The two forms of user groups include (a) informal networks of users of the same type of equipment $^{2}$ and (b) a more formal cooperative arrangement, the Local Government Computer Joint Committee (LGCJC). The latter is composed of in excess of twenty small to medium sized TLA's who have jointly purchased equipment and are sharing the cost of software development. Potentially membership also offers access to information on new technical developments and systems support benefits to geographically isolated member TLA with limited in-house technical capability. Currently both these groups have a strong preoccupation with the financial accounting aspects of local government.

## 6. Factors from the External Environment which have Implications for the Development of Pavement Maintenance Management Systems in New Zealand

This section contains a short synopsis of current international views on the factors which need to be synthesized in the development of an appropriate pavement maintenance management systems for New Zealand TLA use. Treatment, though not exhaustive, focusses on the following four selected issues:

\- Management Accounting in Public Sector

\- Accountability and Performance Evaluation

\- Technological trends and innovation in public sector administration

\- Development in pavement management system's theory and application.

Management accounting is “a system that collects, classifies, summarizes, analyses, and reports information that will assist managers in their decision-making and control activities” [14,xi]. Originally developed to meet information needs of managers in industry and commerce, part of the array of management accounting techniques and procedures is being applied in public sector situations. Terminology differs somewhat, as can be seen when Anthony and Herzlinger [15] translate the familiar planning and control concepts of the private sector into requirements for public sector officials to work effectively, efficiently and economically. Figure 11 clarifies these public sector obligations in a specifically pavement system context. Though public and private sector managers function under different conditions, scrutiny of the very wide range of activities and public services in which local government participates [16] quickly reveals the wide scope for systematic and selective use of management accounting.

Lacking widely accepted and convenient measures such as profit or return on investment, it is not surprising that the public sector process of accountability for funds spent, lacks the refinement of corporate reporting. Figure 11 also emphasizes the importance of performance reporting, though it is apparent that the content of reports is still subject to debate. One of the reasons for this is that the aims of social programs are often far reaching and their outcomes hard to express [17]. Another reason for the lack of reporting direction is a failure by politicians to specify target objectives when adopting new programs. These problems notwithstanding, a perceived deficiency in public sector management and performance disclosure has caused the Canadian government inter alia to stress the need for senior managers to “- take firm control of public service activities in seeking value for money productivity;

\- accept responsibility for the timeliness and quality of the services they provide to their special public;

\- take positive steps towards upgrading their competence as managers and, within these steps, to develop and display a professional integrity and commitment to managing;

\- support moves toward a system of accountable management built around the concept of up-the-line responsibility" [6].

From Australia too, there are clear signs of rising public expectations of its local government managers, e.g., “that the local authority should consider its resources and activities as a corporate whole and that it should plan and review them in relation to its needs and problems of its environment” [18, p. 136].

In the United States, the need for progress in the search for valid and acceptable measures of public sector performance is being recognized by the General Accounting Office (GAO) [19] and other agencies [20,21] $^{11}$ . The impact of worldwide economic recession and of efforts to reduce inflation have been felt at local government level, causing either cuts in expenditure or services or sometimes both. Greater external pressure for better performance as well as reduced resource availability, should stimulate organizations to introduce more systematic management systems. Such actions will represent significant challenges to information systems designers.

Forces for change described above coincide with the flurry of innovation in office technology especially in the field of computer equipment for small scale situations. Though it is easy to presume that any information deficiencies can be bridged through office machinery acquisition alone, one study of local authorities very properly suggests that it will be essential to take “a broader view of all the social and organizational systems that affect productivity in office settings” [22, p. 52] and in particular the process of organizational change [23].

Looking specifically at the direction and level use of computers in local government, reports covering the USA [24] and Japan [25] suggest that usage at least is becoming universal. Microtechnology is also opening up opportunities for information processing which combines traditional accounting with geographical and graphics capabilities [26].

These additional systems capabilities are potentially applicable to a number of local government information needs. The usefulness of geo-data and graphics approaches is being increasingly recognized [27]. This background of shrinking technology limitation and the potential it offers to enhance local government performance must be viewed with caution as changes could still be slow. King [28] makes the observation that “local government will shape the technology to their agendas, not the other way round.” Key factors in this shaping process are likely to include the considerations suggested by Danziger and Kling [24, p. 60] who state that “the extent of computer use seems to increase with both a strong value orientation toward professional management practices and greater user control over computer decisions.”

In many countries, including the United States, the deteriorating condition of infrastructure including pavements is gaining wider recognition [29]. The comments by Morris [8] reported in Section 3 are based on World Bank experience and reflect the high level of concern about the financial implications of poorly maintained aging highways and bridges in developing countries.

This theme of maturing pavement networks is extended by Sheffley [30] who identifies the swing in resource allocation from construction to maintenance and the need for “scientific management of the available resources of materials, money and personnel.” A number of consequences flow from inadequate, ineffective or deferred maintenance. These include reductions in safety and highway operating life, and increases in vehicle operating costs [31]. Significant increases in maintenance expenditure are also likely to be experienced for repairing the same section of road if deterioration is allowed to proceed to more advanced levels.

In 1981 a series of workshops on pavement management was conducted by the Federal Highway Administration. Key maintenance concerns were isolated and system alternatives debated. One conclusion from these discussions was that pavement management systems (PMS) were feasible and their successful use would require “an integrated, coordinated treatment” [32, p. 7]. Another firmly stated conclusion was that any PMS system should be linked into the budgeting process. It was further concluded that information technology was available to meet foreseeable systems needs [32, p. 3].

The pavement inventory is an important element of the pavement management system. Many technical attributes over the long life of a pavement require to be monitored and evaluated by management. All this adds considerably to inventory information requirements. In addition to the inventory record, a significant amount of data is needed to develop optimum maintenance strategies $[33]$ . Engineering evaluation models such as 'Paver' $[9]$ and the World Bank's HDM $[8]$ , have been developed to provide computer derived solutions to large scale problems for particular types of pavement surface. Other decision support systems currently available include Yeamen's PMS developed for Australian urban roads $[34]$ , the State of Washington system $[35]$ and the Arizona statewide computerized system described by Golabi and Kulkarni $[36]$ .

An Australian study [37] of accounting information output requirements also classified maintenance activities and made proposals for standards of production and performance. Many state Highway Authorities in the United States have produced comprehensive manuals covering pavement and vehicle maintenance functions.

There are few documented statements of the benefits derived from more rigorous and systematic maintenance systems. A maintenance control system has been used by the State of Washington Department of Transportation for the past decade. Analysis of performance has shown significant planning and performance gains $[38, p. 2]$ . The Arizona statewide system has also claimed considerable improvement in value for money performance $[36]$ .

Almost all the efforts to establish pavement maintenance systems have taken place at the State level. The literature contains only isolated references to efforts to develop standardized approaches at the local authority level. $^{12}$ On the West Coast there have been cooperative actions by territorial authorities e.g., cities and counties, to assess pavement maintenance needs at network and local levels [39].

Obtaining adequate funding for pavement maintenance is an ongoing road engineer preoccupation. Local authorities are characterized by multi-service responsibility and very active internal competition for available funds. To ensure that maintenance is not unreasonably deferred those responsible for presenting the maintenance budget need valid data and good communication skills. Graphics and visual aids are needed for outlining alternative strategies and generally informing elected representatives and the public about this important function and the consequences of neglect [40].

It is one thing to attract financial support for pavement maintenance but another to demonstrate comprehensively that this investment has achieved an acceptable ratio of value for money. Pavement management consists of many functions with impacts spanning one or more time periods. As Poister et al. [41] has indicated in the context of pavement management in Pennsylvania, great care is required to identify appropriate administrative and performance monitoring measures. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation now produces a report card for public dissemination which gives a statewide comparative summary of some twenty indicators measuring inter alia output, efficiency, road condition and program effectiveness.

In conclusion is is worth noting that performance measurement and evaluation is sterile unless managers are willing and able to respond to adverse indicators.

Unfortunately, decision making users of information, in local government especially, have a poor past record of satisfaction with the reports they receive. As a contribution to improving information quality Keen [23] stresses the importance of implementation as a means of enhancing user benefits from new systems.

To strengthen the prospect of acceptance and assure ongoing momentum, two tactical approaches are suggested – Down and Out (DO) and Up and In (UI). “DO is based on direction from the top, lengthy design stages, and a formal system for planning and project management. UI relies on small groups, with face-to-face involvement and participation management” [23,26]. With either of these approaches there is a complementary task of a political nature which is “the process of getting commitment or building support, or creating momentum for change” [23,27].

## 7. Building Towards Better Local Authority Management with Particular Reference to Pavement Maintenance Systems

In previous sections, New Zealand and international perspectives have been provided on information system trends and pavement management developments. In New Zealand, the cost of maintaining the pavement network is without exception the largest item of expenditure for territorial local governments. The ongoing importance of safe and sound pavements and the consequent need for proficient management, are the primary reasons for the focus on PMS. It should be noted that although roading is the largest item there are other technical activities like water reticulation and sewage, to which similar considerations apply.

The purpose of this section is to identify factors and directions which should be recognized by policy makers concerned with building more effective and efficient pavement management systems in New Zealand. Established by statute with responsibilities for the disbursement of funds for new highway construction and maintenance subsidy, the National Roads Board (NRB) is a natural choice as leader in the pavement management enhancement process. Although the simplest solution to the issue of promoting change is to legislate for compliance by way of a set of detailed requirements, this approach so overrides the spirit of local government autonomy that it is likely to be invoked only as a last resort.

The analysis which follows will seek to formulate an approach which will result in the widespread and effective “institutionalisation” of pavement management procedures among the 230 TLA’s in New Zealand. Recognition will be given to political, financial and technical considerations and to prevailing attitudes of interested parties. The thrust will not be to detail requirements for a pavement management system but rather to make judgments on the best way to build up momentum among TLA’s themselves which will lead towards more systematic and effective pavement maintenance.

As a starting point Table 7 contains a synopsis of factors derived from Sections 4 and 5 above which characterise New Zealand TLA's and the status of their information systems.

Additional features which policy makers should consider are the emerging TLA cooperative arrangements to exchange computer information and share development costs, and efforts by the Controller and Auditor General in particular, to have TLA's demonstrate a greater ability to provide more value for money.

Although New Zealand is geographically remote it is influenced by developments in other parts of the world through its participation in Pacific Basin affairs and traditional links with Europe. Though it can claim leadership in some areas of endeavour, it is also true that many innovations come from abroad. ## 8. General Guidelines for Promoting Enhancement

Previous sections have described findings of an investigation, the central aim of which was to evolve an approach which would lead to an improvement in pavement maintenance management in New Zealand. The scope of the investigation was developed by identifying logical data requirements and key policy questions and ensuring that data and questions were well matched. It seems safe to conclude that for the New Zealand problem at least, a sufficiently comprehensive information base was established from which policy makers could work to develop an enhancement promotion strategy.

![](/api/attachments/JSK847ZP/fulltext/images/886e41391b991aa9b0004008b1772d7ce56a7b00d5f5623296787a973f622846.jpg)  
Fig. 13. Prospects for Progress with Alternative Intervention Strategies. Note: These terms have been drawn from [23] and used to illustrate strategy in this interagency context.

The focus of the remainder of this paper is on identifying issues of general importance to a central authority wishing to take the initiative in performance enhancement.

Of primary importance in this kind of exercise is knowing that the effort is worth making. As early as possible policy makers should be convinced that the actions they propose to take are justified in terms of anticipated benefits. This knowledge helps increase motivation and the likelihood that adequate resources will be committed to give a reasonable prospect of success. Part of the process of assessing the resources needed to implement change is an accurate assessment of forces opposing innovation.

There are many reasons why agent institutions, especially in local government, might be inclined to resist new requirements proposed by a central authority. In addition to a desire to defend the principle of local autonomy coupled with a distrust of the motives of central government, at least six other factors need to be recognised as potentially negative reactions. These are listed below with brief explanatory comments:

\- Aggressive response to disturbance of existing positions of power – as information is not devoid of impact, those currently controlling the information flows cannot be expected to give this up without a struggle.

● Bureaucratic response to the timetable of change - committee procedures can by accident or by design cause the momentum for change to evaporate.

![](/api/attachments/JSK847ZP/fulltext/images/70527868cb0807efb6da4618f6846f6fd222f88d04db708be9005dc3187eb56a.jpg)

\- Complacency – there may be no recognition on the part of key personalities of any need for changes.

\- Divided Viewpoints – voices of dissatisfied users are drowned out by those who currently enjoy a better than average share of information services.

\- Elementary Fear of Change – a widespread lack of knowledge about a subject such as computing permits rumours and scaremongering to flourish.

\- Fickle or lukewarm support – the extent of the commitment to support change may alter radically during the implementation process.

In addition to these generally prevalent constraining attitudes there are practical barriers to change. An example would be the existence of a significant number of agencies whose information processing resources in quality or capacity are below minimum future requirements. Another barrier is the tension which may arise if new proposals contain performance requirements for this service more onerous than before or out of line with other sectors in government.

Taken individually each of these negative factors is a challenging hurdle. In combination, they are often dauntingly larger than any local positive impetus created by public pressure or professional ambition. Regrettably, with few exceptions, opposition to change is the local force which has to be countered. On experiencing local resistance, the realistic central authority policy maker would generally begin by rechecking the figures used to justify innovation in case prospective benefits have been overstated. It is worth noting that the local agencies' recognition of proposed new approaches is limited to items which figure in their own accounting systems, and they are not easily swayed by claims of external benefits to others.

Convinced that changes would be beneficial and are desirable, the central authority has the initial task of getting agencies committed at least in principle, to the long run objectives to which the enhancement is aimed. Stressing the positive financial and political benefits likely to flow from improved value-for-money performance is one of the better ways of creating the important climate of voluntary local participation. In the final stages of the process of getting support hardy opponents not swayed by claims of improvements, may change their positions as it becomes clear that the authority is prepared to legislate or even by-pass the agent altogether by setting up its own branch operations.

Assuming that the parties are reasonably accommodating towards each other, the next aspect to which attention should be given is the design process. Though general agreement may have been reached on objectives, more detailed discussions will be needed to agree the details from which final designs can be developed. The central authority would be wise to give local agencies genuine opportunities to take part in these discussions, thereby sharing local knowledge and experience. The central authority should carefully state its position on any matters in which it feels unable to negotiate, in order to minimise the prospect of wasted effort. These meetings will be difficult anyway, especially where many agencies are involved.

Where the group is really large, giving everyone a direct right of participation may be impractical. The existence of a body representative of agents and possessing authority to bind all parties, would be helpful as a way of getting participation and agreement in the design phase, as well as being potentially important during subsequent implementation.

Though far more easily said than done, once the administrative and technical composition of the changes has been agreed, other important strategic options need to be faced. Clearly the degree of importance is governed by the extent of the new requirements. For example a simple change in the date by which some action is required would not require much implementation other than sending out a circular. In contrast, no one could pretend that introducing an entirely new computer based decision-support-system required anything but the most comprehensive implementation treatment.

The central authority promoting performance enhancement through major changes has a range of implementation options from which to choose, of which the following are examples:

(i) specify output requirements and leave it to each agent to decide how to accommodate the changes using existing or to-be-acquired data processing equipment.

(ii) specify both the output requirements and the system from which these will be derived but allow each agent to decide what hardware is needed and how the system is to be programmed.

(iii) program a system which will meet requirements using standard hardware and supply these to agents on a turnkey basis.

(iv) specify output requirements and arrange with selected agents to develop pilot systems which can be evaluated, improved and only then distributed.

Obviously option (iii) has the attractive features for the central authority in terms of economy of scale, speed of development and control. It does not rate as highly in terms of the risk of failure as all eggs are in one basket, nor does this single approach have the potential to be easily integrated into existing accounting procedures. Using a medical analogy, the rejection factor from the approach is potentially high.

On similar criteria the first option is generally unattractive as it imposes development costs on all agents, lacks cohesion and is relatively unmanageable. Option (ii) is more convincing than option (i) as it gets much of the systems design done collectively but recognises that as each agent may want to build on existing data processing capabilities and experience, leaving hardware purchase to the agent has merit.

The approach of strategy option (iv) is that through wholehearted involvement in all phases of development, agents will identify more enthusiastically with the details of the new proposals. The setting 'in which the prototype system is developed is also the 'real world' and evaluation can begin as soon as the first reports start flowing. Revisions can be made more easily 'in house' than through most kinds of representative committees. Unfortunately interagency rivalry may make criticisms of such pilot systems as severe as of anything produced and handed down by the central authority.

As none of these proposals seems ideal, policy makers may wisely seek a hybrid approach which would avoid the main weaknesses and also give greater recognition to training and trends in technology, factors important to getting a modern approach installed. Involving agencies in an educational setting from an early stage has the potential to achieve training objectives and form a useful part of the design process by providing feedback and opinions to the designers. Beginning with a focus on underlying principles and objectives, the educational content can through time be shifted to an operational level of detail.

The technology question is more difficult to resolve. Because of an inevitable lag in adopting innovations, agency data processing systems will range across a spectrum from reasonably modern to positively out of date. Like aiming at a moving target, the promoting authority will wish to take advantage of recent technological advances, but may not know which of the advances will contribute cost effectively to the search for a robust, practical way of doing the work.

This suggests that a part of the implementation strategy should be consciously directed towards an examination of new technology and the feasibility of its use on a large scale. Using an outline of the proposed approach as a test case, one or more configuration alternatives can be used to see what special benefits or problems are experienced using innovative technology.

A combination of feedback from the training groups with the results of the technological assessments should give useful leads to those involved in design and implementation choices.

From this point onwards policy makers should be concerned with speed. Getting the detailed work done by the quickest most competent professional service seems a good investment. While this is being completed the promoting authority should be on the lookout for a limited number of test sites among its agents which might be suitable to take the new systems on a trial and demonstration basis. Only after the screening process has confirmed that the objectives have been met, should general introduction be considered.

Though not directly critical of this methodical approach, some may be concerned about time, pointing out quite validly that the rest of the ‘world’ does not stop while the various stages of design are being carried through.

The promoting authority should recognise that in a large group of agents there are some anxious to begin preparation for the new system and others faced with new computer purchase decisions. To reduce the prospect of frustration or unilateral action, early attention should be given to topics which will help those being pressed by events. Examples of essential advance advices are matters such as standard coding definitions and any hardware or software conventions to be followed. Though not a perfect way of overcoming a real difficulty, it does permit agencies to move ahead in the general direction while detailed work is proceeding.

Another important issue which can and should be addressed early, is the relationship between new requirements and existing accounting and administrative systems. In addition to the concerns expressed about opposition to change within agencies there is also a worry that one department may not be responsive to requests by a service centre, for data in a new form. Yet without access to accurate, timely and appropriately coded financial and other data, the new system would be hamstrung. To avoid this undesirable situation, policy makers should address the data provision issue. At the same time a decision should also be taken which would determine the long term level of integration of the main and service centre accounting systems.

This occurrence of myopia in connection with data linkages suggests that there might be dangers of the same kind elsewhere. For example, a central authority promoting enhancement of performance in its special field of service may not be conversant with other sections of an agency's overall workload.

The promoting agency is thus not well placed to establish whether the direction in which the system improvements are moving for its service, are also appropriate for other services undertaken by the agent. Yet the level of overlapping is considerable in cases where services are deriving financial data from the same source, using the same data processing equipment and having decision makers with multiple responsibilities.

The potential for solving common agency problems in one combined exercise should not be overlooked. The difficulty is that the central agency is by definition not well placed to do this because of its single service interest. It is nonetheless in the wider interest that a way be found to avoid piecemeal increments to the work patterns of local institutions.

Paramount among the topics which policy makers should act upon is the matter of incentives. Agents may be coerced to adopt new ways by regulation and other forms of external pressure but their autonomy softens the impact of these approaches. If ‘more carrot and less stick’ is the best formula for enhancement then determining the incentive arrangements is potentially one of the most important functions of the central authority.

In setting about this task, the four objectives which should receive concentrated attention are concerned with getting agents to:

(a) adopt the new methods

(b) provide the service effectively on an ongoing basis

(c) strive continuously to improve the service delivery process

(d) respond readily to future changes in requirements and technology.

Each situation calls for careful tailoring of the package of rewards and sanctions to achieve the objectives listed above. The relative political strength of agents and the central authority will determine the final details including monetary arrangements. In anticipation of a bargaining session with agents, the central authority policy makers should recognise that there are general guidelines which should give a rational and constructive tone to these negotiations.

From the perspective of the agent, the important consideration is that the remuneration offered covers all costs of providing the service and there is no drain on the agents' other sources of funds. Naturally it would be more attractive to agents if the consideration generated a surplus. Unfortunately out-of-pocket reimbursement arrangements do little to stimulate ongoing efforts towards improvement. By simply undertaking to refund what is spent in providing the service, with no distinctions made between good and bad performance, the central authority blunts any spur towards the search for efficient and economic ways to do the work.

An alternative approach would be to base the agents' recompense for services provided, on some monetary and performance standards. This would fix the cost of service provision for the central authority and offer progressive authorities the chance to make money if they can get their actual costs below the level of the standard reimbursement rates. There are clear difficulties in setting up standards particularly for new services for which cost patterns are not yet known. Furthermore, variations in size of the task undertaken by each agency may call for variations in the standard rates. Output measurement and quality of service assurance are important too. In spite of these technical difficulties, the advantages of using standard reimbursement rates over an out-of-pocket approach are considerable.

To encourage agencies to modernise their equipment and systems, the central authority would need to demonstrate that there are measurable advantages to the agent. Furthermore, unless it could also be shown that the capital outlay on new equipment is covered by these savings, not much chance exists of getting more than a small fraction to respond to the plea to modernise their service delivery methods. The smallest agents may be quick to point out that because of small volumes, unit cost savings will never be sufficient to justify new investment. Unless the service arrangement with them is to be withdrawn, or old ways retained, the central authority may have little alternative but to subsidise the cost of the changes including associated equipment, up to 100%. This may not be too high a price to pay for standardisation across the full range of agents.

By engaging in bulk hardware acquisition and joint development of programmes, the initial cost to all can be kept low as well as the cost of any support given to small agencies. As cost is always going to be important in decisions about new equipment purchases, some form of central purchasing needs to be kept going either by the central authority or various groups of agents. In the field of training and education similar arrangements need to be made aimed at getting courses presented at prices which are affordable by all.

Important though monetary matters may be, consideration should also have to be given to non-monetary incentive for good performance. Making awards in various categories as public recognition for good work is one of the better ways, provided the measurement process is reasonably objective and all agencies have an equal chance to gain recognition.

## Conclusion

Throughout the Western world, the public sector attempts to find ways of providing services effectively, efficiently and economically. In the search for optimum solutions, local institutions are frequently used to provide the service on an agency basis. This paper has given attention to the process which a central authority might follow when wanting to enhance the service delivery performance of its agents. A policy making perspective has been followed and an illustrative background provided in the form of a report on field studies in pavement maintenance management in New Zealand.
