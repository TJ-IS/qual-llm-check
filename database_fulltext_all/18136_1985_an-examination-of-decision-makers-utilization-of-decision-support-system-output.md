# An Examination of Decision-Makers' Utilization of Decision Support System Output

Jack T. Hogue

College of Business University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, North Carolina 28223, USA

and

Hugh J. Watson

College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602, USA

A study was conducted of 18 decision support systems in major U.S. corporations in order to examine their methods of operation and the ways in which the DSS contribute to the decision making process. These DSS were studied with respect to organizational level of the decision maker, phases of the decision making process, interaction among decision makers using the DSS, requirement for and regularity of DSS use, impact on job tasks and performance, and perceived value of the DSS. Study results provide substantial support for generalized conceptualizations in the literature. Decision support is primarily for upper and/or middle management working in interaction. Use of the DSS is at the discretion of the decision maker and the majority of DSS users are highly satisfied with their systems.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Decision Making, Information Utilization, Information System Operation

## 1. Introduction

After the generally perceived failure of other computer-based information systems (CBIS) to provide the information needed by strategic decision-makers in organizations [7,13,27,34], many researchers have recognized the potential of decision support systems (DSS) as a remedy for this problem [2,3,5,6,11,14,15,17,22,26,31,32]. A decision support system is most commonly directed toward providing information to managers faced with those ill-structured problems that are typical of strategic decision-making. ## 2. Current Research and Opinion

While relatively little empirical research has been conducted with regard to the six stated topics, there has been significant speculation. Sprague [32], McCosh and Scott Morton [26] and Keen and Scott Morton [22] all suggest that DSS should be utilized at all managerial levels of the organization. In a survey of organizational users Naylor [29] found that most were from middle and upper management.

Considering the decision-making process as a series of phases (e.g., intelligence, design and choice in Simon's terminology), many writers have advanced the notion that decision support systems should be able to assist with all phases. Gerrity [11] recognizes the same potential but uses a five step process adding implementation and control.

Sprague [32] and Keen and Scott Morton [22] have stated that DSS should be used to support individual and group (“sequential interdependent” and “pooled interdependent,” according to Simon) decision-making. Also, Sprague [32] and Keen [19] have indicated a greater likelihood for a general improvement in communication as many decision-makers utilize similar systems for decision-making. One potential threat to interaction and communication between individuals concerns the use of a “personal DSS.” As Davis [8] and Hackathorn and Keen [14] point out, this may lead to less participation in decision-making.

Requirements for use and regularity of use of a DSS have received little attention. Because a DSS is created to support a decision, it is generally regarded as an aid to be used whenever needed. Alter [1] and Donovan and Madnick [9] dichtotomize DSS into “institutional” and “ad hoc” depending on whether or not they are used on a regularly scheduled basis, respectively. There is also the possibility that the use of a DSS may be mandated if supervisors believe that its use will improve decision-making [4,22,26,31,33]. Several writers have noted that use of a DSS may be assumed if the quality of the output is accepted [10] and if the DSS is readily available [30].

At a general level, the roles of the managers and others may be expanding as a result of DSS: some writers simply observe that changes have taken place, while Ginzberg [12] states that the DSS generates a change in the definition of the manager's task and Nash [28] observes that DSS use led to “expansion of the decision process of users” in a major bank. Also, Loughran and Cocks [25] argue that the installation of a computer based planning system changed the “nature of work” for managerial and staff employees of an airline.

The literature suggests the importance of evaluating the success of a DSS. In practice, however, rigorous evaluations appear to be rare. If a DSS continues to be used, this fact is taken as a measure of success. Sprague and Carlson [33], have provided an overall framework for evaluating a DSS. Welsch [35] has also conducted a comprehensive investigation of measures of DSS success.

## 3. Study Framework

In order to investigate decision-makers' utilization of DSS output with respect to the organizational level of the decision-maker, phases of the decision-making process, interaction among decision-makers using the DSS, requirement for and regularity of DSS use, impact of the DSS on decision-maker's jobs, and perceived value of the DSS, the authors spent five months conducting a study of DSS use.

## 3.1. Subjects

The subjects of this study were the decision-making users, and/or their intermediaries, for 18 DSS in the Dallas-Fort-Worth-Atlanta areas. These areas were chosen for researcher convenience and for high concentrations of corporate level decision-making. The DSS dealt with a wide range of issues (see Table 1) and included companies from eight different industrial classifications. The geographic scope of company operations ranged from state (16 percent) to international (44 percent). Seventy-eight percent of the companies had annual sales revenue of over \$500 million and 89 percent of the companies employed over 1,000 individuals.

## 3.2. Procedure

Each DSS was investigated by means of a personal field study interview. The authors used a questionnaire to provide structure to the interviews, but allowed for open-ended probing of topics. To allow for a fluid and open inquiry, sessions were taped recorded and responses were evaluated after the interview. Each DSS was described through a written case study, and data across cases were aggregated through descriptive summary tables. These are the basis for the findings and conjectures in the remainder of this article.

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. Organizational Level of the Decision-Maker

There are similar statements in the literature as to who uses a DSS in support of their decisionmaking tasks. While some writers indicate that the DSS should be used at all organizational levels, they also indicate that the DSS is directed primarily at middle and upper management. Naylor found that most DSS support upper management, but provide significant support to middle management. Our study confirms the statements in the literature. As Table 2 shows, the majority of individuals being supported in their decision-making are from middle and upper management (67 and 89 percent, respectively). Only 17 percent of companies supported lower management or non-management with the DSS, though such support exists. One hundred percent of the companies investigated support either or both middle and upper management.

## 4.2. Support for Phases of the Decision-Making Process

The decision-making process is generally considered to be composed of phases, and there is fairly unanimous agreement in the literature that a DSS should support all phases. In our study, decisions are considered to include three major phases – intelligence, design, and choice. In order for a DSS to support the intelligence phase, it must provide information of a descriptive nature. This would include information about the past, the present, and, perhaps, the future. For a DSS, this information should be highly accurate for the relevant time period. Table 3 indicates that the intelligence phase was found to be supported by all of the DSSs.

The design phase includes inventing, developing, and analyzing possible courses of action. Two related yet different capabilities are required here: analyzing existing alternatives and suggesting new alternatives. The analysis capability was explored by asking users whether the DSS is able to answer “what if” questions. As can be seen in Table 3, all of the DSS investigated possessed this capability. The ability to suggest new alternatives was investigated by asking users whether the DSS can perform goal seeking. Sixty percent of the DSS have this capability.

The choice phase involves assistance in the selection of the alternative to be implemented. Thirty-three percent of companies report that the DSS recommends a specific course of action. As will be discussed later, decision-makers are rarely (6 percent) required to rely on such results.

## 4.3. Form of Decision-making Interaction

The decision-making process is not normally limited to one individual isolated from others. Some writers indicate that the DSS should support both individual and group decision-making. Four types of interaction were considered in our study (see Table 4). Only one company indicated that the DSS is used to support a decision made by one individual. Generally, the decision may be the responsibility of, and ultimately made by, only one individual, but opinion and discussion is solicited from others to assist in arriving at a decision.

A sequential interdependent decision is one which involves multiple individuals at sequential points in time: a decision is made and this becomes the starting point for another. As Table 4 indicates, 33 percent of companies report this utilization of the DSS. In most of these (six) reported cases, the context of the sequential interdependent decision was preparation of company plans and/or budgets. Typically, a decision was first made at the corporate level, then decisions followed at the subsidiary or departmental level, with a final return to the corporate level.

Pooled interdependency in a decision involves more than one person being involved in the ultimate decision. Two or more individuals jointly evaluate the DSS output and reach a decision. Sixty-seven percent of the companies report this kind of utilization of their DSS. When all data from Table 4 are considered, there appears to be considerable interaction between decision-makers. This interaction would suggest that a significant amount of communication takes place as a result of the DSS. Successful communication and interaction would not be likely with a “personal” system but very few (6 percent) of the DSS included in this study are used in this manner.

## 4.4. Requirement for and Frequency of Use

Both operation of the DSS and use of its output tend to be primarily at the discretion of the user, as suggested in the literature. In over 50 percent of the companies, the operators, whether decision-makers or their intermediaries, can always choose whether the DSS is to be used in addressing a problem (see Table 5). In 84 percent of the companies, the operator normally has the choice of whether or not to use the DSS. However, in 45 percent of the companies, operators are sometimes told that they must use the DSS.

While operation of the DSS may occasionally be required, rarely is a decision-maker required to use the output without any discussion of its worth (see Table 6). In all but one company decision-makers can choose to completely ignore any or all of the output supplied by the DSS. Even in this one, use of the DSS output is required only occasionally. It should, however, be pointed out that frequency of DSS operation and use is quite high (see Tables 7 and 8).

## 4.5. Impact of the DSS on Jobs

One topic of interest is the impact that a DSS has on individuals involved in their development and use. Ginzberg, Nash, Loughran and Cocks all suggest that establishment of a DSS alters and redefines the job of both management and staff. The companies indicated that DSS affect the user's job in two ways: first, the DSS alters the nature of what is actually done; second, it changes the decision-maker's effectiveness.

The primary impact is on the decision-making process. Companies indicated that the DSS increases the number of alternatives evaluated, improves confidence in the decisions, and speeds up decision-making. A general and recurring impact is that the computer and its output are now used, whereas previously, the computer was used relatively little by upper and middle management.

## 4.6. Perceived Value of the DSS

The primary justification for the development of a DSS is that it will be of value to the decision maker. In order to evaluate the success of the DSS included in this study, three measures of success were explored: satisfaction/dissatisfaction, decision-maker acceptance, and frequency of ad hoc operation.

For each company, a measure on a seven-point scale of dissatisfaction to satisfaction with their DSS was obtained (see Table 8). A score of seven represents very satisfied, four is indifferent, and one is very dissatisfied. None was dissatisfied, and 95 percent of responses were slightly satisfied or better. The “General Management” category in Table 8 covers respondents not able or wishing to differentiate by management level.

## 5. Conclusions

DSS support decision-making at the middle and upper levels of management. Decisions typically require contributions from multiple individuals, which implies that the DSS should support the alternative styles of decision-makers rather than one specific style. Only rarely is significant interaction not required in the process of making the decision, which may reflect on the importance of the decision being supported. A DSS can support all types of decision-making situations: individual, sequential interdependent, and pooled interdependent.

Regardless of the system's specific capabilities, decision-makers do not rely on the DSS to make a decision, but simply to support the decision-making process. Support for the decision-making process is typically present in three general forms. First, the DSS should provide accurate, timely information which supports the intelligence phase of decision-making. Second, the DSS should assist in designing alternative courses of action. The DSS may develop alternatives on its own (through a goal seeking capability), and it should be able to analyze different alternatives (through a what-if capability). And finally, many DSS recommend a specific course of action to follow in order to support the choice phase of decision-making.

The DSS should be available for use on a continuous basis because it typically will be used as need arises. However, since the DSS contains a substantial and accurate set of information, some regularly scheduled reports may be derived from it.

The general impact of the DSS on personnel is best considered in terms of specific types of organizational personnel. Upper level managers interact directly with computer output. This output is directly usable. Upper level managers should expect output from the DSS to be accurate and timely, permitting decisions to be made more quickly, thus allowing more time to think about a wider variety of alternatives with a greater level of confidence.

Middle managers are affected as much as upper managers. For many, the DSS becomes a major part of their job. Since middle managers often fill roles of intermediary and decision-maker, “hands-on” interaction is to be expected. As managerial intermediaries, operation results in increased confidence in recommendations passed on to upper management and the ability to give upper managemen what they request in less time. As decision-makers, middle managers benefit in the same ways as upper management.

Lower management is largely excluded as an affected group. Their interaction is either nonexistent or minimal in terms of using a DSS to support their own decision-making or to serve in an intermediary role to higher management.

Staff from a functional area using a DSS is affected significantly. Very little interaction is required in terms of the approval/administrative process or use of output (since staff is not normally in a decision making role). Actual development of the DSS is, however, performed primarily by staff. For many, computer interaction is a new role, which occupies large amounts of their job. A final major impact of the DSS is on the quality and speed of performing the job of providing management with needed information (the intermediary role). The acquisition and presentation of information wanted by management is now faster and more simple. Quality of the DSS has further led to a much greater confidence in the quality of information provided to management.

A final area of interest is the evaluation of user satisfaction, or the measurement of the value or success of the DSS. Monetary assessment of success is probably not likely since it is rare for a DSS user to formally evaluate the impact of the DSS on profits. It may be that the use of a DSS constitutes a successful system. If use is discretionary, then frequency of use may be quite relevant. Operationalization of frequency of use will require a definition of “frequent” operation. This study has suggested some combination of satisfaction, acceptance, and frequency of use as potential indicators of success.
