# Executive Workstations: Issues and Requirements

Daniel J. Power and Alan R. Hevner

College of Business and Management, University of Maryland.
College Park, MD 20742, USA

An executive workstation is an integrated hardware/software system that can provide business executives with powerful capabilities for making and implementing decisions. In this paper, relevant and sometimes controversial, issues and workstation requirements are discussed in the context of a scenario that portrays an executive using a workstation. The scenario anticipates fifth generation computer technology and it guides a specification of features and functions. The goal is to clarify what is possible and desirable in an executive workstation design.

Keywords: Executive Workstation, Office Automation, Decision Support Systems, Information Systems Design, Requirements Analysis, Scenarios.

## 1. Introduction

One of the fifth generation computer products that can be anticipated is a powerful executive workstation. Current products associated with office automation are sometimes called executive workstations. However, even the most sophisticated, for example, the systems from Apollo and Xerox, provide only a fraction of the functional capabilities that can be expected to help individual managers manipulate, anticipate, and control complex, rapidly changing organizational and competitive environments.

In this paper, the various interfaces an executive will require in a computerized decision making environment are examined. These interfaces will be integrated into an executive workstation. An executive workstation is an integrated hardware/software system that provides executives with powerful capabilities for making and implementing decisions. Executives will use the workstation to gather, store, and retrieve information critical to the decision-making processes in an organization. Expert capabilities in the system will support decision-making activities. Decisions will be communicated by wide ranging and varied telecommunication facilities integrated into the workstation.

Three forces are spurring the development of such sophisticated systems:

1. Office environments are undergoing an important transformation from paper-based information handling to electronic, computer-aided information systems [20]. There is a tremendous need to help executives analyze and understand the large amounts of information required in business.

2. The decreasing costs of computer systems and the rapid development of innovative hardware and software products make sophisticated workstation development feasible.

3. Following Nolan's model of the growth cycle of information system development [16], one can anticipate more applications for executive workstations in the next ten years. As information systems move from the initiation stage to the maturity state, the functionality of the systems changes from completely operational tasks through largely management support tasks to a significant percentage of strategic planning functions. Executives can perform planning and decision making faster and more effectively using an executive workstation.

Despite these forces, a major issue that must be confronted in the development of executive workstations is the reluctance of many executives to personally use computers and information systems. This attitude is understandable given that most firms have not reached the stage in Nolan's information systems development model where executive applications are available. As new applications are developed and as younger managers are promoted into executive positions this issue should diminish in significance. At present, however, designers do not have a clear understanding of how the executive will use information systems. Designers have not adequately dealt with at least two crucial questions: How should information be structured and presented to support executive decision making? [19]; and What are the appropriate interfaces, hardware and software, to facilitate the use of information systems by executives?

The major purpose of this analysis is to determine how to create an integrated system that executives will use. Such an analysis will help designers structure information so that it can be presented to executives. A workstation design needs a vision as well as a requirements analysis of executive work environments.

## 2. Workstation Requirements Analysis

Determining system requirements for use in the design of executive workstations is a challenging task. A variety of techniques must be used including those typically used to design workstations for office automation. In today's office environment, there is rapid growth in the use of computer systems for electronic mail, word processing, spreadsheets, appointment calendars, and data management. However, these capabilities are used primarily by staff personnel for the support of management functions. Requirements analysis for designing executive workstations must emphasize decision and command functions over staff support functions. Techniques are needed that supplement the approaches used to design workstations for office automation.

Traditionally, requirements analysis for office automation has involved a detailed study of existing office facilities and procedures. Since the procedures and office tasks are well understood, requirements analyses generally result in comprehensive lists. These can then drive the office workstation design. Two recent studies illustrate techniques. An IBM case study describes the use of an Office Analysis Methodology to study an existing environment to determine its requirements for an advanced office workstation [2]. The study monitored current usage patterns to design improved office systems. Another technique is demonstrated by Malone [14]. He studies how people organize their desks and the implications of this for systems and workstation design. With no automated system, the objective is to analyze current work techniques and use these to guide a systems design. These two and other studies [6] have concentrated on office automation uses and have not dealt with the decision and command facilities needed.

These methods have obvious shortcomings for designing an executive workstation. Until sophisticated prototypes are built, we cannot monitor the usage of such systems to create an advanced design. It would be possible to record and analyze the decision-making and command activities of executives; but the danger is that sophisticated, innovative computer supports for decision making and command may be ignored. Executives may find that new tools will change their methods of decision making; initial workstation designs must allow users to employ a variety of decision and command tools and styles, while encouraging efficient and effective management and decision making activities.

The approach used in this paper is to develop and analyze a futuristic scenario. Variants of this scenario are discussed in order to suggest alternatives. Scenarios allow analysis of executive work patterns without being restricted by present technology and operational constraints $[23]$ .

## 3. Executive Workstation Scenario

The following scenario forecasts the activities of an upper level executive in a large organization. His work environment is hopefully typical of those in which executive workstations will be used.

## 3.1. Scenario

Executive Vice-President Smith works for a large oil company headquartered in Houston. He is in charge of all oil field operations in the U.S. On a typical day, Mr. Smith arrives at his office at 7:30 AM. He walks to his executive workstation and places his thumb on the identification recognition unit. The recognition unit moves the system from standby to a full activation status. Smith glances at the terminal display and notes that all of his waiting messages are status two or three; lower level priority messages. He also notes the green operations monitoring light, indicating that all operational field units report a satisfactory (green) status. No major problems have arisen overnight.

Mr. Smith sits down at the workstation and uses his hand controller to select the main menu, which is displayed on the screen. Smith calls up his daily schedule. No major appointments are scheduled and he decides to spend most of the day working on a strategic plan to improve the productivity of the company's oil fields. First, however, Smith returns to the main menu and selects the news headline file. This file contains new information about major world, national, and political events (since last scanned); this information is automatically stored and updated at the workstation. Smith quickly scans the file and marks information for later reference. To save a briefing, and comments, he positions his controller and pushes the control button.

After reviewing the headline briefings, Smith returns to the main menu and uses the hand controller to display current messages. He reviews messages received overnight. As he encounters a message that needs a response, he moves the cursor to the top of the screen and activates the “message response” block. He pushes a button and a separate work area that contains a memo form is overlaid on the screen. Smith has the option of typing a message or providing a voice response, which can either be sent as a verbal message or typed later by a secretary. Smith uses his executive keyboard to indicate the name of the respondent. He moves the pointer to the top of the screen, to the “send message” box, and pushes a button on his hand controller, automatically routing the message. The message is stored and cross-referenced to the original message. Smith normally keeps records of his messages active for two weeks and then the system automatically moves them to long-term archival storage.

After reviewing his messages and sending out responses, Smith returns to the main menu and selects the “operations report.” The operations report of the field units under his direct control are displayed on the screen. Smith can move the cursor, using the hand controller, to a specific unit, and request additional information about it. As he reviews the status of his units, Smith is able to send messages, either as telemail or voice, using the built-in telecommunications system. Smith usually requests written reports from field managers, but sometimes he speaks with them personally using the built-in videophone.

Smith now turns to the task of planning for oil field expansion. He calls up the main menu and requests the oil fields planning program. Based upon advice from the company's exploration research group, Smith has been considering shutting several less productive oil fields and opening new ones. He decides to use a planning and decision support system (DSS) to help make the decisions. The program contains cost modelling capabilities that provide a detailed estimate of costs and benefits. By varying the probabilities of success in finding oil in the new fields and the amounts of oil in the new fields, Smith is able to study the probable effects of his decisions. In addition to the costs and benefits, the program also asks questions that force Smith to recognize the non-quantitative aspects: Can the new fields be opened immediately? Are environmental impact statements necessary? If so, have they been completed? Can the appropriate drilling field teams be formed and moved to the new fields quickly? By using the DSS, answers to these and other crucial questions may be considered.

Based on his interaction, Smith decides to close two small fields and open a new one that is highly recommended by the research group. Smith uses the reports from the DSS to document his decision. He calls the project management program to rough out an initial implementation. With graphical support on his workstation screen, Smith lists the activities, personnel, time schedules, and assignments. The project management program prepares a multi-color project network that illustrates the timing and synchronization needed to complete the plan rapidly. Finally, he collects all of the information into a written directive that includes an activities list with responsibilities, time deadlines, and assignments for all personnel.

Smith edits this document, adding comments in appropriate places, and determines that the information should be reviewed by three of his most-trusted assistants. He moves the cursor to the top of the screen and activates the “message transmittal” mode; a second screen appears in the lower right hand corner. Smith indicates the three assistants who are to receive his message and then types “current document” as the contents of the message. He adds a message with the document stating that a briefing on the document will be held at 4:00 PM that afternoon. The system queries Smith about the priority code and the security classification of the message. Since the plan is still rough and Smith does not want the information leaked to the competition or even within the company, he requests a “secret” classification and priority one (urgent). The system then automatically encrypts the message; Smith moves the cursor to the “transmit box” at the top of the screen, pushes the button on the hand controller and the message is automatically sent. Smith moves the cursor to “save,” and the plans are stored. He then notices a flashing reminder of a lunch meeting in thirty minutes; Smith puts the workstation on standby.

Following lunch, Smith decides to review some trade periodicals. He activates his workstation and uses the publications retrieval program. Smith types in his choice of topics, indicating that he wants articles from the previous two months. During the search, Smith returns to the main menu and uses the “logistics support” program to order three terrain maps of the new oil field region. Soon the display indicates, in the lower left hand corner, that his search is complete and Smith returns to the main menu, positioning the cursor over “data retrieval.” The screen provides a list of articles in the topic areas requested. Smith uses the pointer to scan through the titles.

As he scans the titles, Smith notes that the status light at the bottom of his screen is flashing red, indicating an important message is arriving. Almost immediately, the terminal clears and a message is displayed indicating he is urgently needed at a high-level briefing. Smith returns to the main menu and moves his pointer to the “standby position.”

When he returns thirty minutes later, Smith again activates the workstation. He notes no important messages are waiting and all units are green. He decides to return to the articles. Smith notes one title which appears especially interesting. He pushes the button on his hand controller, and the article appears on his screen. He scans the abstract and decides the article seems quite appropriate. Smith reads it and decides to keep it in his files for use during a future meeting with his assistants. He moves the cursor to the top of the screen, presses “save,” and then moves the cursor to the “remind” block. A window appears on the screen with the query: “WHEN DO YOU WANT TO BE REMINDED ABOUT THIS DOCUMENT?” Smith types in “2:00 PM, Friday, 4/14/94.” The system responds “REMINDER NOTED” and the window closes. Smith scans a few other articles, saving some, rejecting most. When finished, he returns to the main menu.

Near the end of the day, Smith requests the “message” mode and sends voice messages to two assistants at headquarters, requesting that they come to his office in fifteen minutes for the previously announced briefing. The third is on a trip to the company’s office in New York. The planning document has been transmitted to that office. Communication arrangements have been made for her to participate in the briefing over video and audio hookup. All of the assistants acknowledge receipt of the message.

Fifteen minutes later, the managers enter and Smith pushes a button to switch the display from the workstation to a large-screen monitor on his office wall. Smith sits down, and takes the hand controller in his left hand. A keyboard is attached to a movable chairarm on his right hand. Smith explains the purpose of the briefing and retrieves the planning documents, displaying them on the screen. They discuss his plans for closing and opening the oil fields. As they talk, one of the assistants enters changes to the document on the display screen. They retrieve maps of the terrain from laser disk storage and note the potential difficulties of drilling the new field. After reviewing the plans, Smith adjourns the meeting, saves the revised plans, and routes copies to each assistant, who has several assigned tasks to complete for the final version. Updates are made only to a 'master' version.

After ensuring that all action items on that day's schedule have been handled, Smith returns to the main menu, selects "standby" and goes home.

## 3.2. Scenario Validity

No one scenario can capture all needs for future executive work environments. Three criteria were used to develop and analyze the scenario; credibility, intelligibility, and utility [23].

Given current technological developments, the scenario is credible. All input and output techniques have been forecast to be available within the next decade [5]. Microprocessor architectures and parallel architectures are developing rapidly. Storage devices are increasing in capacity and the cost per bit of information is falling dramatically. Also the tasks and workload seem credible and typical of executive duties; (e.g., information searching, reviewing, planning, decision making).

The scenario is intelligible and useful. Sufficient detail is provided so that features and functions of the workstation can be recognized. The scenario has proved very useful in structuring discussion and provoking thought about executive workstations both among students and among business executives. While these discussions have led to agreement and disagreement with various aspects of this scenario, we emphasize that the role of the scenario is to recognize the capabilities needed in an executive workstation rather than to accurately predict the future. For this purpose it has proved more than adequate.

## 3.3. Alternative Scenarios

By changing one or more aspects of the scenario, a number of alternatives can be conceived. Scenarios can be proposed in which the executive will have no direct interface with an information system. However, we believe that a majority of executives will want and need executive workstations $[17,21]$ . Plausible alternative scenarios vary in the following aspects:

1. Support Staff-Secretaries and administrative assistants may use a specialized workstation as much or more than the executive. Support staff may have direct links to the workstation of the executive.

2. Keyboard-less Environment-The executive may rely solely on voice and pointing devices for input; lack of typing skills should not deny an executive efficient use of a workstation.

3. Organizational Structure-The structure of an organization will affect the role of an executive in decision-making [10,15,17]. In a centralized structure, group decision aids and conferences may figure more prominently. In a more decentralized decision environment, executive control may be granted to more individuals throughout the organization.

4. No Video Capabilities-The deliberate pace of video implementation and resistance to it may limit its use in executive situations. However, the cost may be insignificant. Local area networks should have sufficient capacity to carry video signals with voice and data.

5. Portable Workstations-An executive may require sophisticated workstation capabilities at home or while traveling. An alternative scenario could address such needs.

## 4. Specifications for an Executive Workstation

The scenario demonstrates that an executive workstation must have enormous power and flexibility to serve executives in upper management $[11]$ . The workstation must have capabilities for supporting planning, logistics, staffing, command, communication, control, and other generic management tasks $[13]$ in all types of situations. Although workstations may be regularly used to support routine administrative management tasks $[11,15]$ , executive workstations must be primarily designed to perform sophisticated decision making functions and to handle crisis management situations.

Both the hardware and software for workstations must be carefully developed to ensure modularity, expandability, user friendliness, and reliability. This is difficult in today's marketplace because hardware is often constructed before a full range of applications is considered [22]. Our fundamental approach to the system design is that software requirements precede the specification of hardware.

## 4.1. Software Specifications

An executive workstation should at least include the following classes of software. Within each class, further refinements and enhancements will be needed, depending upon the application environment and the specific uses of the workstation:

## 1. An Integrated Operating Environment

Based upon the scenario, one can conclude that a crucial component of the workstation is the operating environment. It must smoothly and transparently integrate functional subsystems and application programs. It provides a common and consistent user interface that supports several means of user interaction (e.g., keyboard, mouse, voice, touch, etc.). It must be expandable, modifiable, and provide acceptable interactive performance [3].

## 2. Office Management Software

Even if staff support is more extensive than indicated, executives will still need many standard functions. These include screen editors for word processing and document processing, file and database management, electronic mail, accounting software, scheduling and reminder software, and other subsystems to support operations and administrative management.

## 3. Operational Status and Scheduling Software

In the scenario, information on the status of organizational units is readily available. Software that maintains unit readiness, personnel status, and equipment status information is generally necessary. Project and task scheduling should be supported by the workstation, along with logistics management. While some of this software must be customized for a specific organization, generic software can be developed.

## 4. Graphics Software

User interfaces (screen and/or hardcopy) must be supported by sophisticated graphics. Graphics packages need rules to display data in a manner that best illustrates the objective of retrieving the data. The use of color and three dimensional perspective can help to make the presentation of graphics more useful in the workstation environment.

## 5. Communication and Networking Software

A large number of applications associated with the workstation require automatic communication with other workstations, larger computers, and communication devices. In the scenario, software for various types of communications are integrated into the workstation.

Networking software should be based upon standard network protocols. As noted in the scenario, security can be enhanced by adding encryption/decryption software or hardware to the workstation. The transmission of various types of information as input and output to a workstation may require interface software to translate digital data to voice, graphs, pictures, etc.

## 6. Expert Systems for Planning and Decision Making

An important feature of the executive workstation in the scenario is the software support for planning and decision making. Expert systems are proposed that use knowledge bases and expert rules to support planning and decision making $[9,18]$ . Expert systems will use specific knowledge from other information systems. Short range and long range (strategic) decisions will be tested and supported by using a software expert. DSS will use interactive modelling and simulation programs to test hypothetical situations and potential solutions. Decision aids will include planning checklists, project management tools (e.g., PERT and CPM), cost/benefit analysis charts, spreadsheets and scheduling tables.

Research and development on expert systems is a major impetus for the great promise of executive workstations. Powerful languages, such as Inter-Lisp, Prolog, and Smalltalk [7], can be used to develop software for executive workstations. The structuring of management knowledge into expert systems for specific tasks and environments remains a principal research question [18].

## 4.2. Hardware Specifications

The functional subsystems must be supported by hardware that can enhance software capabilities. Business executives should not be constrained or inconvenienced by inadequate tools. Based upon the software specifications, the following hardware seems necessary.

## 1. Micro-frame Unit

The basic machine should include a 32-bit main microprocessor with subordinate microprocessors for the control of the screen, multiple input and output channels, automatic updating, background expert systems, and memory management. Internal memory should have a minimum of 3 megabytes of RAM and 1 megabyte of ROM, to increase the speed of applications. The unit also needs adequate printer and communications buffers. Sufficient expansion slots should be provided and the components should be easily upgradable to incorporate new hardware and software capabilities. Analog to digital conversion may be required for certain types of sensors [22].

## 2. User Interfaces

Keyboards should be easy-to-use for both typists and non-typists. Research is being performed on more efficient keyboard alignments than the “QWERTY” format. New designs may include non-linear placement of keys to conform better to the hands’ curvature. One-hand typing designs are also being considered.

A number of non-keyboard interfaces need to be supported, for example, a mouse, touch screen or joystick may be preferred. The development of improved user interfaces is continuing. Voice recognition and synthesis units are becoming cost effective for workstation use.

## 3. Display Screen

The display should be a large, easy-to-read screen with high resolution, bitmap capability and fast refresh rate. The display should be capable of showing 60 rows by 132 columns of text. Current technology provides a 25 inch diagonal display with 4096 by 1096 addressable raster points. Large flat screen displays are also needed. Current projection systems are large and bulky, but they will improve.

## 4. User Recognition Unit

A thumb-print or voice-print recognition unit must be an integrated component of the workstation to provide authentication and security. Considerable research and development will be needed to provide an inexpensive and effective recognition unit.

## 5. Storage

A minimum of 35 megabytes of storage is probably needed to support workstation processing. Hard disk, erasable laser disk, and similar secure storage media are preferred to transferable storage media (e.g., floppy disks or cassette tape) for the workstation.

## 6. Communications and Networking

The workstation should include a voice recorder and storage system, an internal modem, a speaker telephone, and an interconnection to one or more local and/or national networks. Development of high speed, high bandwidth local area networks should provide the capabilities of audio, video, and data communication.

## 7. Video Input Camera

A camera should be built into the workstation to provide video teleconferencing and document scanning capabilities.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper outlines general software and hardware specifications for an executive workstation. The specifications were derived from a realistic, futuristic scenario.

The demand for executive workstations is growing rapidly as organizations realize the advantages of providing executives with powerful and flexible decision-making and planning tools. Development of workstations has progressed to the point where several companies are now offering integrated hardware-software systems as office automation workstations. While none of these provide the full functionality proposed, a review of currently operational workstation products indicates the state-of-the-art is developing rapidly [4,8,22].

While there are several products currently available, the gap still exists between state-of-the-art and required executive workstations. Advances in hardware technology allow us to predict that moderately priced equipment that could satisfy the specifications will be available within the next four to six years. The important development breakthroughs must come in providing the software. Integrated operating environments for workstations are now being developed with high resolution graphic interfaces. These must be expanded to include high level DSS and expert system software $[1,12,18]$ . Software advances must be made in the areas of networking, communications, security, and input/output interfacing in order to support the command, communications, and control functions required in an executive workstation. New storage, retrieval, and updating methods need to be found in order to provide reliable and efficient performance from the workstation. Current research projects are studying these software problem areas. We estimate that approximately five to ten years will be needed to develop the software presented in this paper.

Executive workstations will have a major impact on organizational decision making and design [17]. Therefore, managers need to understand and consider the costs, benefits and tradeoffs associated with this powerful tool.
