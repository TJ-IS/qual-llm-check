# A Survey of Data Processing Steering Committees

D.H. Drury

McGill University, Faculty of Management, Samuel Broufman Building, 1001 Sherbrooke Street W., Montreal, PQ, Canada H3A 165

An approach to attempting to plan and control data processing functions is to use a steering committee. The committee composed of user, top management, and data processing representatives, is intended to bring a broad perspective to focus on systems issues.

This paper summarizes the results of a survey on data processing steering committees. The empirical results indicate the actual practices of companies in utilizing steering committees. The reasons for implementing the approach structure, and operating processes are presented. Business experience with the success or failure of these committees is evaluated. The results are interpreted within the context of the current steering committee literature and practices.

Keywords: Steering Committees, MIS Management, Strategic Planning, Data Processing Evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

From the earliest computer applications, the importance of bridging the gap between the significant technological advances in electronic data processing and their implementation in management contexts has been recognized. The frameworks and structures which have been developed over the past several years have tended to emphasize the needs of individuals and their relations to MIS. An important realization is that many decisions concerning MIS directives are made in groups, not individual contexts. While ultimately it is individuals in the organization which are affected, groups in addition to providing liaison functions, also generate policies and practices.

Groups which are concerned with MIS issues and typically composed of management, user, and data processing representatives have generically been referred to as steering committees. The steering committee approach to management has been widely advocated in the systems literature. Foremost among their intended benefits are that steering committees (1) assure that the needs of various corporate functions are properly considered [1], (2) assure management is kept informed of progress and plans in data processing [5], and (3) establish formal communication links between users and data processing personnel [9]. However, there is considerable diversity concerning the nature of the ideal steering committee, its form, and its functions among its proponents. Whether it provides only guidance [4] or makes decisions [2] is an important functional difference. Whether its members are high level [8] or middle managers [3] affects its short or long term perspective. Whether it is composed of major users [6] or only token representation by departments [7] affects its direction. There remain important questions about whether steering committees are effective in promoting mutual understanding and cooperative advancement or whether the technical nature of information processing activities overrides such considerations.

The purpose of this paper is to discuss the functions, composition, effectiveness, and deficiencies of steering committees, emphasizing the actual practices currently employed by firms. The concern is with the composite picture rather than the evaluation of particular organizations or circumstances. This paper is based on the analysis of a questionnaire which was completed and returned by data processing executives from across Canada. In the next section, the sample characteristics are presented which is followed by analytical investigation of results. In the final section, the role of the steering committee approach in achieving MIS objectives is reconsidered.

## 1.1. The Sample

In the initial phases of the study, managers from a group of firms were interviewed concerning their data processing committee structures. The information obtained was supplemented from the descriptive literature and used to construct a questionnaire which focused specifically on steering committees. In the trial stages, various academics and practitioners examined the wording and instructions for clarity and length. In these trials, the questionnaire was adjusted to eliminate inconsistencies and ensure completeness. The final edition of the questionnaire was distributed by mail.

Senior data processing executives completed the questionnaires. The data base consisted of data from 144 companies from a random sample. The firms surveyed were from a wide variety of industries, including manufacturing, distribution, service, utilities, oil, food, and construction. The firms are in the medium to large size category and most are growing. They are split approximately equally between those that operate out of one location and those with branch offices or divisions. Most respondents have used computers longer than ten years and finally, as in the general population, the use of IBM equipment outstrips the total of all others. They represent 63% of the sample.

## 2. Survey of Structure

The sample organizations have had data processing steering committees ranging from one to twenty years. The average period is approximately five years. This duration should be sufficient to assess successes and failures. The reasons for having adopted the steering committee approach have tended to vary between organizations as Table 1 indicates.

The introduction of a steering committee has been primarily influenced by major changes including company reorganizations, and the implementation, introduction or problems with systems. The “other” reasons stem primarily from external rather than internal organizational pressures such as the advice of consultants or auditors. Few organizations depended upon the recommendations of vendors. In general, the introduction of steering committees has been primarily influenced by a major internal or external influences and rarely through planned progression of management practices in data processing.

There are also differences between organizations in the structure and composition of steering committees. The size of the typical committee ranged from three to seventeen persons, including the chairman. The median number of committee members is 6, not including the chairman. The organizational level of the chairman of the steering committee also tended to vary considerably. The distribution of the chairman level appears in Table 3.

Table 3 indicates that the steering committee chairman is usually drawn from higher levels of the organization, and typically from outside of the data processing area. Chairmen who are vice presidents or managers are principally from finance or planning but a diversity of other functions may also occur. In only 17 percent of the cases was the chairman from the data processing function itself indicating that other, high level external influences, are intended to be brought to the steering committee.

The remaining composition of steering committees exhibits diversity between organizations. The major functional areas represented on the committee are (a) user, (b) data processing, (c) controller and (d) other departments or individuals. Table 4 presents the percentage breakdown.

The widest diversity in the number of personnel on the committee occurs with user departments. User departments also have the highest average number of people on the committee (2.56). Data processing departments were represented by typically one or two but usually not more than three individuals. Also, no cases were found where users or data processing were not represented. Most of the organizations. 92%, did include a controller or his representative on the steering committee. In thirty-eight percent of the cases, there was no other type of representation. In the remaining cases, when there were additional individuals, he was usually a senior level person such as vice president or treasurer. If still additional personnel were added, they would typically be scientifically oriented representing research and development, engineering, or manufacturing applications.

The results suggest that the steering committees have significant authority primarily in the strategic policy area such as determining objectives, priorities, and capital expenditures which influences the level and direction of data processing activities in the organization. Being typically composed of users, resolving conflicts is one of their prime functions in generally a longer term framework.

Minor authority functions such as monitoring projects, evaluating applications, and resolving operating problems are all within the management control sphere. These functions are of secondary concern to the steering committee although they are not precluded from steering committee discussion as the need arises.

Finally, in Table 5, the functions over which steering committees have little or no authority, are in the area of data processing operating activities and problems. The specificity and technical detail involved in operating activities would generally, not be compatible with steering committee composition and rationale which is derived more from general representatives rather than a need for specific technical expertise in data processing.

## 2.2. Operating Process

Steering committee operating procedures were also examined. An initial operating procedure which was investigated was the regularity of steering committee meetings. The distribution of questionnaire responses is contained in Table 6.

In more than half of the companies (55.1%), the steering committee meets regularly, usually monthly or quarterly. In general, ad-hoc meeting schedules tend to be reactive rather than proactive. Still, in 44.9% of the cases, the committee meets only on an ad-hoc basis which implies a crisis or events approach to management of data processing in those companies.

Another issue affecting the conduct of meetings is the source of items which are included on the agendas. The focus was on the process by which user issues come to the attention of the steering committee. The major alternatives are contained in Table 7.

It is not the usual practice that the steering committee becomes involved with user/data processing issues in the initial stages. Only 2.6% of the firms adopted the “first step” approach. Usually, steering committee consideration occurs after other approaches towards resolution have been attempted.

A related issue was the primary sources of agenda items to be considered by the steering committee. The most frequent source was from data processing personnel (54%), followed by other committee members, and external parties (26%). The least frequent source was, “all committee members approximately equally”, (20%).

In only 20.8% of the cases did the steering committee make direct public announcements of its decisions and recommendations. The steering committee is usually not the final authority. Instead, 43.9% of the committees report to higher level management committees or executives (31.6%) from which action is taken, if necessary.

While data processing personnel are the major contributors to steering committee deliberations, they do not tend to dominate steering committee decisions. In 43.6% of the cases, decisions were not dominated by any group. However, in the other organizations, decisions tended to be dominated by the senior member of the committee (26.9%), data processing personnel (22.0%), and by users in only 7.7% of the committees. Dominance by a single member of the committee was mentioned as a major problem by 43% of the respondents. Approximately an equal number stated that the major problem with the steering committee was that compromises tend to prevail over the best solution. The least serious of all the problems is that the steering committee tends to make premature decisions (14.0%). Instead, the steering committee operating process suggests that data processing issues are usually aired and deliberated over various time frames within different levels of the organization.

## 3. Steering Committee Effectiveness

A list of advantages of steering committees was obtained from the normative literature which was surveyed in the introduction $[1,2,5]$ . This list was supplemented with information obtained from the pilot interviews and contained in the questionnaire. Responses were made on a five point scale ranging from whether each of the advantages has been successfully or unsuccessfully attained within the organization.

The sample proportion of organizations which fully, or partially, successfully achieved each steering committee advantages was separated from the proportion of organizations which were not successful. The binomial test was used in order to determine whether the successful proportion was statistically greater than the not successful proportion of the sample.

The proposed advantages of steering committees are displayed in Table 8. From the separation contained in Table 8, improved long and short term planning is among the attained advantages of steering committees. The primary advantages, however, are concerned with top management attention, user and data processing awareness and involvement in each other needs and requirements. While in some cases the convergence of technical and non-technical specialists, or suppliers and users of data processing may result in conflict or animosity, the information exchanges in steering committees appear to be beneficial. The steering committee influence on the actual management of data processing in the indeterminate category is more questionable. Resource allocation, support services to users, and the evaluation of alternatives may slightly improve but are probably related more to the circumstances involved that a direct advantage from having a steering committee. As the last or not realized category indicates, the steering committee does not result in improved efficiency of computer operations, better equipment purchases nor measurement of performance of data processing. Proposed advantages in these areas have proved to be very unsuccessful. Instead, the presence of a steering committee puts considerably more emphasis on planning through centralization and the involvement of top management and user groups.

## 4. Discussion

Data processing steering committees have often been normatively defined and occasionally purported to be the ultimate solution to many management problems. In empirically examining current practices, the survey results should provide a guide as what the major alternatives are and the choices which have been made. The primary characteristics examined were structure, composition, operating processes, functions, benefits and problems. However, the usual caveat is warranted since the results are dependent upon sample survey data.

The general observation to be made from examining respondents comments and analyzing their responses is that data processing steering committees serve useful purposes to computer users, data processing, and top management. While many benefits have been realized, others, especially those involving operating problems, have not been. Certainly, there is no hesitation in pointing out that it may be possible to achieve these benefits by other types of committee structures such as project or task force committees involving data processing.

A steering committee in itself has benefits and tends to override many structural questions, but it also can have several problems. For example, while there are benefits in having a chairman from top management or a heavy representation from data processing, there is also a danger in the committee being dominated by them. The committee can be ineffective if forced to deal with operating issues rather than management control or strategic planning issues.

If much of the groundwork is laid for discussion by computer users and data processing personnel before reaching the steering committee, problem definition and resolution can be successfully achieved. Continuity of decision making is more likely to occur with regular meetings rather than crisis or ad hoc approaches. Awareness and involvement are enhanced when steering committee decisions are agreed upon rather than imposed Cognizance of these factors has empirically contributed greatly to attaining the highest benefits.

The study results indicate that steering committees have generally been initiated in response to problems or outside influences rather than planned and progressive management. In spite of these beginnings, they have endured. Until the functions which they perform are replaced by other management techniques or integrative management mechanism, steering committees will continue in existence with the attendant management advantages.
