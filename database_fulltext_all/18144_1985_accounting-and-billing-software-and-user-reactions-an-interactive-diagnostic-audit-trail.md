# Accounting and Billing Software and User Reactions: An Interactive Diagnostic Audit Trail \*

Avi Rushinek

Department of Accounting, University of Miami, P.O. Box 248031, Coral Gables, FL 33124, USA; Tel. (305) 284-5492

Sara F. Rushinek

Department of Management Science and Computer Information Systems, University of Miami, P.O. Box 248237, Coral Gables, FL 33124, USA; Tel. (305) 284-6595

The selection of an accounting and billing software (ABS) system is a complicated process. The overall satisfaction derived from a system depends on many variables. This study analyzes the influence of ABS (predictor) variables on overall satisfaction. It confirms our hypotheses that ease of operation, computer reliability, and ease of programming are the major determinants of overall computer user satisfaction. These factors were used in the design of an ABS diagnostic system based on audit trails (DSAT).

Keywords: Accounting Software, User Reactions, User Ratings Mini, Micro, and Mainframe Computer, Diagnostics, Audit trails, Reliability, Maintenance, User Satisfaction, User Expectations, System Costs, Interactive Online Systems.

## 1. Introduction and Overview

The information obtained by user ratings of accounting and billing software (ABS) systems, could be very useful to buyers and sellers who would like to see some type of rating scale about such systems before deciding which to buy or sell [38].

Traditionally, buyers or sellers who are interested in evaluating overall user satisfaction of a potential new system have two options. One is to study the technical specifications of the different systems and their respective user satisfaction reports. The disadvantages of this option are that the buyer or seller may not have the time, nor the technical expertise to understand the specifications. Moreover, many user satisfaction studies are incomplete, vague, inaccurate, subjective, ambiguous, non-quantitative, and/or too narrow to be statistically generalizable [5,37].

Another option that is available to buyers and sellers is to hire consultants who can understand the technical specifications, discount inaccuracies and subjective judgments of trade publications, and use generic information to make suggestions custom-tailored to a particular installation. The main disadvantage of this option is that such experts are hard to come by, disruptive to the normal operation and rather expensive.

This paper presents another option: a data base system which has tabulated user ratings from online questionnaires and makes direct comparisons to industry standards. The original data, manually collected by impartial company in an extensive survey, is used to analyze variables which most contribute to user satisfaction and to generate a smaller questionnaire for the interactive component. Information in this data base can be made available to prospective buyers, but would remain in place to monitor user satisfaction on an ongoing basis. Utilizing a diagnostic system and audit trails (DSAT), the system could help users isolate problem areas and suggest solutions.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations and Literature Review

Theories of Consumer Satisfaction (CS) suggest that, before purchasing, consumers form expectations concerning future performance of products. After using the products, the consumers compare the actual performance with the expected performance; this leads to confirmation or rejection of their expectations, which may change the level of satisfaction. The greater the confirmation, the higher the satisfaction; this is the case of ABS users $[2,22,23,34,35]$ . Accordingly, the authors hypothesize that a positive relationship exists between the degree of meeting user expectations, and overall user satisfaction.

Traditionally, there was a clear difference between micro, mini, and mainframe computers. But the power and capabilities of ABS have improved over the years; memory costs have reduced and performance distinctions have blurred $[32]$ . This challenges the traditional size distinction. Thus, it is important to investigate whether the traditional characteristics of size actually do have effect on user satisfaction. Thus, one objective of this study is to determine whether the computer size has any effect on user satisfaction.

Choosing the right ABS from the bewildering array of systems, manufactures and different configurations of components can be frustrating and expensive [3]. If the parameters of ABS users' satisfaction were known, then users' satisfaction could be maximized and the frustration level reduced or controlled.

Buyers of ABS systems should look at advantages and/or disadvantages in cost, ease of operation, system reliability, and vendor reliability [7]. Accordingly, the cost should be one of the most important determinants of user satisfaction. This may also suggest the inclusion of criterion variables that indicate the popularity of the vendor. Other such criterion variables include the number of systems, their average useful life, and the number of system users.

Due to rapidly changing technology, management must be willing to commit time to conversion of an outdated system; thus the ease of conversion should be included in the study. One can hypothesize that the easier the conversion, the less annoyed the users. Comparison of ABS systems should be made in the areas of support, ease of operation, compatibility and reliability of the computer, peripherals, compilers, and assemblers, as well as the cost of purchase and operation [9]. Therefore, the authors expect these variables to be positively correlated with satisfaction.

The importance of having a written contract with the vendor has been discussed in the literature $[6]$ . A thorough contract should cover reliability, performance, operating system compatibility, effectiveness, training, costs, and trouble shooting. Some studies cite maintenance, service, education, and documentation as the top concerns. Applications availability and reliability have been next highly rated, with price being the most important criterion after that $[25]$ . Therefore, such criterion variables have been incorporated into the model and are expected to correlate positively with overall satisfaction.

Some research reports that user support (education and documentation) seems to affect satisfaction. Scannell [33] has cited that users find software and support to be major problems. However, complaints that the computer industry does not provide adequate training, documentation, and manuals for users have been rebuffed by industry representatives [17].

Questions have been raised about the effectiveness and responsiveness of traditional system maintenance services $[1,13]$ . The issue of centralization vs. decentralization concerning maintenance contracts has also been addressed $[18]$ . The authors explore a different facet, the impact of the effectiveness and responsiveness of maintenance services on overall user satisfaction.

Another issue is the method of acquisition. According to Kelly [15] the impact of buying or leasing may be substantial. Therefore, these criterion variables are evaluated in the present model.

## 3. Accounting and Billing Software Practice and Theory

Both practitioners and theorists have been struggling with various aspects of ABS systems. Some practitioners have raised perplexing questions [8,10,20].

Choosing an appropriate ABS system and recognizing its limitations have been among the most difficult and confusing tasks for the accounting profession $[4,12]$ . A better understanding of the determinants of ABS user satisfaction could not only partially answer the above questions, but could also facilitate the task of choosing a system and recognizing its limitations.

Theorists have wrestled with the problems from a more scientific point of view. They have examined the demise of generalized audit software packages $[19]$ , evaluated controls in advanced computer systems $[40]$ , and investigated the characteristics of computer controls $[39]$ . Much like the present study, surveys of ABS techniques have been conducted, although they fell short of rigorous, empirical, and comprehensive coverage of ABS user satisfaction.

Meanwhile, strides have been made in developing diagnostic systems through telecommunications as they relate to ABS [11,29–31]. However, as in previous studies the issue of user satisfaction has not been fully addressed. Therefore, the principal objective of this study is to supplement previous studies, and to integrate the issue of user satisfaction into the DSAT model, while building upon prior theoretical work.

## 4 DSAT Design

A less traditional option is to use a diagnostic system (DS) to aid in the evaluation of overall user satisfaction. Audit trails (AT) have facilitated the use of DS by frequent updates to computerized data bases. Although AT have been applied to diagnostic systems $[16]$ , they have not yet been applied to evaluating user satisfaction of ABS systems. The objective of the present study is to apply DS and AT to user satisfaction of ABS systems.

Using AT combined with a computerized DS can help resolve some of the aforementioned problems. In such a system, a summary of industry statistics is placed in a data-base accessible to users, buyers and sellers via telecommunication networks.

A diagnostics program interrogates the users about the status of their ABS system. For each installation, the system accumulates user responses and saves them. Subsequently, a diagnostics program weighs responses, and compares them to the industry standards. Based on these comparisons, the diagnostics program can make specific recommendations based on industry statistical standards but also custom-tailored to a specific ABS installation.

To accomplish the design of a DSAT, several steps are taken: First, theories of consumer satisfaction are reviewed to identify and define their determinants. These, along with the overall user satisfaction, are quantified through a questionnaire. Then determinant scores are regressed against satisfaction scores to compute their respective weights and the industry averages. Subsequently, the model is tested statistically to decide whether it is generalizable to the population of ABS users. Finally, if it is, it can be incorporated into an interactive on-line system.

Such a model compares industry standards (averages) to those of an individual ABS installation. It accumulates the responses, identifies the ABS system weaknesses, rank-orders, and proposes priorities and recommendations for improvements in an audit trail report. In addition, it also updates the data base, measures the adherence to previous recommendations and issues a progress report. This indicates the overall ABS user satisfaction compared to the entire industry.

The above literature review sheds some light on the importance of different criterion variables and their consideration for ABS systems. System rating information could be very useful to managers who are designing the acquisition of ABS systems, as well as to vendors, who must decide which to develop, market, and/or support.

Measurement of ratings is quite complex and requires a selection of various criterion (independent) variables. It also requires an analysis of these to determine how they are related.

## 5. Survey Methodology and Data Collection

This survey was based on result received from questionnaires mailed to a very carefully controlled nth-sampling from randomly drawn subsets of computer user lists. A total of 15,218 questionnaires were sent out. The specific subsets were identified and qualified by a panel of experts. In an effort to improve the response rate, and thereby increase the statistical validity, the users were contacted twice; the first request was followed 8 weeks later by a second request. The response rate was 32%, representing 4,597 users responding to 4,870 questionnaires (some users evaluated more than 1 computer model).

Judges invalidated 379 responses, including 178 users who rated two different computers at the same time; another 43 users rated more than 2 different systems simultaneously. Datapro [38] batched the remaining 4,448 valid returns by vendor, model, users, and computer types [mainframes or plug compatible mainframe computers (maxis), minicomputers and small business computers (minis), and desk-top personal and microcomputers (micros)] as depicted in Table 1.

Each questionnaire allowed the user to rate one system. The recipient was encouraged to reproduce the form if he/she wished to rate more than one system. For each, the responses were averaged and recorded. Labels were used as initial validation vehicles and for identification and elimination of duplicate returns. Recipients were asked to summarize their experiences with the systems currently being used.

## 6. Methods and Procedures

A total of 178 computer systems were represented in the survey. The authors coded and stored the responses to 23 questions (variables). The data were tested for validity and consistency. For example, the percentage values were checked for the range between 0 to 100. Nonresponse bias was evaluated with an F-test and found to be insignificant.

Multiple regression analysis determined the relationship between the overall satisfaction (dependent variable) and the independent criterion variables. Some of the assumptions which must be met: 1. The sample is drawn randomly; 2. The relationships among the variables are linear and additive; 3. The regression assumes a multivariate normal distribution of variables, equal variance, no multicollinearity, and no autocorrelation [14,24].

Viewing the distribution of the residuals (Figure 1) confirms some of the assumptions. The plot suggests that the residuals are randomly distributed, without forming any specific abnormal patterns. The residuals are the deviations of the observed satisfaction scores from the estimated satisfaction scores. They also measure the error component. A direct examination of the residuals can best demonstrate the absence of possible violations of the aforementioned regression assumptions.

In examining the scatterplot, one most important feature is the overall pattern of the scatter. The plot shows the standardized residuals on the vertical axis and the standardized satisfaction scores on the horizontal axis (the variable against which the residuals are being plotted). There are no abnormal patterns (panning-out, diagonal or arched patterns) that emerge. Likewise, the straight band pattern, which indicates relative freedom from abnormalities, most closely resembles the scatter plot. Therefore, the authors infer that the regression assumptions have been met.

The procedure of data collection and data based updates is done through an interactive on-line questionnaire (IOQ) which is a model of the DSAT. This method of data collection avoids the pitfalls of the traditional manual questionnaires. Some of these pitfalls include: (1) incomplete, illegible responses, (2) non-response and sampling bias, (3) low response rate, (4) long elapsed time from distribution to the analysis phase, (5) time consuming, error prone data transcription and key-punch operation, and (6) disruption, resentment and anxiety produced in the respondent and most importantly, (7) ambiguity in questionnaire items. This IOQ controls the above problems by validation procedures, and most importantly, it clarifies ambiguities through help files. A respondent can enter a “?” instead of an answer, to obtain clarification concerning an ambiguous item.

## 7. Objective Problems and Hypotheses

The objective of this analysis is to use this sample data to infer and generalize to the entire population. The procedure is:

1. Estimate the population parameters from the sample regression statistics, and

2. Test the statistical hypotheses about the population parameters.
The difficulties are in:

1. Fully meeting the regression assumptions;

2. Maximizing the overall goodness of fit of the multiple regression equation;

3. Excluding insignificant variables from the model; and

4. Designing, developing, and programming the DSAT model.

The overall test for goodness of fit is based on the values of the coefficient of determination (R square) and multiple correlation coefficient (R).

The hypothesis concerning these can be stated formally as follows:

Null hypothesis (Ho1):

$$
R = 0,
$$

$$
\text { Alternative   hypothesis(Ha1): } \quad R \neq 0
$$

The test for regression coefficients are based on multiple regression coefficients, standardized (Beta) and unstandardized (B). Thus the Beta and B hypotheses can be stated formally as:

Null hypothesis (Ho2):

$$
\text { Beta1 } = \text { Beta2 } = \dots = \text { Beta } i = 0,
$$

Alternative hypothesis (Ha2):

$$
\mathrm{B} 1 \neq \mathrm{B} 2 \neq \dots \mathrm{B} i \neq 0,
$$

or, at least one of the i coefficients is not equal to 0. Statistical significance used in these tests is the 0.05 level.

The ranking of the independent variables affecting the overall satisfaction of ABS users reveals some interesting results. On the one hand, it appears that one standard deviation change in the ease of operation and reliability of the computer (ranked 1 and 2) have the largest effects on the dependent variable. On the other hand, the method of purchase acquisition and microcomputers have the smallest effects.

The majority of variables have expanding (positive) effects on the overall satisfaction except the variables mainframes, minis, microcomputers, number of users, life of computer, responsiveness of the maintenance service, rental from manufacturer, and overpricing of the system had a contracting (negative) effect. Interestingly, the negative coefficient of microcomputers is substantially smaller than minis and mainframes. This may be explained by the greater user control over the microcomputer and lower cost of software per unit, thus the lesser aversion to them [4].

The ABS DSAT interactively interrogates the user about the system. User responses are underlined and recorded anonymously. Subsequently, the DSAT generates the ABS diagnostics audit trail (Figure 3). This report provides immediate feedback. Later, it may also be used by an internal or external auditor, manager, or user for system development. This audit trail compares the user's installation to industry standards based on the frequently updated data-base information.

The ABS diagnostics audit trail sorts the report items in ascending order of the current deviates (the relative weaknesses $(-)$ or strengths $(+)$ of this installation relative to the industry). It generates a current overall user satisfaction score (57.131 in the example), compares it to the prior score (40.141), and computes the gain or loss in overall satisfaction.

This ABS DSAT decomposes the change in overall satisfaction and identifies the sources of the change. Based upon that, it also generates prioritized recommendations for further improvements (Figure 4). The responses of the user, along with the diagnostics audit trail are stored in a transaction file, and eventually merged with the old data-base master file to form the updated master file.

## 10. Summary, Conclusions and Implications

In summary, the multiple regression has been used to study the dependence of overall satisfaction of an ABS system with many ABS variables. The overall significance tests of the goodness of fit of the model have been conducted. The multiple correlation coefficient was 0.905, thus the null hypothesis that the correlation coefficient was zero was rejected. A sample run of the DSAT was illustrated together with an ABS audit trail report and prioritized recommendations.

In conclusion, many independent variables had regression coefficients which were significantly different from zero. The variables were rank ordered according to their BETA values. Ease of operation was ranked the single most important factor for determining satisfaction. Other variables which contributed overwhelmingly were: computer reliability, ease of programming, whether the installation was a mainframe, and effectiveness of the maintenance service. ABS applications invariably have a negative effect on satisfaction. However, this effect diminishes as the applications are down loaded from mainframes into minis and micros. This may indicate more attention should be devoted to ABS user satisfaction, especially for the mainframe computers.

It appears that the satisfaction depends on ease of operation and computer reliability, while leasing from a third party had a minimal effect on overall satisfaction. The variable, acquisition method, did not contribute to any major extent to the overall satisfaction of the computer system. Therefore, it was excluded from the model.

The implications of the present study are many. The overall satisfaction of system users can be measured by answering certain questions. These results can be very useful to system users as well as buyers and vendors. ABS system buyers can compare different variables and thus can calculate the overall satisfaction they would derive by buying the system. The vendors and designers can build ABS systems based on the criteria which are important to users. Thus, they will maximize user satisfaction and eventually increase their sales.

ABS vendors of the computer systems can determine the variables which would increase the overall satisfaction of their products. Thus, they would be more likely to incorporate these features in their systems; this could lead to better ABS systems. Vendors could also use these data as a marketing tool for their products. If their ABS systems have the features, which were highly ranked, they could advertise them and attract additional customers. These kinds of studies could promote vendors who are concerned with user satisfaction, and provide them an advantage over the competition.

Most importantly, this DSAT for ABS provides the buyer or user with an effective tool for system selection and upgrade. Buyers can evaluate potential ABS systems based on their user satisfaction scores, and eventually choose a system that will yield them highest satisfaction. Current users can evaluate the satisfaction at their installation and compare it to market standards, identifying weaknesses and strengths. Moreover, they can apply remedial action to improve their satisfaction and gauge their progress by running the DSAT on a regular interval basis.
