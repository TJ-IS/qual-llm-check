# Perceptions of Equity, Job Satisfaction, and Intention to Quit Among Data Processing Personnel

John E. DITTRICH, J. Daniel COUGER and Robert A. ZAWACKI

College of Business and Administration, and Graduate School of Business Administration, P.O. Box 7150, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

consultant, Dr. Dittrich is also the author of several books and numerous scholarly articles. As a consultant, he has served a wide range of client organizations, including regional hospitals, city and state government departments, and companies such as IBM, Kemper Insurance, ITT, Hewlett Packard, The Hartford Group, and General Mills.

More than 1,200 personnel (963 systems analysts and programmers and 261 operations employees) from nine companies provided questionnaire data on perceptions of fairness (equity), job satisfaction, and expressed intention to quit.

Results disclosed that job satisfaction for both groups is significantly related to perceptions of equity in treatment by the supervisor and the employing organization. Fairness in providing latitude is the most significant individual variable in dealing with job satisfaction for both employee categories. Intention to quit is also strongly related to perceptions of equitable treatment. Fairness in rules for compensation is by far the most significant fairness element relating to intention to quit among both groups.

The research shows that the key factors which affect job satisfaction and intention to quit are under the direct control of management.

Keywords.: Job Satisfaction, Equity, Intention to quit, Data Processing Personnel, Fairness, Data Processing Turnover

## 1. Introduction

Perceptions of inequity have been found to cause a number of attitudinal and behavioral changes among workers. Adams [1] theorized that the presence of inequity causes distress (“an unpleasant emotional state”) and that means of reducing or eliminating the distress of perceived inequity include: a) altering inputs (contributions to the job or organizations in the form of effort, skill, diligence, etc.), b) altering outcomes by petitioning for more compensation, c) cognitively distorting inputs and/or outcomes, d) leaving the field (quitting, transferring, or being absent), e) acting against the comparison person, to change the inputs or outcomes of the other person, or by cognitively distorting the perceptions of the comparison person, f) changing to another comparison person or persons.

While laboratory research related to Adams' equity theory formulation is extensive [3,18,21], field research in equity theory has been very limited. In 1978, in a literature review covering the period 1966 to 1976, 10 field research studies of equity theory were cited [3]. Since 1978, five studies have been reported.

Dittrich and Carrell [6] found significant correlations between employee job satisfaction and perceived fairness in the structure of pay within the organization. In addition, the perceived fairness of the overall level of pay, that is, fairness based on the comparison of one's pay and the pay for similar work in other organizations, was found to have a high and statistically significant correlation with employee absence, and a lower and less significant correlation with employee turnover.

Carrell [2] reported the results of a field experiment in which a number of employees had jobs re-evaluated (upward) and their pay raised as a consequence of the re-evaluation. Pre, post, and longitudinal measurements revealed that the perceptions of the experimental group did not change. Contrary to expectations, control group perceptions of fairness in pay structure dropped, but returned to base-line level after a 9-month period.

Lord and Hohenfeld [9] hypothesized that baseball players perceiving themselves as un-ercompensated as compared to salient others and to themselves in a previous year would produce lower performance. Support for this hypothesis was found in three out of four performance measures (batting average, home runs, and runs batted in).

Neumann [14] examined equity theory as a predictor of pay satisfaction among managers and found that under conditions of moderate and low uncertainty, equity has a stronger relationship with pay satisfaction than does a maximization (of immediate rewards) norm. Where uncertainty is high, maximization has a stronger relationship with pay satisfaction than does equity.

Campion, Lord, and Pursell [4] in a study of 313 production employees found that age, sex (female) and number of employees in the department were positively related to the refusal of promotions. Years of education and pay had a negative relationship to promotion refusal.

The research reported here is an extension of Dittrich and Carrell [6] of 1979, which studied clerks in a municipal office. The present research is based on the perceptions and feelings of an increasingly significant category of employees, data processing professionals (systems analysts and programmers), and data processing operations personnel.

These highly paid creative white collar workers have been in short supply for some time. As a consequence, turnover rates over 20% are not uncommon and data processing managers have expressed considerable interest in improving employee satisfaction and reducing turnover.

The present study not only presents an analysis of two groups of employees quite different from the governmental clerks who provided data for the 1979 study but also extends that work by the application of two additional dimensions of perceived fairness: fairness of the supervisor in permitting subordinates latitude for individual choice and decision making (labeled LATITUDE) and fairness of the supervisors in distributing tasks among subordinates (labeled DISTJOBS). Table 1 is a listing and brief description of the dimensions of perceived fairness.

## 2. Method

The survey was administered to 963 systems analysts and programmers and 261 operations employees from data processing departments in nine companies. Questionnaires included nine items of the Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) [7] and 21 questions from the Organizational Fairness Questionnaire [6]. For this research, perceptions of fairness, in seven dimensions, are the independent variables. Job satisfaction and intention to quit are the relevant dependent variables.

Job satisfaction is measured by items found in Section 4 of the Hackman-Oldham Job Diagnostic Survey [7]. The cautions expressed by Hackman and Oldham [7, pages 313, 314 note 3] and reservations about question wording resulted in the use of only nine job satisfaction questions from the JDS. Questions asking for beliefs about the satisfaction of co-workers, for example, were omitted. The questions used closely approximate dimensions measured by the well validated Job Descriptive Index [16], and were found to be statistically reliable (Cronbach Alpha = 0.8146).

The degree to which employees intend to quit has been used as a convenient, if somewhat less conclusive indicator of turnover in a number of studies: [8,10,11,19]. Mobley [12] maintains that “Empirically, behavioral intention to quit-stay measures appear to be among the best individual-level predictors of turnover” (page 107). For this study, the single item of the JDS which states “I frequently think of quitting this job” was used. This question is measured by responses on a seven point scale ranging from Disagree Strongly (one)

to Agree Strongly (seven).

The seven fairness dimensions have been derived by means of categorizing frequently heard sources of discontent over fairness (e.g., “This supervisor plays favorites. He/She gives all the good jobs to his/her buddies”). Questions were then developed to measure these feelings, and factor analysis was used to assure that the dimensions retained their independence. Three questions in each dimension were used as a shortened version of the original fairness questionnaire. Eighteen of these 21 questions provided the most acceptable internal reliability coefficients for the seven dimensions. Alphas ranged from a low of 0.6259 for LATITUDE to a high of 0.8439 for PAYADMIN. Dimensions, sample questions and reliability coefficients can be found in Appendix I.

For data analysis, two approaches were chosen. First, as in the earlier Dittrich-Carrell research, stepwise multiple regression of fairness variables on employee satisfaction and employee intention to quit was used to assess the combined strength of fairness variables as predictors. $^{1}$

Secondly, all variables in the regressions on Intention to Quit and on Job Satisfaction were then tested for relative importance, by examining the change in sum of squares as each variable was removed, in turn, from the total set of variables in the regression equation. The extent of the change in sum of squares as each variable is removed from the regression serves as a general indicator of the relative importance of variables in their relationship to employee satisfaction and intended turnover. Precision in the weighting of the importance of variables is only possible where independent variables are completely independent, a condition not found here. The relative size of the change in sums of squares, however, should serve to guide managers toward improvements in satisfaction and turnover performance. $^{2}$

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Job Satisfaction

The results of stepwise multiple regression of fairness dimensions on employee job satisfaction for the two sets of data (963 Professionals and 261 Operations Employees) can be seen in Table 2.

Perceptions of fair treatment strongly and significantly contribute to feelings of job satisfaction. Six of the seven fairness variables significantly affected job satisfaction for analysts and programmers, while only four significantly affected job satisfaction for operations staff. LATITUDE, the fairness of the supervisor in permitting personal planning, and decision making, accounted for most of the variance in job satisfaction and entered the stepwise regression equation first. PAYRULES, perceptions of fairness of one's pay relative to co-workers and of the rules for pay and promotions, entered second in both the equation for analysts and programmers, and in the equation for operations personnel.

Individually, the fairness variables display a wide range of importance in their relationship to job satisfaction, as evidenced by the change in variance as variables are removed from the regression equation (Table 3). For analysts and programmers, LATITUDE, PAYRULES and PAYLEVEL, in that order, clearly affect the general satisfaction of these highly paid professionals much more than do the other four variables.

For operations staff, the data in Table 2 clearly shows that LATITUDE's importance is masked by the entry of DISTJOBS. The latter is also a highly significant variable in its relationship to job satisfaction.

## 4. Discussion

The 1979 study by Dittrich and Carrell found a multiple correlation coefficient of 0.563 on employee job satisfaction for two of the five fairness variables developed at that time. In the present study, operations and employees correspond most closely to the governmental clerks who were subjects in the 1979 research. Looking at Table 2, we again see that perceived fairness has a significant relationship to Job Satisfaction (r = 0.632, p < 0.001), consistent with the finding of the earlier research.

In the 1979 study, the relationship between the fairness variables and actual departmental turnover was found to be strongest (although not statistically significant) for PAYLEVEL (r = -0.424) and PAYADMIN (r = -0.252).

In the present study, again examining data for operations personnel (Table 4), we see a significant relationship $r = 0.387, p < 0.001$ between perceived fairness and intention to quit. Again, these findings are consistent with the 1979 study. In this research, however, we find (in Table 5) that PAYRULES clearly has the strongest relationship to intention to quit rather than PAYLEVEL, as in 1979.

No obvious or simple explanation exists for this difference. Turnover rates are strongly affected by such extra-person factors as 1) opportunity for transfer within the firm, and 2) availability of employment elsewhere. In addition, personal financial circumstances and differences in an individual's reluctance to change employment relationships can also affect turnover. All of these factors tend to results in relatively low correlation coefficients in a wide range of studies relating employee attitudes to turnover [12, page 107].

One possible explanation may be that municipal clerks are not only paid a much lower salary than data processing operations staff, but also are part of a less active and less homogeneous labor market. Economic need, and a less standardized price for clerical skills may be reasons why the metropolitan clerks would respond more quickly to perceptions of a lack of fairness in the level of pay than might the data processing operations personnel.

A second possible explanation for these differences may lie in the work schedules of each group. The municipal clerks worked a conventional nine to five daylight hours work schedule. Data processing operations personnel are scheduled for work around-the-clock, and many hold second jobs during off-shift hours. One can reasonably suggest that the presence of a second job would make less distressing the perceptions of a lack of fairness in pay level. Holding a second job might also slow the search for another primary job, particularly where a move to a new location is involved. Firm conclusions on these speculations, however, must be reserved for additional study.

For managers of data processing personnel it seems clear that fair treatment is significantly related to job satisfaction and to intention to quit. Comparisons across the two groups in this study may also be of interest and use. For analysts and programmers (the more highly paid group) the fairness of supervisors in permitting individual choice and decision making (LATITUDE), fair rules for rewards and a fair reward structure (PAYRULES), and the perceived fairness of pay relative to external comparison persons or jobs, (PAYLEVEL), are extremely important in day to day management because of their impact on employee job satisfaction. (See Table 3).

Latitude for free choice is a job design dimension which can be used for increasing job scope in job enrichment programs because of its impact on Autonomy, Task Identity, and to a lesser extent Skill Variety – three of the five dimensions included in the Job Diagnostic Survey [7]. From research on job design conducted by Couger and Zawacki [5] it has been found that systems analysts and programmers have a measured need for personal growth (Growth Need Strength, or GNS) higher than any other occupational group measured to date. The effect of supervisory treatment in permitting latitude would, therefore, have a heightened impact on the character of work for these high GNS employees, and thereby contribute very directly to their sense of job satisfaction. Attention to fairness regarding latitude, it seems clear, should be materially helpful in maintaining and building job satisfaction.

Fairness in relative pay (compared internally) and in the rules for granting pay increases an promotions (PAYRULES) is not only second in importance as it relates to job satisfaction, but also is clearly the most significant element in predicting intention to quit (and thereby turnover) among analysts and programmers. It is important for the data processing manager to know that employee satisfaction and turnover performance can be improved by assuring that the structure of compensation is not only fair in objective terms, but is seen by employees as fair. More carefully developed job evaluations in data processing with more extensive involvement in job evaluation by data processing personnel would directly help to achieve both of those objectives.

For operations personnel the implications for management are somewhat less clear. Job satisfaction is affected most strongly by fairness in distributing work (DISTJOBS), and by fairness in choice and decision making (LATITUDE). Fairness in the structure and rules for rewards (PAYRULES) is also very important. The supervisor can affect perceptions of fairness in the structure of pay (PAYRULES) indirectly, through attention to job evaluation and by communication with employees. The most important fairness variables, however, are directly under the control of the supervisor. The technology aspect of computer operations activities has resulted in highly structured and fairly specialized tasks with tight work schedules. The ability of the supervisor, therefore, to distribute tasks differently, or to grant additional latitude, is unfortunately much more restricted than that of the systems supervisor. Upon reflection one might suppose that if inequities do exist in the distribution of tasks and the delegation of decision making for operations staff, that the rigidity of schedules, job assignments, and supervisory approaches may in effect institutionalize inequities, a state which not only makes correction more difficult but one which but leads to an accumulation of inequity perceptions. A careful search for sources of these forms of perceived inequity seems very worthy of further attention in improving employee satisfaction among operations and clerical personnel.

Since PAYRULES is the most important single predictor of intention to quit among operations personnel the necessity of providing managerial attention to the rules for raises and promotions and to the wage structure should be clear to managers of operations departments. Additionally, we may be seeing evidence that the compensation programs and the compensation factors used for job evaluation in work populations at large are not satisfactory (or not satisfactorily adjusted or applied) for jobs in the rapidly developing occupational group of data processing.

## 5. Conclusion

Recently a DP manager commented to one of the authors, “I wish that I had more control over the factors affecting my employees – I am hamstrung by bureaucratic procedures”. This study shows that the factors which most significantly impact job satisfaction and intention to quit are under the direct control of managers.

For both operations and programmer/analysts personnel, latitude is a key predictor of job satisfaction. Equity in payrules (rules used to give pay raises) is by far the most significant predictor of intention to quit, for both categories of DP personnel.

The findings reported here reinforce the concepts of Critical Success Factors (CSF). Managers can concentrate on those factors most critical to DP employees' perceptions of equity. Without the need to devote equal attention to all seven fairness factors, managers have more time to spend on other elements critical to success, e.g., acquiring technical resources, systems planning, and the solution of other technical problems.
