# An Empirical Study of Occupational Stress, Attitudes and Health Among Information Systems Personnel

John M. Ivancevich,

University of Houston, Central Campus, Houston, TX 77004, USA

H. Albert Napier

Jones Gr. School of Adm., Rice University, P.O. Box 1892, Houston, TX 77251, USA

and

James C. Wetherbe,

Management Info Systems Research Center, School of Management, University of Minnesota, 269 19th Ave. South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

This paper investigates occupational stress, Type A behavior pattern, work attitudes, health symptoms, and health behaviors among information systems personnel. Hundreds of research studies have been conducted on the stress associated with working in various occupations. Unfortunately, information systems is one occupation that has not been included in these stress studies. The present study investigated the stress, work attitudes, and health behaviors of 446 information systems personnel employed in 18 different organizations. Type A behavior pattern was found to be a significant moderator for some of the stressor-criterion associations. The findings suggest that more managerial understanding of person-environment fit in general and the individual employee's predisposition toward the Type A behavior pattern specifically may be beneficial in attempting to initiate, nurture, and sustain a productive and healthy work atmosphere.

Keywords: Occupational Stress, Type A Behavior Pattern, Tension Discharge, Health Symptoms, Individual Differences, Information Systems Personnel, Stress

## 2. The Stress Concept

The theoretical and empirical literature suggests that occupational stress is not a characteristic of either the work environment or the person, but is the consequence of the interaction of these two. Lazarus [23] emphasized that it is an individual's perception of a situation, the "cognitive appraisal", that defines a situation as stressful; the individual's response depends on the appraisal. A person's appraisal can be irrelevant, benign positive, or stressful. In an irrelevant appraisal, the person's encounter with the situation (e.g., job, environment) has no impact. In benign-positive appraisals, the person's encounter is construed as positive. When a stress appraisal is made, the person perceives harm, threat, or challenge.

Stress occurs when a person feels he or she has been exceeding the resources and endangering well-being. Consequently, when an information systems employee's environmental demand exceeds his or her capability, stress results. Figure 1 presents the person-environment framework used to examine potential stressor-stress-outcome linkages among information systems personnel [39]. Stressors are any potentially stressful environmental elements. Stressors are appraised by an individual and he or she determines whether they are stressful or not. Certainly not all stress is bad for an employee; however, the issue of bad versus good stress is not addressed here.

A fit between a person and the environment implies conditions that an information systems employee would appraise as harmonious or “low in stress” [9]. In such a condition, it is suggested that the information system employee would report low job-related tension, high levels of organizational commitment, and high job satisfaction [4,6,18]. This type of appraisal would result because of the match between personal characteristics and the environment (stressors).

## 3. Type A Behavior Pattern: A Description

One personal characteristic which has been found to be associated with increased coronary disease is the Type A behavior pattern (TABP) [12]. Friedman and Rosenman's pioneering work [12] resulted in the following definition of the TABP: An action-emotion complex observed in any person aggressively involved in a chronic, incessant struggle to achieve more and more in less and less time, and often, against the opposing efforts of other things and persons.

The TABP is a constellation of three interrelated concepts – a set of beliefs about oneself and the world; a set of values converging in a pattern of motivation or commitment (e.g., hard-driving or job involvement); and a behavioral life style (e.g., impatience, time urgency). Although researchers have emphasized one or the other of these, these should be regarded as interdependent psychological facets of the same phenomenon [24]. TABP is a set of overt behaviors that is elicited from susceptible individuals by an appropriately challenging environment (e.g., work, family, community); it is a continuum of behaviors ranging from the hard-core and pronounced (Type A) to what is called Type B (TBBP), a less intense, hard-driving, and competitive behavior pattern [24,35].

Determining which information systems employees possess the TABP is particularly relevant, because Type A's:

1) are two to six times more likely to die from coronary heart disease (controlling for other risk factors) than their Type B counterparts [38];

2) live a life which appears to be less joyous [12];

3) are less content and have significantly greater feelings of being locked into their jobs [17];

4) report higher rates of respiratory, acute gastrointestinal, and non-specific viral illnesses and injuries [36]; and

5) report higher levels of stress symptoms [21].

## 4. Occupational Stress and Type A

Since the TABP was implicated as a major factor associated with coronary heart disease, negative job-related attitudes, higher incidences of illness, and dissatisfaction, researchers began to examine its relationship to stress. One of the earliest attempts was conducted by the Institute for Social Research at the University of Michigan [4]. In this study, 23 occupational groups from 67 different sites were selected to provide a wide range of stress situations. Physicians, scientists, air-traffic controllers and managers were administered a questionnaire designed to measure stress and TABP. The results showed that administrators and physicians scored highest on the TABP scale, but there was no relationship found between TABP and such behavioral correlates of stress as work overload, job satisfaction, and somatic complaints. It should be noted that information systems personnel were not included in the occupational groups studied.

In a study of company managers, Howard, Cunningham and Rechnitzer [17] found differences in the work habits of Type A and other managers. The extreme Type A's indicated that they worked longer days, worked more extra hours, and reported less positive feelings about the job, with Type A's having significantly higher blood pressures, serum cholesterol levels, and smoking.

A study of 339 managers examined Type A behavior as a moderator between stressors and various psychological and physiological outcomes [18]. The high Type A managers were more adversely affected by such stressors as work overload, lack of career progression, and role conflict. A second part of the study examined the role of the TABP in the occupational stress-outcome linkage among 57 registered nurses. The Type A nurses reported significantly higher levels of work overload and role conflict and showed higher serum cholesterol and systolic blood pressure levels. They also indicated significantly lower levels of intrinsic job satisfaction.

This brief sample of findings suggest that occupational stress may have significant implications for the health, attitudes, and physiological reactions of Type A information system personnel, with higher levels of stress, increased physiological reactivity, and coronary heart and other diseases [6], as well as accidents, peptic ulcers, and migraine headaches [20]. Since no research involving stress, TABP, and outcomes among information systems personnel has been reported in the literature, only one exploratory hypothesis was used to guide the investigation. It was stipulated as follows:

Hypothesis 1: Among information systems personnel the Type A behavior pattern will be a significant moderator between work environment stressors and outcomes. Therefore, Type A information systems personnel, when compared to Type B counterparts, will report significantly:

a) higher levels of stress

b) more job-related tension

c) less ability to discharge job-related tension

d) lower levels of job satisfaction

e) higher levels of organizational commitment

f) fewer health disorders

g) more severe health disorders

## 5. Method

Sample. - Information systems personnel from 18 companies were asked to complete a questionnaire called the MIS Survey. A total of 950 survey forms were distributed and 580 usable questionnaires $(61\%)$ were returned. The respondents in the study ranged in age from 19 to 68 years with a mean age of 34.4 years. Approximately $80\%$ of the respondents were systems programmers, systems analysts, and programmers. The average time with present employers was 8.3 years.

Stress Scale. - A survey scale was devised specifically for information systems personnel to assess their: 1) appraisal of stressors; 2) job attitudes; and 3) health symptoms and behaviors. The stress scales in the questionnaire (called the MIS Survey) were partially developed using a pilot test: One hundred information system personnel were asked to indicate which categories of stressors on a list were most stressful to them in performing their jobs. This procedure permitted the researchers to acquire an occupation-based set of stressors. The pilot test ratings of stressors were used as the basis for developing the scales. The 16 highest ranked stressors served as the a priori categories for which a 62-item, 7-point MIS survey was developed.

Individual Behavior Activity Pattern (IBAP). - Classification of subjects' behavior pattern was determined by the (IBAP), a 32-item scale developed as a self-report measure of TABP. It consists of 32 pairs of statements with one member reflecting a Type A behavior and the other the equivalent Type B behavior (e.g., "I tend to wear myself out by trying to do too much" or "I like to get things done, but not at the expense of exhausting myself").

The IBAP has been used in a study of 144 hospital employees and correlated 0.59 with the Jenkins Activity Survey $[18,19,34]$ . The IBAP was also compared to the Structured Interview Method of assessing TABP $[18]$ . There was 82.7% agreement between A and B classifications made by the two methods. In another study $[34]$ the IBAP correlated 0.64 with the Type A scale developed by House $[16]$ .

Factor Analysis. - Since the stressor of the MIS Survey was specifically developed for information systems personnel, it was necessary to first examine the psychometric quality of the scales. Subject responses for the 62 stressor-items on a 1 to 7 scale tapping the 16 a priori categories were separately intercorrelated, factor-analyzed by the principal components method, and rotated by the varimax procedure [29]. All factors with eigenvalues greater than one were rotated. The final factor solution generated seven factors (see Table 1).

Other parts of the MIS Survey were subjected to factor analysis and in each case the a priori constructs were supported and verified. Each of these factors will be briefly discussed.

Job-Related Tension. - The 15-item, 5-point Likert scale Job-Related Tension Index (JRT) was originally developed to assess role strain [25]. Previous research indicates that the JRT taps such strain as problems arising out of the necessity of exerting influences without legitimate authority and direct conflicts with others.

Tension Discharge Index. - The Tension Discharge Index is a six-item scale to determine the rate at which subjects discharge job related tension (e.g., I stay in "higher gear" and have trouble relaxing once I leave work). The Type A person has more difficulty in discharging tension [36].

Job Satisfaction. - Job satisfaction was measured using the 20-item Minnesota Satisfaction Short Form [40]. In this study job satisfaction is defined as a set of attitudes an employee has toward various dimensions of the job. Each item has a 5-point rating scale ranging from very satisfied (5) to very dissatisfied (1).

Organizational Commitment. - Organizational commitment was measured by a 15-item, 7-point

Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). This scale attempts to assess an employee's feeling of commitment to the organization [33].

Health Symptoms and Disorder – The number of health symptoms and disorders experienced by the subjects over the past six months were assessed. A list of 28 disorders or symptoms (e.g., temper tantrums, lack of energy, periods of depression, dizziness, and loss of appetite) were presented. The subjects indicated which of these they had experienced. A summated count yielded a frequency count.

The severity of each disorder or symptom was rated on a (1) slightly bothersome to (4) totally disabling scale. A multiplicative score was derived. For example, if seven disorders were experienced and the severity rate for four of the disorders/symptoms averaged 2.0, while the severity rate for the remaining three disorders/symptoms averaged 4.0, the score would be calculated $(4 \times 2.0) + (3 \times 4.0) = 20.0$ . The higher the total score, the greater the health problem being experienced.

## 6. Results

The information systems personnel responses were first categorized as Type A or Type B using responses on the Individual Behavior Activity Profile. The sample was split into a highest score group (40%), a middle group (20%), and the lowest score group (40%). The highest score group (n = 221) was designated Type A, and the lowest score group (n = 225) was designated Type B. These two groups were used for statistical analysis. The middle group, because subject scores indicated both Type A and Type B patterns, were not included in the data analysis. This type of partitioning was suggested by Zedeck [41] in order to give more rigor to the analysis.

The means and reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha) of job stressors and criterion variables are presented in Table 1. An interesting finding is the difference between Type A and Type B respondents, especially with regard to time pressures/work overload, work relationships, job-related tension, tension discharge, and health disorders and severity.

Type A's reported significantly higher levels of time pressure/work overload and work relationship pattern stress than did their Type B counterparts. Thus, hypothesis 1a is only partially supported. For five of the seven stressor categories no statistically significant differences were found, although on four of five of the non-significant stressor categories Type A's reported higher scores.

It is also interesting to note that the degree of stress appraisal by the information systems respondents is slight to moderate. The highest average stress being reported by Type A's is for time pressure/work overload (m = 30.7, average/item = 3.1) and communication (m = 9.2, average/item = 3.1). Likewise the highest average stress reported by Type B's is for communication (m = 8.9, average/item 3.0) and rewards (m = 19.9, average/item 2.8).

The results indicate that the Type A's perceive significantly more job tension than Type B's (m = 38.1 versus m = 35.3). Thus, hypothesis 1b is supported. Also it was determined the Type A's report significantly more difficulty releasing job tension. This finding supports hypothesis 1c.

Significant differences were not found for the job satisfaction or the organizational commitment comparisons. Therefore, hypothesis 1d and 1e are rejected. Note that both the Type A and Type B respondent aggregate means on job satisfaction are below the median of 80.0 for the scale ( $m_{A} = 72.6$ and $m_{B} = 72.0$ ). This indicates a relatively low degree of job satisfaction being reported by the total sample of information systems personnel.

The health disorder and severity data are also summarized on the basis of the Type A B partition in Table 1. The Type A's reported significantly fewer disorders than Type B's (m = 10.9 : m = 13.8). However, the degree of severity of disorders reported by Type A's was significantly higher.

Previous research suggests that Type A individuals do not report disorders as frequently as Type B's during performance of a task [5,6]. Matthews and Brunson [28] have suggested that Type A individuals report less symptomatology during performance because they focus their attention on salient environment and thereby suppress attention to peripheral events. Lack of symptom reporting may contribute to risks associated with TABP. For example, Type A individuals may be less likely to seek medical care or to rest when they are experiencing symptoms associated with various disorders.

The data presented in Table 1 supports hypothesis lf. This finding was also found in studies of other occupations. For example, Kobassa [22] studied a sample of middle- and upper-level executives. She found a significant positive relationship between life stress and self-report illness-symptom reporting. High stress/low illness executives described themselves as possessing many of the characteristics associated with Type A behavior. They were actively involved with and vigorous toward the environment, were highly self-involved, and felt powerful.

Subgroup analysis was performed in order to test the moderating effects of TABP and is presented in Table 2. This technique is used when the proposed moderator variable discriminates among relatively discrete groups such as TABP or TBBP. Only correlations within the top 40% and bottom 40% on the IBAP scale were compared in order to give more rigor to the analysis. The top 40% is the TABP group and the bottom 40% is the TBBP group.

Out of a possible 42 comparisons between TABP and TBBP, the Type A variable significantly moderated 16 (approximately 38%). According to the test suggested by Brozek and Tiede [3], the probability of this number of differences occurring by chance is less than 0.0005.

## 7. Discussion

As already indicated, the literature presently lacks studies that investigate the role that individual differences such as Type A behavior patterns play in the stressor-criterion association among information systems personnel. Instead of applying to information systems personnel available occupational stress research results based on such diverse samples as air-traffic controllers, nurses, medical technologists, executives, and police officers, it is more reasonable and relevant to focus specifically on an information systems sample [31]. The findings of the present study suggest potential areas for managerial attention and for further investigations of information systems personnel.

The findings indicate that seven job-related stressors have the most potential for being appraised as stressful in the work environment of the information systems personnel included in the present study. Previous stress research using other occupation groups identified role conflict, people responsibility and the political climate as the most pressing and important stressors $[2,7,8,15]$ . The pilot test and present findings using information systems personnel suggest other kinds of stressors as being significant.

The present findings indicate that the Type A factor presented in Figure 1 (TABP) should be considered by MIS managers when addressing information systems employees' stress, attitudes, health symptoms, and health behaviors. The findings of the present study in conjunction with other research findings present a rather "gloomy" picture of TABP information systems personnel. The TABP employees appear to be experiencing more adverse consequences of job stress than TBBP counterparts. Researchers and MIS managers interested in the responses of information systems personnel are encouraged to continue to identify:

1) Those occupation specific stressors in the work environment that are stressful,

2) TABP predispositions, and

3) the health and illness consequences associated with TABP among information systems employees.

The results suggest that determination of a person's Type A predisposition is necessary before developing any particular intervention program designed to improve the person-environment fit. As the research results point out, TABP information systems personnel view their work environment somewhat differently than their Type B counterparts. Initiating the same intervention approach for all information systems personnel when attempting to improve the person-environment fit would probably be inappropriate.

The present study did not examine physiological outcomes or organizational performance outcomes. Still unanswered about information systems personnel are such questions as these: Do TABP information systems personnel indicate poorer physiological indicators such as serum cholesterol, triglycerides, and blood pressure than their Type B counterparts? In what type of work environment (rigid, placid, harmonious, uncertain, turbulent) are these physiological indicators the poorest? Are the TABP information systems personnel better organizational performers (e.g., quantity, quality, cost containment) than their Type B counterparts? Do TABP information systems personnel advance faster in their profession than their Type B counterparts?

## 8. Concluding Remarks

This initial research step has called attention to the appraisal of job-related stress among information systems personnel who are today an extremely significant occupational group. As we move into the era of increased application of information technology the understanding of the person-environment fit among information systems personnel becomes even more crucial for coping with the rapid pace of the use of information technology. The pace of change will require astute, timely, and courageous decisions by MIS managers about creating the most productive work environments to fit the personnel characteristics of information systems personnel. This study suggests that it is important to continue rigorously and thoroughly studying the TABP information systems employee.
