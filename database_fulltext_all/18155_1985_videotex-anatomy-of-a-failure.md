# Videotex: Anatomy of a Failure

A. Michael Noll

Annenberg School of Communications at the University of Southern California, University Park, Los Angeles, CA 90089-0281, USA; tel: (213)743-7415

Considerable excitement has been generated in Europe and North America in videotex – namely, the concept of electronic access in the home to centrally – located computerized databases containing vast amounts of data. However, the public offering of such services has thus far been unsuccessful in achieving significant penetrations in the residential market.

Videotex is examined in this paper to illuminate possible reasons for its failure thus far in the residential marketplace. Comparisons are made with somewhat similar on-line systems for accessing computerized data-bases and for performing transactions and transmitting messages to other people. The final conclusion is that most consumers simply do not have a need nor a desire to access vast computerized data-bases of general information. Videotex or any other similar system that attempts to satisfy consumer needs for information in such a fashion will surely fail.

Keywords: videotex, viewdata, teletext, electronic mail, database access, information systems.

## 1. Introduction

Considerable attention has been generated throughout the world in the concept of computerized public-information services, particularly as implemented about five years ago by the British Post Office in England. These services consist of a network of regional computerized data-bases which are accessed over telephone lines from terminals that utilize home TV sets for display. The content of the data-base is organized in the tree fashion, and searches through the data-base are made by pressing numbers on a hand-held numeric keypad. The information is a frame of textual and graphical content in color. These services are known generically as videotex, or viewdata, and the specific service offered by the British is called Prestel.

The Prestel videotex service was launched commercially about six years ago with much fanfare, but success if defined as significant penetration and use by consumers has been elusive for the British market. Videotex itself has undergone changes over the years and is presently available commercially or as a trial service in a number of other countries. Adapters for personal computers are now available. A variety of new display standards have appeared offering enhanced graphics capabilities. In some countries, the term videotex has been so broadened and diffuse in its definition that virtually any and all forms of time-shared data-base access – and even such services as centralized fire and burglar alarm services – are encompassed.

This paper explores the history and hype surrounding videotex in an attempt to illuminate the reasons for its failure thus far. The British experience is particularly relevant since it is based on over five years of actual commercial availability and hence is examined in some detail.

## 2. Videotex in England

The results of the British experience with its Prestel viewdata service are both disturbing and enlightening. After two years of initial commercial operation, well over \$50 million in development and other expenses, hundreds of information providers, and content of nearly 200,000 frames of information, Prestel achieved penetration in only a little more than 1,000 British homes [1]. Although the total number of Prestel terminals, including business and residential use, had increased to about 13,000 terminals at the end of 1981, this was called a “pathetic performance” when measured against a forecast made by the British Post Office in 1978 of 1 million users by the end of 1981 [2]. One major consulting firm considered the performance of Prestel to be a “dismal failure” [3]. The situation has improved somewhat, and it is reported that Prestel had 42,000 terminals in mid-1984 and 40 percent of these were in homes [4]. However, this number falls far short of predictions, and given the large investment in funds and time to achieve even this number, Prestel most certainly cannot be considered a success.

From the beginning, the British were able to attract a considerable number of firms who were eager to provide information for storage in the Prestel data-base. To a large extent, the enthusiasm of these information providers appears to have been responsible for the Post Office's launch of a public offering in 1979. However, as was later discovered, just because information is provided does not mean that the residential consumer is willing to pay to retrieve that information! The Prestel experience demonstrated once again that there “... are limits to the scope for forcing new products on consumers” [5,6].

The exact causes of the failure of Prestel to achieve significant penetrations in British homes are not known, but there are many factors that might have influenced the negative outcome. At the beginning, there was a serious shortage of sets that resulted in something of a bad start for Prestel [7].

Another potential problem was cost. A color Prestel set cost £1.100 or more [8] and leased for about £25 per month. The charges for accessing the data-base ranged from nothing to about 50p per frame in addition to a time charge of 3p per minute during the day and 1p per minute at other times [9,10]. The telephone call to the data-base was billed at standard usage-sensitive pricing.

Newer sets cost less, and relatively inexpensive adapters that cost under L200 appeared [11]. However, a past Director of Prestel did not believe that cost was the main obstacle to success. He believed that “graphics may be a snare and a delusion” and that such non-information-retrieval services as user-to-user messages are important for success $[12]$ . It was observed that the crudeness of the graphics might be a drawback along with the inability to use existing computer terminals with the Prestel system $[13]$ .

In addition to the preceding, there are many other factors that might have led to Prestel's lack of significant penetration thus far in the British residential market. The home TV set might place the user in too passive a mood for the active interaction required by Prestel [14]. The large size of the data-base – over $\frac{1}{4}$ million frames – might be incomprehensible to the average consumer. The supposedly user-friendly tree searches might be too time consuming and frustrating to use. The differences and variation of approaches among the information providers in format, updating, and frame layout might be too confusing.

The British Post Office, now British Telecom, has sunk such vast resources into Prestel that it is very difficult to admit defeat. The British are in the position of having a product in search of a market – a situation that has been likened to the Picturephone (TM) system [15].

Since Prestel appeared to be more acceptable to the business community, British Telecom accordingly began to shift the emphasis of their marketing program to more specialized data-base services targeted to the needs of specific business segments $[16]$ . However, it should be remembered that the British were interested in the business community from the very beginning $[17]$ . Early in 1982, it was reported that “because of its lack of success in selling viewdata to businesses, British Telecom has decided to return to its original aim of selling Prestel mainly to private homes” $[18]$ .

Whether all these “new” changes in marketing and evolutions in the definition of videotex will cure Prestel’s ills remains to be seen.

## 3. Teletext: A Success In England

But whatever the exact causes of Prestel's failures, the exact causes for the apparent, though limited, success of teletext in England are equally baffling. As of mid-1981, there were about 160,000 teletext-equipped TV sets in use in England [19].

By mid-1984, this number had grown to more than one million and was growing at a rate of about 45,000 new teletext-equipped TV set sales per month [4].

As opposed to Prestel viewdata service with its more than $\frac{1}{4}$ million frames of general information, the British Ceefax and Oracle teletext systems offer only a few hundred frames of information each. Perhaps this limited content is easier to comprehend for the consumer.

Teletext transmits its content during the vertical retrace interval of the conventional broadcast TV signal along with the normal TV signal. Teletext reception is free as opposed to Prestel which charges for usage and content. This too could be yet another reason for the success of teletext in British homes.

In many ways, teletext and videotex are very similar. They both emphasize information retrieval, utilize the home TV set for display, and offer frames of information in color with graphics. Yet teletext and videotex are dissimilar in apparently significant ways. Teletext reception is free as opposed to Prestel which charges for usage and content. The size of the data-base transmitted by teletext is considerably smaller than the vast amount of information contained in the Prestel data-base. That one should succeed and the other be a relative failure is a statement of residential market need that cannot be easily ignored or dismissed.

## 4. Replication of the British Videotex Experience

The Prestel experience might somehow be unique to the British market, and more positive results might be expected in other countries. However, similar negative findings were experienced in Germany where the German Post Office has been offering a viewdata system since June, 1980. German consumers have also shown a lack of interest in the system [20]. Furthermore, the Swedish government concluded that it was not considered probable that videotex would gain a widespread acceptance among the general public during the eighties [21]. Thus the negative experience in England is not unique.

Similar problems have occurred in the United States and Canada with videotex. A few years ago, Knight-Ridder's Viewdata Corporation of America announced with much enthusiasm its plans to launch a market test of a videotex service in Southeastern Florida in 1983 [22]. The information in the electronic data-base was supplied by Viewdata Corporation of America (VCA) and a number of information providers that VCA had assembled. The home terminal was manufactured by AT&T Consumer Products who had worked jointly with VCA in developing the service. As might be expected, given the failure of videotex thus far, the service failed to achieve the targeted 4,500 subscribers in the first year [23], and the 2,800 who actually signed up probably included a fair number of AT&T competitors, consultants, and friends of the information providers. Knight-Ridder's response was to fire 20% of the VCA staff and cut spending [24].

The lack of consumer response to videotex has also occurred in Canada where videotex trials and services have been offered for the past few years. As a result of poor market performance, the commercial videotex service being offered by Teleglobe, Canada's international telecommunications carrier, was shut down in mid-1984 [25]. Most other Canadian activities in videotex have either ended or are about to end.

The French have been promoting the use of a videotex-like service to replace paper telephone directories. Thus far, the use of the service by French telephone subscribers has been very low, and the French are now promoting the use of the terminal for other services, such as home banking [26].

While there are some differences between these videotex systems and Prestel, they are all basically identical in the sense of being large information-retrieval systems utilizing the home TV for display of frames of information in color with graphics. Prestel has failed in the British residential market, and the improvements in color repertory and graphics to be offered by the newer systems have not, and will not in my opinion, turn failure into success. It will be argued later in this paper that the only real hope for videotex-like services is to go in the other direction.

It can be claimed that the American consumer has much more discretionary income to spend for electronic information services than the British consumer, and hence videotex will succeed in the United States. However, over $\frac{1}{4}$ million video cassette recorders were sold in 1980 in England [27].

It would appear that at least some British consumers are able to purchase expensive consumer-electronics products if they want them.

The British have had some limited success in promoting videotex for business use. Some videotex advocates in the United States have suggested a similar approach in this country hoping that residential consumer acceptance might then follow business acceptance. However, computerized data-base services are already very prevalent in the United States. These services are predominantly ASCII text and are specific to the needs of the industry served. It is highly questionable what new needs would be served by videotex to justify the new cost.

## 5. The Euphoria Continues

An interesting aspect of videotex is the extent that its advocates are able to continue their enthusiasm in the face of such overwhelmingly negative evidence of any consumer acceptance. The problems with videotex are certainly not a surprise, and some thorough and learned studies clearly forecast the difficulties with videotex.

The problems with videotex in Canada were clearly forecast in a study of the prospects for videotex in Canada commissioned by the Canadian Department of Communications [28,29]. The study forecasted the demand for videotex over ten years and concluded that an unsubsidized videotex service would be too expensive for the average Canadian home. In the business market, videotex would face stiff competition from a number of other technologies, according to the study.

A study of the prospects for videotex in the United States reached the conclusion that “videotex will not become a major service until the end of the century” [30]. There thus have been thorough and learned studies that have correctly forecast and documented videotex’s problems and poor prospects in achieving consumer acceptance.

Published articles continue to appear that take an extremely positive attitude toward videotex $[31,32]$ . One article a few years ago credited the management consulting firm of Booz, Allen, and Hamilton, Inc. with the prediction that within 10 years from 1983, 17 to 30 million households in the United States would have videotex $[33]$ . It is intriguing that in the same time frame of this particular rosy prediction, Viewdata Corporation of America's service was failing in southern Florida and the British had already chalked up years of a lack of consumer acceptance. But there is that general feeling that many consulting firms simply tell their clients what they want to hear!

Surveys of supposed experts do not appear to produce results that are accurate or meaningful forecasts of the videotex market. For example, the results of a questionnaire survey of 77 experts interested in videotex predicted that from 1.7 to 4.3 million households in the United States in 1985 would have videotex [34]. If videotex means an interactive, information-retrieval system from a central data-base of frames of information in color with graphics, then the actual number of videotex subscribers in the United States at the beginning of 1985 surely is not more than 10 thousand. It would thus appear that this prediction was in error. But this should not be that surprising since interested parties and experts frequently err in their predictions. A survey of 210 experts conducted in 1970 predicted that in 1985 there would be about 3 million Picturephones (TM) in the United States [35]. There are none. It would appear that the predictions of experts are sometimes only wishful thinking!

At virtually the same time that the positive articles and surveys appear, cynical and in some cases, downright negative, articles are also being published [36-40]. All this must certainly be confusing for the reader with no knowledge of the actual facts that document videotex's failures in England, Canada, the United States, and elsewhere.

Given the overwhelming negative response to videotex by consumers, one could only wonder with amazement that any sensible people would be willing to continue to spend large amounts of money in this area. But motivated perhaps by a fear of being left behind and an overfascination with technology, a number of large corporations continue to flirt with videotex.

IBM, Sears, and CBS have formed a venture called Trintrex to launch a new videotex business. Times Mirror and Infomart of Toronto have formed Videotex America which is the national sales representative for a videotex service launched late in 1984 in Orange County, California. Although one would expect that the vast sums spent by AT&T thus far in videotex coupled with the lack of consumer acceptance in southern Florida were enough to convince AT&T to cut its losses, AT&T has recently entered in a joint venture with Time, Inc. and a number of New York banks to explore the videotex market further.

## 6. Videotex Characteristics

Videotex was designed to be easy to use and inexpensive for the residential consumer. To accomplish this objective, videotex was given a number of characteristics that would supposedly increase its utility and acceptability to residential consumers. However, these very characteristics have been something of a mixed blessing, with some of them creating more problems than was originally perceived.

## 7. Beyond Information Retrieval

The British Prestel viewdata system, and most other videotex systems, emphasize information retrieval from a very large data-base. To a considerable extent, videotex has become nearly synonymous with information retrieval in the home utilizing the home TV set for display. This limited definition might be too restrictive.

On-line data-bases have been available for many years for access from any conventional computer terminal [41]. These data-base services offer specialized information to meet specific needs, such as airline reservations or stock quotations. This technology is also used to send textual messages to other people within closed-user groups or to cause transactions to occur.

The terminals used for these on-line systems consist of an alphanumeric keyboard and CRT display integrated together in a single terminal dedicated solely for on-line use. The display is overwhelmingly text by in black and white. The service is two-way and high interactive.

There are thus similarities with videotex yet many differences. It is tempting to postulate that videotex is nothing more than a “friendly,” “inexpensive,” on-line service for the home. Yet the color and graphics might have added costs that take the service beyond the financial reach of most residential consumers.

Furthermore, the emphasis on information retrieval might be ignoring transaction and interpersonal-message services that might be needed for success in the general consumer market. The later hypothesis is given some credibility by the reported failure of Dow Jones and Sammon Communications to sell unlimited access to the Dow Jones' news-retrieval service [42], a service that like Prestel ignores transactions and interpersonal messages. But based upon the experience of Warner Amex in an experiment with 32,000 cable subscribers in Columbus, Ohio, it is reported that little interest appeared to exist for electronic shopping, banking and, mail [43].

There is a fair amount of interest by banks in promoting the use of videotex and communicating personal computers for banking from the home [44]. The involvement of banks and major financial institutions in many of the newer videotex activities is particularly significant in this respect. However, there are serious security problems that occur if a number of computer terminals are given access to the bank's computers. Computer systems simply are not that secure, and computer experts could penetrate most computer systems quite quickly and expeditiously. It would only be a matter of time before some bank lost vast sums through a theft of the financial resources maintained in the bank's computers. Other impediments to home banking with home computer terminals are the costs to the consumer, consumer discomfort with computers in general, and the inability to obtain cash from the home terminal [45].

Transaction and interpersonal messages require only text in black and white. Furthermore, their interactive, two-way nature is more suited to a dedicated terminal that is used more as a communications terminal rather than as an entertainment device [46]. What all this means is that videotex might need to become more like on-line systems, possibly to such an extent that the distinctions between two systems would no longer exist [47].

## 8. Interpersonal Messages

Videotex has been promoted primarily as an information-retrieval service. However, this form of visual telecommunications can also be used in a much broader context for conducting transactions and for sending textual messages to other people.

The interpersonal messaging aspects of visual communications are very exciting and encompass three subcategories:

\- Electronic Mail

\- stored and forwarded messages

\- individual to individual

\- Chatting

\- real-time

\- individual to individual

\- Bulletin Board

\- stored and forwarded messages

\- individual to group

\- broadcast messages

Considerable promise is held for such services in the continuing automation of the office of the future where they are known as electronic message systems, computer mail, and computer conferencing [48].

Such interpersonal message systems are seen as a substitute for the well over 500 billion pieces of paper that flow within organizations in the U.S. each year and are predicted to reach prices comfortably under 50¢ per message [49]. Continental Bank has installed a computer mail system that links together more than 1,000 employees [50]. Texas Instruments has 9,000 interactive terminals connected to an electronic mailing system that handles 120,000 documents per day [51]. It is quite clear the computer mail has been well accepted in several large organizations [52].

Although the business market for interpersonal-message systems appears to exist, a question arises as to whether similar needs exist in the residential market. One answer to this question comes from the users of the Source.

The Source is a time-shared-computer information service marketed to residential consumers. The most popular services are electronic mail, bulletin board, and chatting [53]; these services are all forms of interpersonal messages.

Home computer owners are equipping their computers with modems and communications software to gain access over telephone lines to a large number of electronic bulletin boards covering a variety of topics and purposes $[54,55]$ . Many of these bulletin board systems are operated by other computer hobbyists and are very popular. Bulletin boards and electronic messaging have also been very popular and heavily-used by the participants in videotex trials in the United States.

The British Post Office made a number of technical decisions that made it difficult to use Prestel as an interpersonal-message service. This could have lead to the low use of the system also, but apart from these technical difficulties, it is reported that many people feel that Prestel will fail to develop a mass market for its viewdata services, thereby ruining even its chances for electronic mail [56].

Electronic-messaging and board systems are textual in nature and to not require the fancy graphics and color offered by videotex. Simple conventional ASCII text in black and white is all that is needed for interpersonal messaging and is also all that is needed for most transaction services. Videotex thus is the wrong technology for the one service that consumers appear to want and to use.

The one essential issue for electronic messaging is how to create a viable business offering the service. One possible route might be to offer an electronic bulletin board with terminals located in supermarkets and staffed by students. A customer would request information on some desired topic, like used automobiles, and the information request would be entered into the terminal. A print-out would be then sold to the customer. Very local data-bases could be generated this way, and these data-bases could then be linked through telecommunications. As more and more consumers obtained communicating computers in their homes, access could be offered to them over telephone lines. Indeed, this type of service could threaten the classified ads listed in newspapers.

## 9. Misunderstood Needs For Information

Underlying the videotex movement is the fundamental hypothesis that the information needs of consumers can be satisfied by a large, centralized, computerized data-base of general-interest information. In many ways, all this sounds familiar to the past claims made by the proponents of such other centralized, computerized services as management information systems and computer-assisted instruction. However, like so many other “solutions” thrust upon the consumer from a purely technological motivation, these past computerized information systems failed to achieve the great successes predicted by their proponents $[57,58]$ .

Although information is essential to the functioning of nearly all components of society, the specific information needs of most people and their ways of satisfying these needs are not well understood. The nebulous quality of information coupled with its importance have endowed it with something of an ethereal mystique that has been compounded through the addition of the mystery of sophisticated computer technology.

Consumers satisfy many of their present information needs through such existing media as books, newspapers, magazines, and television. In addition, interpersonal communication with friends, either by in-person meetings or by use of the telephone, serves as an essential, informal source of information. How a large centralized, computerized data-base of information will mesh with these existing sources of information remains to be determined. Such a computerized data-base might very well be too time consuming and difficult to use, costly, and inadequate for most consumers. This view is somewhat supported by the results of an evaluation of a videotex service for farmers, called Green Thumb, which concluded that the information needed by most farmers was quite small in quantity and could probably be satisfied most easily by a teletext system $[59]$ .

Information systems for the home need to be designed from a thorough understanding of the use and deficiencies of existing sources of information. When this is done, I believe that little need will be found for a large, centralized, computerized data-base of general-interest information. In essence, the fundamental hypothesis underlying the videotex movement might be incorrect, and this, if so, would explain the failure of videotex in the residential market.

Many management information systems failed because of a lack of understanding of the nature of the information needed by managers in making their decisions $[57]$ . Managers spend much of their time in interpersonal communication and not in browsing through large files of computerized data. To considerable extent, a main advantage of forthcoming computerized messaging systems might be in facilitating these interpersonal communications. If the situation is similar for the home, the consumer need will be for systems designed to facilitate interpersonal communications, to perform transactions, and to access specific, timely information.

The British tried marketing videotext to businesses after the acceptance by residential consumers was poor. A similar trend has occurred in the United States. It is claimed that videotex for businesses will offer easy access by unsophisticated users and will help managers make decisions by providing access to information $[60–62]$ . Of course, all this was claimed for videotex in the residential market and by management information systems of the past. There thus is nothing new here at all. Also most personal computer systems in business are used for word processing and spread-sheet calculations and not for accessing management information systems. Furthermore, most business personal computers are predominantly ASCII text oriented – and not the color and graphics of videotex. Thus, this supposed “new” application for videotex has the aura of failure written all over it!

## 10. Standards

It is claimed that underlying the videotex movement is the idea of a “...much needed single, universal standard for interactions between terminals and computers” [17]. The use of a single standard will supposedly stimulate the provision of both terminals and data-bases.

The essence of videotex is the use of graphics and color. The Prestel videotex system invented by the British uses a very simple form of graphics, called mosaic graphics. With this system, graphic symbols replace alphanumeric characters in the frame of information. Graphic symbols are created from three-by-two vertical-by-horizontal arrays. The mosaic system is simple and quite robust in the presence of noise.

A new system is called geometric graphics. In this system, pioneered by the Canadians, pictures are created from rudimentary geometric shapes, such as rectangles, circles, and lines. Geometric graphics are more sophisticated than mosaic graphics, and the images can be far more complex. However, complex images take longer to transmit and require more storage space in the data-base.

A battle raged in the international standards organizations over the supposed superiority of one system over another. The whole affair became one of national pride, and the British, the French, and the Canadians each had their own national standard that they were advocating. AT & T attempted to resolve the controversy by suggesting a new standard that would accommodate all the various systems [63]. The AT & T standard became advocated by the Canadians and ultimately was adopted as a North-American standard. The standard is called the North American Presentation Level Protocol Standard, or NAPLPS for short.

Advertisers and many videotex advocates believe that videotex should be an advertiser-supported medium and that hence fancy and sophisticated graphics are necessary to promote their products. Hence, these people insist on the fancy capabilities of the NAPLPS. Thus the NAPLPS standard is being used for the Viewdata Corporation of America's videotex system in southern Florida and also the Times-Mirror videotex service in Orange County, California. The NAPLPS is also being used for the CBS teletext service.

Many of the services that can be delivered by videotex are primarily text and do not require color or graphics. Most personal computers and bulletin board systems utilize conventional ASCII text and hence are not compatible with NAPLPS. If one were planning to offer some service based on an electronic, centralized data-base, it would be prudent to make the service available to as many users as possible, and this would mean ASCII – not NAPLPS. In this way, all the users of existing personal computers would only need a relatively inexpensive modem to access and use the data-base service. In this regard, the banks offering electronic home banking from personal computers have shown some sense in their use of the ASCII standard. Thus, it is no surprise that it has been reported that AT & T will abandon NAPLPS in its new venture with the banks [64].

All the emphasis on graphics might then indeed be a mistake. The additional flexibility and excellent graphics accommodated by the NAPLPS might ultimately be superfluous and too costly.

Although teletext has been successful in England, teletext has not done well in the United States. This might be because the television industry in the United States was not content with a simple system as offered by the British. Time, Inc. experimented with a teletext system that could transmit tens of thousands of frames of information over a CATV system. This system was ultimately withdrawn. NBC has a more conventional teletext system, but it too was withdrawn leaving only the CBS system which utilizes the sophisticated NAPLPS.

The beauty of teletext, in my opinion, is its simplicity, fast access, and the easily comprehended small size of the data-base. The British mosaic graphics are well suited for teletext signals broadcast over the air and subject to multipath interference. Somehow, the television industry in the United States has over complicated teletext making it incomprehensible to the average consumer and too costly for the required circuitry in the television receivers.

For videotex, the power of a centrally-located data-base computer can eliminate the need for a single standard. This computer with suitable interface software has the capability to accommodate many different types of terminals with corresponding different standards. The user might dial a port depending upon the type of terminal being used or alternatively the computer might request information from the user to identify the type of terminal. The data networks operated by Telenet and Tymnet already offer such capabilities.

In the end, given the uncertainties about the videotex market, as experience is gained and the definition of home information service is refined, changes in the definition of videotex and any standard might be required. And if the failure thus far of videotex in the residential market continues, any videotex standard might ultimately become a moot standard for a non-existent market.

## 11. Cynicism And Caution

The evidence presented in this paper clearly substantiates serious doubts, and possibly even downright negativism, about the viability of videotex for the residential market. It would appear to be highly probable that videotex will need to make considerable changes in its nature as presently defined to be acceptable to most consumers.

The videotex experience in the United States has closely mirrored the British experience. Large numbers of information providers have been enthusiastic about participating in trials, market tests, and other activities. Their enthusiasm has been reflected into the service providers who have thereby become even more convinced that there is a market for videotex. Predictions about impressive market predictions are made, and market tests quickly become full-fledged service launches. Not until a year or so after the launch does the bubble begin to burst as the market fails to materialize. The only money made in all this activity is by consultants and by computer vendors.

What this all means is that one should be cynical about this market. The consumer response to videotex has been very negative thus far based upon the British experience, and the situation in the United States has not been much different.

Although considerable euphoria continues to exist in the United States about the supposed great prospects for videotex and similar systems, the consumer need remains as doubtful as ever. One would be wise to avoid contributing to this euphoric pandemonium and instead should be questioning, cautious, and objective. So doing will offer the flexibility needed to make changes in the nature of the test services offered and to withdraw quickly those services that fail. Similar words of caution were offered in the past by this author, and they certainly continue to appear appropriate for the present and foreseeable future [65].

The discovery of videotex by the British was a result of early experiments that they conducted using videophones to access computerized data banks [66]. The British modified the videophone to work on a standard television set and to use telephone lines to transmit the data to be displayed. Thus, videotex was born from the videophone. It is interesting that neither service has met with much consumer acceptance although both consumed vast amounts of money in their development and attracted a great amount of publicity.

It will take fortitude to remain calm as the videotex craze continues in the United States. The situation thus far has paralleled the British experience, and considerable interest has been stimulated among information providers thereby resulting in much hype. One needs to remain calm and avoid getting caught in the excitement of the moment. It is difficult to prove conclusively that a market for videotex, including some future yet undefined manifestations, does not exist. But one must remember that great interest and enthusiasm among information providers does not mean consumers will purchase the service, and in the end, it is indeed these consumers who will determine the future of videotex!
