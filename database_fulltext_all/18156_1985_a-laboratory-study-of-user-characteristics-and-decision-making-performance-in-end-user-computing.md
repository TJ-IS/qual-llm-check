# A Laboratory Study of User Characteristics and Decision-Making Performance in End-User Computing \*

George M. Kasper

College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, Texas 79409, USA; tel.: (806) 742-3167

and

Robert P. Cerveny

School of Management, Jacobs Management Center, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, New York 14260, USA; tel.: (716) 636-3250

A laboratory study using a simulated business setting was conducted to examine relationships among user characteristics, DSS usage patterns, and decision-making performance. MBA-student subjects self-selected into one of two categories: (1) "end-user programmers" who chose to use the DSS modeling language to develop personalized decision models, or (2) "command level users" who used only "canned" models and query language retrievals. The results show that early development of personalized decision models was positively related to the end-user's previous computer experience. The findings also support the contention that end-user programming improves decision-making performance. End-user programmers consistently outperformed the command level users in terms of their firm's stock price, market share, and return on assets.

Keywords: End-User Computing, Decision Support Systems (DDS), Decision Aids, Management Information Systems (MIS), User Characteristics, User Performance

## 1. Introduction

The capability of users to have direct control over their computing needs is known as end-user computing (EUC). EUC is expected to be the fastest growing type of computer use. Projections are that EUC may represent one-third of information system spending by 1990 [4]. EUC already accounts for 40–50 percent of the computing resources used in some companies [19]. Despite this activity, there have been few studies of EUC [19].

## 2. Problem Statement

One of the most powerful capabilities of general-purpose software tools is that they allow end-users to develop their own applications $[18]$ . These DDSs provide end-users with interactive access to the data and tools needed to support decision making. This flexibility allows them to develop their own decision-supporting models. This helps to create more effective decision makers by facilitating their abilities to identify, create, and evaluate alternative solutions through the use of applications $[11]$ . A central assumption of this approach is that the design and use of these applications results in better decisions and improved performance $[5,21]$ .

While Rockart and Flannery [19] found a diversity of end-users with no single distinguishing user characteristic affecting end-user computing behavior, one might expect end-user programming to require more computer-relevant training than command level usage. Further, differences in quantitative skills and experience might be expected to affect the decision to become an end-user programmer or a command level user.

## 2.1. Research Hypotheses

While the effect of user characteristics on MIS/DSS use would seem to be self-evident, attempts to measure these effects have provided little in the way of concrete results $[12]$ . Much of the part research has not been directed at end-user computing, where the user actually develops his or her own software model. A priori, one would expect both quantitative and computer modeling experience to be positively related to the end-user's choice to develop his or her own application(s). Further, the MIS/DDS literature suggests that these, as well as management experience, are important user characteristics affecting information processing and computer-based system use $[10,22]$ . To investigate the effect of these user characteristics on end-user computing, the following hypothesis was tested:

Ho $_{1}$ : Neither the subject's management, computer, nor quantitative modeling experience will significantly differentiate subjects choosing command level use from those choosing end-user programming.

A secondary objective of this study was to investigate the effect of end-user programming on decision-making performance. Again, one might expect subjects who develop their own applications to perform better than those who use only the canned software applications and the system's query capabilities. This led to the hypothesis:

$Ho_{2}$ : There will be no significant difference in performance between command level end-users and end-user programmers.

## 3. Experimental Design

An experimental gaming study was conducted to test the hypotheses. The tools employed con-

sisted of: (1) a modified version of a computer-simulated business game known as the Business Management Laboratory (BML) $^{1}$ , (2) an extended version of a DSS software package known as the System Laboratory for Information Management (SLIM) $^{1}$ , and (3) a background questionnaire designed to assess the subject's management and computer experience, and knowledge of quantitative modeling tools. An eight-period repeated measures experimental design was employed with each subject's performance and ad libitum software usage behavior automatically recorded. Conducted during part of an academic semester, subjects were required to generate a set of decisions each week for an eight-week period. During each decision period subjects could opt to use the command level procedures available on the SLIM package or to augment this system by developing their own application program(s). Because a primary purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of the selected user characteristics on the subject's end-user computing choice, participants were allowed to self-select into the command level and end-user programming categories.

## 3.1. Subjects

All ninety-six students enrolled in a second-year MBA operations management course participated in the experiment. The course consisted of two sections, taught by different instructors, with an equal number of students in each section.

The general applicability of research findings derived from student samples remains an issue $[9]$ . Studies contrasting the decision behavior of students as surrogates for “real world” decision makers have met with mixed results. Alpert $[1]$ , for example, found no significant differences between executives and MBA students as far as the experimental task was concerned. However, Jenkins $[13]$ and Lucas and Neilsen $[17]$ found significant differences in performance. In a recent study, Jenkins $[14, p.10]$ states that “Post test interviews in all PRIMIS experiments indicate that managers, as opposed to students, tend to be biased subjects.”

Because of the importance of management experience to the generalization of the findings, a partial assessment of the study's measure of management experience was conducted. The results of this analysis, which are presented below, showed a significant relationship between the study's management scale and the length of full-time managerial experience.

## 3.2. Experience Measures

Management, computer, and quantitative analysis experience have been suggested as important factors in formation processing and system usage [10,22]. A self-reporting questionnaire was developed to assess the subject's functional experience in management activities, computer-related activities, and math (finance/accounting and quantitative analysis). With the exception of questions pertaining to management experience, subjects responded to each question by indicating both their relevant work and academic experience on a seven point Likert-type scale.

Questions related to each area within the questionnaire were analyzed independently. The internal reliability of each area as measured by Cronbach's [8] Alpha are presented in Table 1. The alphas for the individual scales are quite high and indicate that there was very little error in measurement.

Two simple regression models were estimated to examine the management scale's validity. There was a significant relationship between the subjects' management score and the extent of management work experience ( $t = 9.08$ , $R^{2} = 0.46$ ). There was no relationship between the management score and the extent of non-management work experience ( $t = 0.019$ , $R^{2} = 0.01$ ).

## 3.3. Gaming Simulator

The Business Management Laboratory [15,16] was used as the experimental gaming simulator for this study. The BML is a management “game” which simulates the quarterly operations of a manufacturing firm. Participants assume the role of the firm’s management, and make decisions in the areas of marketing, production, financing, and limited strategic planning such as capacity expansion.

Since the BML is a group decision-making game, some software and procedural modifications were required to allow it to be played by an individual. Also, only a selected subset of decision constituting the BML simulation was chosen for this study. These included operational control decisions (e.g., raw material orders), management control decisions (e.g., short-term cash management and product pricing), and strategic planning decisions (e.g., plant expansions and capital structure). The intention was to provide a setting rich enough to support a variety of alternative strategies, yet be manageable by a single subject decision maker.

## 3.4. End-User Software Support

To support the decision maker, a software package developed by Courtney and Jensen [7] was employed. The System Laboratory for Information Management was selected as the software support for several reasons: (1) it is a model of commercial database management system, (2) it provides a generalized data access and analysis capability which includes the ability to address “what if …?” questions, and (3) it was specifically designed to support BML decision making [7]. SLIM consists of a database, a database administration subsystem, an interactive query facility, and a limited modeling sublanguage. SLIM is a “data-oriented” system. The query language, however, may be used to write models that may be stored by the user for later execution. To use Sprague’s term [20], SLIM might be called a “DSS generator,” as it allows end-users to create their own applications [6].

The objectives of this study required extensive modification of the SLIM software system. Using Alter's [2,3] terminology, model-oriented capabilities were added to the original data-oriented package. These additions included financial statement routines and pro-forma generators, an on-line HELP facility, multiple regression and exponential smoothing routines, and the ability to retrieve SLIM command files containing end-user developed programs (models). Subjects could retrieve and execute any of their programs from within the SLIM software system, making their applications both easily accessible and easily applied at any point during an on-line session. A complete discussion of the usefulness of SLIM and the BML as research vehicles may be found in [6].

## 3.5. Database Development and Dummy Firm Decisions

For purposes of this experiment, it was desirable to keep the environment faced by each subject consistent. This was achieved by having subjects compete against the same three “dummy” firms which followed the same decision strategies for each participant. This was carried out by creating an historical database for four firms consisting of twenty “quarters” of play.

While each dummy firm's results were subject to the competitive nature of the gaming simulator, their individual decisions were developed by the experimenters following a strategy specific to each firm. Three strategies were depicted in the database and followed by the respective dummy firms both to develop the historical data and to run the experiment. The decisions of one firm were based on a low-price, low-quality, high-volume strategy with extensive advertising and promotion, whereas a second firm followed a high-price, high-quality, low-volume strategy with moderate advertising and promotion expenditures. Both the remaining dummy firm and the firm run by the subjects followed a moderate strategy during the historical period. Each subject then assumed the management responsibilities for the same moderate-strategy firm at the beginning of the experiment.

## 3.6. Procedures

The experiment was conducted during an academic semester. At the beginning of the study, subjects were asked to complete the background experience questionnaire. BML and SLIM User's Manuals also were distributed.

One class session was devoted to a presentation of SLIM syntax and capabilities by the experimenters. The focus of the presentation was strictly on the BML and how to access and use SLIM. There was no discussion of strategies for the game, the procedural use of SLIM in decision support, etc.

For each decision period, subjects were given a week to make their BML decisions. Gaming decisions could also be made and/or changed at will during the week. All final decisions had to be entered via SLIM no later than 5:00 p.m. Tuesday of each week. The simulation was then run and the results were available to the players by 8:00 a.m. Wednesday, when they could begin work on the next period. The BML automatically recorded the subject's performance in terms of return on assets (ROA), market share, and stock price.

## 3.7. Methodology

Two forms of the analysis of variance model were used to investigate the hypotheses $^{2}$ . To analyze the effect of the user characteristics on end-user programming a $2 \times 2$ MANOVA model was utilized. The independent variables of this model were the four subject characteristics, while the main effects were command level usage versus end-user programming and the two instructors.

To analyze the effect of end-user programming on decision-making performance, a repeated measures multivariate analysis of covariance model was used. This model consisted of the instructor between-subjects main effect, the within-subjects performance measures (ROA, market share, and stock price) for the eight replicated decision periods, and five covariates. Each of the four user characteristics served as covariates. The fifth covariate was the running number of decision periods in which the subject used a self-developed application to formulate his/her gaming decisions.

These results show that computer experience significantly affected end-user programming during the first four decision periods. However, computer experience became less important as the experiment progressed. Thus, while $Ho_{1}$ cannot be rejected, these results show that, at least in the early stages of the experiment, computer experience was an important contributor to end-user programming. An examination of the parameter estimates of the user characteristics showed that quantitative analysis and computer experience were positively related to end-user programming, whereas finance/accounting and management experience were negatively related to program development. While these differences were not statistically significant in every decision period, the direction of each relationship remained consistent for all eight decision periods.

## 4.2. Results Concerning Performance

To analyze the effect of end-user characteristics and programming on decision-making performance, a repeated measures multivariate analysis of covariance model was used. The mean performance of the command level end-users and end-user programmers for each of the three dependent variables (ROA, market share, and stock price) over the eight decision periods is presented in Table 4.

The subjects who developed and used their own applications (end-user programmers) in the current and/or prior decision period, consistently out-performed the command level users. The data further show that during the first two periods the groups' performance remained relatively close but by period three the end-user programmers began pulling away from the command level users and did so at an increasing rate for the remainder of the study. This was especially evident in the groups' market share performance differences.

To further investigate the effect of end-user programming, an analysis was conducted using only the results of the forty-seven subjects who comprised the end-user programming group. Table 5 presents the mean differences in performance within the end-user programming group by their application use per decision period.

When only the self-selected end-user programming group is examined, Table 5 suggests that their applications improved their decision-making performance. Those who used the application(s) they had developed performed better than those who chose not to use their application(s) during the period in question. Since these results are based on the performance of only those subjects who developed their own program(s), these findings further support the contention that end-user programming contributed to improved performance.

Table 6 summarizes these results by showing the mean performance across the eight decision periods for the three performance measures for the end-user programmers and command level end-users. These results show that all subjects improved their ROA and stock price performance. When the performance of end-user programmers is compared to command level end-users, the results show that those who developed their own application consistently outperformed those who did not (Table 4). This was true for all decision periods and all three performance measures.

## 5. Summary

The results of this study show that computer experience significantly affected end-user programming. Subjects with computer experience were more likely to develop their own applications in the early stages of the experiment than were subjects with little computer experience. As users became more familiar with the DSS, the effect of previous computer experience on end-user programming decreased. Neither the subject's management, accounting and finance, nor quantitative experience were shown to be related to end-user program development. None of the user characteristics examined in this study were found to affect decision-making performance.

The results of this study provide strong positive support for the contention that end-user programming improves decision-making performance. The artificial environment, the use of MBA students as surrogate managers, and the study's design (self-selection) prevent us from conclusively stating that the observed difference in decision-making performance was caused by end-user programming. Despite these problems, the results are encouraging and provide a strong foundation for future research in the area of ad libitum DSS model development and decision-making performance.
