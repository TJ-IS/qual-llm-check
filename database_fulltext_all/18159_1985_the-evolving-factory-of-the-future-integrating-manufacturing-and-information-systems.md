# The Evolving Factory of the Future: Integrating Manufacturing and Information Systems

F. Nelson Ford, William N. Ledbetter and Brian S. Gaber

Department of Management, Auburn University, AL 36849, U.S.A.

In recent years there have been rapid developments in both manufacturing and information technologies and their systems. Advances have occurred so rapidly that often the results has been “islands” of automation and information subsystems, such as CAD/CAM, robotics, group technology, and office automation. Some recent developments, however, provide capabilities for integrating these “islands” into systems which both improve manufacturing productivity and produce more meaningful management information. Integrated systems seem imminent. Suggestions that could help ensure a smooth evolution toward these systems are made and some conclusions are drawn.

Keywords: Factory of the Future, Manufacturing Information Systems, Manufacturing Management, Management Information Systems, Computer Integrated Manufacturing

## 2. Historical Background

Modern manufacturing systems can be traced to the steam-driven machines of the industrial revolution. The development of computing technology following World War II produced a new industrial revolution, one powered by information-driven machines.

In such areas as metal fabrication, needs for increased precision led to the development of numerical controlled (NC) machine tools. These perform programmable automation tasks which are controlled by instructions encoded on punched tape. APT (Automatically Programmed Tools) was an early higher level language for programming such NC machines. It greatly increased the usage of NC machines $[11,23]$ .

The success of NC machines, advances in technology, etc., let to the development of direct numerical controlled (DNC) and computer numerical controlled (CNC) machines. DNC have NC machines in direct connection with a real-time computer system. As technology advanced, this evolved into computer numerical control, which utilizes a dedicated, stored program mini or microcomputer to perform some or all of the basic NC functions.

During this period, information systems were being designed to increase clerical efficiency and assist management decision making. Sophisticated systems such as cash management, financial modeling and advanced purchasing systems were developed.

During the 1960s there was great interest in creating a comprehensive information system for an entire organization. This was known as the “total systems” concept. It was expected to aid in providing data and process capabilities for management decision making and strategic planning.

Since the total systems approach was concerned with the interlocking of various systems, the organization as a total entity was the focal point.

Although the conceptualization of the “total system” seemed reasonable, it was never realized. Numerous factors contributed to its failure, including complexity and attitudes of management, as well as lack of technical sophistication and cost. Managers often required information that was not available or had not been collected. In addition, lack of planning and user education created mistrust and fear of new systems.

Throughout the 19th and most of the 20th century, manufacturing plants were considered a necessary but separate part of the organization. Executives tended to under regard the manufacturing function. Recent trends, however, have impressed upon executives the importance of viewing manufacturing as an integral part of the overall strategy for a successful organization.

## 3. Computer Integrated Manufacturing

Manufacturing and information related technologies in recent years have created numerous subsystems in the design, planning and scheduling, production and materials handling areas; these are loosely incorporated into computer integrated manufacturing (CIM) [12,14]. This encompasses everything within a plant – from receiving a customer's order to shipping the finished product. Elements of CIM include computer-aided design (CAD), computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), robotics, group technology (GT) and computer-assisted process planning (CAPP), material requirements planning (MRP) and manufacturing resource planning (MRP-II), automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS), database management systems (DBMS), office automation (OA), and computer networks.

## 3.1. CAD/CAM

CAD embraces four engineering activities: geometric modeling, analysis, testing, and drafting [19]. Independent CAD systems automate design work (reducing design time), perform drafting and revisions, store key specifications, and allow the simulation of a mechanism's parts. As CAD systems grow in sophistication, they will be integrated into other facilities. A main by-product is the development of the data base needed to manufacture the product. Some computer systems today create much of the data and documentation needed for manufacturing.

CAM is the use of computers and NC equipment to aid in the manufacturing process and the control of production. Computers within CAM can be used for process monitoring and control and for manufacturing support. Some applications include job costing, CAPP, computer-assisted NC part programming, production and inventory control and material requirements planning [11].

The CAD/CAM data base contains engineering specifications, part and subassembly numbers, and instructions; this enables specifications to be converted into directions for specified tools. It is believed by some that group technology is the missing link in the total integration of CAD and CAM.

## 3.2. GT & CAPP

Group technology is a coding and classification technique that groups parts according to similarities of their geometric or manufacturing characteristics. Subsequent evolution led to standardization of both design and manufacturing information. With standardization, it became possible to use the computer to generate process plans $[15]$ .

CAPP is the use of computers to generate a process plan based upon input data relative to the workpart. The plan contains the sequence of work centers and operations required in manufacture $[11]$ . Unnecessary design variations can be determined and a preferred method identified through the analytical application of GT. Since GT is a prerequisite for CAPP, it is a step in the development of an integrated information system $[20]$ .

## 3.3. MRP & MRP-II

Simplistically, MRP is a technique designed to determine what to produce, how much to produce, and when to produce it. Although originally an order launching system, MRP evolved into a full-fledged manufacturing information system involving production requirements and feedback from the shop floor [16]. MRP utilizes master schedule, bills of material, lead times, lot sizes and inventories, etc., and the sheer magnitude of the data complicates this technique [11,19]. Manufacturing resource planning (MRP-II) is an enhancement of MRP. Whereas MRP is limited, other production resources (labor and equipment) are included in MRP-II, which is a planning and control system based on a dynamic simulation model of the manufacturing environment [8]. It impacts all corporate departments, such as engineering, finance, personnel, manufacturing and marketing and ties them together via computer. For a complete organizational system to function properly, a common data base and a formal information system are essential. This aids in the development of an organization's integrated information system.

## 3.4. AS/RS

The progressive evolution of materials handling has produced the AS/RS, which has been called an automated warehouse system. It automatically handles, stores, and retrieves materials faster and with greater safety and efficiency than conventional material handling methods $[24]$ . The AS/RS contains four essential elements: the storage/retrieval machine; conveying devices (such as fork lifts, automated guided vehicles, or robots); storage structures (racks); and control equipment (generally computer driven). It provides substantial savings in both land and building costs and personnel.

## 3.5.DBMS

The effectiveness of a data base depends upon benefits received by the user. The integrated data base enables various people to access the system for various uses. Data integration eliminates or reduces data duplication and increases data control. The DBMS makes a large data base practical by 1) creating the data base, 2) maintaining the contents in an up-to-date manner, 3) making the data available to application programs and queries, 4) safeguarding the data from loss or destruction, and 5) providing statistics for data use.

DBMS have evolved considerably and are widely recognized as the key to the successful design of nearly all totally integrated computer application systems.

## 3.6. Robotics

A robot is “a reprogrammable multifunctional manipulator designed to move materials, parts, tools or specialized devices through variable programmed motions for the performance of a variety of tasks” [26]. The efficiency and effectiveness of industrial robots is dependent upon various technical features: freedom of motion, speed and precision of movement, weight carrying capacity, motion control systems, memory capacity, and type of programming [10]. General-purpose industrial robots can be used most effectively while performing tasks that involve hazardous working conditions, the movement of heavy workparts or repetitive actions. Industrial robots perform such tasks as simple assembly, spot and continuous welding, spraypainting, and parts handling. Their effectiveness is dependent upon supply and take away systems such as conveyor belts or automated guided vehicles (AGV). Recent technological advances have added a new dimension; e.g., the Japanese have utilized computerized sight to develop a 17-axis snake robot that can slither through walls inspecting pipes [3].

## 3.7. Office Automation

Office automation is the application of computers and communications technology to improve productivity of office workers. OA has emphasized the rapid, more efficient production of invoices, checks, purchase orders, and similar paper documents $[24]$ . Advances now include text processing, electronic mail, color graphics, information storage and retrieval, teleconferencing systems, DBMS, and task management through computer networks.

## 3.8. Computer Networks

An organization's ability to experience advances in efficiency, flexibility and productivity depends upon the linkage of its subsystems [1]. Communication networks have been classified according to speed, switch technology, transmission medium, network configuration, distance, etc.; e.g., there are wide-area (WAN) and local-area networks (LAN). Typically, LAN's provide an exchange of voice, video, facsimile, computer data, or other forms of electronic messaging. LAN's utilize various transmission media: twisted pair, coaxial cable, radio-light beam and fiber optics. Until recently, factory environments were not extensively networked due to the harshness of the environment; problems included noise and interference and the complexity of the linkage.

## 4. Total System Overview

Many of the “gaps” that contributed to the failure of the “total systems” effort can now be bridged through the application of recent technological innovations. Awareness of and experience with information systems has greatly increased at all levels of management. The evolving management information systems (MIS) staff has enabled companies to improve management’s decision making. The proliferation of computers and peripheral devices has alleviated much of the fear of computers by employees.

One “gap” in the “total systems” effort was the inability to integrate the various subsystems; computer networks are now able to inter-connect all areas through common databases.

## 5. Toward the Integrated System

The basic components of today's manufacturing environment provide the foundation of an integrated system (see Figure 1). On the shop-floor, machines (NC, DNC, robots, etc.) will be networked in a hierarchy of computers and databases. Integrated information from various technologies will flow down through the hierarchy to drive the manufacturing process as status and control information flows up through the hierarchy for use by management. The data will be processed and stored in integrated databases, and thus greatly improve the information for management decision making.

Most factory networks today are application oriented. They operate between similar equipment, but inter-vendor communication is difficult. While cost may be the primary reason for the limited number of industrial networks, the lack of standardization certainly ranks second [13]. Besides the cost of providing and developing the integrated database system, a database administration staff is required.

Larger systems will be designed, developed, and placed into operation; these will be conceived as “total systems” even though they will be implemented module by module. Transformation to an integrated system can be accomplished through careful attention to every phase of the development process. Attention to compatibility and standards are essential.

Manufacturing information is critical to the decision-making process in major functional areas of the organization, and consequently must be integrated with all the other functional areas for a truly effective MIS.

## 6. Conclusion

In an effort to improve a particular aspect of the manufacturing process, the advanced manufacturing and information techniques and technologies of today have evolved and are generally continuing to evolve independently of one another. The organization seeking the factory of tomorrow must integrate these elements.

The failures of the “total systems” effort were identified; these problems can be avoided through the application of evolving technologies and management concepts. To insure the successful implementation, the evolutionary process of the system should be closely monitored. Because of the complexity, development and implementation must proceed in a modular fashion. As the technology evolves, particular attention should be paid to the interfaces which allow integration of related modules as they are designed and implemented so that “islands” of automation and information sub-systems can be avoided. If this approach is taken, and lessons learned are heeded, the goal of a comprehensive, integrated factory is attainable.

There is, however, one missing element: a formal, comprehensive model which describes and defines the relationships among the components. Research on this is an important element of the total effort.
