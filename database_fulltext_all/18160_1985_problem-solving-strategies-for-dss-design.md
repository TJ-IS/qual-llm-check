# Problem-solving Strategies for DSS Design

Harish C. Bahl and Raymond G. Hunt \*

State University of New York at Buffalo, School of Management, Jacobs Hall, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA

For a successful design and implementation of DSS, competent understanding of decision-making processes in organizational settings and sensitivity to the interpersonal and organizational dimensions of the relationship between decision-makers and DSS designers are essential. Towards this end, a three-faceted strategy is broadly outlined and proposed. The strategy encompasses (a) a diagnostic attitude to the empirical tasks and contextual properties of decision-making, (b) emphasis on decision-makers rather than on decisions, and (c) the concept of DSS design as a joint undertaking for organizational problem-solving.

Keywords: Decision Making, Decision Support Systems, Descriptive Models, Systems Design

## 1. Introduction

Technical arrangements for the acquisition and timely delivery of information – “management information systems” – have the common purpose of rationalizing managerial choice, on the assumption that it will thereby be improved over the prejudice, whimsy, or chance that might otherwise govern such actions. Of particular interest to us is current work on the development process for decision support systems (DSS): special interactive computerized systems that help decision-makers use data and models in personalized ways [23].

The design of effective DSS requires application of a novel array of skills and attitudes $[12]$ . In addition to technical proficiency, these include (1) understanding the decision-making processes in organizations; (2) appreciation of the complexity of empirical DSS design tasks; (3) awareness of and a willingness to accommodate DSS design to variation in decision environments and diverse tasks of managers $[21]$ ; and (4) sensitivity to the interpersonal and organizational dimensions of the relationships between decision-makers and DSS designers. This paper deals mostly with the last of these, from a social psychological point of view.

The point we wish to stress is often overlooked: that designing an effective DSS is a complex problem-solving task that requires close collaboration with its prospective user. Briefly, analysis and design must be oriented towards diagnosis of the nominal choices and not derived from a priori normative models; design attention is best concentrated on decision-makers rather than on decisions; and DSS design is necessarily a joint undertaking, a co-production of manager/decision-makers and design specialists.

## 2. Diagnostic DSS Design

Diagnosis involves a sorting or categorization of cases. A taxonomy of decisions would serve the diagnostic purpose provided it also included practical information on how to treat the pigeon-holed cases in order to achieve meaningful results. Concepts, models, and “theories” of decision-making abound in the literature, but no standard taxonomy exists, though some have been proposed [7]. Presumably the situation would be improved if we knew more about the nature of decisions [6,24,25], though Dery [5], for one, is doubtful.

## 2.1. The Role of Theory in DSS Design

From the perspective of DSS design strategy, “encoding” a design problem in terms of a “theory” can be useful because it suggests which body of knowledge could help the designer; e.g., to understand its nature, recognize its support requirements and problems, and suggest design alternatives. In short, a good theory is a diagnostic tool. Conscientious DSS designers can see the need to examine their assumptions and seek critical guidance from the literature. Unhappily, however, a large and confusing range of concepts, models, and theories awaits them!

The essential idea of decision-making is simple. It refers to behavior between a stimulus (S) and a response (R). In essence, decision-making is response selection. What can be concluded from the literature, however, is that decision-making is a highly variable affair; often it is not orderly, much less “rational” [2]. Decisions are subject to endogenous and exogenous influences that are hard to identify and anticipate or control. Consequently, for a concrete case, a general theory or model of managerial decision-making is less useful to a DSS designer than may be supposed.

What is needed appears to be a special theory for empirical cases of decision-making: but numerous “special theories” exist – often masquerading as general theories and purporting to specify the exact nature of all decision processes. Some examples are summarized in Table 1. The various ideas there are misleading because they are commonly treated as substitutable alternatives, but they are not. Instead, they are different cases, or aspects of, or different perspectives on the general phenomena. Each doubtless describes some part of organizational choice, but none deals adequately with the entire phenomenon [2].

## Econological Model:

This model assumes that individuals are fully informed, economically rational maximizers and that decision making proceeds through a series of analytic steps which typically are these: define the problem; develop a criterion against which alternatives can be evaluated; list all alternatives available; determine the consequences of each alternative and the likelihood of occurrences of each; select the best alternative; execute the decision $[12,19,24]$ .

## Bounded Rationality Model:

The model recognizes several limitations on information processing capabilities, time available, and importance of decisions. Consequently, managers are believed to make decisions which are of “limited” rationality. Significant aspects of this model are: an emphasis on sequential rather than synoptic consideration of alternatives; use of heuristics for identifying “promising” alternatives; and selection of alternatives according to a “satisficing” rather than a maximizing principle $[17,18]$ .

## Organizational Process Model:

Here, the organization is viewed in terms of internal coalitions, each of which has its own priorities, goals, and objectives. Organizational decision-making necessarily involves bargaining among these coalitions and the factoring of large-scale problems into subproblems. Each unit of the organization relies on its standard operating procedure or past practices for responding to the problems [24].

## Implicit Favorite Model:

This model was developed after examining the job choice processes of graduating business students. Significant processes in this model are these: the decision-maker identifies a favorite alternative on the basis of one or two primary characteristics; perceptual distortion of information and criteria factors occurs to produce a biased decision rule which confirms the “superiority” of the implicit favorite; the list of alternatives is thus quickly reduced to the implicit favorite and its selection is followed by a more or less lengthy confirmation process [25].

## Political/Competitive Model:

Strategic and policy level decisions are considered to be made in relation to political constraints, aspirations and interactions. Decision making is perceived as a process of conflict and consensus building [4].

## 2.2. Modeling Managerial Choice

Dery [5] complains that the literature exhibits great carelessness in equating its central “problem” with decision and choice “as if goals were given and alternatives given.” It tends, moreover, to presume that decision-makers are “facing predefined problems” – those stated independent of the decision-maker’s perceptions – and that decision “theories” are about “objective” conditions. Instead, Dery suggests that managers do not “normally face a choice situation, but events that call for evaluation and interpretation." Decision theories are a priori or a posteriori models of these cognitive constructions. He further argues that problems "do not present themselves as structured or ill-structured, nor do they come as decision-problems, complex or simple.... To state the facts or describe the situation is to interpret not to copy it. Structured decision problems are structured because we choose to treat them as such".

## 2.3. Pre-Decision Analysis

Such a critique directs the designer-analyst's attention away from the decision (where it has traditionally resided) to the pre-decision process – i.e., to the cognitive precursors that frame managerial responses. Pre-decisional activity consists primarily of developing models of managerial tasks – mental constructions that capture and organize an actor's beliefs and knowledge about what is to be done (how, where, and when) to produce some useful outcome. These provide an implicit framework of premises and plans – behavioral templates – for fashioning specific responses and evaluating their outcomes.

Pre-decision analysis seeks an explanation or specification of a “presentation of choice” or definition of a situation. This is an interpretation that provides a basis for managerial action. It is the result of interactions among psychological variables during the pre-decision period. It details the managerial problem and possible responses that will motivate decision-making.

Thus, analysts and decision-makers, as Dery stresses, are constructing realities for action, not “discovering” or “copying” independently existing ones. Their choices, in this sense, are fundamentally subjective and theory-guided.

## 2.4. Models of Choice

The implicit “theories” according to which “presentations of choice” may be modeled seem to be basically only four. We call these four rational (Type R), bounded rational (Type B), functional (Type F), and nonrational (Type N). We can briefly describe the characteristics and assumptions of these models, and their implications for DSS design.

## 2.4.1. Type R Models

These are instrumentally rational (Weberian)

models which picture situations as having four main attributes: (1) “simple” information processing requirements for interpreting and evaluating decision parameters; (2) complete capability for this processing sub-task; (3) static and determinate conditions of action; and (4) the feasibility of technically “optimum” solutions (i.e., choices). The decisions implicit in Type R models are like the ones envisaged by so-called rational actor and econological theories of decision making. According to these “theories” decision-makers are fully informed, economically rational maximizers. Decision-making is presumed to proceed through a series of fully informed analytic steps which typically are these: define the problem; develop criteria against which alternatives can be evaluated; list all the alternatives; determine the consequences of each alternative and the likelihood of occurrence of each; select the best alternative; execute the decision [cf. 19, 22, 12].

Type R models are micro-level schema that consider decision-making from the perspective of an individual decision-maker. They tend to conceive of macro- or organization-level decision as either simple linear combinations of separate individual decisions, or as the action of a single decision-maker (e.g., a leader). Operationally, Type R decision models presume a set of conditions where it is possible to specify and structure (i.e., control) recurring events so as to enable their programming in ways that entirely determine all relevant future action-outcomes (at both micro- and, presumably, macro-levels). These are, of course, the kinds of decision tasks Simon [24] called “programmed.” Keen and Scott Morton [13] call them “structured,” citing as illustrations of them inventory reordering and linear programming for manufacturing and arguing that they are decisions which “do not involve a manager”; they are routinizable to the point of allowing their transfer to clerks or machines for performance.

It is important to remember that models are templates imposed on situations and not the other way around. Consequently an actor's handling of a situation is limited (if also facilitated) by the model used to define it. What (s)he sees and does is a function of its perspectives. Alternative models would invite alternative perceptions and alternative actions, good and bad.

The “MIS fraternity,” as Keen and Scott Morton [13] suggest, customarily holds a Type R perspective on decision support tasks, assuming “structured” decisions as their objects. Some situations (or parts of them) are undoubtedly manageable with these limited mechanistic models and are amenable to determinate DSS designs. But try as one may to bend situations to fit them, as interpretations of decision-making and its support requirements, the restrictive assumptions of Type R models severely limit their utility [cf. 9].

## 2.4.2. Type B Models

These are “bounded” rational (or neorational) models. They are essentially qualified micro-level rational models that assume systemic uncertainty, including ambiguity about decisions (problems) themselves. Type B models and prescriptions are therefore necessarily more judgmental than Type R varieties; and context is more important as a source of information for an interpretation of the decision task, as well as for its performance. Type B models imply that it is desirable to shift DSS design focus from the task or decision, where it may seem to be appropriately directed in the Type R case, to the decision-maker. We will come back to this point again later. Type B models are divisible into two sub-types.

Sub-type B1 models are of the classical Simonian “organizational process” variety [24]. They assume a person who is confronted with complex information and who has limited capacities for processing it. They emphasize a decision-maker who “intends” rational choice and accepts evaluation on “rational” standards, but who is nevertheless forced by uncertainty or time pressures to rely upon interpretive and evaluative heuristics and sup-optimal “satisficing” solutions to decision problems [17]. If potentially helpful (e.g., with processing sub-tasks), prescriptive programming under such models is inevitably imperfect and can only be partially determinative.

Plainly, the DSS design task is fundamentally different under a Type B1 model from what it is under Type R. The latter can operate with a control-oriented design objective, imagining the development of automated decision systems wholly determinative of a given output. DSS under Type B models, however, are quite different. They must necessarily orient to an auxiliary role as aids to human actors. Their objective is not to “make” decisions, but to help people cope with problems.

Sub-type B2 models not only envisage decision-makers as limited capacity machines, they also add a further caveat on their ability to achieve rational choice, namely, the idea that they are systematically biased. In some “individual differences” models [cf. 13], this bias may be linked with personal characteristics of the decision-maker – prejudices, bents of personality, and so forth. In other formulations, bias may arise from implicit favorites [25] among choice alternatives and/or from the social system within which decisions are made. Cyert and March [4], for instance, directed attention to the biasing effects on decision-making of standard operating procedures, organizational roles, goal-oriented self-interest and other socio-political processes and structures within organizations.

All Type B models are individualistic. Their reference is to the individual decision-maker. Moreover, they tend to assume a single standard for evaluating alternatives for choice; and they look upon decision-making as perhaps a fallible but nonetheless an orderly process. If decision-making is inevitably judgmental, still it is susceptible of evaluation and improvement on prior rational criteria, say, of efficiency or effectiveness [cf. 8]. In addition to compensatory DSS tactics generally suggested by Type B decision models, designs assuming Sub-type B2 cases might explore ways of controlling discernible decision-maker biases.

It is Type B.models, of either sub-type, “where managerial judgment alone will not be adequate,” that Keen and Scott Morton [13, p. 86] have nominated as defining the particular domain for DSS. They, of course, are talking less about particular kinds of decisions than about particular computer applications. In other words, what they are talking about is an image of reality consistent with a line of activity in which they wish to engage. In this reality, decision support may be retrieval of relevant information from a data base, timely and interactive data availability, a model to assist in a selection from alternatives, and presentation and analysis of massive amounts of information for use by a decision-maker. Other realities, however, may suggest other support systems.

## 2.4.3. Type $F$ Models

These are neorational models, too, but they depict realities quite different from those of Type B forms. Type F models have not yet been widely considered among DSS technologists although they probably make sense of many managerial situations, especially at macro levels. Type F are functional (or effectively rational) models which assume complex information and limited/biased processing capabilities, but together with dynamic social-contextual conditions.

A key feature of the Type F view is that is is sociologistic rather than individualistic, macro instead of micro. Organizational decisions (actions and outputs, really, which an observer may interpret as “decisions” [cf. 1]) it conceives as products of negotiation and social interaction, i.e., as functional noncognitive resultants of broadly distributed mutual adjustments by partisants of political “interests.” Decisions in Type F realities are fundamentally ambiguous; and “choices” are subject to evaluation on pluralistic standards which include explicitly political as well as technical criteria [15]. Moreover, in Type F models decisions are holistically non-recurrent, although their details may be separately recapitulated.

In further contrast with Type B models, which abstract decision-making from its social-organizational context, Type F models fully “contextuate” it. The process of decision-making envisaged by Type F models, therefore, may be a disorderly one, but, thanks to the market-like wisdom of mutual adjustment, its outcome is “unbiased”, – an inversion of Type B presumptions. In Type F decision-making, “rationality” is in the macro-level outcome rather than the micro-level process. It is an emergent rationality analyzable only after the fact, and hardly predictable. In a Type F reality, rational outcomes may result from thoroughly disorderly processes (and vice versa, probably). And to insist on comparability in macro- and micro-level processes is to commit what Burton Klein [14] calls, a “compositional fallacy”.

Judgmental Type F decision-making is technically programmable and supportable only in general, via provision of “intelligence” information useful to individual decision-makers’ judgments on the “status” of the system (e.g., opinion surveys, market intelligence, political forecasts). Problemsolving and decision-making in a Type F scheme is inherently and properly a process of “muddling through” in ambiguous social-technical environments. Its outcome, in effect, is the “best” that can be expected – a kind of unpredictable “realized” rationality.

## 2.4.4. Type $N$ Models

These are frankly non-rational. They, too, conceive of decision-making as subject only to post hoc analysis and nondeterminative rationalization of prior action. The garbage can idea exemplifies such models [16]. A kind of extension of Type B1 ideas to a macro-level, the garbage can envisages decisions as adventitious associations of problems and choices, conceived as separate flows. The linkage of problem and choice – the contents of the garbage can – depends upon the timing of these flows and patterns of participant access to them. In the garbage can, criteria for the relevance of decision alternatives – what solutions are relevant to what problems – are ambiguous. Choice is an almost fortuitous meshing of loosely coupled problem-choice streams. Furthermore, choices go looking for problems as much as the reverse; and problems are apt to be defined in terms of a decision-maker's existing repertoire of solutions. Moreover, there is a chance that output from a DSS will have a strong impact on what problems are identified, how they are defined, and what is made of them. Thus, a DSS may transform (and bias) the managerial system it was intended to support – something that may happen in any case of course (and may even be intended), but is especially likely in a Type N “organized anarchy.”

Thus, under the Type N model, a decision is an outcome or interpretation of several relatively independent “streams.” Rationality enters into them only in regard to the belief systems of actors, which may bear on their individual conduct, and on the ex post “explanation” or “rationalization” of some decision process by an observer (e.g., a decision analyst).

Huber [10] has suggested certain “environmental scanning” prescriptions for DSS designs in garbage can-like decisions environments. His advice comes down to a rather simple proposal that managers need to have a lot of information about their environments. But, since it is not possible to become very specific about what information is to be provided about which environmental segments, the task of providing it is inherently impossible. DSS design specifications for “intelligence” gathering, like Huber’s, are quite appropriate, given a Type F reality; but in Type N cases, they reduce to little more than advertisements for a “liberal education” and “sophistication” on the parts of decision-makers. A suitable Type N DSS might as well then consists of advice to managers that they religiously read the New York Times. What Type N models really imply is the thought that some important decisions are unsupportable in an explicit rigorous sense, not only because they are “unstructured” [13], but, more basically, because they cannot be anticipated.

## 2.5. Models and the Construction of Managerial Realities

We can apply the garbage can metaphor to restate our general argument. Managers and analysts, we have said, hold theories and models of tasks and decision-making. The garbage can thesis suggests that they may go about looking for situations where their theories and models “fit” – where they make sense. This simply means that there are situations that are manageable in terms of the model. It implies nothing about the utility of other models. As Dery observes, situations are structured because we treat them so. Often, obviously, this “works”. But alternative constructions might also work, possibly better. Unfortunately sometimes, the use of one model tends to foreclose the use of others, even others that might be preferable on some acceptable criterion. The “trick”, then, is to be flexible in modeling problems, tasks, and decisions and to avoid the sin of confusing the model with the modeled.

In a way, a DSS designer's task is more complex than a manager/decision-maker's. Not only must (s)he model the design task, per se, but this must be done in a way that meshes with the manager's model of the decision task.

## 2.6. The Contingencies of Managerial Tasks

The primary function of a DSS is to provide information useful to a manager. Hence, a DSS must be technically sound; but it must also be tailored to decisions and their contexts and to the manager-user as well. What information will be useful depends on the manager's “needs”. But these are not always easy to define. For they depend upon how the manager interprets a situation and prefers to deal with it. Operationally, a DSS must focus selectively and differentially on particular decisions or, rather, on managerial tasks involving them; but the basic purpose of DSS is to support a decision-maker who is a distinctive personality playing out an institutional role.

Therefore, successful DSS designs will take into account the roles and personalities of their users. As Mason and Mitroff [18] have observed, an information system includes, among other things, a “person of a certain psychological type”; and “what is information for one type will not be information for another”. Furthermore, they add, “as designers (of information systems), our job is not to get (or force) all types to conform to one, but to give each type the kind of information he is psychologically attuned to and will use most effectively” (p. 478, [18]).

Two ideas follow from this, one directly (i.e., that DSS designers might better focus on managers instead of decisions), and the other by implication (i.e., that DSS design is a social as well as a technical process).

## 3. Focusing DSS on Managers Instead of Decisions

It seems to be part of the conventional wisdom that large areas of managerial decision-making are essentially inaccessible to DSS: situations, for instance, where “decisions” are excessively unstructured, which is to say undetectable. The problem here is a pseudo-problem, however, born of poor advice about DSS strategy. It is true, of course, as the garbage can and organized anarchy ideas (Type N decision models) suggest, that some choice situations are extremely vague and disorderly. If one insists that DSS exist to support decisions, then DSS designers may well have nothing to contribute in these so-called “unstructured” cases. If, however, one shifts from a preoccupation with decisions to an emphasis on decision-makers, then the relevance of DSS technology to managerial tasks widens considerably.

Such a manager-oriented attitude implies a particular concept of the DSS designer's role: as a complement to the manager-decision-maker. The function of a DSS (and its designer) is to help a manager cope with the problems he or she faces. It does not follow from this that a manager's version (model) of his or her tasks is necessarily the final word on the subject. A designer's role in a DSS project need be no more passive than a manager's is in problem-solving and decision-making. A designer can make significant organizational contributions by helping to stimulate the definition or redefinition of the task models on which a system operates, as well as by installing technical devices for their execution. Still, the decision-maker must necessarily be the focus and point of departure for the special perspective and technical skill of the DSS designer. As Mason and Mitroff note, managers need information geared to their problems and psychology and not to those of designers. Deficiencies of DSS often are traceable to failures to integrate managers in DSS design loops, and to conjure with continuing design (or redesign) problems that arise from using and managing a DSS. They arise, too, from failing to take seriously the individual and social psychology of design.

## 4. DSS Design as a Joint Venture

People learn ways of interpreting and dealing with situations (tasks, problems, and other conditions) by interacting with them. The results of this social learning are stored in cognitive models or “schemas” which are dynamic conceptual outcomes of interwoven cycles of action, interpretation, reaction, evaluation, and reinterpretation $[11]$ . These mental constructs are the immediate precursors of action, serving, among other things, to organize and guide choice behavior. DSS designs, therefore, implicate their architects in the construction of cognitive models to guide performance of their design tasks. These models are necessarily based on the analysis (or diagnosis) of others’ (i.e., decision-makers’) models of the “decisions” that are the objects of support. Hence, in order to conceptualize their own tasks effectively, DSS designers need the help and input of decision-makers at least as much as the other way round.

If a manager is not simply an organic machine or merely a source of error variance, but is a self-conscious, sense-making, role-playing personality who is also a contributor to the decision-making process; and if a DSS is intended to assist the performance of this role player, then it would seem that DSS design must be not only diagnostically guided and manager-focused but negotiated as well. The task of DSS design is a joint undertaking, a coproduction of technically skilled specialists and functionally-politically knowledgeable managers.

A managerial decision-making task, especially when it is ambiguous, presents interpretive problems both for the manager and the DSS designer. The questions in either case are: What is the task? What's to be done with it? And how is it to be done? In developing answers to these questions, decision-makers and DSS designers can help one another. Designers can help decision-makers recognize and consider alternative ways of modeling managerial tasks; or they can facilitate a decomposition of tasks into sub-tasks which may be variously modeled. But, in the event, DSS designers must rely heavily on decision-makers for contextual cues essential to a reasonable conceptualization (model) of the design task and suitable ways of handling it. Since DSS designers must construct judgmental models of their support tasks, and these models must “interface” with managers' personalized images of their tasks (i.e., decisions and their relevant contexts), it seems plain that effective DSS designs can only emerge from close collaboration with decision-makers. The focal tasks which are objects of DSS designs cannot be defined abstractly, only contextually and personally, and therefore only in collaboration with decision-makers.

## 5. Conclusion

In this paper we have emphasized an elementary point that is often overlooked in practice, namely, that designing an effective decision support system is a complex, cognitively guided, problem-solving task that can only be done well in close collaboration with the system's user. DSS design involves consideration of the designer's role in the support project, and the analysis of managerial tasks. Its analytic basis must be oriented in terms of a diagnostic attitude toward the empirical tasks and the contextual properties of organizational choice, not one mechanically derived from a priori normative models. Design attention needs to concentrate on decision-makers rather than on decisions; and this implies that DSS design is necessarily a joint undertaking of manager/decision-makers and design specialists.

DSS designers, if they are to be effective in their auxiliary roles, need to become organizationally and interpersonally sensitive, as well as technically competent. In fact, the need to maintain the user-manager in DSS design loops is a potent argument for turning the design task over to the manager. Some corporate Information Center operations (that of Marine Midland Bank, for instance) have sought to do just that. Their staffs provide information, training, design ideas and resources, and, of course, technical assistance, but they do not initiate literal managerial DSS designs. The general idea is that the best judge of a useful DSS design is its manager-user. But a DSS specialist-collaborator can play a powerful developmental role assisting managers in modeling their decision environments and in finding technical means of coping more effectively with them. Notice, however, that this scenario casts the DSS designer in a role more as teacher or organizational “change agent” than as technician; and this suggests a need for careful evaluation of the training and skill needed if DSS specialists are to play such a role at all well.
