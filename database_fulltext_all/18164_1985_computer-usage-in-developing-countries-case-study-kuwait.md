# Computer Usage in Developing Countries: Case Study Kuwait

Rosalind L.R. Ibrahim

Kuwait University, Department of Mathematics, P.O. Box 5969, Safat, Kuwait

This study is an examination of the position of computing technology in the developing country of Kuwait. The status of computer usage is described. These findings are assessed relative to technology trends, evolutionary computing stages, and/or results reported in more developed countries; problems and concerns of computer users are identified; and factors potentially related to more successful computer utilization are explored. The following aspects of computer usage are analyzed: computing resources (hardware, software, staff, expenditure), application development methodologies, applications and evaluation. The study provides insight into the results of the transfer of computing technology to a developing country where computer usage is relatively widespread.

Keywords: computer utilization, computing in developing countries, computing status, transfer of computing technology

## 1. Introduction

Computer usage can have a significant impact on a country's development, an organization's operations, or an individual's way of approaching a task. The rapid expansion of computing worldwide calls for the study and assessment of this pervading technology especially with respect to its use in developing countries.

For developing countries, both the potential of computing to accelerate development and the foreseen obstacles to achieving successful utilization have been broadly outlined (eg. [4,24]); further, some identify computing as one of the few fields where developing countries can successfully bypass evolutionary phases and “catch up fast” via direct high level transfer, yet others argue that the opposite is true (eg. [4,20]).

Thus an important issue for developing countries and for those concerned with the diffusion of computing technology is to study the transfer process to investigate whether developing countries can directly absorb technologies evolved over years; i.e. to ask: What is the current status of computer utilization in developing countries? What benefits are being realized? What problems exist?

Here these questions are investigated by studying the position of computing in Kuwait. Computing has been introduced into the organizations of Kuwait, a country that does not have the longstanding educational, institutional, and organizational traditions and experience of the countries where computing originated and for which its applications were first developed. Further, Kuwait is one of the richer, less populated developing countries. Its financial capabilities, dynamic development programs, receptivity to technological innovation, and propensity towards labor-saving technology make it a natural computer marketplace. Thus Kuwait affords the researcher an opportunity for empirical rather than theoretical exploration into the above issues.

This research describes computer utilization in Kuwait, assesses its technological status, reports on benefits being realized, investigates factors possibly related to more successful utilization, and depicts problem areas. The scope of coverage is limited to the usage of mainframes and general purpose minicomputers, and the results of computerization are examined at the level of a computer using organization (and not beyond).

Although there is relatively little published information on computing in developing countries, some articles on computing in Kuwait and other Arab countries are available: a special issue of Arab Oil [2] includes vendors' views of the Kuwait market and an article on international aspects of computer manpower; results of a 1978 study on computer usage in Kuwait were published in Datamation [17]; some articles investigating selected aspects of informatics in Arab countries appear in [19] and [22]. However, no published results were found describing computer usage in developing countries or in Kuwait using an approach similar to that pursued here.

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. The Basic Approach

Computer usage in Kuwait is viewed as a system of interacting components, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Inputs are the computing resources: hardware, systems software, staff, and capital; processes are the methodologies used in application development; outputs are the computer applications; and control is the evaluative aspects of these three in use.

For each component: its current position is described; problem areas are noted; and its status is assessed relative to technology trends, evolutionary computing phases, and/or indicators established for more developed countries. This approach was taken in order to: try to provide a complete picture of computing technology at an organizational level; see if computing technology is transferred at a uniform level across the many aspects of the computing infrastructure of an organization; facilitate investigation of factors that may explain why some organizations derive more benefits from computerization than others.

## 2.2. Sources of Information

The major sources of information for this research were the computer using organizations in Kuwait. Questions were directed to the DP manager at the installation, normally in the best position to provide information on resources and applications, and in a good position to know the benefits. Another source was the computer vendors; for an estimate of the population of computer users and in the classification of equipment.

## 2.3. Survey Design

The user survey was structured, according to the basic methodology, to cover computing resources, methodologies, and applications. Within each part, questions were designed to find out what was currently being used or done, how it was decided that this would be used or done, and what problems were common regarding this aspect of computer usage.

## 2.4. Sampling

The sample of computer users was drawn up from many sources, including lists of organizations having personnel attending local computer meetings, directories of institutions, vendor information, information provided by users, and personal knowledge.

The user sample upon which these findings are based included 25 different user sites, with 47 computers installed. Based on population figures and estimates provided by vendors, the sampling error of the sample proportion for user responses is no greater than 3%, at $\alpha = 0.05$ .

## 2.5. Data Collection

Data were collected in late 1981, using a personal approach; this probably contributed to the high response rate. The process was usually in two steps: a study team member met with the respondent, explained the nature of the study, and then arranged for a follow-up visit after a few days to clarify any questions and pick up the completed questionnaire.

## 2.6. Data Analysis

Responses were analyzed using SAS (Statistical Analysis System). Unless otherwise specified, results are reported at a significance level of 0.05.

The sample of computer users in Kuwait included 44% public, 40% private, and 16% jointly owned institutions. By economic sector, 28% of the users were in banking and finance, 16% ministries, 16% industry, 16% services and transportation, 12% trade and commerce, and 12% research and education.

## 3.2. Computing Resources ... The Inputs

Four basic resources are examined: hardware, systems software, staff, and capital.

## 3.2.1. Hardware

(i) Hardware Utilized. Hardware technology level is often described in computer generations based on attributes such as computer speed, storage capacity, input-output speed, type of peripherals, etc. Studies in the U.S. have investigated the relationships between hardware technology levels and application performance; e.g. [23] and [25]. In Kuwait however, regardless of the number of years an organization has been using computers, most users are operating with equipment at a high technology level. Vendors are offering and users are acquiring the latest technologies. Thus the description of hardware utilized is presented in terms of numbers rather than levels.

From the sample of 25 users, there were 23 mainframes and 24 minicomputers installed, with from 0 to 2 mainframes, from 0 to 4 minicomputers, and from 0 to 5 installations at a site. About two-thirds of the sites had 2 or more installations, and 8% had none but were linked to facilities at another location. There is substantial variation in the number of terminals, with the maximum being 220. The median was 26.

Resource sharing is common: 56% of the users had remote sites linked into their central systems; 28% were linked to configurations at other sites; 24% were part of a distributed processing or networking system; 16% were using facilities of organizations to which they were not physically linked; and 22% were providing services to organizations not physically linked to them. (None of these options are mutually exclusive).

(ii) Hardware Selection. The hardware selection process provides an indication of how dependent a country is on the advice of salesmen or if it has reached a more advanced level. Fourteen percent of the users had switched vendors in order to acquire their current configuration, 43% acquired their current configurations as a major upgrade from the same vendor, and 43% were using their first configuration. About half of all configurations were leased, the other half purchased. Against this background, users were asked who was involved in their selection process and what procedures were followed.

Hardware requirements were determined by in-house staff in 88% of the sites, by a consultant in 21%, and by a vendor in 17% (again these options are not mutually exclusive). Eighty-eight percent issued a request for proposal (RFP) which was prepared in-house for 75% of them, by a consultant for 20%, and by in-house staff with consultation for the rest.

The major criteria used in hardware selection were: hardware capability (memory size, disk-drives, Arabic I/O and on-line capability); compatibility and growth potential; ease of use; vendor capability (local support, reliability); available application software; ability to recruit staff with system experience; and cost.

Table 3 gives the selection methods used. These figures indicate that the hardware selection process is at a fairly sophisticated level for most Kuwait users.

(iii) Hardware-related Concerns. When DP managers were asked to assess hardware utilization, the results were as given in Table 4. Although high-pressure marketing may often result in more equipment than is needed or can be used in many developing countries ([9]), this does not appear to be the case in Kuwait. The fact that DP managers in Kuwait are generally satisfied with their hardware (75% agree their hardware is well supported and about 9% feel they have too much hardware down-time) also may reflect the attention paid to the selection process. However, 42% of the DP managers are not sure or else agree that their hardware does not perform as well as they had expected.

## 3.2.2. Systems Software

(i) Systems Software Utilized. General trends in systems software (SSW) technology are towards the use of: interactive systems; data base management systems (DBMS'S); high level structured languages; products designed for distributed processing; and performance monitors.

In Kuwait, users have from one to three different operating systems, most featuring multiprogramming, virtual storage, and an on-line facility. About half of the users have a DBMS, and another 16% are planning to acquire one. Half of the installations are using performance monitors and 18% are charging back for computer utilization. Selected country comparisons for DBMS usage reveal that 54% of companies in Finland have a DBMS [12], and in the U.S. Jenkins et al. [11] reported 55% while Merten et al. [16] reported 85%. Merten also reported performance monitors in use in 46% of U.S. companies.

The most heavily used programming language by an overwhelming margin was COBOL (92% reporting it was installed and 80% reporting heavy usage). Next came assembler language and FORTRAN, each with about 1/3 of the users indicating moderate or heavy usage. PL/1 and BASIC are installed at 20% of the sites, with mixed usage; 8% reported heavy APL usage; and ALGOL and

PASCAL are installed at a few sites, neither being used much. About 20% reported moderate to heavy usage of other languages. Language usage surveys worldwide typically find COBOL on top. It may be followed by FORTRAN [10] or PL/1 [12], or assembler [16], all exceeding usage of PASCAL, BASIC, APL, or ALGOL.

(ii) Systems Software Related Concerns. When asked to rate their operating systems, about 60% were “highly satisfied” and felt they were “easy to use”; 40% indicated “average” satisfaction and ease of use. DBMS evaluations were mixed: 10% were not satisfied, 70% expressed average satisfaction, and 20% were highly satisfied.

Two thirds of the users reported that their SSW was well supported but 25% have too much SSW down-time. Obtaining adequate training on SSW is a problem for about half the users; about 1/3 consider SSW prices excessive. Many expressed concern about overhead on the system caused by SSW and again more contact among users of similar software was desired.

## 3.2.3. Staff and Organization

(i) Staff Profile. User sites had staff sizes ranging from 3 to 248 and an “average” computer center in Kuwait would have 35 staff members: 1 DP manager, 2 project or program managers, 3 systems analysts, 8 applications programmers, 2 systems programmers, 4 operations/data control supervisors, 11 operators/data entry personnel, no data base administrator (DBA), data communication specialist or researcher, and 4 others. Twenty percent of the sites however did report having a DBA, 13% have at least one communications specialist and 5% have computer science or management science researchers. Comparing the 20% DBA figure with the 50% DBMS figure (section 3.2.2) may parallel the situation reported by Perez [18] where a DBMS may be bought first (to acquire technological sophistication) with application decisions and design methodology upgrades coming later.

A priority for Kuwait like other developing countries is in developing indigenous expertise in developing and managing its own systems. Kuwait though, unlike many developing countries, has a scarcity of national manpower in general, which reflects in computing staff as well as in all other job categories. However, by nationality, 62% of all DP staff are Arabs (including Kuwaitis) and 19% are Kuwaiti showing that from a regional point of view, Arab dependence on imported expertise is not excessive and the percentage of Kuwaiti staff is not too low.

(ii) Organization. The status of computing within an organization can often be determined by looking at an organization chart. Traditionally, computer departments were first located within one of the functional areas, often under accounting or finance. As usage evolves, the department attains a functional status of its own reporting to a vice presidential level executive. In the most mature organizations the information systems manager reports directly to top management and computing is identified as a major organizational resource [1,3].

In Kuwait 20% of the computer departments are located within another functional area, 42% report to second line management, and 38% enjoy top organizational status, reporting directly to the chief executive. Thus, in most organizations, the importance of the information processing function is well acknowledged.

(iii) Staff-related Concerns. The most pressing problems reported were difficulty in recruiting qualified staff: first in managerial or supervisory positions, second in technical positions. 64% felt this was due to inability to offer sufficiently high salaries; 60% felt organizational hiring policy was a factor; and some hoped for more local graduates with the required qualifications. Several expressed the need for recognition of DP as a professional career. About half felt their current staff was underpaid, but most did not experience excessive staff turnover.

## 3.2.4 Expenditure

Trends in computer expenditure have been studied by several leading industry analysts. Lecht [13] and Cortada [5] reported that current EDP expenditure (worldwide average) approximates 35% on hardware, 45% staff-related, and 10% other items, with expectations that hardware % will decline, software % remain constant, and % on staff and other items will increase. In the U.S., Delone [7] reported about 44% of EDP budgets are spent on hardware, with less experienced organizations spending a higher % on hardware. In Kuwait, average expenditures were found at 38% hardware, 15% software, 35% staff, and 12% other (with 8% confidence interval due to sample size reduction for these questions).

## 3.3. Application Development ... The Process

## 3.3.1 Application Selection

In the U.S., the degree of user awareness and involvement prior to the operational phase has increasingly been acknowledged as a major ingredient of a successful application $[6,14]$ . One indication of user participation is the existence of a steering committee; another is the extent to which users suggest new applications. Other factors correlated with successful computer usage are top management involvement $[15]$ , and the existence of a long-term information systems plan $[6,15]$ . In Kuwait applications were selected as given in Table 5.

These results, plus the fact that about 2/3 of the organizations had long-term (3-5 year) information systems plans, indicate that application selection procedures are frequently similar to those found to be successful in the U.S. (See also section 3.5.3).

## 3.3.2. Development Methodologies

Two general approaches to application development are to use an existing package, or to develop a tailored system. Then a tailored system might be developed: by a consulting firm or by in-house staff; using a generalized application development package (such as a DBMS), or via full implementation in a standard programming language. Various life cycle management techniques as well as automated and non-automated development methodologies could be utilized.

Regarding applications and their method of development, a Datamation survey conducted in the U.S. [21], found 69% of applications being developed by in-house staff, 19% using existing packages, and 12% other. By comparison, in Kuwait 69% of applications are also being developed by in-house staff, 16% using existing packages, and 15% are turn-key projects. In Europe, Disman [8] investigated application software purchased as a percentage of total systems developed and found for example, about 12% for Italy and 35% for the United Kingdom. Comparing Kuwait (at 31%) with these figures for selected Western countries shows that Kuwait is at a similar degree of dependency on external organizations for software development.

## 3.3.3 Development Concerns

Major concerns centered on user involvement during development: the need for more user commitment, more ease in obtaining test data from users, user education, better user liaison groups. Many managers hope to take full advantage of productivity aids recently introduced at their installations. Upgrading current applications to data base implementation was another common concern. Managers frequently reported “difficulty in keeping up with requests for new applications” and “difficulty in specifying requirements”; about 1/3 encountered excessive delay in application development and felt documentation was often inadequate. These are all well-known human and software engineering problems receiving considerable attention in the developed countries.

## 3.4. Applications ... The Outputs

## 3.4.1. Application Areas

Users were asked to identify computer applications that were either in operation or under development in their organizations. The results, including average number of years of operation for each area, are presented in Table 7.

## 3.4.2. Application Level

To help assess the level of computer applications in Kuwait, several measures were derived:

(i) Functional level. This factor measures the level of an application by examining and assessing its functions: record-keeping functions were considered “basic”; integrated functions, “advanced”; and decision support functions, “very advanced”. Scientific applications were also categorized by functional complexity.

The average functional level, by application area, is shown in Table 7. Financial and management levels were very advanced mainly due to the number of users with integrated management information systems and financial modeling applications. The average functional level for all user applications was “advanced”. These findings, compared to “basic” levels reported in [9] and [17] (both based on 1978 data), are indicative of the advancements being made in a few years in Kuwait.

(ii) Operational level. This factor measures the level of an application by examining the operational mode and degree of user interface, with batch, on-line, and real-time applications at "basic", "advanced", and "very advanced" levels respectively, and more direct user involvement representing a more advanced operational level.

Almost all users were running at least “a few” on-line applications (40% had “many”); 72% ran “many” or “all” applications in batch mode, and 15% ran “all” real-time applications. By comparison, Jenkins et al. [11] reported about 45% of systems in large U.S. organizations were on-line, the rest batch; Merten [16] reported 28% of software was interactive. Regarding user interface, 75% have at least “some” applications where data entry is decentralized and under user control, while 87% have at least “some” applications where users can query the system directly. Combining these features, and considering all users and all applications, the operational level averaged between “basic” and “advanced”.

(iii) Application technology level. This factor measures the level of an application by considering whether the following technologies were used: database technology, query systems, graphics, or distributed systems. Where more were applied, a higher level was indicated. On the average, computer users applied one.

(iv) Combined level. An overall application level was assigned to a user based on the average functional level of all the user's applications, plus the user's operational level, plus the user's application technology level. Possible values for this measure range from "very basic" to "very advanced". For computer users in Kuwait, the range was from "basic" to "advanced", and the average level was "low advanced".

## 3.4.3. Application Counts and Growth Rates

For a typical computer using organization, between 10 and 11 applications were in operation and between 5 and 6 new applications were under development. The average ratio of new to operational applications was thus a high 0.68, an indication of the rapid expansion of computer usage in Kuwait.

## 3.5. Usage ... The Control

Users were asked to provide their assessment of the results of computerization by indicating to what extent they agreed or disagreed that several possible results of computerization had been realized in their organizations, by listing major benefits thus far derived, major obstacles encountered, and greatest needs for enhancing effective computing. (Note that the term “benefits” here will thus refer to “perceived benefits” as indicated by survey respondents).

## 3.5.1. Benefits

The benefit most often being realized by the users of computers in Kuwait is “improved customer service” followed by “the ability to perform tasks not previously possible”; “greater efficiency in organization operation”, and “more accurate information for decision makers”. From 85 to 93% either agreed or strongly agreed that these benefits were in fact realized in their organizations and no users disagreed (the rest were not sure). Most users also indicated that computer usage led to more timely information for decision makers; 65% agreed that computer usage had improved the quality of their work environment; and 60% agreed that computer usage had resulted in large savings in clerical labor.

Operational management was deriving the most benefits (in 94% of the organizations), middle management next (in 87%), followed by top management deriving benefits in 75% of the organizations.

As a general assessment, users were asked whether usage of computers had actually resulted in the savings and benefits they had anticipated. About half of the users agreed or strongly agreed that this was the case, 10% disagreed and the rest were not sure.

## 3.5.2. Obstacles and Needs

Most obstacles reported related to user interface: lack of compute awareness by users and management at different levels; problems with quality of original data; and organizational resistance to change. Nevertheless, respondents noted that it was not excessively difficult to integrate computers into their organizations' operations, and about 2/3 felt that computer usage was well supported and received by most users. Respondents noted the needs for: enhanced training (for staff and users at all levels); more local interaction and international communication; and more evaluation and follow up on both successful and unsuccessful computing experiences.

## 3.5.3. Internal Assessment

By combining responses, a measure of total perceived benefits was derived for each user. This measure was used to investigate whether selected components of the computer usage system relate to more effective computer utilization in Kuwait.

At $\alpha = 0.05$ , there was no significant difference in total benefits realized when users were classified as follows:

● with or without an information systems plan

● with or without a steering committee

● with or without a DBMS

\- by organizational level of the computer department

● by level of expenditure

● by sector of the economy

The small group of users who had all applications developed by consultants did show a significantly higher total benefits measure than users with few or no applications developed by consultants. Assuming external consultants had more experience than in-house staff for these users, one could interpret these results to mean that experience (for developers) leads to more benefits for organizations.

When the total benefits measure was regressed against selected continuous factors, it was found that those organizations using computers the longest were deriving the most benefits, but neither the number of applications, nor the level of the applications was significantly related to benefits derived (again at $\alpha = 0.05$ ).

Thus the nontransferrable in tangibles of time (for organizations) and experience (for developers) lead to more benefits being realized.

## 4. Summary and Conclusions

An important concern for developing countries is the successful transfer of computing technology. Computing technology relates not only to the tangible hardware and systems software that can be purchased; it pervades the entire computer usage system including, i.e., decision making procedures; software development methodologies; planning and organization of the information resource; training and interfacing staff and users; developing and integrating successful applications into an organization; and evaluating computing experiences. A study of all these aspects enables a better understanding of the transfer status in a developing country.

Whether computing technology can be successfully transferred to developing countries at a high technology level has been discussed theoretically from opposing points of view, and the potential benefits and obstacles to successful computer utilization in developing countries have been noted. Few empirical studies concerning these issues appear in the literature however.

Kuwait is a developing country where due to its favorable financial position, scarcity of human resources, and innovative outlook, computers are widely used: there are many different types of organizations, large and small, with varying years of computing experience, that have been using computers for a variety of applications. Thus this sample and this case study can provide insight into the results of computerization across a wide spectrum of usage in a developing country.

This study has found that computers are well utilized as illustrated by generally high utilization rates and application growth rates, and that computing technology has been transferred successfully as measured by perceived organizational benefits being realized. Comparative analysis (based on studies cited) revealed:

\- By technology trends and standards: hardware and systems software are at a high level, hardware selection, fairly high.

\- By software usage comparisons with developed countries: DBMS usage is less than in U.S., similar to Finland; performance monitor usage, similar to U.S.; language usage, similar to U.S., Europe, and worldwide.

\- By application development methodology comparison with developed countries: application selection, similar to U.S.; development methodology, similar to U.S. and Europe.

\- By application level definitions: advanced functional level, basic/advanced operational level; low-advanced overall level.

\- By evolutionary stages of computer center organization: mature organizational status.

\- By problem areas in software engineering: current pressing problems are apparent in Kuwait.

\- By factors leading to more successful applications: organizational and methodological factors found to be related to increased benefits in the U.S. were not significant factors in Kuwait; a major ingredient of success in Kuwait was time: the number of years computers had been in use.

Based on these findings we conclude that a developing country can acquire computing technology at a high level and apply it to realize benefits; and that organizations using computers longer derive more benefits (regardless of the number of applications or their levels).
