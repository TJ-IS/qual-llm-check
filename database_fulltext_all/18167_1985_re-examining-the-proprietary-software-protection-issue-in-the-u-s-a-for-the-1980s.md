# Re-examining the Proprietary Software Protection Issue in the U.S.A. for the 1980s

Charles R. Franz

University of Missouri-Columbia, MO 65211, U.S.A.

Recent developments in statutory and case law in the USA now make it possible to protect by copyright, proprietary computer programs from unauthorized copying provided that the copyright-holding party attempts to display the copyright notice publicly. Considerable progress has been made toward patentable computer software, but the issue of whether a computer program itself may be patented still remains unresolved. The Patent and Trademark Office (PTO) and the Court of Customs and Appeals (CCPA) have agreed that the application of a computer program, containing a mathematical algorithm, in a process may constitute patentable subject matter. The cases pertaining to copyrightable and patentable software together with an explanation of the wording of the new Section 117 of the 1976 Copyright Act are shown in the Appendixes to the article. The paper presents the issues pertaining to copyright and patent protection of computer software and some relevant cases for each issue. It concludes with a discussion of unresolved questions in protecting proprietary software.

Keywords: Computer program protection, proprietary software protection, copyrightable computer software, copying computer programs, software patenting, Copyright Act of 1976, patentable software, amendment to Copyright Act of 1976, machine readable copyright notice

## 1. The Basis for Software Protection

The issue of proprietary software protection in the United States has changed considerably in the past few years. Until recently much confusion has existed regarding the proper method for protecting proprietary software $[16]$ . When Congress made amendments to the Copyright Act of 1976, some dramatic effects on recent court rulings about copyrightable computer programs in read-only-memories (ROM) resulted. Also, recent rulings in the patentability of computer programs has established a standard that both the CCPA and the PTO have accepted.

It is an elementary principle that copyright law only protects the specific expression of an idea and not the idea itself [4]. Copyright protection for an original work of authorship does not extend to just any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, or principle of discovery [41]. This restriction is the source of difficulty in attempting to protect computer software by copyright law. Copyright protection is most questionable when software becomes so embedded in the machine that it functions as a procedure or a process, and thus is not protected by copyright law. If this is the case, then patenting software may be the proper action.

## 1.2. Basis for Patent Protection

The Constitution of the United States provides the basis for patent protection in Article I, Section 8 [37]. A patent grants the exclusive right of use for 17 years to “whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter or new and useful improvement thereof…[43]”. Since software cannot be classified as a “machine, manufacture or composition of matter,” it has historically been difficult to patent software. Patent protection therefore is only viable when software is classified as a “process”. The United States Supreme Court has defined “process” as an act or series of acts for treating certain materials so as to transform or reduce them to a different state or thing [6].

The court recently stated that the clue to patentability of a process claim lies with insuring that transforming and reducing something to a different state or thing does not include particular machines $[13]$ . It is a basic rule of patent law that laws of nature, mathematical formulas, scientific truths, or natural phenomena do not constitute patentable subject matter $[14]$ . The Supreme Court furthermore stated that ideas themselves are not patentable, that abstract principles are fundamental truths and no one has the exclusive right to claim these for themself. Thus, for computer programs to be patentable, they must fall within the definition of “process” outlined above, while avoiding classification as an idea or fundamental truth.

The legal structure for providing proprietary software protection is just beginning to take shape with the amendment to the Copyright Act of 1976 (December 12, 1980) and recent computer software patentable and copyright decisions. The purpose of this paper is to present the current state of computer software protection under copyright and patent laws, but also to examine and clarify the obstacles still remaining in computer software protection under the law.

## 2. Copyright Protection

Until 1978, the effective date of the 1976 Copyright Act, copyrighting computer programs was governed by the Copyright Act of 1909, which held that material that could be read by the human eye was copyright protected. When Apollo Co., copied a player piano roll from White-Smith Music Publishing Co. [39], the United States Supreme Court ruled that no infringement had taken place, since the player piano roll was not a “copy” of the underlying sheet music. By analogy, this case suggested that the copyright of a computer program would not prevent the unauthorized duplication of the program from ROM, floppy disk or magnetic tape because the copyrighted program embedded in such material objects is not a “copy” of the underlying program on paper [34]. Hence, prior to the enactment of the 1976 Act, the unauthorized duplication of a copyrighted computer program would constitute an infringement only if the computer being copied was in eye-readable form.

The Copyright Act of 1976 more broadly defines “literary works” and makes computer programs proper subject matter for copyright protection for two reasons. First, the 1976 Act includes in its definition of literary works, any work expressed in words, numbers or special symbols regardless of the material object it is stored or embodied in. Second, the 1976 Act defined “copy” to be any stored work capable of being perceived, reproduced or otherwise communicated directly or indirectly with the aid of a machine. In essence the 1976 Act abrogated the White principle of eyereadable copies and made computer programs proper subject matter for copyright protection.

## 2.1. The Enactment of Section 117

Congress was not prepared to address the changes in the law to fully protect software developers, mainly due to the complex nature of computer technology and computer software. Hence, they enacted Section 117 as part of the 1976 Copyright Act. As a result, section 117 of the 1976 Act preserved the status quo by stating that the owner of a software copyright had no more or less rights with the use of his “literary work” in conjunction with a computer-type system than was provided prior to the law’s enactment. Considerable confusion resulted due to the interpretation required to determine the applicability of Section 117 to a particular case. Three important cases (See Appendix A) illustrate not only that computer programs can be proper subject matter for copyright, but how the issue of interpretation of Section 117 led to different conclusions as to why programs should be copyright protected.

The Tandy court and the GCA court (second and third copyright cases) concluded that object code is protected by copyright, but through different reasoning. The Tandy court concluded that a program in object code is proper subject matter for copyright. But the GCA court did not hold that a program in a ROM was copyrightable; it merely said that since the source code was copyrighted, the object code would be protected also. This merely seems to hold that the object code is a “copy” under the 1976 Act and thus is protected by the copyright of the original program.

## 2.2. New Copyright Law

The confusion that resulted led Congress to repeal Section 117 by amending the Copyright Act of 1976. In 1975, a National Commission on New Technological Uses of copyrighted works (CONTU) was established to study the problems of copyrighting new technologies. While the study's results were incomplete at the time of enacting the 1976 Act, the commission made its recommendations to Congress in its Final Report of July 31, 1978 after three years of study [8]. Congress accepted the findings of CONTU and repealed section 117 by making the changes to the Copyright Act of 1976 (effective December 12, 1980) which permitted copies of computer software to be made under certain restrictive conditions. (See Appendix B for details.)

As a result of the 1980 amendment, the 1908 White doctrine is no longer available to would-be infringers. The repeal of old section 117 makes the

1976 Act applicable to all uses of computer software beginning December 12, 1980, and grants more copyright protection to software developers than ever before.

The case of Apple Computers, Inc. v. Franklin Computer, Corp. affirmed the copyrightable nature of operating systems and computer programs embedded in ROM [47]. According to the Court of Appeals that ruled in favor of Apple, Franklin was guilty of copyright infringement when it copied operating system programs developed and used by Apple so that programs designed for the Apple II computer would also run on the Franklin Ace computer. This decision by the court does little to clarify Section 117, but will discourage many companies from producing compatibles by copying operating system software.

## 3. Patent Protection

Patent protection is provided by Article I, Section 8 of the United States Constitution to anyone who invents or discovers a process, machine, manufacture or new substance, for a period of 17 years [43]. Since computer software is not a machine, manufacture or a new man-made substance, to be patentable it must fall within the definition of “process” and also avoid being classified as an idea or fundamental truth.

However, the issue of patentability of computer software has long been in dispute. The Patent and Trademark Office (PTO) has consistently denied patent applications for software. The PTO enunciated a basic policy in 1968 which ruled that computer programming is not patentable [45]. Although the PTO later rescinded this guideline in favor of a case by case approach, their basic policy remains in strong opposition to patentability of software [46]. Almost as consistently as the PTO denies patentability, the CCPA advocates the patentability of software.

Historically, the basic question of patent protection of computer software has revolved around the implementation of mathematical algorithms on a computer. The basis for disagreement between the PTO and the CCPA has been whether computer programs (algorithms) constitute “mental steps” (see In re Abrams [1]) or technological or useful arts [25]. The CCPA has used the “technological arts” test [25] to approve patents on software inventions.

The Supreme Court had two opportunities to consider the software patentability issue in the case of Gottschalk v. Benson and Parker v. Flook. (See Appendix C for a detailed description.) But in both cases it ruled that the processes containing computer programs were not patentable.

The CCPA, which had long advocated the patentability of computer programs, was dissatisfied with the Supreme Court's position in the previous two cases and announced a new test for determining whether processes containing a computer program were patentable subject matter (see In re Freeman [17]). The test contained two parts. (1) The computer program (algorithm) must be implemented to refine or limit steps in a process, i.e., it must be necessary for successfully implementing the process.

(2) The computer program cannot just be a mathematical algorithm, which is simply presented and solved by the claimed invention (as in the Benson and Flook cases). It must be applied to some physical element or process steps to be patentable. (See In re Walter [38] for a legal description.)

## 3.1. New Criteria for Patentable Processes

The Supreme Court last addressed the issue of software patentability in 1981 in the case of $Di$ - $amond \ v. \ Diehr$ [12]. In this case $Diehr$ firmly established the court's willingness to allow patents on processes utilizing mathematical algorithms. The $Diehr$ court was of the opinion that $Diehr$ was not seeking patent protection for a mathematical formula, but for a process consisting of many steps, including a mathematical algorithm, for curing synthetic rubber. Thus, the $Diehr$ court ruled that the process must be viewed as a whole, seemingly overruling the Supreme Court's inventive application test (see $Flock$ in Appendix C). The $Diehr$ court concluded that an “application” of a mathematical algorithm to a known process may well be deserving of patent protection. Although the Supreme Court seems to have implicitly adopted the CCPA's new two-part test (based on $Flook$ and $Walter$ ), the effect of the ruling in the $Diehr$ case remains unclear at best.

## 4. Summary and Conclusions

This paper has attempted to analyze significant cases to determine the extent that the law provides protection for proprietary software. The major conclusion of this paper holds that an examination of the Copyright Act's legislative history clearly establishes that computer programs are appropriate subject matter for copyright protection. The 1976 Act clearly established than an original computer program fixed in any tangible medium of expression from which it can be perceived, reproduced or otherwise communicated, either directly or with the aid of a machine or device is eligible for copyright protection.

Some ambiguities still remain regarding the implementation of new Section 117 of the Copyright Act. First, full impact of the new Section 117 must await judicial interpretation, even though the latest interpretation of Section 117 has been the Apple decision, which found Franklin guilty of copyright infringement. The Philadelphia Appeals Court ruled in favor of Apple's copyright claims on its Apple II operating system software. However, all that has really been settled is that computer programs are a category of copyright protectable materials such as are books. The court did not rule definitively on copyrighting specific types of software (such as operating systems) and instead elected to leave open the question of whether Apple's operating system software is governed by the long-standing idea-expression doctrine, and therefore, not copyrightable [48].

Second, Section 117 allows the “owner” of a copy of a computer program to make certain copies. But it is not clear whether this language prohibits the “rightful possessor” of a copy from making the same copies that an owner could. It probably does, since CONTU recommended the use of “rightful possessor” and Congress deleted such reference and replaced it with “owner” [18]. However, since “owner of a copy” is not defined by the 1976 Act, just how broad it will be interpreted by the courts is not now known. The computer software industry is hoping for a very strict and narrow reading of “owner” so that the long awaited protection afforded by the 1976 Act will not be diluted.

Third, Section 117 requires owners to destroy all archival copies once possession of the original copy ceases to be rightful, but this destruction requirement does not apply to “use” copies or adaptations. The reason for this distinction is not clear. The statute gives no guidance as to when the continued possession of the original copy ceases to be rightful so as to require all archival copies be destroyed.

A copyright notice must be placed on all publicly distributed copies from which the work (computer program) can be visually perceived, such as on floppy disk jacket, a video display terminal, or on visually perceptible printouts near the work. The software developer no longer loses his copyright protection if the notice has been omitted from a relatively small number of publicly distributed copies, or if the work (computer program) has been registered within five years after the publication without notice and a reasonable effort has been made to add the notice to all publicly distributed copies.

The patentability issue between the PTO and CCPA for so many years has apparently ended. In the August 1982 decision of In re Pardo and Landau [27] the CCPA restated the two-part patentability test: First the patent claim is analyzed to determine whether a mathematical algorithm is directly or indirectly utilized. Second, the claim as a whole is further analyzed to determine whether the algorithm is applied in any manner to steps in the process.

Thus, it seems that the application of a computer program in a process may constitute patentable subject matter. However, this leaves unanswered the issue of whether a computer program itself may be patented. The Diehr court did not form an opinion on this fundamental issue $[12]$ . What is clear from Diehr is that (1) mathematical algorithms are not patentable subject matter; and (2) process claims containing otherwise patentable subject matter are not rendered unpatentable simply by the presence of a mathematical algorithm. The most important aspect of Diehr is that it established a standard with regard to the status of computer software in the patent system that has been accepted by both the CCPA and the PTO.

## Appendix A

## Copyright Cases

The case of Data Cash Systems, Inc. v. JS&A Group, Inc. [10] provided the first opportunity for a court to interpret section 117 of the 1976 Act. Data Cash developed a program that was stored in ROM for use in its hand-held computerized chess game. The source program had been copyrighted.

JS&A marketed a similar chess game with an identical ROM and the federal district court assumed that JS&A directly copied the ROM chip from Data Cash. The court interpreted Section 117 to mean that Data Cash's ROM was not a copy of the original source program. Hence, JS&A was not guilty of violating the copyright law. This court's decision seemed to suggest that all problems in the area of computer software usage were to be resolved in accordance with pre-1978 law.

The view of what constituted a “copy” in the Data Cash case was flatly rejected in the case of Tandy Corporation v. Personal Micro Computer, Inc. [36]. The Tandy court interpreted Section 117 to apply only to problems of copyrighted materials such as books, magazines and computer programs that were input into a computer. In this particular case, Personal Micro had duplicated Tandy’s silicon chip, which had a copyrighted computer program imprinted upon it. The court ruled earlier, in accordance with the wording of the 1976 Act, that a computer program imprinted on a silicon chip (ROM) was protected by copyright. The court was convinced that a computer program (under sections 101 and 102 of the 1976 Act) is a “work of authorship” subject to copyright and that a silicon chip is a “tangible medium of expression” within the meaning of the law. The Tandy court ruled that Personal Micro was guilty of copying a program from a silicon chip and thus, rejected the Data Cash view of computer programs as simply “machine control elements” that only perform mechanical work [9].

The third case of GCA Corp. v. Chance [18] follows the reasoning of Tandy and holds that an object code in a ROM is copyright protected since the source code was copyrighted. GCA had copyrighted its source code but not the object code and Chance argued that copyrighting the source code does not protect the object code. But the court decided that Chance was guilty of unauthorized duplication of object code, since the object code is the encryption of the copyrighted source code and must be treated as one work.

## Appendix B

## Amendment to the Copyright Act of 1976

1. That section 101 be amended to add the following definition: A "computer program" is a set of statements or instructions to be used directly or indirectly in a computer in order to bring about a certain result.

2. That section 117 as enacted be repealed.

3. That a new section 117 be enacted as follows: #117: Limitations on Exclusive Rights: Computer Programs. Notwithstanding the provisions of #106, it is not an infringement for the owner of a copy of a computer program to make or authorize the making of another copy or adaptation of that computer program provided: (1) that such a new copy or adaptation is created as an essential step in the utilization of the computer program in conjunction with a machine and that it is used in no other manner, or (2) that such new copy or adaptation is for archival purposes only and that all archival copies are destroyed in the event that continued possession of the computer program should cease to be rightful. Any exact copies prepared in accordance with the provisions of this section may be leased, sold, or otherwise transferred, along with the copy from which such copies were prepared, only as part of the lease, sale, or other transfer of all rights in the program. Adaptations so prepared may be transferred only with the authorization of the copyright owner. [31]

## Interpretation of Section 117 Amendment

A Computer program is a set of statements or instructions for the purpose of producing certain results in a computer. Section 117 as enacted was repealed and is not part of the law anymore.

A new Section 117 was enacted that permitted owners of computer software to make copies or adapt the software to their circumstances if the copy is essential to the running of the owner's copy, such as creating object code (during compilation) from a purchased source code program. A copy be made for archival purposes to have a backup copy in the event that the original copy becomes damaged and rendered useless.

Furthermore, exact copies may be sold, leased or transferred only if this is permitted by the original lease or license agreement. Any adaptations prepared in this manner must be authorized by the copyright owner who has issued the lease or license.

## Appendix C

## Supreme Court Cases

In the 1972 case of Gottschalk v. Benson [20] the court denied a patent to Benson for developing a method for converting binary-coded decimal numerals into pure binary numerals for use with a digital computer. The court, concerned about protection of natural truths or ideas from patent monopoly, decided that the conversion process had no practical application except with a computer and thus was not a patentable process.

A second case, Parker v. Flook [28], attempted to obtain a patent on a rubber curing process which involved the use of a computer to test certain temperature conditions. The Supreme Court ruled that a process involving a computer program was not patentable. But the court formulated a new test, the inventive application test, which required that the process itself must be new and useful, regardless of the novelty of the mathematical algorithm or computer program employed.
