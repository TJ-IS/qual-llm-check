# Assessing the Organizational Climate for OA Implementation

William N. Ledbetter and Charles A. Snyder
Auburn University, Dept. of Management, 322 Thach Hall, AL
36849, U.S.A.

and

James F. Cox

University of Georgia, Georgia, U.S.A.

The attitudes of employees toward any new system implementation are a critical factor in the system's success. Prior to implementing an office automation system, a study was performed in a large department comprised of ten offices in one large firm. Attitudes of employees toward office automation were assessed, as well as their level of understanding of office automation concepts and techniques, and the general communications climate within the organization.

Three different questionnaires were administered, and the results were analyzed for significant differences by job level and by office, using Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA procedures. Interesting and significant findings are presented and discussed. This approach appears to have merit in gaining a clearer understanding of the organizational environment prior to implementing an office automation system. This information is critical in developing the strategy and plans for successful implementation of an office automation system.

Keywords: Office automation implementation, information needs assessment, organizational climate assessment, attitudes toward OA

Fears of automation, particularly office automation, run the gamut from the fear of job loss or displacement to the physical harm due to video display terminal radiation [23]. Managers as well as secretarial and clerical workers exhibit fear of change in the nature and content of their jobs [26]. Cyberphobia is a word coined to describe this phenomenon. It provokes behavior ranging from passive resistance to active sabotage of the system [23,26]. Certainly the problem is serious enough to warrant assessment early in the development process to identify potential problems and undertake preventive measures.

The firm in which this study was conducted is very prominent in the communications industry. The department is divided into ten offices, headed by a vice-president (whose office is designated as an eleventh office for analysis). Office managers are designated as job level 3, staff professionals 2, secretaries and administrative assistants 1, and clerical personnel 0.

Of the approximately fifty employees in the department, responses were received from forty-six. Due to the nature of their work, almost all, including managers, had basic typing skills, generally with access to word processing equipment. The managers presumed that employees' attitudes toward office automation were positive, and that general knowledge of office automation concepts was high. Employees were aware that the organization was considering the development and implementation of an office automation system and that the study team would be interviewing them to assess the need for an OA system.

## 2. Literature Review

The literature on office automation has a deficiency in research covering planning and implementation of integrated OA systems. In this paper the term “integrated OA” describes a system capable of performing a variety of integrated functions with a uniform and consistent user interface. Certainly, such systems range from simple to complex, with the most basic offering word processing, electronic mail/message distribution, and electronic filing. At the more advanced level, these are integrated with record- and file-processing, electronic-calendar, spread-sheet, graphics, and even voice store-and-forward [18]. There is no system today offering all OA and data-processing functions. Wang [34] outlines the six technologies required for this.

The literature also lacks discussion of human factors effects on OA implementation. There is, however, acknowledgement of the importance of employee opinion and attitude on the implementation of new technology $[2,3,5,12,14,19,22,27,$ and 33]. Many reports outline the use of review committees for drawing up plans and encouraging future user support while instilling cooperative attitudes $[8,9,13,24,25,31]$ .

The task force approach has proven acceptable for some firms, but some (e.g., [30]) see a need for a permanent organizational entity to aid in developing the critical interfaces, user education, and training.

While there is some disagreement (e.g., [16]) most authors strongly support OA planning [1,8,9,12,17,20,29]. Most emphasize the need to assess the impact of technology on the users, but few provide guidance. There is a clear recognition of the impact of change [4,11,21,32] and most identify the introduction of change as critical to success. The assessment of user attitudes, skills, and knowledge is seldom viewed as a prerequisite.

Telesca [30] reports an effort undertaken by Aetna Life & Casualty to develop programs and policies on issues surrounding the introduction of technology. Aetna's management viewed employee ability and willingness to use technology to be the critical factor. They established a People/Technology Program (P/TP) unit to check that OA implementation would balance technology and the needs. The P/TP group obtained employee opinions that showed the chief problem was lack of a say in the decision whether technology would be introduced. The means of opinion assessment was not given, nor was any detail on size of the sample, or analysis method, etc.

Springer [27] reported on the experiences of State Farm Insurance Companies in OA planning activities. These took place over a four-year period and involved worker attitude surveys and interviews during the problem definition and field test phases. A user survey was also conducted after a simulation or operational test phase. Springer noted that “Although many fine examples of high-quality, empirically based research do exist, it has been State Farm's experience that many guidelines and recommendations are not supported by data, do not transfer directly from Europe to the U.S., and should be examined critically." Guidelines and recommendations are seldom bolstered by empirical evidence of their efficacy in applications.

## 3. Methodology

The literature review provided a focus for developing the study instruments. Three questionnaires were developed or adapted to obtain a measure of the general communication climate within the organization, specific attitudes and perceptions of various facets of OA implementation, and the relative degree of familiarity with concepts, terms, and techniques.

## 3.1. Dennis Communications Climate Inventory

The Dennis Communications Climate Inventory was chosen to measure the relative status of communications within the organization [10]. This is a forty-five item questionnaire (see Appendix 1) in which the items can be grouped into five factors: superior/subordinate communications, quality of information, superior openness/candor, upward communication opportunity, and reliability of information.

It was reasoned that if there is not already a relatively healthy state of organizational communication, then this problem needs to be addressed and corrected first. Clearly any attempt to implement any significant organizational change will be successful only if there is clear communications.

Responses to each item were obtained on a five-point Likert scale (1 for “to a very little extent” to 5 for “to a very great extent”). Data were analyzed to obtain mean responses and standard deviations for each item and factor (after grouping). The data were further analyzed to determine significant differences between job levels and between offices for each of the factors utilizing the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA procedure.

## 3.2. Office Automation Questionnaire

A questionnaire was developed to measure the attitudes and perceptions of personnel toward OA (see Appendix 2). One-hundred items were developed and/or adapted from a questionnaire used to audit manufacturing information systems [6]. Items are grouped into six factors: education, resistance to change, acceptance and involvement, management commitment, communications, and planning. The education group of items deal with employees' own perception of their OA knowledge and need for additional education. The second group attempts to assess the resistance to change due to this project, while the third group focuses on acceptance of and desire for involvement in the OA project. Perceptions of management's commitment to the project comprise the fourth group, while organizational communication focused specifically on the OA project is the fifth group. Finally, the sixth group assesses employees' view of the amount, degree, and quality of planning for the-OA project.

Similar analysis was performed on this data as in the last section.

## 3.3. Office Automation Components and Functions Survey

A questionnaire was developed to assess the degree of knowledge and familiarity with some of the components and functions of OA (see figure 1). All respondents were asked to indicate their familiarity with each of the thirty-one terms. Responses on a five-point scale ranged from 1 (“none”) to 5 (“extensive”) knowledge.

Mean responses for each item were computed, with attempts to analyse significant differences by job level and between offices utilizing the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA procedure.

The last two items comprise the fifth factor (perceptions of the reliability of information), as shown in table 5.

The results of a Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA of mean ranks for each of the five factors by job level is given in table 6. Two were statistically significant; first, the differences in perceptions of superior/subordinate communications at the 0.09 level (inversely related to job level – the higher the job level, the less positive the response). While this is difficult to interpret, the overall mean response rate of 3.60 seems good, and one could argue that this result would be preferred to one in which the perceptions of superior/subordinate communication grew worse at the lower levels.

The second statistically significant factor measured perceptions of upward communication opportunity; it was significant at the 0.05 level. Interestingly, this factor was directly related to job level, i.e., the higher the job level, the more positive the response. To some extent this result is a function of the nature of the job level. The higher level jobs allow greater control of the work environment, and this is certainly one dimension of the items which comprise this factor. Nevertheless, the results suggest that issues of upward communication should be examined and some remedial actions taken.

The other three factors were not significantly different between job levels. However, the second factor, dealing with the quality of information, had an overall mean response of only 2.94; this is certainly lower than desirable. The quality of information could be critical to effective OA implementation. Employees will have to believe they are being treated honestly, and kept fully informed, or serious problems could arise, especially during the implementation phase.

## 4.2. Office Automation Questionnaire

While examining individual items can provide some insight, grouping common or related items and analyzing them by job level and by office provides more illumination.

The one hundred items were grouped into six categories summarized in tables 8 through 13. They represent:

1) Education - dealing with various facets of perceived understanding of OA.

2) Resistance to change – anxieties or concerns over the impact of OA.

3) Acceptance and involvement - degree of acceptance and desire for involvement.

4) Management commitment - perception of higher management commitment and support.

5) Communications – perceptions of the relative status of communications regarding office automation.

6) Planning - perceptions of the amount, degree, and quality of OA planning.

The results of a Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA of mean ranks for each of the six factors by job level is given in table 14. No statistically significant differences were found, probably due to the small sample size. However, table 8 indicates that the perceived need for education varied inversely with job levels; the lower levels needing more. This was not surprising, since several managers had received training or other exposure, whereas many clerical and secretarial personnel had not. The relatively low response at all levels certainly suggests that an educational and training program is a desirable, maybe essential, prerequisite to OA implementation.

## 4.3. Office Automation Components and Functions Survey

Common terms, such as “word processing” and “electronic typewriters” were the most familiar terms, but none had a mean score as high as 3 (1 is for “none” and a 5 for “extensive” knowledge and familiarity). Nineteen of the thirty-one terms had a mean score less than 2, suggesting a relatively low level of familiarity.

The data were further analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA by job level and by office, see table 16. The differences by job level were significant at the 0.02 level and directly related to job level. Familiarity and knowledge of OA components and functions increase significantly at the higher job levels. This was not surprising; several of the managers and mid-level employees had been exposed to OA concepts and terminology. This does suggest that greater emphasis will probably be needed on education for lower-level employees.

## 5. Conclusions

This study has demonstrated that a multidimensional approach with three separate study instruments can provide significant insight into areas of organizational life critical to successful OA implementation. Specifically, dimensions of organizational communications, of attitudes and perceptions of the OA climate and efforts, and general knowledge and familiarity with OA terms and concepts can be more readily assessed through these instruments. This in turn becomes valuable input in designing strategies and efforts to minimize problems, and plans for successful OA implementation. This study has dealt with data from a relatively small number of observations in a single firm; thus the specific results should not be generalized to other firms. Nevertheless, the instruments can be used in any organization seeking a greater understanding of employees' attitudes and understanding of OA.
