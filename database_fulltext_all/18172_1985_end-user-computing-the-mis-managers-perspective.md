# End-User Computing: The MIS Managers' Perspective

Maryam Alavi

Management Systems&Strategy, College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, Texas 77004, USA

Despite the enthusiasm and its rapid growth, End-User Computing (EUC) is not well-managed. This article investigates EUC and its current practices. EUC experience at 31 organizations representing 12 industries was surveyed. Areas of study include: MIS/DP managers' perceptions and attitudes toward EUC, perceived benefits and difficulties of EUC applications and issues related to management and support of EUC activities. Implications of the survey findings for effective EUC applications are also discussed.

Keywords: Management of End-User Computing, User-Developed Systems.

## 1. Introduction

Recently the phenomenon of End-User Computing (EUC) has received considerable attention from practitioners and researchers in the information systems field. End-user computing means that the user of the results of the computing also creates the software specifications necessary to effect the computing itself [2]. Software specifications may be created by the end-user alone or through assistance from a systems analyst or a programmer.

Some authors view end-user computing as “inevitable” and as a rapidly expanding phenomenon $[3,9]$ . For example, in a recent study $[7]$ it was estimated that by 1990 four out of five professional and administrative workers will be engaged in EUC activities. Another study $[9]$ estimated that the computer capacity employed by EUC activities in large corporations will grow from 10% in 1981 to 70% of an expanded capacity by 1990. With the growth in EUC activities, many information systems and data processing managers face difficult questions concerning the support and outcome of these activities. This article explores MIS/DP managers’ attitudes toward EUC and discusses issues related to its management and support.

## 2. Background

Many users are enthusiastic about development of their own application systems. This enthusiasm can be attributed to three major factors:

1. Progress in hardware/software and information processing technologies. Until recently end-user computing, was not a common practice in organizations. A typical end-user was neither interested nor had the highly specialized technical knowledge required for development of computer systems by traditional tools and programming languages such as COBOL. The advent of powerful software tools for application creation has rapidly changed the picture. “English-like” non-procedural languages and user friendly software $^{1}$ for application creation, combining data management, report generation, and modeling capabilities, have enabled users to develop their own systems with minimal DP training. Furthermore, improvements in the performance/price ratios of computer hardware have made computing power and hardware resources (e.g., micro-computers, computer terminals, etc.) available to end-users on a large scale.

2. Due to a growing application backlog, DP departments are often not highly responsive to user demands for new application systems. Most corporations now have a backlog of three of four years [4]. Many users cannot wait three to four years for their systems. They find the inability of DP to respond to their needs very frustrating.

3. Computer literacy of the users and their awareness of computer capabilities and potentials are increasing. This is attributed to the availability of low cost personal computer hardware and software and readily available sources for acquiring computer literacy (e.g., trade journals, vendor publications, advertisements, computer classes, etc.).

Advocates of EUC feel that it is desirable because end-users possess the vital knowledge of subtleties of the application and its environment. Other reasons for desirability of EUC include [8]:

Ad hoc demand for information reporting may be satisfied rapidly.

By developing their own applications, end-users can obtain results faster (This will relieve or avoid frustrations with the DP department.)

EUC facilities allow the user to modify the system quickly in response to changes in the requirements.

## 3. Research Issues

The purpose of this study was to investigate benefits, difficulties and issues related to the management and support of EUC. Understanding this phenomenon is a prerequisite for its effective management and applications in organizations. EUC experience at a variety of companies was surveyed. The following areas and specific research issues were addressed in the survey.

DP managers' perceptions and attitudes toward EUC.

\- Is EUC successful in the organizations that engage in it?

\- Is EUC beneficial to organizations that engage in it?

\- Should EUC be encouraged in organizations?

\- What is the impact of EUC in the user departments?

\- Are DP managers in favor of EUC or do they feel threatened by it?

\- What are the DP managers' attitudes toward EUC?

Hardware / software resources and configurations used for support of EUC in organizations.

\- What are the prevalent software tools for support of EUC (e.g., high level procedural languages, DBMS and associated query languages, fourth generation languages, etc.)?

\- What are the prevalent hardware tools and configurations currently used in support of EUC (e.g., standalone microcomputers, time-sharing facilities, etc.)?

Specific organizational tasks and functional areas engaging in EUC activities.

\- How prevalent is EUC in different functional areas (e.g., accounting, finance, personnel, etc.)?

\- What is the nature of applications currently served by EUC (modeling, report generation, information access, etc.)?

\- How should these application areas be changed in the future?

Issues related to management and organizational support of EUC.

\- Who manages EUC activities in organizations (user departments, DP departments, steering committees, etc.)

\- How should the current approaches to management of EUC be changed?

\- What are the current practices in support of EUC in organizations (e.g., information center)?

## 4. The Study Approach

The research method selected for the study was the survey method, using mail questionnaires. The survey was conducted between February and June 1983. Three types of questionnaires were administered.

1. Background questionnaire. The purpose of this questionnaire was to obtain background and demographic data from the respondents. Data on items related to the company industry, size, respondents' job title, age, education, tenure, etc. were collected.

2. Inventory questionnaire. This questionnaire contained items related to the current and projected state of end-user computing in the respondents' organization. Items related to the EUC facilities and hardware/software resources were included in the questionnaire.

3. Perceptual questionnaire. This questionnaire was designed to obtain the respondents' attitudes and perceptions toward the support, management and benefits/risks of end-user computing. The perceptual questionnaire items were measured on 5-point Likert type scales. The questionnaires contained 31 structured questions as well as open ended questions for capturing respondents' comments. In order to ensure the clarity and conciseness of the questionnaires, they were used in personal interviews with two MIS/DP managers prior to the mail survey. The two MIS/DP managers were not included in the research sample. Furthermore, in order to evaluate the consistency of the subjects' response to the perceptual questionnaire items coefficient $\alpha$ was calculated. The value obtained, $\alpha = 0.71$ , indicates a relatively high consistency in the responses to the questionnaire items.

The research questionnaires were mailed to 50 senior information systems or data processing managers in nine states. Thirty nine responses were received. Eight responses were not included in the analysis because the respondents were not MIS/DP managers. The companies that were represented in the survey came from 12 different industries. The frequency distribution of the industries represented in the sample is displayed in Figure 1.

The age of the MIS/DP executives who participated in the study ranged from thirty-nine to fifty-one years of age with the average of 43.8.

## 5. Survey Findings

Descriptive statistics and the Wilcoxon test were used to analyze the research data collected. Descriptive statistics were used for analysis of perceptual and attitudinal items measured on 5-point Likert type scales. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-ranks test was used to measure the perceived need for change in the current EUC activities and practices in organizations. The perceived needs for change in EUC activities were measured on Porter type scales. The Porter-type questionnaire items were statements regarding EUC in the organizations where the respondents were asked to rate each statement on two 5-point scales; one scale corresponding to the degree to which the statement described an already existing situation, and the other scale corresponding to the degree to which the statement, in the respondents' view, should describe the situation.

## 6. MIS/DP Managers' Attitudes Toward EUC

As the mean values of the attitudinal items displayed in Table 1 indicate, MIS/DP managers generally had positive attitudes toward EUC activities and were in favor of these activities. However, these managers believed that some MIS/DP professionals (systems analysts and programmers) and some end-users may be threatened by EUC because:

\- EUC is a new phenomenon and requires changes in some of the traditional approaches to information systems development and implementation. Such changes may be perceived by some MIS/DP professionals as disruptive and threatening.

\- Some MIS/DP professionals may be concerned that their skills and knowledge in traditional tools and methods would be devalued. They would like to continue practicing what they are good at [8].

The managers also believed that users may likewise feel threatened by EUC because of their lack of the required computer skills. Also, as indicated in a recent survey [1], some users (particularly functional managers) are uncertain and concerned about the effect of EUC on their careers. They are concerned about becoming “computer-identified,” and being locked in staff positions. Furthermore, EUC changes the way in which the end-users have traditionally performed their jobs. Changes in routines and traditional methods may be resisted. It is important that these issues be explicitly addressed and resolved before attempting to engage in EUC activities on a large scale. Despite the positive attitudes and the current enthusiasm about EUC, some end-users as well as some MIS/DP professionals need much education and support in adapting to EUC.

## 7. Support and Management of EUC Activities

The managers were asked to identify ways in which their MIS/DP departments currently support EUC. The primary support role played by MIS/DP departments is that of hardware and software selection followed by central database administration, end-user education and consultation. Fifty-nine percent of the managers stated that their departments were currently supporting

EUC activities through an Information Center (IC) organization. In addition, a majority of managers felt that MIS/DP should increase the level of support of EUC activities in their organizations.

Issues concerning the management of end-user computing were also investigated. In a majority of the organizations represented in the survey (70%), the MIS/DP department managed the end-user computing hardware and software resources (In these organizations mainframe computers and software were used for EUC). On the whole, however, specific policy guidelines and master plans for EUC activities were uncommon. Steering committees for overseeing EUC activities and management existed in only 37% of the organizations. And, while EUC standards were reportedly developed in 63% of the organizations, they were enforced in only 37% of the organizations.

The following changes in EUC management were prescribed by the MIS/DP managers (in the descending order of the frequency of the recommendation).

1. The MIS/DP department should enforce EUC standards

2. Policy guidelines defining the scope and nature of end-user computing activities should be developed

3. A master plan should define specific EUC activities in the organization

4. End-user computing activities should be coordinated by a steering committee comprised of end-user and MIS/DP managers

There seems to be a demand pull for EUC services in organizations. In fact one MIS/DP executive stated that “EUC within my company has been forced upon the IS (Information Systems) department by the end-users.” This means that MIS/DP managers will be struggling to respond to a demand for EUC services with already overburden resources. This situation leads to an important issue regarding EUC: the need for top management support and involvement in the EUC movement. Some MIS/DP managers seem to underestimate this issue. Most likely the top management support will not be need to “sell” the EUC concept to the end-users, but it will be critical for obtaining resources and authority needed for planning, and management of EUC in organizations. This survey indicated a low level of top management activity and participation in end-user computing. Top management may be unaware or indifferent toward EUC. MIS/DP managers can and should play an important role in changing this situation and obtaining top management involvement and support in EUC. This may be accomplished through education and other mechanisms such as formation of committees. The DP manager in one of the companies stated that, “EUC took tremendous steps forward when a coordination committee consisting of end-user managers and information system personnel was created.” EUC has been a “bottom-up” movement. To be effective in the long run, a “top-down” approach to EUC planning and coordination should be undertaken. EUC policies and guidelines and the framework for planning should be established by the top management and implemented in organizations.

## 8. EUC Tools

In order to obtain a profile of the software tools used in organizations for support of EUC activities, the respondents were asked to indicate the level of end-user utilization of several categories of specific software tools (displayed in Table 2 in descending order of utilization). Next, they were asked to identify the software tools that should be used by the end-user in creating future EUC applications. According to the results of Wilcoxon test (displayed in Table 3) there should be a significant increase in the end-user utilization of: query languages, modeling languages, very high-level non-procedural languages, word processors, and spreadsheet programs.

A variety of different hardware facilities (mainframes, mini and micro-computers) were utilized by the end-users in the organizations in the survey sample. The most common computer hardware facility used for support of EUC activities was large mainframe computers (e.g., IBM 3033), reported by 70% of the managers.

Similarly, a variety of hardware configurations (centralized, decentralized and distributed) were reported. The most prevalent hardware configuration (reported by 70% of the managers) involved supporting end-users through interactive terminals connected to a centralized computer facility. More than half of the managers (54%) reported supporting EUC activities by stand-alone disconnected computers (presumably microcomputers). Outside time sharing facilities were occasionally used by the end-users in one-third of the organizations represented in the survey.

When asked to specify hardware tools and configurations that should be used for support of EUC, MIS/DP managers prescribed a distributed microcomputer network (versus only large mainframe or stand-alone micros).

Hardware/software facilities and configurations have important implications for support and management of EUC. The user interface and utilization of multi-user, time sharing mainframe systems are typically complex. Log-on procedures, file and database access can be complicated in these environments. On the other hand, stand alone microcomputer hardware and software is frequently less complicated and offers “user-friendly” interfaces. While the microcomputer user may be "self-trained", mainframe users need formal computer training. Some degree of computer access control and data security (through user IDs and passwords), and back up procedures are typically offered by large and centrally administered mainframes whereas stand-alone microcomputers are usually located in easily accessed areas with data and programs typically stored on single copy small portable diskettes that may be easily destroyed and misplaced. With the growing increase in utilization of microcomputers predicted by the MIS/DP managers in the survey, data security and back up considerations will become increasingly critical. Furthermore, the trend toward the increase in utilization of microcomputers by the end-users creates a concomitant need for developing policy and standards for hardware and software acquisition. The uncontrolled growth of microcomputers can lead to such difficulties as multiple versions of incompatible data and incompatible machines that can not easily be linked into networks.

## 9. Organizational Impact of EUC

MIS/DP managers felt that EUC will result in some changes in information systems as well as end-user departments. One DP executive commented, "IS groups are not going to disappear, but they are going to change. Applications will be developed more and more by end-users while the IS group specializes in DBMS, graphics, user interface utilities and networking." A number of MIS/DP managers predicted that a future impact of EUC will be the placement of more information systems professionals in end-user departments for providing local support of EUC activities.

According to the MIS/DP managers, EUC activities have proliferated in all the functional areas of the organizations. In addition to the traditionally DP oriented areas such as accounting and finance, the marketing, personnel and production areas seem to be well involved in EUC activities. EUC was most common in accounting and personnel departments. EUC activities were least prevalent by the top managers. It was reported that in 52% of the organizations the top management did not participate in any EUC activities. Top management occasionally engaged in end-user computing activities in only 44% of the organizations under investigation. MIS/DP managers predicted that EUC activities will significantly increase by the top management as well as in all the functional areas in their organizations. As far as the nature of end-user computing is concerned, the activities were concentrated in the areas of transaction processing (reported in 64% of the organizations), database retrieval and report generation (reported in 62% of the organizations) followed by word processing (reported in 59% of the sample).

## 10. Perceived Benefits and Risks of EUC

Perceived benefits of EUC activities included:

1. Faster response to user needs (reported by 78% of the managers)

2. Smaller application development backlog (reported by 59% of the manager)

3. More successful system implementation (reported by 54% of the managers).

4. Improvements in user work methods (reported by 52% of the managers)

When asked to identify the potential risks associated with EUC in organizations, three issues emerged. The primary issue of concern to MIS/DP managers was inefficient applications created by end-users (mentioned by 59% of the managers). Where corporate mainframe computers are used for support of EUC activities, inefficient and unplanned growth of end-user applications may overtax the computer resources. This may in turn create the need for considerable investments in expansion of the mainframe capacity. The second perceived difficulty associated with EUC, reported by 56% of the managers, was computer (hardware and software) incompatibility. Uncoordinated proliferation of end-user tools (particularly microcomputers) most likely leads to difficulties in integrating and establishing communication links among these tools. The third area of concern to MIS/DP managers involved threats to data integrity and security. It was felt that the tendency of end-users to eliminate formal validation techniques, extensive testing, documentation and audit trails increases the data integrity and security risks. Nevertheless, in general the managers were more enthusiastic about the potential benefits of EUC rather than concerned about the potential risks. One commented, "All of the problems are well worth having for the benefits." Still, while EUC activities may result in significant organizational benefits, they may also create significant problems. Specific mechanisms for controlling and reducing EUC risks are needed.

## 11. Implications of Findings for Effective Applications of EUC in Organizations

MIS/DP managers recognize that EUC requires a change in philosophy from the traditional approach to MIS/DP management and coordination. Furthermore, the expected growth and proliferation of EUC in organizations creates a concomitant need for a proactive management strategy. There are several aspects to EUC management including:

Hardware and software acquisition procedures. In order to control the unplanned and unjustified proliferation of EUC, specific policy statements addressing acquisition of EUC tools should be issued. Acquisition procedures should require appropriate justifications including some form of cost/benefit analysis. Also, developing a set of "standard" hardware/software products for the users to choose from facilitates support and maintenance of EUC applications in the organizations.

EUC applications selection, development and operations. Not all information systems applications are suitable for EUC implementations (e.g., large systems involving a large number of transactions). It is important to develop a process for the identification and selection of appropriate and “high payoff” EUC applications. Also, well-defined standards for development, testing and documentation of applications should be developed and enforced. These standards should be adopted by the users and replace the inadequate end-user application development approaches that may be followed.

Data integrity and security. Management should ensure that specific guidelines are developed and implemented in order to protect the integrity of corporate data resources. Management should also provide a framework for distinguishing between “private” and “shared” EUC data and applications. Standard methodologies and data definitions should be used in the “shared” EUC applications. Policy statements in this area should also address end-user access and update of the corporate databases.

End-User Support. EUC benefits can not be realized without active and on-going organizational support of end-user activities. Aspects of end-user support include guidance in selection of equipment and hardware, training and education, providing controlled access to corporate data and assisting end-users in problem solving and developing EUC application. Management should define the nature of user support and identify specific mechanisms and structures for its delivery. An effective EUC support strategy is the key to the successful application of EUC in organizations. Training programs are an important component of end-user support. A comprehensive EUC training program should address the needs of the management, MIS/DP professionals as well as the end-users.

Organizational commitment to the large-scale application of EUC entails a significant expenditure of resources. It is important to subject the investment of organizational resources in this area to the same evaluation and return on investment considerations as other organizational investments. In order to successfully evaluate the EUC expenditures, corporate management should be actively involved in creation of an evaluation framework. Creation of such a framework involves three key steps [3].

1. Defining EUC success (e.g., reduction and eventual elimination of outside timesharing services, enhancing user productivity, etc.)

2. Developing criteria, methods, and procedures for evaluation (e.g., monitoring EUC costs, periodic administration of user satisfaction questionnaires or interviews, etc.).

3. Implementation of the evaluation procedures (e.g., assigning organizational responsibility for EUC evaluation, periodic management review to ensure that EUC evaluation is performed, etc.). The EUC evaluation framework should be developed through a close collaboration between the corporate management and the management of the unit responsible for EUC administration (e.g., the information center) as well as the end-user management. In the absence of explicit procedures for its management and coordination, EUC may impact organizational stability instead of productivity.
