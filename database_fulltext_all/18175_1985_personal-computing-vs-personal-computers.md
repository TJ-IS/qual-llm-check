# Personal Computing vs. Personal Computers

John A. Lehman

Department of Management Sciences, School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

Most discussion of personal computers in organizations focuses on a peripheral issue: the choice between distributed computer hardware and central computer hardware – between personal computers and mainframes. The real issue has nothing to do with personal computers or other hardware; it is the difference between organizational computing and personal computing in organizational information systems. This paper discusses the differences between organizational computing and personal computing, the connections between the organizational and the personal computing systems within organizations, and the role of MIS.

Keywords: Personal computers, personal computing, microcomputers, Management of Information Systems, Informal Information Systems.

## 1. Introduction

Is the introduction of personal computers into organizations something to worry about? If so, who should be worrying? The issues are productivity and control. How can personal computer hardware make knowledge workers more efficient and effective? At the same time, how can personal computer users be made subject to information system controls? How can centralized information processing departments ensure that end users use the correct data, that the systems which they develop meet their needs, and that such development represents productive use of computing resources?

Most discussion of personal computers in organizations focuses on a peripheral issue: the choice between distributed computer hardware and central computer hardware – between personal computers and mainframes. Personal computers and personal computing as considered the same thing. The real issue has nothing to do with personal computers or other hardware; it is the difference between organizational computing and personal computing in organizational information systems.

Organizational computing is the use of computing resources to create information infrastructures and utilities for use by the organization as a whole. Personal computing is the use of computing resources by an individual to carry out his or her job. Two differences distinguish organizational computing from personal computing. The first is impact on others within the organization. Organizational computing has a direct impact; personal computing has an indirect impact. The second is method of control. Organizational computing is controlled through system integration; personal computing should be controlled through an organization's management control system.

The difference between personal computing and personal computers can be clarified by example. Table 1 illustrates some possible system types.

Traditional MIS Systems are automated versions of an organization's formal information system. Applications include payroll, accounting, and production. Such systems have been supported on centralized hardware since the 1950's

Small Business Computer Systems are no different from traditional MIS systems, except that the processing demands are lower. As a result, personal computing hardware is often cost effective. Applications are the same as those for traditional systems; the only difference is the choice of hardware.

Information Centers were originally developed to diminish applications backlogs. Organizations observed that many user requests needed at most to retrieve data from existing information systems, and that sophisticated users could obtain the information themselves with modern user-friendly tools. Information centers were thus established to provide the tools needed for users to solve their own problems – an approach which falls within the definition of personal computing even though it is done on centralized mainframes.

Personald DSS (Decision Support Systems) include applications like spreadsheet analysis and custom report generation. These are the same applications for which information systems were developed; the only difference is the choice of hardware.

In short, the type of computer hardware selected for a particular application should depend on what provides the most bang for the buck. The type of application should determine what sort of control is appropriate, and who should be responsible. Basically, MIS is responsible for organizational computing, and end users are responsible for personal computing.

The remainder of this paper discusses the differences between organizational computing and personal computing, the connections between the organizational and the personal computing systems within organizations, and the role of MIS.

## 2. Organizational Computing

## 2.1. What is Organizational Computing?

Organizational computing is the creation of information utilities or infrastructures to support the formal information system within an organization. Traditional system design techniques attempt to determine what computer systems will best serve the needs of the organization as a whole, and how these systems can be fitted together into an integrated organizational information system.

Give this organization-wide view of information systems, traditional system design concentrates on questions such as:

\- What are the organization's critical success factors? [1]

\- What information is required to meet these critical success factors?

\- How can existing systems best evolve and be integrated to provide that information?

\- What additional systems have high organizational payoff?

\- What is the most appropriate structure for the corporate database to support the above?

An example of part of an organizational information system is accounting system. Such a system is intended to provide information both for a large group of inside users (the management accounting system) and for outside users (investors and taxing authorities). Other examples of organizational information systems are payroll, order tracking systems, inventory systems, and scheduling systems for projects of interest to the organization as a whole.

## 2.2. Requirements for Organizational Computing

Organizational information systems must be usable by any authorized users who needs to use them. Hence the traditional emphasis on centralized systems. This is due in part to the increased efficiencies which arise from Grosch's law (larger computers provide disproportionately larger capabilities), but also because files and other resources must be accessible to many different users within an organization.

For example, everyone in an organization uses the payroll system. Almost every manager in an organization needs to use the accounting system, since it is the source of information used in budgeting. Inventory systems are of interest to production, marketing, and finance, while project scheduling systems for large projects may determine what most of the people within an organization do.

Because such information is used by a wide variety of users, and because the information system serves the organization as a whole (and can thus have widespread impact), it is essential that the quality and the integrity of the information be safeguarded. Investment in reliable hardware and software, and in security and control measures are justified on the basis of the impact which problems can have on the entire organization.

## 2.3. Control of Organizational Computing

Control of organizational computing divides into control over system planning and system operation. Nolan's stages hypothesis [2] (Table 2) emphasizes the importance of both sorts of control for mature information systems.

Control over planning ensures that the system evolves so as to provide the most benefit to the organization (given resource constraints). For example, planners may have to decide whether to implement an interactive order tracking system or an interactive inventory inquiry facility first.

Control over operations minimizes the chance that an individual user will damage parts of a system used by others within the organization. Control of operations is achieved by limiting access. This might include the use of accounts and passwords to restrict use of the system, limitations on access to files, the inability to write or replace files which one is allowed to read, limitations on who can change what program, and controls on terminal access. An example would be setting up procedures to ensure that unauthorized persons could not access an interactive inventory inquiry facility.

Centralized control of organizational computing systems appears to run counter to trends in the development of organizational structures over the last few decades, where decentralized control and decision making have become the norm $[3]$ . The philosophy behind decentralized control and decision making is that middle level managers are responsible for the results of their operations (whether profit centers, revenue centers, or cost centers), and that following them to achieve these results in whatever way they choose will produce both better decisions and higher motivation. However, evaluation of their performance is based on some portion of the formal information system within the organization, and so central control of this information system has always been regarded as an essential component of decentralized decision making.

## 3. Personal Computing

## 3.1. What is Personal Computing?

Personal computing is the use of computing resources by an individual to carry out his or her job. Traditional system design assumed that the information and processing resources required to do this were a subset of those required for the organizational information system. In this formal, hierarchical view of organizational information systems, each level of management works with a summary of the information produced by the next level down, and thus the information needs and the critical success factors for individual managers are subsets of those for the organization as a whole. In this view, for example, a shop foreman needs information on the production capacities of each machine in the shop, whereas the next level manager needs information only on the capacity of the shop as a whole.

The management control literature (e.g. [4,5]) suggests that this view is inaccurate. Critical success factors for organizational participants generally overlap, but may be in direct conflict [6]. This situation gives rise to the informal information system, or more properly, systems. If one assumes that a user will demand information which contributes to meeting his or her personal success factors, then the information available from traditional information systems may not be sufficient.

This often occurs because meeting a particular user's information requirements has a low priority from an organizational standpoint, especially when those needs do not mesh smoothly with existing systems.

The use of computers as individual managerial productivity tools requires a new set of questions.

\- What are a manager's critical success factors?

• What information is required to meet them?

\- How can that information best be provided unconstrained by the integrated organizational information system?

## 3.2. Requirements for Personal Computing

Personal computing systems must help the user perform his or her job better than would be the case without the computer resources.

In so doing, two forms of access to central data resources may be required. One subset of users requires almost no sharing of data with other members of the organization. Examples are engineers performing calculations or researchers searching external databases. Another class of users, such as users of financial spreadsheets and graphics packages, requires read-only access to organizational information.

Since use of such personal systems by the organizational public is not expected, either centralized or decentralized hardware facilities may be used. The original concept of the Information Center assumed that centralized facilities would be used to provide users with the tools needed to develop their own systems.

In recent years the choice has generally been decentralized, hence the popular identification of personal computing with personal computers. Part of the reason for this choice has been economic (Grosch's law apparently does not apply across different families of hardware), and part of it has been political (use of the centralized hardware implies submission to controls on the organizational computing system).

Because information is used primarily by a single user, problems with the quality and the integrity of the information impact that user rather than the organization as a whole. Heavy investment in reliable hardware and software, and in security and control measures which are justified for organizational computing are often not justified for personal computing. If they are, the approach to system control is often linked with management control; i.e. the user is responsible.

## 3.3. Control of Personal Computing

The issue of how to control personal computing appears to be at the root of most debates about the role of personal computers in organizations. One alternative is to control personal computing in the same way in which one controls organizational computing. Another alternative is to treat control of personal computing as a management control problem, and thus to treat personal computing like any other management activity.

Controlling personal computing in the same way in which one controls organizational computing has appeals in consistency, in MIS theory, and in terms of organizational politics. The consistency argument is that all computers should be treated in the same way. The theoretical argument is that personal computing is subject to the same stages as organizational computing, and by installing the controls which we now know to be necessary at the beginning, future problems can be forestalled. The organizational politics argument is that by charter, all information processing is supposed to be centralized, and what will the data processing department do if every unit in an organization decides to go its own way? A related argument is that the data processing department has responsibility for all information processing within the organization, and so ought also to have authority over it.

The consistency argument can also be turned the other way; why should computer use be treated differently than use of any other productive asset in a decentralized operation? Most organizations have found that decentralization of management decision making, given the proper control and motivation system, produces better results than centralized systems. The argument that a manager who can be relied on to make productive use of several million dollars worth of plant or equipment cannot be trusted to use the right data in the right way is not persuasive outside of data processing circles.

The response to the theoretical argument is that Nolan's stage hypothesis has never been empirically demonstrated, and that even if it were, it is based on observations of organizational computing, and the coordination problems which it addresses are not relevant for personal computing.

The main argument for reliance on existing management control systems for personal computing is consistency. Given that misuse of the systems will not have a major impact on other business units, the manager who is responsible for the results should have the authority over how to carry out the activity. Central control, in this view, should be asserted through the management control and measurement system, and through controls on connections to the centralized information system.

## 4. Connections between Organizational and Personal Computing

There are three possible forms of connection between personal computing systems and organizational computing systems (Figure 1). In the first, data from the organizational computing system are brought into the personal computing system, changed, and then sent back to the organizational computing system. In the second, data from the organizational computing system are brought into the personal computing system, and used for a manager's individual tasks. In the last, there is no connection between the two systems.

If data from the organizational computing system are brought into the personal computing system, changed, and then sent back to the organizational computing system, this is not personal computing. Although such systems may be based on personal computers, the results are part of the organizational computing system. Such systems should be subject to all of the normal controls on organizational information systems. This is the situation which MIS managers appear to have in mind when they worry about personal computers corrupting the organizational information system.

If data from the organizational computing system are brought into the personal computing system and used for a manager's individual tasks, the personal computing system is a direct consumer of information from the organizational computing system. In this capacity, it is like any other authorized user. From the point of view of the organizational information system, the only difference between an individual user and that user's personal computing system is the medium through which the data is delivered. Most micro-mainframe communications packages are intended to support this model.

Where the personal computing system uses data sources other than those provided by the organizational computing system, there may be two varieties. In the first, the tasks which the personal computing system is intended to carry out requires information external to the organization's formal information system. Such systems provide a degree of flexibility which overall organizational needs might not warrant. The other type of standalone system uses what is often referred to as a “vest-pocket database” – information which belongs in the organizational computing system but which is kept separate by the managers involved. This represents a conflict with the organizational information system which, from an MIS viewpoint, should be resolved in the latter's favor.

## 5. Role of MIS Organizations

Davis [7] has pointed out that there are three roles for a systems analyst in most projects:

\- A technical expert in systems design and development,

• An independent reviewer of requirements, and

\- An enforcer of acceptable standards and practices.

The same paper pointed out that most systems are neither purely end-user developed nor purely analyst developed, but somewhere in between. The distinction between organizational and personal computing does not necessarily impact the degree of involvement of analysts or other data processing specialists, whether central or local, in a personal computing project. What it does impact is the locus of responsibility.

It is not likely that an individual manager will have the technical expertise to select hardware and software and to implement complicated applications. The involvement of MIS (or other) expertise in these areas, as well as in other functions of the traditional analyst, is thus likely. The distinction to be made is that in a personal computing system, it is the owner of the system who is responsible for the results, not the MIS area. This responsibility cuts two ways.

The manager is most likely to see it as freeing him or her from controls and other restraints surrounding the organizational information system. Given that the personal computing system user is authorized access to required data from the central system, and given that no data goes back into the central system, the end user can do anything which time, manpower, and budget constraints allow.

The other side of this freedom (probably most obvious to those in data processing) is responsibility. If the equipment is not suitable for the task, the manager who owns the system is responsible, not the MIS department. If the system produces wrong results, the manager who owns the system is responsible, not the MIS department. If, due to carelessness about backup, a year's worth of data is lost and the department suffers severe financial problems as a result, the manager who owns the system is responsible, not the MIS department.

Needless to say, no one wants these unfortunate results to occur. But given that the organization is decentralized (at least in terms of responsibility and control systems), and given that the systems in question are personal and not organizational, the role of MIS is primarily educational. If high level management feels that the risk of a particular individual misusing a personal computing system is too high, the solution is the same as if they feel that the risk of that individual misusing financial or production resources is too high. It has nothing to do with computers.

## 6. Conclusion

Most discussion about the relative advantages and problems of centralized and personal computers confuse technology with use. The organizational computing system is intended to automate the formal information system. Few students of organizations have denied that informal information systems are used by individuals in organizations. Personal computing systems provide a way to automate these informal systems. Personal computing systems are used by individuals within organizations to accomplish tasks which these individuals see as contributing to their success.

Personal computing is not necessarily the use of personal computers, and not all uses of personal computers are personal computing. The distinction is important, because many managers, especially in data processing, worry about the impact of personal computers on the organizational information system. What they should worry about is preserving the distinctions (and the interfaces) between the organizational computing system, which serves the organization as a whole, and the various personal computing systems which individuals within the organization use to forward their own interests.

Support for personal computing, be it technical advice or review of requirements and proposed solutions, is a legitimate function of the MIS department (and subject to that department's perception of overall information system priorities). Control of development and use of personal computing should be a function of the organization's management control system. Responsibility for profitable use of such systems rests with the managers who develop and use them.

Much of the argument about the use of personal computers within organization arises from viewing personal computers and personal computing as the same thing. The uncontrolled use of personal computers for organizational computing is inappropriate. The application of system design controls designed for organizational computing systems to personal computing projects is, however, equally inappropriate.
