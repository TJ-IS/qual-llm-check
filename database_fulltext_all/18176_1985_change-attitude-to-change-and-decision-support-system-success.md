## 1. Introduction

A number of researchers have investigated the implementation of MIS as a change process $[3,4,29]$ . These studies usually adopt a normative change model such as the Lewin/Schein Model $[26]$ , or the Kolb/Frohman model $[14]$ , and examine user attitudes as they influence the change process and its outcomes. The results of these studies indicate that the development of a need for change, the existence of a contract for change, and the diagnosis and resolution of resistance to change are required for successful implementation $[12]$ . It is also evident from these results that the extent of changes brought by the MIS, and the attitudes of the users towards those changes, are important variables that influence the eventual success of MIS implementations.

Sid Huff is an Associate Professor and Computer Director at the School of Business Administration, University of Western Ontario. He holds an undergraduate degree in mathematics, an MSc in engineering, and an MBA from Queen's University (Kingston), and a PhD degree in Information Systems from the Sloan School of Management, MIT. He has worked as a research engineer with Ontario Hydro, and as a management consultant with Woods, Gordon & Co. His current research interests concern managing the information systems function, specifically the development of decision support systems, application development by end users, and the absorption of new information technology into the firm. He has published articles on these subjects in Information & Management, MIS Quarterly, INFOR, and other journals.

These results have special relevance for the implementation of decision support systems (DSS) since, as Ginzberg has pointed out, “... a much greater level of change is required for successful implementation of DSSs than is needed for other types of systems.” [5] The argument is that, among other things, a DSS will change a manager’s concept of the decision process thus requiring “... a change that goes beyond how things are done and extends to what things are done.” [12] We would thus expect change related variables to influence the implementation success of DSS: “This issue [the existence of a commitment to change] should be particularly important for decision support systems, which typically imply a greater degree of change than do other types of systems.” [6]

To date, however, there have been no studies linking the extent of DSS induced changes with DSS implementation success. As the DSS field still lacks standard measures of DSS success, this is not surprising. Despite some recent efforts in this area [25], we are still far from having a standard instrument.

The present study was conducted to develop empirical evidence concerning the association between user perceptions of, and attitudes toward, change and DSS success. Scales for six variables were utilized in a survey of DSS users in nine organizations. Four of the six measures attempt to tap various dimensions of DSS success, and have previously been used independently by other researchers. The present study, having used all four at the same time, also provides some insight into the relationships that exist between these four success measures.

## 2. DSS Success

Sprague and Carlson have categorized the different methods by which one can evaluate a DSS: event logging, attitude surveys, cognitive testing, rating and weighting, system measurement, system analysis, cost/benefit analysis, and value analysis [27]. It is possible, and perhaps preferable, to use more than one method in determining the success of a particular DSS in a particular organizational setting. In research studies that investigate numerous DSS in different organizations, however, the costs involved generally limit researchers to the use of surveys of users' attitudes and behaviours. Methods such as event logging or system monitoring have been used, but such uses have been few and restricted to studies of DSS in single organizations [16,17]. As a result, user surveys have been used most often for assessing the success of a system.

To measure system success, researchers have mainly relied on either of two proxies: user satisfaction, or system use. Measures of perceived user satisfaction with the system have been used in attitude surveys $[6,9,15]$ . Early research in this area suffered from the shortcomings associated with relying on single item measures, but recently several efforts have been made to develop multiple item measures of user satisfaction. These are reviewed by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi $[10]$ , who propose a modified version of an earlier instrument $[22]$ as a basis for a standard measure of MIS user satisfaction. More recently, Sanders has developed and tested a 20 item perceptual measure of DSS user satisfaction $[25]$ .

The extent to which users use a system has been adopted as another proxy for measuring system success $[2,15,18,24]$ . System use can be assessed via user surveys (perceived measures) or sometimes through system monitoring $[16]$ . The appropriateness of these two proxies for success depends on whether the DSS use is mandatory $[15,16,21,23]$ . System use is a more appropriate measure of system success in cases where the use of the system is voluntary $[16]$ . On the other hand, when system use is mandatory, user satisfaction is a more appropriate proxy. For most DSS, use is voluntary; users can choose to use the DSS or not for each decision cycle. It would therefore appear that, in measuring DSS success, we ought to employ system use as a proxy, since we can expect successful DSS to be used more often than unsuccessful ones. This is especially true for DSS that really fail, since such DSS fall into complete disuse. However, among all DSS that are used (i.e., are perceived to be more effective than manual methods), there still exists a large variation in quality. For these, a multiple item user satisfaction measure would provide valuable information over and above that provided by system use: it would allow us to compare DSS that are successful with those that are less successful but not total failures.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

Keen has argued that DSS change managers' concepts of the decision process (in addition to inducing procedural changes), and that whenever appropriate such changes should be used as additional dimensions in evaluating a DSS [12]. It is conceivable that part of a DSS's success depends on its capacity to bring those changes, because users want and expect the DSS to do so. At first, this seems to contradict the common wisdom in MIS and OR/MS implementation, which assumes the existence of user resistance to change [29]. However, when one considers that DSS users are, in many respects, quite different from the traditional users of MIS, it then might make sense to expect DSS that bring a great deal of change to be more successful than DSS that bring less change. This forms our first hypothesis. In null form, the hypothesis is:

H1: There is no relationship between the extent of changes caused by a DSS and its success.

A number of studies have previously shown that overcoming and reducing resistance to change is important in achieving eventual MIS and OR/MS implementation success $[3,5,9,29]$ . Furthermore, individuals differ in their attitudes towards changes in their work environment. Thus, an important question is: when a given DSS is implemented, do different users develop measurably different perceptions of the extent of changes that have occurred in their work environment? If so, then we might expect individuals with favorable attitudes towards change (i.e., low resistance to change) to perceive a DSS as causing a correspondingly smaller degree of change. Also, if the findings of MIS and OR/MS studies are also applicable to DSS, then we would expect to see an association between individual attitudes towards change and DSS success. To test these assumptions, we form two more hypotheses:

H2: There is no relationship between individual user attitudes towards work related changes and the perceived degree of success their DSS enjoys.

H3: There is no relationship between individual user attitudes towards work related changes and the extent of perceived DSS induced changes.

## 4. Methodology

Nine large organizations in the banking, insurance and utility industries were contacted and agreed to participate in the study. Following the initial contact, the DSS in each organization were identified through interviews with senior DP managers and user department managers. In order to qualify as a DSS, the system had to be a software application that had been developed with the specific purpose of supporting administrative decisions, and that enabled the evaluation of the consequences of alternative decisions (i.e., “what if” analyses).

Having selected the systems that fit the above criteria, the senior managers of user departments using these DSS were interviewed to identify the “decision maker/users”. Specifically, they were defined as the persons who decide how and for what purpose the DSS will be used $[1]$ . This way of defining the user of a DSS enables the separation of those who decide how to use a DSS from those who do not, and is similar to the definition suggested by Ginzberg $[7]$ . Subsequently, the decision maker/users were interviewed to detail the characteristics of the decision problem and the DSS they were using. Following the interview, they were given a questionnaire and asked to fill it out at their convenience.

## 4.1. Measures Used

The decision maker/users were asked to fill out a questionnaire for each DSS they were using. Each questionnaire measured user attitudes to change, extent of changes caused by the DSS, and DSS success, via six scales.

One of the scales used to measure DSS success was the short form of the User Information Satisfaction instrument [10]. This is a 26 item scale and is offered as an alternative to the long form of the instrument when questionnaire length is a serious constraint.

Since different decisions are made with differing frequencies, when measuring DSS use it is necessary to measure the relative frequency of use. An absolute measure would make comparisons across a variety of DSS meaningless. Thus, system use was measured by asking decision maker/users the question: "every time you have to make the decision(s) this DSS supports, what percentage of the time do you use the DSS?".

User's perceptions of information systems' success have been found to be strongly associated with the extent to which their initial expectations were realistic [6]. Hence, the extent to which users' expectations are met could be used as another proxy for DSS success. A scale was therefore developed to measure this proxy, and is given in Appendix A.

To provide a basis for comparison with other studies that have used single item measures of user satisfaction, a single semantic differential item asking the respondents to rate their overall satisfaction with the DSS was used as the fourth proxy for DSS success.

To measure the extent of changes brought about in the decision maker/user's work environment, the scale shown in Appendix B was developed and used. The items of this scale were derived from a literature review of the changes shown or believed to be important in a variety of implementation studies.

The measurement of decision maker/user attitudes towards work related changes was accomplished through an instrument consisting of a set of nine Likert-type items [28]. This instrument is thought to provide a reliable and concise measure of general attitude towards change, and has been used in a number of studies [8,13].

## 5. Results

Fifty-seven questionnaires were distributed to 52 decision maker/users in 9 organizations (some of the respondents were using more than one DSS, and filled out more than one questionnaire). Of these, 46 questionnaires were returned by 42 decision maker/users. The resulting sample included 32 DSS used by the respondents on a voluntary basis.

The respondents, i.e., the decision maker/users, occupied a variety of positions in their respective organizations. Using information obtained in the interviews, the 42 decision maker/users were divided into four categories in terms of their seniority in the organizational hierarchy. A reliability and validity analysis was carried out for the four multiple item measures: User Information Satisfaction, Realization of User Expectations, Attitudes Toward Change, and Extent of Changes Caused. ## 5.2. Hypothesis Tests

The correlations between Extent of Changes Caused and all four success measures are significant at a level better than 0.05. H1 can thus be rejected.

The variable Attitude to Change has a significant correlation with only one of the success measures: Realization of Expectations. Consequently, H2 can not be rejected.

The correlation coefficient between Attitude to Change and Extent of Changes Caused, while not significant, is in the direction hypothesized. However, H3 can not be rejected either.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. DSS Success

All six pairwise correlations between the four DSS success measures are moderate in strength and significant at a level better than 0.01. Since they all measure overlapping dimensions of DSS success, this is not surprising.

The correlation between User Information Satisfaction and System Use is significant but relatively small (0.394). These two measures, while related, are clearly not measuring the same thing. Furthermore, when the standard deviations of these two measures are compared, the User Information Satisfaction scores are seen to be more tightly clustered around their mean. Thus, the frequency with which a DSS is used is not very dependent on the satisfaction levels of the decision maker/users.

One interpretation of this finding is that user satisfaction is simply not an appropriate measure of DSS success. A less restrictive interpretation is that user satisfaction and system use are different but complimentary. These two measures provide different kinds of information about what makes a DSS successful.

For example, a particular DSS may be heavily used because it is the only alternative users may have to manual methods. System use by itself clearly would not be a very appropriate way to measure the success of this DSS. In contrast, if a DSS created for a potential user base of 20 people was only being used by three, but those three were very satisfied with it, then user satisfaction alone would not present an accurate index of success either. Combining system use and user satisfaction together into a broader measure would provide a more complete picture of DSS success. DSS research stands to gain considerably from the joint use of both measures in future studies.

Promising results were obtained for the Realization of User Expectations measure. The mean scores for the User Information Satisfaction and the single item Satisfaction measures are quite high. Judging from these high means one might conclude that users are, in general, finding DSS successful. However, since DSS that fail fall rapidly into disuse and are quickly forgotten, the present sample really contains DSS that range from very successful to “somewhat” unsuccessful. This largely explains the relatively high means. The mean for the Realization of Expectations scale, however, is not as high, and the scale also appears quite reliable. This scale is apparently measuring yet another facet of DSS success, one not tapped by User Information Satisfaction or System Use.

The User Information Satisfaction instrument, while potentially useful, requires some modifications if it is to be fully effective in DSS research. Reflecting recent trends towards end-user computing, most DSS surveyed in this study were developed in user departments, usually by staff analysts. Consequently, certain items in the User Information Satisfaction questionnaire, for instance those dealing with communications and relationships between users and system developers and EDP staff, were not relevant. These items in the questionnaire need to be eliminated or replaced with items suited to end-user development environments in order for the User Information Satisfaction instrument to be maximally useful in DSS research.

Finally, the results of the study reinforce again the point that using single item measures for multidimensional constructs such as user satisfaction is not very informative. A single item measure of a multidimensional construct is bound to be very “coarse” and cannot reflect all the variance inherent in the construct. Hence, the single item user satisfaction measure correlates very highly with all of the other measures, while the other three success measures do not correlate as highly among themselves. (Since System Use consists of a single dimension, its measurement via a single item does not suffer from the same shortcomings.)

6.2. Attitude to Change and Changes Caused by the DSS

All four correlations between the variable Extent of Changes Caused and the four DSS success measures were positive and significant. This supports the hypothesis that the decision maker/users perceive DSS which cause work environment changes to be more successful. In addition, DSS that result in changes to decision maker/users' work environments tend to be used more heavily. An important ingredient of a successful DSS is, then, the extent of changes it brings to the decision maker/users' work environment.

The second hypothesis is based on the assumption that the more favorable a decision maker/user's attitude to change, the more successful his or her DSS will be. This was not confirmed: only one of the four DSS success measures significantly correlated with the variable Attitude Toward Change, indicating that the more favorable towards change a person is, the less his or her expectations from the DSS were realized.

A possible explanation for this result might be that a person favorable to change may have greater prior expectations for his or her DSS; i.e., he or she might have a different frame of reference regarding workplace change. The negative correlation between the variables Attitude Toward Change and Extent of Changes Caused also lends additional support to this explanation. Thus, a DSS that causes a given amount of change would be realizing the expectations of an individual with favorable attitudes to change to a lesser extent; that person's expectations would be higher to begin with.

Our results also indicate that DSS implementations differ from OR/MS and from MIS implementations. Earlier research on OR/MS and MIS implementation has confirmed a relationship between the user's attitude to change and system success. However, in the present study, the correlations between Attitude Toward Change and three of the four success measures were insignificant. This, we believe, was because the 'going-in' mindset of the decision maker/users in a DSS implementation is different: workplace changes are anticipated, and expected. This different mindset stems from the way DSS are developed and used [11], and includes factors such as (1) the close relationship between the DSS developer and the decision maker/user that is required during much of the DSS's life cycle; (2) user initiation and control over most DSS projects; and (3) typically rapid completion of DSS projects. Hence, we can expect most DSS users' satisfaction with and use of the DSS not to be overly influenced by their attitudes to change, because they already expect the DSS to bring change.

Finally, it may even be that DSS decision maker/users who view change favorably may place overly high expectations on their DSS, and end up being disappointed. This observation is (weakly) supported by the negative correlations (one significant, two insignificant) between Attitude Toward Change and three of the four success measures. This observation also supports arguments made by Ginzberg [6], and points to the danger of having decision maker/users expect too much from their DSS.

## 7. Conclusions

The results obtained in this study provide empirical support for several notions regarding DSS implementations. One of these is that DSS bring change to the decision maker/users' work environments, and that decision maker/users expect the DSS to bring changes. In addition, DSS that cause changes to decision maker/users' work environments are perceived to be more successful and are used more heavily. In measuring the success of a DSS, both user information satisfaction and system use should be jointly utilized in order to provide a more complete measure. Single-item measures, especially the single-item measure "user satisfaction", should be avoided.
