# Managerial Considerations in Participative Design of MIS/DSS

Evan E. Anderson

School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Box 688, Richardson, TX 75080, USA

There is widespread support for the concept of participation in systems design and development, but inconsistent evidence as to its contribution to systems success. These results are entirely consistent with those of the behavioral sciences where participation has been studied under many different conditions. Managers of design processes need to adopt a more complex view of participation and evaluate its probable contributions and limitations in particular applications. Drawing upon research results from within and outside the field of information sciences, decision guidelines for managing the extent and character of participation have been proposed. These guidelines stress that consideration be given to various contextual aspects of the problem, technology, user(s), job tasks, and organizational design.

Keywords: Participation, User Involvement, Participative Systems Design, Managing Systems Design.

Evan E. Anderson is Professor of Managerial Economics and Management Information Systems in the School of Management at the University of Texas at Dallas. He received an M.B.A. from the University of Wisconsin (Madison) and a Ph.D. from Cornell University. He has held previous faculty positions at Tulane University and Vanderbilt University, and has been a visiting scholar at the University of Chicago and St. Antony's College, Oxford University, England. His papers have appeared in referred journals, such as: Journal of Business (University of Chicago), Operations Research, Omega, Management Science, Operational Research Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Accounting Review, Managerial and Decision Economics, and others.

## 1. Introduction

One of the earliest conclusions to emerge from MIS/DSS design and development experiences was that, apart from the technical capabilities of a system, user and organizational issues were very important in determining system success. In particular, it was argued that a failure to involve users in systems design would likely lead to poor acceptance and low usage of systems, and to wasted development resources $[1,10,20]$ . At a meeting of professional practitioners, for example, over fifty percent of those queried believed that user participation was the most important factor accounting for successful MIS implementation $[42]$ .

With the relatively high costs of involving users and questions about its value as a principle of design, researchers have devoted considerable attention to understanding its contribution to system success, e.g. [11,14,22,29,41,44,38]. Several studies have found positive relationships between various measures of satisfaction or acceptance, and user involvement [3,12,14,18,47]. Others have found weak to inconclusive support [11,25,41,44]. Summarizing the results of twenty-two studies, Ives and Olson (1981, p. 600) concluded that “eight claim to demonstrate a positive relationship between user involvement and various measures of system success, seven others present mixed results, and results from the final seven are negative or nonsignificant”. Hence, it appears that at best there is a neutral to slightly positive relationship between user participation in system design and system acceptance and/or satisfaction. However, there is little or no support for a positive relationship between user involvement and system usage [13,25,29,30]. Additionally, even in the presence of quite favorable experiences with participative design by first time users, there may be reservations about using it a second time [19].

Similar findings have been discovered by behavioral scientists studying the relationship between employee participation in decision-making, and attitudes and behaviors. It has been argued that participation facilities change in job design, working conditions, compensation, company policy, and organizational structure $[33,37]$ . For the individual it is thought to increase cooperation, motivation, satisfaction, productivity, and to facilitate change and communications $[6]$ . However, in a review of this rather voluminous literature covering laboratory studies, correlation field studies, multivariate experimental field studies, and univariate (controlled) experimental field studies, Locke and Schweiger (1979) concluded that participation in decision-making leaves attitudes unchanged or more positive, but most frequently has no effect on behaviors or results in as many negative behavioral changes as positive ones $[33]$ .

In light of these extensive corroborative findings in MIS and the behavioral sciences, it is useful to ask, “Are there any contexts defined by technology, employees, job responsibilities, and organizational characteristics that would seem to argue for user involvement in systems design and implementation?” It is clear that the seemingly established principle that user involvement is required, necessary, and critical to system success is at least exaggerated and at worst incorrect [21]. Nevertheless, there are environments and conditions under which it may have greater potential than others. User involvement in systems design should not be regarded as a given, but rather as a variable to be managed. This paper tries to gain insights into the complexity of user involvement from empirical research. From these insights, we have suggested managerial guidelines for determining the scope and extent of involvement, and have provided suggestions for the creative organization of participative processes in MIS.

## 2. Managerial Questions

1. Does the user(s) have enough knowledge and experience in the technology or its application to meaningfully contribute to system development?

The potential contribution of users is highest during the definition and implementation phases of system development. However, all users are not equally capable of providing meaningful contributions. The more users understand the technology, the tasks and decisions involved, and the social-political environment within which the system will be used, the more likely they can contribute to system development. The probability that users with little or no knowledge and experience can help to resolve uncertainties and facilitate choices between alternatives in systems design is low. Indeed, Zand and Sorenson (1975) found that amongst systems rated by designers as exceptionally unsuccessful, management could not state the problem clearly at the start of the project.

In addition to the substantive contributions of knowledge and experience, it provides a vocabulary and common understanding that facilitates communications between users, and analysts and users. Communications skills facilitate an exchange of ideas, reduce misunderstandings, and help to insure that user expectations of the system are reasonable [15]. Knowledge based communications make the process more productive and help to insure that time schedules can be maintained.

2. How structured are the applications of the system and to what extent do these applications involve numerous exceptions?

The purpose of information in management is to improve task and organizational performance by removing uncertainty. The complexity of systems design and, therefore, the need for user participation is influenced by the origins and degree of uncertainty and the extent to which decision-making requires an exploration of various environments and alternatives. Gorry and Scott-Morton (1971) presented a structure for information systems using two dimensions: the type of managerial activity (operational control, management control, and strategic planning) and the degree of structure in decision processes (structured, semi-structured, and unstructured) [2]. The nine combinations resulting from their intersections range from highly structured processes in the area of operational control to very unstructured decisions and analyses that occur within strategic planning. The former tends to be largely transactions processing and reporting, where the latter involves the specification of a complex environment and set of relationships that are capable of producing many different outcomes.

Whether one uses these dimensions as a point of departure or some others, user participation is more important and likely to lead to the greatest contributions when there are numerous states of nature and decision alternatives, and a complex set of relationships that determines results $[34,46]$ . Additionally, the more changeable the decision environment and the greater the number of exceptions to decision processes, the greater are the likely benefits from user involvement. Finally, complex decision environments tend to create a situation where user needs are more unique. This implies that systems must be designed to reflect the individual character of the decision maker's needs and/or pose enough flexibility to permit modifications.

## 3. How much job, interpersonal, and organizational change is introduced by the system?

Participation should not be viewed simply as an approach for arriving at technical specifications and capabilities [19]. Some information systems have the potential to dramatically affect the economic, social, and political structure of organizations. For individuals it may impact their job design, interpersonal relationships, task performance, and their power and influence within the organization [23]. Lawrence (1954) contends that it is not generally technical change that creates resistance, but rather social change, i.e. changes in human relationships. Newman (1984) conjectures that these changes are most traumatic for middle managers, as they may involve the loss of control and responsibility.

Information has value because of its ability to reduce uncertainty and, thereby increase organizational performance. Hence the person(s) possessing information derives power, prestige, and influence from it. Some part of their worth, actual and perceived, is derived from the information they possess.

Computer-based systems almost always redistribute information and, thereby, redistribute individual power within organizations. Markus (1983) notes that resistance is probably highest when a system changes an individual's ability to control access to information or to define what information will be kept and how it will be manipulated. Systems analyst and project managers need to have a good understanding of the social-political changes created by a new system, and should avoid using computer-based systems as the leading edge for radical organizational change.

The more profound and far reaching the expected job, interpersonal, and organizational changes carried by a new system, the greater the need for user involvement. However, user participation should not be regarded as a “cure all” for resistance to change $[26,27]$ . The involvement process may create, however, a more open environment for communications and a forum wherein doubts, concerns, and reservations can be voiced $[10,22,28,37]$ .

The success of participation as a means for reducing the trauma of change depends on many contextual factors including the user's prior experience with change in the organization [10]. If they are accustomed to fair, open dealings and usually are involved in important changes affecting them, they will expect to be involved in system development that carries important change and will tend to have a much more productive experience [26]. When there is a tradition of participation in important changes, there may be a greater sense of employee security and feeling of control. Hence, there is less fear and anxiety with what is new and uncertain [27].

4. Is (Are) the project leader(s) committed to and skilled in the application of employee participation, and does user involvement have the support and endorsement of management?

The potential of participation in systems design may be influenced by the extent and character of participation in other decisions that affect the user. The experience of employees with participation is generally established by the conduct and behaviors of upper level managers $[26]$ . In the area of systems design, Boland (1978) urges that project leaders give special attention to the process of design and that the designer and user view themselves as equal members of a problem solving team. The type of joint interaction prescribed by Boland (1978) is not likely to succeed unless top management supports and uses participation in other areas where important changes are planned. Productive participation in change and decision-making requires practice. The contribution of user involvement to system success is likely to be least in organizations where analyst and/or users have had little opportunity to develop skills in participative processes.

5. Does the user possess or are you willing to grant him/her substantial influence to determine directions and solve user-analyst conflicts?

Participation in decision-making, whether related to systems design and implementation or something else, may vary greatly in the extent to which participants can affect outcomes. Indeed, participative processes may be conceptualized according to the degree of influence and power sharing of participants $[4,48]$ . In the traditional approach to systems development, analyst regarded users as a source of experience and information that might be useful in defining the problem. Under this mode of development, the influence of the user tends to be low and derives from his skills to present or communicate needs and concerns $[17,5,9]$ . The user's power to resolve conflict or to influence design outcomes is nil except through persuasion.

There are many decisions made in systems design and implementation, and communications under the traditional mode of systems development may not provide for adequate user influence. Decision-making in design will necessarily produce some conflicts. Kaiser and Srinivasan (1982) have stressed the importance of uncovering the reasons for these differences and providing processes by which they can be resolved. It is difficult to meaningfully participate in conflict resolution without influence. After studying the experience of eight organizations, Robey and Farrow [44] found that participation does little to resolve conflicts that arise in the initiation, design, and implementation phases. They further concluded that participation is only effective if it allows users to exert influence toward both conflict generation and its constructive resolution. An indicator of influence and capacity to join conflict resolution is the extent to which the user(s) can initiate change in the design or operational state of the system. The power to initiate change can be enhanced by assigning someone from the user group to serve as project leader and/or by giving users signature authority on the completion of various development phases [41]. Influence involves power and represents a sharing of status, authority, and responsibility. If the influence and power of user(s) is not present by virtue of the knowledge and position of users in the organization, then managers may want to provide it.

The balance of power and influence can also impact communications per se. De Brabander and Thiers (1984), for example, contend that “asymmetry in sanctionary power” is a necessary condition for ineffective communications and failures in system development. If asymmetries are encountered, they argue that management can either redesign the project team and some of the organizational conditions in which the project is embedded or design an adequate form of third-party intervention. Additionally, in striving to provide for meaningful user influence and power within the design process, managers must avoid giving users false impressions as to the extent to which they can influence outcomes [32]. This is sure to create problems.

## 6. Are you willing to accept the time delays associated with a constructive level of user involvement?

Resources for systems development are scarce and costly. The development staff generally has a queue of projects requiring that analysts work within budget and time constraints that limits the number of project meetings and reduces the feasibility of “evolutionary” design processes $[5,39]$ . It is well documented that participation in decision-making requires more time $[7,31]$ . Specifically we may expect the time required by user involvement to increase with differences in the knowledge and experience of members of the project group, size of the group membership, and as the sanctionary power of group members becomes asymmetric. A group with large differentials in knowledge and experience will devote considerable time in trying to understand one another, that is, in defining, correcting, clarifying, and explaining their thoughts and needs. Similarly, as the sheer number of analysts and user increases, it becomes more difficult to schedule meetings, to coordinate their contributions and activities, and to resolve conflicts. Finally, the more asymmetric the sanctionary power, the greater will be the communications problems, and the more time that will be devoted to closing what De Brabander and Thiers (1984) referred to as the “semantic gap”. However, in the presence of a more balanced sharing of power, disagreements and conflicts may not be any more numerous, but they are more likely to surface and probably will require more time to resolve since each party is capable of negotiating from a position of strength.

7. Do(es) the user(s) want to be involved?

Even if one is convinced of the merits of user involvement and the answers to previous questions indicate that it would significantly contribute to system success, the reality is that many people may not want to participate $[24]$ . There are many different factors affecting the interest of users in participation. The more important information is in decision-making and the more potential for improvements in the quality of decisions through the use of the system, the more actively involved managers will want to be in system design. The potential payoff from participation is great enough to justify their time and energy. Gallagher (1974) found that managers in certain upper-level positions may value some information systems more than other managers, and concluded that there may be a relationship between value of information and managerial position.

Attitudes toward participation in systems design is also affected by how the user(s) is (are) treated in other regards where decisions are made that affect him/her [26]. Swanson (1974) has noted that an individual's conception of his organizational role may be important in determining his interest in MIS involvement. In particular, if he regards himself as a change agent, he may seek to be involved in systems design because of the role of management systems in organizational change.

## 3. Other Facilitators of System Satisfaction and Usage

Obvious as it may seem, efforts must be made to build a quality system that is capable of satisfying the operational requirements and needs of users. Managers are likely to become users when a system can enhance their job performance and/or productivity; vice versa, a poorly developed system will generally not be accepted and, therefore, represents wasted resources.

Apart from the technical functionality of the system it must be easy for users to understand and use $[28]$ . The user has time costs related to learning and using the system. The higher they are, the more difficult it will be to gain utilization $[30,45]$ . One approach to reducing these costs is through training.

The potential for system success is also enhanced when the information services function provides support for the system. Problems and limitations of the system will be discovered as managers use it. User commitment to the system will be strengthened when information services is capable of answering questions, identifying sources of difficulties, and carrying out timely modifications.

## 4. Conclusion

Considerable research has been devoted to studying the contribution of user involvement in system design to system success. It appears that user involvement is more likely to have a favorable effect on system satisfaction than usage, but in either case, the research findings are mixed. It is interesting and significant to note that these findings are entirely consistent with those of behavioral scientists studying the effects of employee participation in decision-making.

These findings suggest that user involvement is a complex variable with many different dimensions and should not be automatically invoked by axiom. This author has argued that user involvement should be regarded as a decision variable and, therefore, should be managed. Guidelines for managing user involvement activities have been suggested that include the possibility of no participation. These guidelines stress that consideration be given to various contextual aspects of the problem, technology, user(s), job responsibilities, and organizational design.
