# Power: A Critical Systems Development Factor

Neil E. Swanson

Department of Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, Missouri 65804, USA

Information system development methodologies for complex management support systems require the information analyst to utilize concepts and techniques which are frequently viewed as being too intangible and abstract. The analyst's knowledge and techniques must accommodate the increased variations and complexities which commonly exist for management support systems at all levels of management.

In this article, power is examined as an information systems development concept and as a basis for a technique which will enable the information analyst to identify the information requirements for management support systems more effectively.

Keywords: Systems analysis, Management Information Systems, Systems design, Power, Information Analyst

## 1. Introduction

Power is a critical variable of the information systems development process for those more sophisticated and flexible management support systems which are necessary at the management control and strategic planning levels. It has been some time since Gorry and Scott-Morton [16] and Churchill et al. [7] identified a trend toward the design and development of more decision-oriented information systems which affect multiple functions and departments of organizations. To cope with the greater variety of such information requirements, the information analyst's knowledge base, approach, and techniques must be more extensive and diverse. "Clearly, new approaches are needed to improve the design of all systems and to meet the challenges of designing more sophisticated applications than currently exist in most organizations" [24].

The purpose of this article is: (1) to describe the concept of power, (2) to identify the potential implications and usefulness of power in the information system development process, and (3) to identify a basis of a methodology which will enable the information analyst to develop more effective management support systems.

## 2. The Concept of Power

Power is a complex, intangible variable that dynamically changes and exists in the interactions and interrelations of a system's components. Power is extremely subtle and obscure, has multiple causes and effects, and is difficult to quantify; furthermore, power is widely defined as a relation [10,12,30,39].

Adolf Berle [4] describes five natural laws of power which the information analyst should understand. First, “power is invariably personal.” Information systems become increasingly personal as the information analyst progresses from operational to strategic planning and control information systems. Second, “power invariably fills any vacuum in human organizations.” Whether the organizational chart is representative or not, power structure and power relations exist to reduce risk and uncertainty [39]. Third, “power is invariably based on a system of ideas or philosophy.” The information analyst should be aware of the user’s values as well as overall organizational values and the “fit” between organizational and user values [29]. Fourth, “power is exercised through and depends upon institutions,” and fifth, “power is invariably confronted with, and acts in the presence of a field of responsibility.” The organization’s structure and processes place individuals in a position of legitimate power.

Emerson's investigation of power [12] centered on the concept of dependence; he stated that, "power resides implicitly in the other's dependency." Other researchers have found that a success is derived through dependencies [19,34]. Emerson continues by defining power in the context of a relation: "the power of actor A over actor B is the amount of resistance on the part of B which can be potentially overcome by A." Dugger [11] suggests that the meaning of power is that "power refers to the ability to tell other people what to do with some degree of certainty that they will do it."

The kinds of power which may generate dependencies are numerous, and there are several commonly used classification schemes. Social scientists often identify individual power in terms of five “bases of social power: reward, coercive, legitimate, referent, and expert power” [15]. Hicks and Gullet [18] identify the kinds of power in organizations as physical, economic, knowledge, performance, personality, positional, and ideology. Ogle et al. [30] present the bases of group power as depending upon four major variables: size, cohesion/unit, group/organization, and leadership.

Kemelgor [23] identified three different levels of power – societal, organizational, and individual. He suggests that simple identification of power is not adequate, and that power and power process linkages must be identified; these linkages will identify sources of power. Dugger also notes that “institutional roles, then, and the superstructures supporting them are the ultimate sources of power." These thoughts appear to be an outgrowth of Berle's five natural laws. Furthermore, Mintzberg [25] identifies a "superstructure" in the form of power coalitions and configurations, and Saunders (35) identified a theoretical framework that relates the use of management information systems to determinants of departmental power.

The power process is a dynamic process that balances the power-dependence relation. Cyert and March [9] formulated the “dominant coalition” concept; it recognizes coalitions as a major source of structural variation in organizations. This is commonly recognized as a balancing act in the power process. Chandler [6], in Strategy and Structure, develops the concept of strategy and indicates that decision choice power by a dominant coalition is a major source of organizational variation. Jacobs [21] suggests that external coalitions have power to the extent that they have concentrated and that the organization is dependent on them.

Organizations are control systems in which power, power relations, and a balance of power are maintained in a state of dynamic equilibrium. Crozier [8] explained power maintenance (power relation equilibrium) as the process of controlling uncertainty. Perrow [31] amplifies and notes that it is not the uncertainty, but what is done about uncertainty that confers power; he then proceeds to identify four contingencies – uncertainty, coping, centrality, and substitutability – which differentiate power.

The importance of the concept of power and power relations is widely recognized by many disciplines $[1,5,10,12,14]$ ; its significance in organizational literature has intensified over the last decade $[17,19,22,28,35,36,40]$ . Its importance relative to information systems has been emphasized $[24,35,39]$ . Lucas $[24]$ stated that “the real problem resulting from the design of information systems at the organizational level is associated with an intangible variable, power.”

## 3. Implications and Usefulness of Power

The information analyst's view of power should be formulated from an interorganizational, intraorganizational, and interpersonal perspective – similar to the way Hickson et al. [19], Mechanic [25], and Emerson [12] have treated power.

Conceptually, the discussion relates to the power-dependency relation and the information necessary to support and maintain. This implies that an information-dependence relation exists for the user; however, the identification of power-dependence relations would naturally precede definition of the consequent information dependencies. The usefulness of the concept of power in the systems development process thus relates to the identification of the information necessary to support, control, and maintain the user power-dependence relation. Tricker and Boland [39] emphasize the need to examine where each decision maker derives his power and how he is able to take action.

The information analyst must therefore recognize that the information systems development process within an organization may be accompanied by a dynamic shift in power and power-dependence relations; power transformations may become an organizational problem. Power is present in all organizations, and it dynamically shifts in terms of distribution, expression, and possession. Subunits rarely have equal amounts of power, and a new computerized information system potentially alters power relations and the balance of power.

The process of understanding power and power relations provides the information analyst with sources and information that are critical to maintaining or transforming the balance of power. It is speculated that information is gathered from a power-dependency perspective, regardless of the costs, because of: (1) the need to maintain a perceived level of certainty, and (2) the need to balance or maintain the power-dependence equation. This speculation is supported by a study by Hickson et al. [19], in which they conclude that the ability to cope with certainty, not simply the existence of uncertainty, is what gives power.

Since power is invariably personal, is the power concept equally valuable for all information systems development projects? For information systems supporting managers who function primarily in semi-structured and unstructured decision-making environments, these systems are highly personalized. The development process is more complex and the information support requirements, for similar decision making activities, frequently vary from manager to manager. The inclusion of the power concept is important to the definition of the information requirements for these managers. However, for many routine, structured decision-making environments the information systems development process should seek to minimize the need for change when managerial changes occur. Hence, for information systems supporting these types of decision making environments the argument for including power as a critical systems development variable is perhaps less viable.

## 4. Basis for the Development of an Approach

The primary basis of an approach must incorporate the power-dependence concept in the information systems analysis process. The dependencies reflect important information needs and requirements. These information requirements are necessary to maintain the state of power equilibrium.

The basis includes two phases: user linkage analysis to identify power-dependence relations, and power-dependence relation analysis to identify the related information needs and requirements.

## 4.1. User Linkage Analysis

This phase requires the information analyst to identify and describe the internal/external systems which interact and interrelate with the user. Interorganizational power can best be understood by examining linkages between organizations and their larger environments [3]. This phase should identify the existing user system; the user system linkages; the user's system components – procedures, humans – including boundary spanners, and hardware; and the user's horizontal and vertical system interactions and interrelationships.

It should logically follow that intraorganizational power can best be understood by examining linkages between functional departments and strategic business units, or their larger environments.

## 4.2. Power-Dependence Relation Analysis

Power-dependence relation analysis is necessary to identify the user's information needs and requirements in a more effective manner. The process is an “interaction process that should be identified and studied to locate the factors leading to perceived power and dependencies in self and others, and the conditions under which power will be employed" [12]. Emerson also emphasizes that the study of power networks would lead to an adequate understanding of the power structures that exist. Furthermore, Ogle et al. [30] emphasize the need to understand power relations, because it is directly related to the psychological needs.

Several techniques have been described; these may assist the information analyst in performing power-dependence relation analysis. First, influence diagrams may be used to help understand the political processes within the organization [33]. Second, graph theory is a technique that has been used to examine complex organizational networks and to predict the outcome of power. Efforts to apply graph theory to power have been made by French [14]. Third, functional analysis is a method widely used and it identifies functional requisites [30]. These, at the system level, are general conditions that must prevail, including general functions which must be performed if the power-dependence relation balance is to be sustained, or if the user's system life is to be sustained. There must be a reliable and adequate system for the regular provision of information; if not, the system's life may be shortened or the nature of the system's power-dependence structure modified, hence, the information needs will be modified.

## 5. Conclusions

Power is widely recognized as an important concept and variable in many disciplines. This article has identified a rationale for understanding the concept of power and a basis which the information analyst may utilize in the development of management support systems.

Simple direct attempts to solve the more complex systems analysis and design problems have not always been successful for managerial information systems. Through recognizing and better understanding power, information analysts will be less guilty of ignoring the system's environment; the inclusion of power should help maintain a holistic view of the users' information needs. If the information analyst is to ask the right questions, utilize the most appropriate techniques, and generate the best information necessary for effective systems analysis and design, it is necessary to know the total relevant system – power is part of that total relevant system. Even though the information analyst cannot be certain that the right information has been identified, the power related concepts and techniques should provide a more complete and accurate analysis of individual, group, and system information requirements.

It seems evident that a knowledge of power is essential to the information analyst.
