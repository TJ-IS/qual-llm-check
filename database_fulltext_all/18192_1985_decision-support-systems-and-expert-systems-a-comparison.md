# Decision Support Systems and Expert Systems: A Comparison

F. Nelson Ford

Department of Management, 322 Thach Hall, Auburn University, AL 36849, U.S.A.

In recent years there have been rapid developments in two technologies aimed at improving decision-making: decision support systems (DSS) and expert systems (ES). A DSS is an interactive system that helps decision-makers utilize data and models to solve unstructured or semi-structured problems. An ES is a problem-solving computer program that achieves good performance in a specialized problem domain that is considered difficult and requires specialized knowledge and skill. While both types seek to improve the quality of the decision, there are some distinct differences between the two. To understand and distinguish them, DSS and ES are compared in four primary areas: 1) objectives and intents, 2) operational differences, 3) users, and 4) development methodology.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Expert Systems, Decision Making Systems, Knowledge Based Systems.

In recent years the number of applications of decision support systems (DSS) has been noticeably increasing. This trend has been stimulated by the use of personal computers, advances in data base management systems, incorporation of “user-friendly” software interface techniques, etc. Many users have found that “DSS” is a useful technology that can improve their decision-making. Recent advances have also occurred in the area of artificial intelligence, where expert systems (ES) are being developed and, to a lesser extent, used in decision-making. Applications of both DSS and ES in decision making in the business arena is expected to increase significantly; but while both are intended as approaches to improve decision-making, it is also true that the two have clear and significant differences. Here, we explore the similarities and differences between DSS and ES.

## 2. Decision Support Systems

The concepts of DSS began to evolve in the 1960s and early 1970s. In 1971 M.S. Scott Morton described a DSS used to coordinate production planning for laundry equipment [13]. That study focused on those aspects of the decision process changed and improved by the system [12]. During the mid 1970s the concepts were further developed, but only by a few academics and practitioners [3]. The late 1970s and early 1980s saw a rapid increase in the development and application of DSS, as evidence by surveys of and reports on new applications.

## 2.1. Definition of DSS

A DSS helps decision-makers utilize data and models to solve unstructured or semi-structured problems. It incorporates features found in the area of management information systems and of management science and operations research. DSS do not provide answers to structured problems; rather, they emphasize direct support for decision-makers in order to enhance the professional judgments required in their decision-making. To be effective, they require a symbiosis between the users and the system. As a result, there is an interplay between user and computer that produces a total effort greater than that attained by the user and the computer operating independently; this provides synergistic decision-making [see 14 & 15].

## 2.2. Characteristics of DSS

A good way to gain a better understanding of decision support systems is to consider the observed characteristics of existing systems. Primary among these are the following four characteristics $[13,14]$ :

1. They tend to be directed at the less well structured, under specified problems that typically face upper-level managers. Appropriate DSS can be used by managers at various levels of management – however, lower levels of management normally deal with well structured problems for which known solutions exist – hence a DSS would be less appropriate.

2. They attempt to integrate the use of management science methods with traditional data processing functions. Certainly, an important function of DSS is to create an environment in which a decision-maker can, with relative ease, apply quantitative analysis techniques that would otherwise be too awkward and time-consuming to warrant their use.

3. They focus on features that make them easy and convenient to use interactively by noncomputer people. This characteristic reduces the intimidation often felt by managers unaccustomed to computer use. It also ensures timely delivery of information to the decision-maker.

4. They emphasize flexibility and adaptability to accommodate changes in decision-making. This is particularly important for the continued use and effectiveness of a DSS. Four levels of flexibility can be identified:

1) Giving the user the ability to approach a problem analysis in a flexible, personal way.

## 2.3. Structure of DSS

The structure of a DSS can be described by identifying the major subsystems or components of the system. One such framework splits the DSS into three basic subsystems: dialog, data, and models, as illustrated in Figure 1. The importance of the dialog subsystem cannot be over-emphasized, since all the capabilities of the DSS must be articulated and implemented through it. The data subsystem performs all data-related tasks; i.e., it maintains, stores and retrieves data from the data base, extracts data from various sources, etc. The models subsystem contains the library of models and routines to maintain and manage them. These are interfaced with the dialog subsystem so that the user has control over the operation, manipulation, and use of the models, and with the data subsystem so that appropriate model data can be provided [14].

## 3. Expert systems

Expert systems are an outgrowth of artificial intelligence (AI). Until recently, AI research was restricted to laboratories. Now the fruits of that research, in the form of practical applications, are appearing.

In general, current AI work can be classified into the somewhat overlapping areas of problem solving, pattern recognition, and comprehension; one result of work in the first of these is expert systems [5].

## 3.1. Definition of ES

An expert system is a problem-solving program that achieves good performance in a specialized problem domain that generally requires specialized knowledge and skill. The systems process the knowledge of experts and attempt to mimic their thinking, skill, and intuition.

## 3.2. Characteristics of ES

Because fewer ES have been developed, the identification of their general characteristics is more difficult. We divide them into two categories: those that apply to most expert systems and those that might be “desirable” but now apply to only a minority of ES. Characteristics of most expert systems include [see 8,10,11]:

1. The areas of knowledge have four prerequisite qualities: their domain of application is well-defined and sufficiently narrowly bounded so that the successful coding of relevant knowledge is likely; there is at least one human expert who is known to perform the task well and is available to help develop the knowledge base (special knowledge, judgment, and experience); and the expert is able to articulate this knowledge base and explain the methods of applying it.

2. ES employ heuristic problem solving; i.e., the results or decisions have been determined by rule of thumb or intuition instead of optimization and formal reasoning. The tasks have no algorithmic solutions and inferences must often be made based on incomplete or uncertain information.

3. ES contain and use three different general kinds of information: task-specific (that data relevant only to current ES analysis), domain-specific (the knowledge base, including problem-solving rules and data relevant to the field), and control (the inference engine, which applies the axiomatic knowledge in the knowledge base to the task specific data to arrive at possible solutions).

The “desirable” characteristics that describe some few expert systems include:

1. An explanation capability that allows the user to challenge the system's conclusions and examine the underlying lines of reasoning. For users to feel confident in a solution and therefore for the ES to gain wide acceptance and use, this is essential.

2. A clear separation of the knowledge base, the task-specific data, and the inference engine within the system. This makes updating and expanding the system relatively easy, especially when compared to conventional computer programs.

3. The ability to interface with the ES in “natural language”. This facilitates use of the system during development and application.

Several of these characteristics are closely related to the structure commonly used to construct an expert system.

## 4. Comparison of DSS and ES

A comparison of DSS and ES helps in understanding and distinguishing these two types of systems.

## 4.1. Objectives and Intents

The fundamental goal of DSS and ES is basically the same; they seek to improve the quality of the decision. However, their underlying philosophies and objectives are quite different.

The objective of a DSS is to support the user in making a decision by providing quick and easy access to data and models relevant and applicable to that decision. The data and models are at the user's disposal, to be used as desired.

The objective of an ES, on the other hand, is to provide to the user a conclusion or decision that is correct all the time. Since this is not possible, an ES performance level close to, at, or better than, that of the expert is normally satisfactory. The ES, then, provides conclusions or decisions to the non-expert user that are more likely to be correct than that user could provide.

## 4.2. Operational Differences

DSS allow the user to confront a problem in a flexible, personal way by providing the ability to manipulate the data and models in a variety of ways while progressing through the decision making process. The user, through the dialog system, chooses relevant data and models and directs their use to explore and analyze the problem and possible solutions.

With ES, however, the user retains little if any flexibility in the way that the system analyzes the problem. Rather, appropriate segments of the knowledge base are executed, as directed by the inference engine, based upon the control procedures of the inference engine, the domain-specific heuristics and data in the knowledge base, and the data describing the current problem under investigation. Information is related to general rules and lines of reasoning that enable the system to choose what additional information is needed to arrive at conclusions and recommendations. While the user of a DSS directs and controls its various components, the user of an ES is directed by the system.

Another distinct difference is the type of programming language used to construct the systems. For DSS, the core language used is most frequently a traditional high-level language, such as BASIC or FORTRAN, or a nonprocedural modeling language system developed primarily for DSS construction. The nature of the models, typically mathematical algorithms, used in DSS accounts for this approach. ES, on the other hand, are typically constructed using LISP and other languages that are more effective in representing and processing the symbolic type of information needed for expert system development and application.

A related capability of some ES is their ability to expand and refine their knowledge bases automatically by recording the value of various lines of reasoning and conclusions. That knowledge is then built into the knowledge base so that the system “learns” through time and experience $[1]$ . Comparable capabilities are not incorporated into decision support systems.

## 4.3. Comparison of Users

A third significant difference is in the typical users of the two. DSS are primarily applied in a business or organizational arena. They are aimed at the decision-making effectiveness of individuals within a business organization. Although DSS users are not a homogeneous group, they have at least two general characteristics: (1) they are mostly middle and upper-level business management; and (2) the user of a particular DSS is often the decision-maker who helped design that system. Indeed, a DSS is designed for a particular user group.

In contrast, expert systems are typically associated with research, as opposed to business, activities: (1) the users are principally scientists or researchers in a narrow field of study; and (2) expert systems are mainly built to “clone” the knowledge and experience of particular highly regarded experts, so that anyone in that field can use the system to draw on that knowledge. Most of those who use an ES, therefore, had no effect on its development.

A few examples serve to further compare users; first for DSS:

(1) GADS – The Geodata Analysis and Display System was developed in the early 1970s by IBM. It is used by police departments to analyze data on "calls for service". Based on the analysis, it helps design a more efficient allocation of police to their beats. Its use has expanded to other situations [14]. (2) PMS – Portfolio Management System is used by investment managers in bank trust departments. It facilitates portfolio management by helping managers perceive the portfolios as a whole, while allowing portfolio comparison with useful standards [12].

(3) CIS - Capacity Information System is a graphics-based DSS used in a truck manufacturing company by product planners. It is built to support complex planning tasks, such as capital investment for a given sales forecast and product plan [12].

(4) Ski resort design - A DSS was developed to help contractors design the layout of a ski resort on a mountain site. It used data on the expected percentage of skiers at various skill levels in order to help design appropriate trails to accommodate this mix of skiers [9].

Some examples of ES are:

(1) Internist – This system, designed at the University of Pittsburgh in Pennsylvania, was written for and marketed to doctors of internal medicine. It can analyze 3,000 symptoms and diagnose about 500 different diseases.

(2) Puff - A system developed at Stanford University can diagnose lung diseases; it was written for use by doctors interested in purchasing it as a software package.

(3) Prospector - This system, designed by SRI International, aids geologists in their search for minerals. The program enables the computer to compare the characteristics of the geographic area of interest with models and data about different mineral deposits. The system then states the probability of finding particular minerals in the area.

(4) Molgen - A system developed at Stanford to help molecular geneticists organize DNA experiments [6].

It is generally believed that businesses will soon reap the benefits of new expert systems.

## 4.4. Development Methodology

Both DSS and ES are generally designed using an iterative or prototyping approach. In this, basic information and operating requirements are identified, while a working prototype is developed. This performs only the most important, identified functions using a small representative data or knowledge base. Then a demonstration of the prototype allows identification of enhancements, expansions, and other changes needed or desired, and a new version is developed incorporating appropriate changes. The process is then repeated until desired performance levels are achieved $[16]$ .

The choice of this approach for DSS is based on three underlying philosophies. First, the approach allows a high degree of user involvement needed to insure that the DSS is appropriate and effective. Secondly, it allows the system to evolve and adapt to the needs of the user, providing flexibility. Finally, it often provides usable decision support in the early stages (i.e., before the complete system is evolved).

For ES a prototyping approach fulfills other requirements. Primarily, it allows the knowledge base to be refined and expanded so that the ES can progress toward higher levels of performance. This is critical, since researchers have found that the performance level of an ES is primarily a function of the completeness and quality of the facts and heuristics in the knowledge base, rather than the sophistication of the reasoning techniques $[8,11]$ . The typical development technique includes construction of a prototype with a knowledge base that handles only simple cases and then iterating until the knowledge base is expanded and tested for more complex cases and the system proves itself to be acceptable. This usually takes much longer than for a DSS.

To facilitate the development process for both, system development tools and system generators are sometimes used. These provide a set of capabilities appropriate for building a system more quickly and easily. At present, they are more fully-developed and frequently used with DSS than with ES [14].

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

In summary, decision support systems and expert systems have been compared in four ways: (1) objectives and intents, (2) operational differences, (3) users of the systems, and (4) development methodology.

The objective of a DSS is to support the user in the decision making process by providing access to data and models. The objective of an ES is to provide the user with a conclusion or decision significantly better, or more often correct, than the user could reach. A DSS allows the user to confront a problem in a flexible, personal way in manipulating the data and models. With an ES, the user has little or no flexibility.

For DSS, the computer language used is most often a traditional high-level language such as BASIC or FORTRAN, or a non-procedural development language. ES commonly use an artificial intelligence language, such as LISP. Users of DSS are mostly upper and middle managers who helped design the system. ES users, on the other hand, are typically scientists or researchers who did not develop the system. The development approach commonly used for both DSS and ES is an iterative design or prototyping approach, although the development of ES is more time consuming.

Some general observations can be made:

(1) Some ES concepts may be transferable to the area of DSS, which may soon include inference capabilities as well as heuristic decision structures and models. Frameworks for such capabilities are already being developed [see 4.7].

(2) There may be many business areas appropriate for ES application. As business becomes more aware of its potential their development and application will increase.

(3) The application of both DSS and ES is likely to increase, especially as the tools for their development and generation become more sophisticated.

Decision support systems and expert systems will have an increasing impact on the world of decision analysis and decision making.
