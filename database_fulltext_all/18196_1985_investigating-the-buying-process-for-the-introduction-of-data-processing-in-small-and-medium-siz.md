# Investigating the Buying Process for the Introduction of Data Processing in Small and Medium-sized Firms

Joachim Griese und Ralf Kurpicz \*

Institut für Wirtschaftsinformatik, Universität Bern, CH-3012
Bern, Switzerland

Analysing the buying process and the post-purchase experiences may be of interest to both the supplier and potential buyers. The buying behaviour during the introduction of data processing and computer-assisted information processing in 69 small and medium-sized firms were investigated using a standardized questionnaire. For all firms, the decision to buy involved a decision to obtain a system, with unity of hardware, system software, and application software – thus software support had the major influence on the decision. The assumption that in producer markets buyers decide more rationally and with greater market transparency only holds true for so called “intensive decision-makers”. This subset is the result of a cluster analysis that groups the firms according to their decision-making behaviour. A comparison of the “intensive decision-makers” with all others on their post-purchase development of computer-assisted information processing presents the following finding: Over an equal time span, the “intensive decision-makers” achieved a higher level of information processing (as measured by the number of DP-applications installed, the possibility of on-line operation of the firms’ departments, and the creation of integrated solutions) than the rest of the sample.

Keywords: Empirical investigation, Buying process, Decision process, Small and medium-sized firms, Software selection, Hardware selection, Introduction of DP, System selling, Cluster analysis, Information activity and decision effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

The essence of decision-oriented marketing theory lies in the decision to build up a marketing-mix to meet corporate goals. The main problem of finding an efficient combination of marketing methods arises from the uncertainty about individual and combined effects [14]. One important factor of uncertainty is buyer behaviour. Decisions about choice and exact form of marketing methods depend on buying habits of potential clients [12] – in other words, the seller has to know a great deal about the decision-making process of the potential buyer before it is possible to influence the decision [13].

## 2. Data Collection

In autumn 1982 we questioned 69 small and medium-sized firms. Using a standardized questionnaire, we investigated the buying process during the introduction of data processing and the current state of information processing in these firms. The questionnaire was adapted from a model consisting of a number of hypotheses; only those concerning the decision-making process are discussed in this article. For the hypotheses concerning the intensity of use of the accounting applications see [1].

With the kind support of Kienzle Apparate GmbH, Nixdorf AG, and Philips Data Systems GmbH, we obtained the addresses of 91 firms, all using data processing in accounting and most located in the Ruhr. In October 1982 these firms were asked to take part in an interview. The interviews were held in November and December, 1982.

Because this sample was limited by time and other resources, the results cannot be strictly regarded as statistically representative. However, we believe that the size of the sample allows us to draw a good picture of the situation in small and medium-sized firms; this will then provide an example of a typical firm's solution.

## 5.2. Grouping Firms by Decision-Making Behaviour Using Cluster Analysis

Frequency distributions for certain aspects of the decision-making behaviour process presented so far, illustrated the range of decision-making intensity measured by number of offers, duration of the process and differences perceived among offers. To support target-group oriented marketing, we then had to define what patterns of behaviour during the buying process played a role within the overall intensity range of each component.

This necessitated grouping the respondents 3) by cluster analysis [16]. Decision-making behaviour was broken down into the following components:

\- specification of requirements (none - oral - written);

\- number of information sources used;

\- number of offers requested;

\- perception of differences (yes - no - of no interest); with regard to: software, hardware, price, maintenance, installation figures.

\- dominant aspects of the decision (number of software and hardware responses);

– duration of the decision-making process.

5.2.1. Cluster 1 (19 firms) - “Intensive decision-makers”

The 19 firms of cluster 1 are clearly distinguished from the others by more intensive decision-making (measured by the indicators explained above). Requirements for the system to be acquired were always specified and in most cases written down. In the search phase, an average of three different information sources were used (average of all firms: 2.2 sources). Not only the number of sources, but also their type was of interest; the frequency of manufacturers' publications and trade fairs matches the overall average. The frequency in using outside consultants, industry information and reference systems as information sources was above average. Out of the total number of time which each source was mentioned,

\- 57% in the case of outside consultants

\- 60% in the case of industry information

\- $46\%$ in the case of reference systems

came from cluster 1 (= 30.6% of all sample firms).

On the basis of this information the firms of cluster 1 requested an average of 5.8 offers (overall average: 3.5). Depending on their particular functions, the firms noted differences among these offers with regard to software, hardware and price. As for the perception of differences in maintenance contracts, this cluster 1 group proved to have fairly heterogeneous views. The ability to spot differences depends on the number of DP-systems already installed in the firm. Seven firms with an average of 1.7 DP-systems installed registered considerable differences among maintenance contracts of various manufacturers, whereas the other 12 firms averaging 1.1 systems installed, either did not recognize any differences or else were not interested in the maintenance aspect of a contract. Apart from all this information on the performance of various systems, the firms of cluster 1 were also able to state the number of times each manufacturer had already sold his system (this is referred to as “installation figures” in this paper).

Software components dominated the decision in cluster 1: 86.3% of the three most important features had to do with software (overall: 77%). The decision-making process lasted an average of 9.1 months.

## 5.2.3. Cluster 2 (3 firms)

Certain deviations mentioned below set these three firms apart from others although the search phase here was just as intense as in cluster 1: All 3 firms specified requirements for the new system (one firm formulated them in writing) and an average of 2.7 information sources were consulted.

The firms requested 3.3 offers and all perceived differences in software but not in hardware, maintenance or installation figures.

The ultimate decision on a system was dominated by software considerations with 83% of all aspects mentioned. However, the decision-making process – and this is a peculiar feature about cluster 2 – only lasted an average of 4.5 months, the minimum of all clusters. In addition to responding to the standardized questions, the firms were asked to mentioned any of their own specific internal characteristics. The answer led us to conclude that specialized software (e.g. software for transport departments in a particular industry or for hospitals) offered by one of the manufacturers was the decisive factor in convincing these three firms of that product.

## 5.2.4. Cluster 3 (17 firms)

During the search phase these firms specified requirements for a new system, the majority in written form. However, as opposed to cluster 1, only an average of 1.7 information sources were used.

Also, a much lower average (2.6) of offers was requested. For the majority no differences among the offers were perceivable or else they concerned hardware or price. For cluster 3, software considerations also dominated the decision but only with 62.8% of all aspects mentioned as compared to cluster 1: 86.3%. The relative importance of hardware aspects coincided with the fact that differences among the offers were perceived, if at all, with regard to hardware or price only. The decision-making process lasted an average of 8.9 months'. Obviously, a profound lack of knowledge of more than one software package (see number of offers requested and information sources used), caused the more readily compared hardware components and prices to have an important impact on the final decision.

## 5.2.5. Cluster 4 (15 firms)

These 15 firms showed an average intensity of decision-making similar to that of cluster 3, but there were some distinctions. The majority had not specified any requirements for a new system. On the other hand an average of 2.2 information sources were consulted. Apart from the occasional price difference, the majority did not register any differences among the 2.6 offers requested. Software features dominated the decision with 78% of all aspects mentioned. The decision-making process lasted an averaged of 5.3 months. ## 5.2.6. Cluster 5 (8 firms)

These firms revealed a remarkably low intensity in all aspects of their decision-making behaviour. Requirements for the system, although specified, were not written down. An average of one source of information was used. Among the 1.9 offers they had requested, the firms of cluster 5 did not recognize any difference in price or were not interested in it. Nor were they aware of any possible differences in software, hardware, maintenance or installation figures. The decision-making process lasted an average of 7.4 months. Answers to the open questions enabled us to identify some specific decision situations of the firms in cluster 5:

\- brand loyalty with regard to a magnetic ledger cards system;

\- spontaneous deal at a trade fair;

\- change of management with new objectives;

\- suggestion by the parent company.

Having analysed the various patterns of decision-making behaviour, we felt it of interest to determine whether there was any causal relation between the intensity of the decision-making process and the further development of data processing in a particular firm. The specific characteristics displayed in cluster 1 such as

\- written formulation of requirements as a basis for collecting and evaluating alternatives;

\- consulting a clearly above-average number of information sources, particularly those other than the manufacturers';

\- market transparency [10], proved by a wide range of alternatives found and the perception of differences among them,

convinced us that a comparison of the “intensive decision-makers” of cluster 1 with the other clusters was warranted. (See figure 1). The extent of data processing implementation at the time of the interview, which was at least 1 year after the purchase in question, was used to compare the different clusters. It served as an indicator of the effectiveness of the decision, that is, by initiating any further development of computer supported information processing [18].

The extent of data processing implementation was characterized by the following criteria:

\- number of departments per firm (such as accounting, material management, costing) working with data processing applications;

\- degree of integration of DP-applications installed, based on the extent of link-ups;

\- direct staff assistance through office terminals.

The criteria listed above are regarded as necessary components of any efficient implementation of data processing [7]. For each of these components, table 17 shows the values found in the two sample subsets.

Thus we confirmed the anticipated positive relationship between the intensity of the decision-making process and the further development of data processing after acquiring a new system. Over an equal time span of about 4 years the “intensive decision-makers” installed significantly more DP-applications (software packages) and terminals (with regard to internal departments as well) than the rest of the sample. Moreover, the firms of cluster 1 integrated most of their software during those 4 years.

## 6. Conclusions

The following conclusions can be drawn for the data processing manufacturers' marketing: Despite the similarity of the basic goods and services offered (hardware, software, maintenance, education), the different patterns of behaviour in demand call for different marketing concepts in supply. We offer some possibilities.

By advising a prospective client who does not make any specifications, the supplier can expose certain problems, offer solutions, and thus exert more influence, in addition to creating more good-will than merely stressing software-technological qualities of his product. With “intensive decision-makers”, however, it is the performance of the software offered with respect to the clients’ requirements that must be pointed out very clearly. Furthermore, cooperation with information pools such as associations and other industry organisations is an efficient means of gaining indirect influence on the decision-making process of a number of individual firms. Buyers who do not find the software sector transparent, may be persuaded by price-performance tables and readily compared features such as memory size or printer speed.

Finally it should be emphasized that only half the buying firms perceived differences among the offers regarding hardware, software, maintenance or price. This fact stresses the importance of other potential influences on the decision-making process such as the manufacturers' image or some aspect of it (e.g. how the salesman or the system advisor presents himself). The expectation that buyers in producer markets decide more rationally and with greater market transparency than in other markets [3], was confirmed by this investigation only in the case of so-called “intensive decision-makers”.

All these results highlight the considerable importance of aspects in the decision-making process that do not directly involve price-performance relationship and thus warrant further analysis in future research projects 5).
