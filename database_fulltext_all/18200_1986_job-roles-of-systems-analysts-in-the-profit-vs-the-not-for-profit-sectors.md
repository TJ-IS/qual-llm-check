# Job Roles of Systems Analysts in the “Profit” vs the “Not for Profit” Sectors

Albert Kagan
School of Business, Montana State University, Bozeman,
MT 59717, USA

Marion G. Sobol

Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

and

Kevin Quarnstrom

University of Northern Iowa, Ceder Falls, IA 50614, USA

Study of the personal characteristics and job development roles of a nationwide sample of 104 systems analysts in 20 companies in both the “profit” and “not for profit” sectors indicates that there are significant differences in responsibilities. The range of duties is more narrowly defined for the systems analyst in the “not for profit” sector. The analyst in the “not for profit” sector is more likely to write documentation and perform end user training. Maintenance duties are about the same. The analyst in the “profit sector” tends to be younger, has less formal education and is paid more than his “not for profit” counterpart.

The paper specifically covers (1) system development roles, (2) training and documentation duties, (3) systems maintenance duties, (4) staff functions, and (5) professional development activities.

Keywords: analysts, systems analysts, job roles-systems analysts, job responsibilities-systems analysts.

## 1. Introduction

Labor mobility studies have indicated that labor turnover rates in the “not-for-profit sector” and public sector tend to be somewhat lower than those in the private sector [1]. The purpose of this research is to describe differences in the functions performed by individual systems analysts in “not-for-profit” vs. “profit” organizations. Furthermore differences in renumeration and privileges will be contrasted. For example, Do systems analysts working in the private sector perform feasibility studies? Are they responsible for developing cost estimates for alternative proposals? Do they write program code? The same questions can be asked of systems analysts employed in the public sector. Answers to questions about educational opportunities and job privileges are also considered here.

Kevin Quarnstrom has worked in data processing in both the public and private sectors since 1974. In that time, he spent six years as a systems analyst. He holds a B.A. in History from the University of Northern Iowa and was awarded a Master's in Business Administration from there in 1985. Kevin is currently an Information Systems Coordinator in Administrative Data Processing at UNI.

In recent years, with the development of third and fourth generation computer equipment, overall system development costs have increased and the systems analysis phase of the system development life cycle now absorbs approximately twenty percent of system development charges. This is in contrast to the era of first generation systems when systems analysis accounted for only about five percent of system development expenditures $[2]$ . With such a significant investment in one area of the development cycle, it is important to study the tasks performed by systems analysis in different areas.

The systems analysts job involves analyzing problems or informational needs within the organization and solving them through the design of efficient patterns of information flows from the data sources to the computer. He/she also plans the distribution of information, determined by how the processed data is to be used within the organization. The analyst works closely with managers, accountants, and various specialists within the enterprise to determine their informational needs or problems. An understanding of the operation of the functional areas in the organization, such as accounting, production, and marketing, and the ability to communicate effectively with specialists in these areas are crucial to the effectiveness of the analysis and design. Once the information requirements are determined, the analyst goes to work designing a new system or improving an existing one in order to satisfy those informational needs. When the system is designed, it must be related to the requirements of the system and to the capabilities of the computer hardware. Then specifications for programmers to follow in developing software to make the program functional must be prepared.

There are a number of distinct responsibilities within each of these various areas that a systems analyst may be assigned. Past experiences with various employers have shown that each expected its personnel to perform a somewhat different role, that is, a varying set of responsibilities. Thus, although the above list may provide a generic definition of what a systems analyst is and the role he/she plays in a data processing operation, in reality the job may be many different things to many different people and the differences may relate to the size of enterprise and the types of enterprise.

The tremendous increase in the use of data processing throughout all types of corporations and organizations has increased personnel needs. The Labor Department says 21,200 new systems analysts will be hired each year through the 1980s to a total work force of 400,000 by 1990 [3]. As this projected growth occurs, it is possible that a significant changes in responsibilities may come about depending on specific work situations. The analyst may come to wear many hats not covered by the generic definition, some of which he/she may not be equipped to handle. Current literature hints at such an eventuality.

## 2. Previous Studies

In spite of the resources dedicated to information analysis in system development and the importance of systems analysts to the success of an organization's data processing department, there has been little, if any, research done specifically on the precise responsibilities undertaken by individual systems analysts. Studies have been done, however, which have addressed the general skills required for the job. One such study was conducted by Cheyney and Lyons [4]. Their paper detailed some of the skill requirements in the information systems area as seen by the information systems managers of thirty-two of the nation's largest organizations in both the public and private sectors. The project ascertained the managers' perception of the importance of certain specific skills as related to systems analysis. Managers were given a list of specific skill areas and asked to rank the importance of these skills for three groups: data center managers, systems analysts, and programmers. Among the skills included were information gathering techniques, file design, job control languages, minicomputer characteristics and uses, and software package analysis. The study was informative in that it indicated what skills managers thought were important, but did not address the precise responsibilities being undertaken by individual analysts.

Feeney and Sladek [5] addressed the different roles a systems analyst performs and concluded that how well he/she fulfills these roles, and how long he/she survives, depends on the analyst's ability to play the correct role with the right people at the right time within the organization.

The authors suggest that by being sensitive to the organizational settings, one will have a better idea of the extent and force of change which will allow for acceptance and accomplishment of project goals, such as the implementation of a new software and/or hardware system into operation. A systems analyst may have to assume the role of persuader, catalyst, confronter, or imposer in order to bring about change. Although this paper addresses roles of systems analysts, it does so only in generalities and does not zero in on specific skills which an analyst may need in performing job responsibilities nor on the responsibilities themselves.

Vitilari and Dickson [6] compared the thought processes of high- and low-rated systems analysts. This article discussed the types of problem solving behaviors exhibited by the two groups and drew conclusions about the relationship of those behaviors to successful performance. The results suggest that qualitative differences exist in the problem solving methods of the two groups, and that the skills associated with high-rated problem solving may lead to better job performance and may be transferred to other practitioners. This study was typical of much of today's research which examines different skills and attempts to determine the most successful method to perform the task. None, however, address what a specific systems analyst does.

The study by Scharer [7] comes the closest to shedding insight on the specific tasks being performed by specific systems analysts. It described a ranking technique to be used as a pre-cursor to conducting appraisals. It itemized specific job responsibilities and assigned relative priorities of these responsibilities in day-to-day work situations. The article grouped specific responsibilities under general headings which were then ranked by the systems analysts themselves. Although no distinction was made between the employer type of the analyst, it was the analyst who indicated the importance of a set of responsibilities in his/her situation, rather than have a manager indicate what he thought should be important in a general situation. Although the article was written to provide a performance review technique, it was very helpful in helping determine specific day-to-day responsibilities.

In general, even though the current literature has addressed specific broad responsibilities such as problem solving, or determining management needs, there has been very little discussion of specific responsibilities – especially as related to type of employer, i.e., private business, public sector, private “not-for-profit” sector. Both personal experience and informal discussion with systems analysts indicates that a pattern does exist among individuals working within similar circumstances, and at the same time, there are significant differences between the specific job functions performed by those working for different employer types. It was anticipated that those working in business and/or industry would have a much more structured role, and associate themselves more heavily with the analyst functions than those working in a non-profit setting. The conception of the systems analyst working in the not-for-profit sector was that the structure would be more informal, one which called for more involvement in the programming end of systems development with more freedom to design and develop systems which fit the specific need as opposed to following a structured series of steps.

## 3. Methodology

With this background, a research project was undertaken to try to ascertain the functions performed by individuals in the “profit” vs “not for profit” sectors. A questionnaire was developed to be distributed to systems analysts, or to employees performing the generic duties of a systems analyst regardless of position title. The findings reported and analyzed throughout the balance of this paper are based upon responses to the questionnaires mailed to a nationwide sample during April 1985 to October of 1985.

Since access to a list of systems analysts in the immediate area, or a general list of those filling the position was unavailable, it was not possible to use a random sampling technique in the distribution of the questionnaire. Therefore, the sample was drawn via contacts with colleagues in the field in both the public and private sectors. For the purposes of this study, the “not-for-profit” sector was defined as educational institutions, governmental agencies, and all other types of non-profit organizations. The “profit” sector was considered to be made up of profit making business. Accompanying the questionnaire was a cover letter with a generic definition of a systems analyst with a request that a person performing such a role complete the instrument. Also enclosed for the respondent's convenience was a postage-paid return envelope. Approximately 200 questionnaires were mailed out and 104 responses were received. Of the one hundred and four, sixteen were from public and private educational institutes; thirty-seven from government organizations (these two groups are classified as the “not-for-profit sector”), and the remainder (51) came from the business/industry segment.

## 4. Description of Demographic Characteristics of the Sample

The survey instrument contained a number of questions designed to elicit a demographic description of systems analysts, or those who perform the duties commonly thought associated with the systems analyst position. Responses to these questions show that the members of the sample had an average age of approximately 35 years with a range of 22 to 57. Twenty-eight people were found to be over the age of 40, 31 were under 30 and 43 were between 30 and 39. The respondents included 70 males and 33 females.

The group averaged slightly over ten years of data processing experience with a range from two to twenty-nine years. Fourteen people had less than four years of experience while six had over fifteen. The distribution was very well spread over the entire range with six or seven years being the most popular response.

The educational achievements of the systems analysts were varied. Thirty-four people lacked four year college degrees, though twenty-nine had attended a technical school of some sort and nineteen of the group had attended college, but had not earned a degree. At the other end of the spectrum, twelve respondents had earned Master's degrees of some type and two had received doctoral degrees. The Bachelor's degrees encompassed a wide variety of majors including computer science, engineering, and mathematics, as might be expected, but also included music, history, and physical education.

Responses were received from a variety of different sized data processing shops. The median number of employees in the systems and programming areas of the survey respondents was 25, but the responses ranged from 2 to 150. The salary range indicated that 9% earned between \$15–\$25,000, 61% from \$25,001 to \$35,000, 25% from \$35,001 to \$45,000 and 4% earned from \$45,001 to \$55,000.

## 5. Description of Demographic Characteristics of Profit vs Not-for-Profit Employees

While in many ways the sample is somewhat homogeneous in regard to characteristics such as age, sex, and experience of the employees in the “not-for-profit” sector versus employees in the profit seeking sector, there are several areas in which major differences could be noted. One such area is the educational level achieved by the two groups. Respondents who work in governmental or educational settings had somewhat higher educational level than did those working in business and industry. Nineteen of the 50 respondents in the private sector, nearly 38 percent, had not received an undergraduate degree. In contrast, only 15 of the 53 respondents (approximately 28 percent) in the public sector did not have undergraduate degrees. Eighteen percent of the not-for-profit employees had received Master’s degrees whereas 8 percent of the for profit employees were in this group. Approximately the same percentage of “profit” and “not-for-profit” sector employees had attended a technical school. The second area where significant differences were noted was in the salary range of the respondents. In the not-for-profit sector, 87% earned \$35,000 or less while in the private sector 46% earned \$35,000 or less. Sixty-two percent of those employed in the business sector were under 35 whereas 38% of those employed by the “not-for-profit” sector were under 35. Thus “not-for-profit” employees tended to be older and not as well paid as their private sector counterparts. The systems programming staff tended to be somewhat larger (55% over 100) for profit vs non-profit groups (45% over 100).

## 6. Analysis of Questionnaire Results

For analytical purposes, one can look at the occupational role of systems analysts in five major categories: (1) system development, (2) creation of documentation, (3) maintenance of existing systems, (4) staff functions, and (5) professional development activities.

However, certain differences of interest did appear in this category. Not surprisingly, these differences tend to appear in the area of developing prototypes, cost estimates, creation of sample test data, and writing job control language (JCL). These are responsibilities which involve more programmer and business and fewer analyst type skills. In the first case, whereas approximately 28 percent of the profit sector analysts did some coding, over 45 percent of the not for profit analysts did so. In the area of job control language, the discrepancy was similar. Only 65 percent of the profit sector analysts wrote JCL while 79 percent of analysts employed in the not for profit sector did so. The most plausible explanation for this would seem to be one of differential resources. The private sector is apparently able to support their analysts with support personnel freeing its analysts from these coding tasks which can be completed by others, leaving them to concentrate on the broader system level. Moreover, specialization may be a more common practice in the “profit” sector.

## 6.2. Training and Documentation Duties

A second major role performed by systems analysts is that of training end-users and the creation of all types of documentation, including program, system, data technician, and end-user documentation, to be used by others to ensure the smooth operations of the system. In Table 2 we see that the systems analysts employed in the “not for profit” sector are more likely to perform almost all aspects of training and documentation than those in the profit sector. As far as writing systems overviews, this is the only area where an equal percentage of employees (85%) in both sectors perform this function.

The discrepancy is even greater in the creation of documentation for the using department, and these differences are significant at the .10 level. Only 51 percent of private sector analysts perform this function while 68 percent of public sector respondents indicated that they do so. Eighty-nine percent write data technician instructions and job set ups in the educational and governmental areas while only 74 percent of the business/industry analyst respondents do so. These differences may possibly be explained again by the private sector's differential in staff resources. The development of system documentation requires an ability to view and understand the big picture, an ability associated with the role of a systems analyst. The other tasks require less specialization and the decentralization on the private side has meant these duties have been assigned to lower level employees.

## 6.5. Professional Development

The final category consists of all phases of professional development (See Table 5). A high percentage of both sectors (100% in the “not-for-profit” sector, 86% in the “profit” sector) have access to journals and other periodicals at their place of employment, but there is a difference in the freedom to read the periodicals. Sixty-nine percent of the “not for profit” sector responded that they were allowed work time to familiarize themselves with the contents of these periodicals but only 55 percent of those analysts in the “profit” sector responded that they were allowed work time to do so. Similar data was found regarding attendance at classes and seminars. Eighty-nine percent of “not-for-profit” sector employees were allowed to attend while only 80 percent of the “profit” sector respondents could do so. While this difference wasn’t statistically significant, the employer paid the entire cost of such education for all the “not for profit” employees while 24 percent of “profit” respondents indicated that their employer did not pay for such development. These differences are somewhat puzzling as one would anticipate the “profit” sector investing its resources in professional development for its staff. The high degree of participation in the “not-for-profit” sector may indicate that professional development opportunities are used as an inducement to attract and keep qualified applicants in the “not-for-profit” sector in an attempt to offset the generally lower salary levels found there.

## 7. Summary and Conclusions

This paper has been concerned with describing the job of the systems analyst and with comparisons of the specific job responsibilities of systems analysts, or those performing the typical duties of systems analysts, in the “profit” and the “not-for-profit” sectors. The objective was to examine these responsibilities and determine the similarities and differences encountered across diverse employment situations. The research results were an analysis of questionnaires sent to contacts in the field in both sectors.

The respondents were selected from a cross section of public and private employers in different geographical locations and could be truly representative of systems analysts across the country.

The analysis has shown a number of interesting results. Among them is the realization that there are indeed different job responsibilities associated with a systems analyst position depending on the employer type, but there are similarities as well. Similarities were noted in maintenance activities, certain system development functions, some documentation responsibilities and staff activities.

Differences were noted primarily in those responsibilities dealing with end-user training and documentation and professional development. In all documentation activities there seemed to be more activity in the “not-for-profit” sector than in the profit sector and these differences were generally significant at the .05 level.

It would seem that the range of duties seems to have become more narrowly defined in the “profit” sector. More and more responsibilities have been undertaken by others as the systems analyst has become specialized and is now doing what his title implies – analyzing systems – while the “not-for-profit” sector analyst continues to perform most all of the responsibilities associated with the generic role. These differences moreover were not the result of differences in the size of the systems analysis groups in the profit versus the not-for-profit organizations as both groups had similar sized systems analysis staffs. It is interesting to note that as this discrepancy exists, the data indicates that the “profit” sector systems analyst is being financially rewarded at a higher rate than his counterpart in the public sector, even though he tends to be younger and has less formal education. The reward in the “not-for-profit” sector may be a more interesting and varied job as well as more chance for professional development.
