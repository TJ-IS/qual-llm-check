# Information Resource Management in Singapore: The State of the Art

Sumit Sircar \* and K. Venkata Rao

Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Kent Ridge, Singapore 0511

Singapore has made impressive uses of computer technology and aims to be a major source of information technology. This study evaluates its performance in terms of information resource management. Survey data from leading organizations were collected and analyzed, in the areas of planning, information management role, user relations and systems development. The responses were compared against the state of the art in the U.S. as per the literature. A handful of organizations were found to be following most of the practices prescribed in the U.S., but the others appeared to be much less sophisticated.

Keywords: Information Resource Management, Singapore, MIS Management Audit, Information Management, Data Processing Management

Dr. Sumit Sircar is an Associate Professor in the Information Systems and Management Sciences Department in the College of Business Administration of the University of Texas at Arlington. He earned a doctorate degree in 1976 at the Harvard Business School in the field of computer-based systems. Since then his research, teaching and consulting activities have been in the field of information resource management. He has published several articles in various journals on different aspects of information management, including the appropriate management of emerging information technologies. He has also conducted numerous professional seminars on related topics.

## 1. Introduction

The economic importance of the island Republic of Singapore (population 2.5 million) is far greater than its small size might suggest. In 1984, with per capita GNP of U.S. \$6,718, she was the world's 15th ranked importer and 20th ranked exporter. For reasons explained below, Singapore is making a rapid transition from a manufacturing economy to an information economy. An information economy can be defined as one in which the dominant industries produce information processing goods, e.g. computing and data communications equipment, or sell information services in established markets (e.g. consulting and travel agencies). These industries comprise the “primary” information sector. The secondary information sector comprises the information processing activities within private and public organizations, such as planning, coordinating and monitoring [9, pp. 162–163].

The Singapore government's emphasis on this restructuring has come about partly because of the fading strength of the country's traditional bread and butter industries – shipbuilding and repairing, petroleum, construction and manufacturing. Significant progress has been made in this direction as illustrated by the following achievements:

![](/api/attachments/3CNKHJ46/fulltext/images/3e65414dc58f0bc6b4140369f5bd146c7e5509d839946571fa99d73970e8e646.jpg)

\- By 1979, two thirds of the jobs in Singapore were in service occupations, while more than two thirds of the country's total output was generated through services [12, p. 168].

\- There are 40 telephones per 100 people in Singapore.

\- There were an estimated 400 computer professionals in 1984 [3, p. 4].

\- Plans are under way to construct the world's most sophisticated videotext system.

\- The racetrack betting system designed for Hong Kong is reputedly the world's best.

\- The just-completed Stanford-Westin hotel has one of the world's most sophisticated hotel-management systems.

\- The traffic-control system is heavily computerized [2, pp. 155–158].

These examples serve to illustrate Singapore's commitment to using the most sophisticated computer technology. A key question thus arises: How sophisticated are Singapore organizations at Information Resource Management? Put another way, how well do these organizations manage their data processing, office automation, data communications and allied functions?

This paper will attempt to answer the question by comparing the information management activities at several representative Singapore organizations with what is generally considered good IM practice in the U.S. Although we now know that prescriptions for good practice should be contingent on the situation at hand, there is still a core of established procedures that are almost universally considered desirable. Most organizations with sophisticated IM functions have well-accepted guidelines in the areas of planning, project management, training, and so on. There are also some rather startling ideas, such as hiring a “technological strategist” [14], which were used to test how close to the cutting edge of IM an organization is.

The research was restricted to the external aspects of information resource management as opposed to internal aspects. External aspects are defined here as those having to do with the IM role in the organization and organizational position, relationships with top management, and relationships with users (including systems development). Internal aspects have to do with the management of the IM function itself, in terms of personnel, hardware, software, projects, telecommunications, and so on. Results of a separate study concerning the internal aspects of information resource management in Singapore will be published separately [21].

## 2. Research Methodology

In order to answer the research question posed, two problems had to be solved. The first was to determine the present state of the art of IM in Singapore; the second was to identify a prescribed or ideal state of the art.

Although there are 673 organizations with computer installations (excluding computer vendors) [3], many of these are microcomputer installations which have no DP professionals. In fact, the DP installations of the majority of these organizations are too small to incorporate many “prescribed” practices, such as Quality Assurance departments or Customer Service Centers. The members of the Data Processing Manager’s Association (DPMA) constitute the majority of the large computer installations in Singapore, and there are only 57 members. Even these DP departments are rather modest in size and scope compared to those of the bigger firms in the U.S. (the three largest have DP personnel strengths of 250, 200 and 150 respectively). So the task of pinpointing the handful of large, sophisticated computer organizations in Singapore is actually rather simple for anyone with some knowledge of the local computing environment.

By fortuitous circumstance, 52 senior IM personnel representing just such a group of organizations, numbering 20 in all, attended a 13-week course given by one of the authors. Groups of managers (individuals in some cases) from each organization answered the questionnaire reproduced in the Appendix. The 20 organizations represent the major components of Singapore's public and private sectors – ministries, shipbuilding, transportation, construction, manufacturing, petroleum, banking, retailing and publishing. The data collected, therefore, can be deemed to be fairly representative of the state-of-the-art in Singapore. This claim is based on the following four considerations: (1) A broad spectrum of Singapore's government and industry was sampled; (2) Major representatives from these sectors participated, in many cases the "flagship" organization, e.g. in shipping and transportation; (3) Most of the largest installations in Singapore were included, being the only ones with sufficient size to attract the best professionals, and capable of sustaining such features as Quality Assurance departments, Customer Service Centers and Career Pathing; and (4) Even though some other sophisticated installations may exist, the state of affairs in the major firms across this spectrum constitute, by definition, Singapore's "state-of-the-art", i.e. the level of sophistication and expertise on a nation-wide scale.

Discussions during the course also provided a great deal of information beyond that contained in the questionnaire answers. However, since this information is of a qualitative, subjective nature, it will only be presented in the discussion section at the end.

The problem of determining an ideal IM environment was also less difficult than originally expected. Synnott and Gruber's excellent book [22] is actually a compendium of 67 tried and proven management practices in IM. Some of these are still fairly new, such as the concept of a Chief Information Officer. Others were widely accepted, e.g. user participation in the systems development process. In almost every case, the authors selected those IM practices for which fairly wide support and acceptance were deemed to exist in the field and the literature, and would also be applicable in Singapore.

## 3. IM Practices Studied and Survey Results

## 3.1. Planning

In their study of key IM issues for the 1980s, Dickson et al. identified planning as easily the most important issue [6]. A great deal has been written on IM planning, including works by Lucas and Turner [14], and McFarlan and McKenney [17]. A prescription for proper planning can be extracted from these, and includes: (a) a formal corporate plan; (b) a formal IM plan; and (c) a link between the two plans. Our survey also attempted to determine how closely the Management by Objectives (MBO) approach is being followed.

It turned out that exactly half the participants were using the MBO approach, three fourths had a formal corporate plan, and 70 percent had a formal IM plan. However, only 40 percent reported a link between the corporate and IM plan. In talking to the participants, it was apparent that using information technology to drive corporate strategy is not yet being done. It was therefore not surprising that only one of the respondents had definite plans to hire a “technological strategist” – someone who understands the business and tracks new information technologies in order to suggest new corporate strategies to top management [14].

## 3.2. Information Management Role

The information systems literature has consistently made the following recommendations for the IM department. First, IM should be an autonomous department so that no single organizational function has undue influence and control over it. Second, that the top IM executive should be a senior executive, for several reasons: the position will attract high quality people; he can negotiate at arms length with other executives; he has easier access to top management; and it will be appropriate to include other information resources in his domain, e.g. office information systems. Third, IM should represent a coalescence of information resources – word processing, telecommunications, mail room, library, etc. – because the barriers between these functions and technologies are fast disappearing [24, pp. 124–127].

Furthermore, Nolan [18] has noted that many of the problems relating to IM stem from the very poor communication between the IM manager and the CEO. Ives and Olson [9] actually measured how much time a sample set of IM managers spent with their superiors and found it to be very low (7%).

Our survey results showed that IM is a separate department in two thirds of the cases. Moreover the same proportion have the IM manager within only one or two levels of the CEO. In only one quarter of the sample is data processing integrated with word processing, and almost half has data processing integrated with telecommunications.

Time spent with the CEO was estimated by the respondents, so the figures must be considered very approximate. One third reported spending one or two hours a month with the CEO, while a quarter spent three to five hours with the CEO each month. It is difficult to compare these figures with U.S. data, but they appear quite reasonable from IM's point of view.

When asked if they expected the IM manager to become CEO in the next 10 years, 10 percent indicated they did, which is similar to the results of a U.S. study. Interestingly, a quarter of the organizations had moved in a person from outside IM to take the top IM job. Although high, this figure is considerably smaller than the 77% mentioned by Scott Morton for his U.S. survey [20].

## 3.3. User Relations

The concept of IM as a business has evolved over the last few years [17, pp. 183–189]. The cornerstone of this concept is that IM is a marketer of IM products and services, and the user is its customer. Since the customer is king, IM must make every effort to satisfy the user. Standard marketing techniques can be used, such as user satisfaction surveys and the establishment of a Customer Service Center. The backlog of undeveloped applications is usually a sore point with users. Some of the pressure can be alleviated by distributing some of the data processing services, provided formal standards for doing so are established to prevent chaos. This would mean a changed D.P. environment, where the users can access and manipulate information required to solve their problems fifty percent of the time without D.P. help. The role of the analyst would also change – he will be acting more as a consultant, guide and trainer of users. Furthermore, he will be seen more as assisting the users in showing them the possibilities and new methods [15].

Another source of user complaints is the perceived high incidence of breakdowns, slow response time, late deliveries, reruns, and similar operational characteristics. The proper way to approach this is to sign mutually negotiated User Service Contracts [21, pp. 114–118], whereby norms for service are established. Actual performance is then tracked to that hard evidence exists to settle arguments or counter claims made by users.

Yet another source of strife with users is the chargeout mechanism. Users are often unable to understand their charges and feel that the charging algorithm is unfair to them. The least sophisticated chargeout method is, of course, not to charge the user. The most sophisticated system is to charge the user for each transaction processed, rather than for computer resources used, because this figure is more intelligible and useful to the user.

Survey results showed that only one organization had performed a user satisfaction survey. Data processing facilities are still largely centralized, with only two installations having any users with programmers or analysts. Only two organizations have standards covering all aspects of end-user computing – hardware, software, security and data communications. Distributed data processing standards exist in six cases for hardware and software, while security and data communications standards exist only in four and one organizations respectively.

Two organizations had set up separate customer service centers, but none had formal user service contracts.

Regarding chargeout mechanisms, thirteen did not charge at all, six charged for computer usage only, and three charged for both systems development and computer usage. In the U.S., computer usage is usually an overhead item for beginning users. Users in established computer installations generally are charged for all DP services.

## 3.4. Systems Development

Systems development is an area of information resource management where the leading-edge companies and the literature provide ample guidance for successful tactics. Examples of prescribed procedures include: (I) A Steering Committee, which has responsibility for IM policy making; (2) Very high level membership in the Steering Committee, preferably including the CEO; (3) Selection (prioritization) of applications for development by the Steering Committee; (4) Formal standards for project requests; (5) Inclusion of users on project development teams; and (6) Formal post-implementation audits [5, pp. 612–616].

The situation in terms of these issues turned out to be as follows. Three quarters of the organizations had set up formal Steering Committees, with the CEO chairing it in slightly over half the instances. The Steering Committee was responsible for application systems selection in slightly less than half the organizations. The DP department did the selection in almost as many instances.

Formal standards for project requests existed in two-thirds of the organizations. Users were included on project teams in about half the installations. A formal post-implementation audit was performed in a third of them.

The backlog of undeveloped systems was generally within three years, two years being the most common (a third of the installations). This is somewhat less than the backlog in large U.S. corporations, which is from two to four years [16, p. 18]. Information Resource Centers, which provide a means for users to circumvent the backlog, have been set up by almost half the organizations.

## 4. Discussion

The results of the survey showed that even within this elite group of organizations, there was a wide spectrum of sophistication in respect of information resource management. Some of the organizations have adopted several of the prescribed strategies, whereas others are still fairly primitive from an IM management perspective.

Some differences between the Singapore DP environment and that in the U.S. should be pointed out. The most apparent is the highly centralized nature of IM in Singapore, resulting from its small size. There is also apparently little enthusiasm on the part of the user to take on the burden of either development or operations. Distributed data processing is therefore much less of an issue in Singapore than in the U.S. Another difference is that even the largest installations are relatively small in size, and therefore cannot support some useful functions, as pointed out earlier. A third difference is more subtle, and has to do with the fact that Singapore managers are much less likely to criticize the status quo (at least publicly) and changes are therefore likely to occur at a slower pace. A related problem is that top management is often viewed as not being fully conversant with IM issues and information technology, and therefore not supportive of many needed changes.

In terms of IM practices, the biggest differences compared to those in the advanced U.S. companies are in the related areas of chargeout and consideration of IM as a business. In the U.S., the user is carefully charged for information goods and services consumed. He also increasingly has access to alternative suppliers, such as computer utilities, vendors, software houses, contract programmers, and the like. The IM department has therefore to literally market its products, much like a retailer. Standard marketing mechanisms such as market research (interviews and satisfaction surveys), customer service centers, and quality assurance departments are becoming increasingly common. Since users have to pay for computer services, they are very conscious of value obtained for their dollar and their other alternatives.

Although user satisfaction surveys are not performed, some data is available from a recent Singapore study of end-user computing [23]. Users felt that their computer systems were inflexible and response to ad hoc requests for information is unsatisfactory. Their ability to meet requirements of higher management was inadequate. They also complained of not being sufficiently involved in the system development process. The present study shows that the most advanced organizations are working towards alleviating these problems. Half of them do include users in their project development teams and almost as many have set up Information Resource Centers. These are extremely useful for quickly generating ad hoc reports for higher management, through the use of fourth generation languages and database management systems. They also solve the problem of inflexibility which is characteristic of older computer applications. There is also growing recognition of the need for formal post-implementation audits – one-third of the organizations perform them. This is, of course, the appropriate way to evaluate the total development cycle of an application.

## 5. Conclusion

This study has compared the state of the art of Information Resource Management in Singapore's leading DP organizations against a prescribed or textbook state of the art. The latter is not some theoretical set of strategies and tactics, but what is supported by the information systems literature as the practices prevalent in the most successful DP organizations in the U.S. In a sense the study is a management audit of the organizations surveyed, concentrating on the external relationships of the IM function, and not on the management of the IM function per se.

We have found that a handful of the elite organizations have adopted many of the measures generally considered necessary for successful IM management. These organizations are still not on par with the leading edge DP organizations in the U.S. in such areas as information technology serving as the prime mover of corporate strategy; the consideration of IM as a business; and the role of the CIO as general manager.

The rest of the organizations, although comprising some of Singapore's best known organizations, must be considered well behind in terms of sophistication in information resource management. Some of this can perhaps be attributed to small size, although the authors are reluctant to say that small organizations have necessarily to be less sophisticated.

Singapore proves, once again, that hardware and software acquisition are relatively easy compared to the long and painful task of implementing information resource management strategies, procedures and structures.
