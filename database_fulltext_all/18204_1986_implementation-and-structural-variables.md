# Implementation and Structural Variables

Robert P. Cerveny and G. Lawrence Sanders
School of Management, Dept. of Management Science and Systems, Jacobs Management Center, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA

Considerable attention has been given to the importance of structural and contextual variables in the successful implementation of computer-based information systems. A perusal of the empirical studies, however, reveals only moderate evidence to substantiate the critical nature of macro organizational variables. This paper reviews the hypothetical and empirical studies concerning organizational factors and gives several recommendations for future research strategies.

Keywords: Implementation, Structural Variables, Contextual Variables, Macro Organizational Variables, Organizational Factors, System Success.

## 1. Implementation Research

Early in the development of computers, organizational decision makers observed the efficiency of developing systems for routine tasks such as payroll processing and inventory reordering. It was speculated that the real potential of the computer would be realized when it was applied to managerial decision making. Indeed, the term 'MIS' was in part the result of the anticipated benefits of electronic information processing. The history of MIS however, has been punctuated by system failures. Academicians have devoted a considerable amount of effort towards identifying the factors and processes contributing to the successful implementation of computer-based information systems.

Interest in the successful implementation of computer-based information systems is evidenced by the numerous articles, books, and workshops on the subject $[36,5,43]$ . It has been suggested that MIS success be considered the primary variable for MIS behavioral research $[13,42]$ . From a pragmatic standpoint, a major goal of information systems research is to provide insight into the phenomena affecting system success. A case could also be made for using ‘success’ as the dependent variable for technical MIS research. This view of implementation differs from the classical view (EDP era) which considers implementation to be the actual installation of ‘program code’.

This paper is a review of the factors and processes which have been presented in the literature as affecting the successful implementation of computer-based information systems. In particular the discussion centers on organizational or macro variables and suggests future strategies for studying and conceptualizing the organizational context of MIS.

## 2. Categories of Implementation Research

Implementation research can be separated into two categories: factor research and process research $[16,18,36]$ . Factor research deals with the influence of static organizational abstractions and technical system quality upon successful implementation. Factors are considered ‘static’ primarily because of the data gathering techniques used in such research. Generally, the data gathered represent snapshots of users at a given point in time. Factor research assumes that the factors remain in the same state throughout implementation. Table 1 presents an overview of the major factors which have been demonstrated empirically to influence implementation.

User involvement and top management support have been the only factors which have received consistent support from investigators as being critical to successful implementation. Reviews of the literature on the impact of participative systems development and user involvement in system success can be found in Hirscheim [23] and Ives and Olson [25]. Early research in MIS concentrated on the technical considerations. The Minnesota experiments are a good example of research into the technical interface [10]. However, attitudinal, cognitive, and individual differences research has gained popularity as evidenced by Ginzberg [19], Keen and Bronsema [30], Kasper and Cerveny [27], and Barki and Huff [1]. An overview of research on how individual differences can influence computer usage can be found in Zmud [47].

Three areas of factor research have received limited empirical attention. The issues of power redistribution within the organization after MIS intervention and user resistance to the system due to loss of power have been studied $[37,3]$ . Another factor which has received scant attention is the effect of user motivation on system usage. In one of the few studies in this area, DeSanctis $[9]$ found that motivation is a significant variable affecting DSS usage. The final factor which has garnered limited empirical attention is labeled ‘Organizational Context and Structure’. The research in this area is primarily conceptual $[18]$ and lacks empirical support.

Process research views implementation as a continuing cycle in which special attention is given to active user participation during all phases of system development. Process research highlights the importance of the relationship between user and designer during development. Zand and Sorenson [46] found that forces favorable in each stage of the Lewin/Schein change model contributed to successful management science projects. Ginzberg [17] demonstrated that successful implementation was the result of attending to user's concerns in the various stages of the Kolb/Frohman model of the consulting process. Ginzberg [18] has suggested a model which integrates many of the complex issues of factor research and process research which have been discussed so far. His model is depicted in Fig. 1.

As noted earlier, this paper examines the influence of the organizational context and in particular the organizational context on MIS success. The following section reviews the research in that area.

## 3. Organizational Research on Implementation

There is a paucity of empirical research examining the relationship between the organizational variables and system success; however, a number of investigators have reflected on the argument. Mason and Mitroff [38] noted the importance of the problem type and organizational context. An earlier work by Klahr and Leavett [32] foresaw the need and eventual development of decision support systems (DSS). Their argument was that upper levels in the organization deal with tasks which are ill-defined and require real time, nonhierarchical, heuristic programs (systems). They further point out that “…ill-defined novel tasks generate flexible, open nonhierarchical structures. Top levels of organizations usually work on ill-defined tasks, often converting them into a programmed state.” ([32], p. 129). This work was an early attempt to show that system success is contingent upon the level at which the system is to be installed, the type of problems the user faces, and the system typology.

Whisler [45] looked at the other side of the implementation issue dealing with the impact of information technology on the structure of the organization. He hypothesized that departments such as engineering design, research and development, industrial relations and advertising would experience little structural change after the introduction of an information system. Production, accounting, and product distribution departments, according to Whisler, would undergo greater structural changes. In essence, Whisler was saying that mechanistic organizations exhibit greater structural changes than organic organizations with the introduction of an MIS. The ensuing research on the effects of computerized systems on organizational structure has been sparse and contradictory [34].

Litt [35] conducted closely related research using the mechanistic/organic approach. He found that individuals from organic organizations possessed greater ability to solve unstructured, non-programmed problems than individuals from mechanistic organizations. This raises the question of whether DSS can be effectively implemented in mechanistic organizations where the individuals have less capacity to deal with unstructured decisions. If so, will the DSS assist individuals in mechanistic organizations in the structuring of unstructured problems? A parallel question is: Which type of organization will benefit most from the introduction of a DSS?

Gordon and Miller [20] theorized that the organizational context should influence the design of accounting and information systems. It was their thesis that three global constraints – the external organizational environment, the organizational structure, and the decision making style of executives – should influence the design of information systems. They defined the organizational environment in terms of its dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility. Organizational structure consisted of the following attributes: (1) decentralization, (2) differentiation, (3) integration, (4) bureaucratization, and (5) resources. Decision making style of executives referred to the following dimensions: (1) analysis, (2) time horizons, (3) multiplexity, (4) adaptiveness, (5) proactivity, and (6) consciousness or explicitness of strategies and objectives ([20]), p. 63). Further, they defined three organization types: adaptive, running blind, and stagnant bureaucracy. The adaptive firm operates in a dynamic environment, tends towards decentralization, and consists of decision makers who are responsive to trends in the firms operating environment. The running blind firm operates in an environment characterized by high levels of dynamism and heterogeneity. Unlike the adaptive firm, the running blind structure is highly centralized. Decision makers in the running blind firm often rely on intuition for problem solving. The stagnant bureaucracy uses hierarchical control, rules, and rigidity for integration. Because of environmental stability, decision makers resort to conservatism and past actions to guide them. An overview of the Gordon and Miller model for designing accounting and information systems is presented in Fig. 2.

Ein-Dor and Segev [11] presented 22 proportions concerning the effect of the organizational context on successful MIS implementation. The organization context consisted of uncontrollable variables (organizational size, structure, time frame, and the extra-organizational situation), partially controllable variables (organizational resources, maturity, and psychological climate), and controllable variables (rank and location of MIS executives and the existence of a steering committee for MIS projects). Recently, Cheney, Mann, and Amoroso [6] proposed a conceptual scheme based on the Ein-Dor and Segev model which links the organizational context to the success of end-user computing. Few of the hypotheses outlined by Ein-Dor and Segev have been substantiated with strong empirical evidence. Examples of research on the propositions may be found in Ein-Dor and Segev [12], Gremillion [21], Raymond [40], and Franz and Robey [14].

Ginzberg [18] set forth twelve propositions involving the relationship between organizational variables and the design and implementation of accounting and information systems. This work extends the research framework of Gordon and Miller [20]. The twelve propositions delve into the effect of the organizations environment, technology, and structure on the implementation of accounting and information systems.

Ginzberg [18] has also addressed the issue of task interdependence and its affect on successful implementation. The concept of task interdependence was adapted from Thompson [44]. Thompson defined three types of interdependence within organizations, pooled interdependence, sequential interdependence, and reciprocal interdependence.

‘Pooled interdependence’ occurs when “…each part (organization) renders a discrete contribution to the whole and each is supported by the whole” ([44], p. 54). Organizations which have a high level of pooled interdependence demand that each component perform its task effectively. ‘Sequential interdependence’ implies that one sub-task precedes another and the prior task must be completed before another can begin. Thompson also refers to sequential interdependence as being serial whereby the output from one task is the input for another, ‘Reciprocal interdependence’ is the case where there is an exchange of inputs and outputs between tasks. Ginzberg combines Thompson’s three categories of interdependence into an interdependent and independent dimension. He defines independent-tasks as those which do not require the assistance of others in order to be completed. Interdependent tasks, however, are intertwined with one another and require the assistance of others for completion. This concept has received considerable attention in the literature.

Bean, Neal, Radnor, and Tansik [2] found conflicting evidence on the influence of decentralization on the number of OR/MS projects completed and the OR/MS manager's perception of the success of his department. In three of the six stages of development of the OR/MS activity, they found a small negative correlations between decentralization and the rate of OR/MS innovation. In the 'organizational development' stage, where the OR/MS function is vying for acceptance and recognition, there was a small positive correlation between decentralization and the success of OR/MS. However, organizations were not measured in terms of degree of centralization but were binarily judged to be either centralized or decentralized.

Robey and Zeller [41] conducted a study which demonstrated that attitudes toward MIS performance and the urgency of the problem addressed by the system affects successful implementation. This study took place within two units of one organization. They categorized the organizational units which rejected or accepted the system according to structural and contextual characteristics (a subjective method was used for identifying the contextual variables). The organizational unit which adopted the system was judged to be more formalized, less complex, and more centralized than the organizational unit which rejected the information system.

Keen [29] presents a model of organizational issues which influence the development and subsequent success of Decision Support Systems (DSS). The contextual forces affecting DSS within the organization are presented in Fig. 3. In reference to the model, the ‘organization’s technology’ (T) consists of both hardware and data resources of an organization. The ‘organization charter and location’ refers to the physical and psychological distance between the user and builders of DSS within the organization. The concept of ‘task representation’ is related to the type of problem to be addressed by the DSS. The essence of ‘task representation’ is that semistructured tasks and the decision making process can be structured and defined through adaptive design. The final category (0) emphasizes the influence of organizational procedures and systems on successful DSS development.

Halladay [22] has conducted a pilot study in which it was hypothesized that variation in the organizational subunit would have an influence on successful MIS implementation. Line control of the work flow was the only variable found to be related to successful implementation. Other dimensions studied by Halladay, but not statistically related to success, were standardization of activities and centralization of decision making. His study was conducted on 87 information systems in 26 departments across 7 organizations.

Recently Sanders and Courtney [43] investigated the influence of several organizational variables on DSS success. The study involved 378 users of DSS in 124 organizations and was conducted at the individual level of analysis. They found no relationship between task standardization, task authority and DSS success. They did, however, find a small significant relationship between the level of task interdependence and DSS success for managers.

Franz and Robey [14] found inverse relationships between organization size and age and the usefulness of computer-based systems. Users in older and larger organizations perceived systems to be less useful. The issue of the impact of organizational size on system success has been studied extensively. Gremillion [21] concludes from his study that organization size does not appear to be a good predictor of system use. Lehman [33] however, has found a relationship between organizational size and the use of sophisticated computer-based tools (telecommunications, database, high level languages, graphics devices, etc.). Raymond [40] found for his sample of small firms that firm size was not significantly related to user satisfaction or system utilization.

To summarize, this section has reviewed the conceptual foundations and empirical studies related to the impact of organizational variables on system success. Mason and Mitroff [38] provide the basic framework for the study by highlighting the organizational context as an area for concentrated inquiry. Whisler [45] considers the reverse issue, the impact of systems on organization structure. Gordon and Miller [20] theorized that organizational attributes (decentralization, differentiation, integration, and bureaucratization) should be considered when designing information systems. Ein-Dor and Segev [11] tendered 22 propositions regarding the effect of the organizational context on information system success. Investigations on these propositions have been conducted by Ein-Dor and Segev [12] and Raymond [40]. Ginzberg [18] hypothesized that organizational variables (environment, technology, and structure) affect system success. Keen [29] suggested that organizational procedures and systems may influence DSS success. Empirical studies by Bean, Neal, Radnor, and Tansik [23], Robey and Zeller [41], Halladay [22], Gremillion [21], Raymond [40], Sanders and Courtney [43], Lehman [33] and Franz and Robey [14] were inconclusive on the effect of the organizational context and structure on system success.

## 4. Future Directions

While there is a prepondency of assertions, propositions, and hypotheses concerning the apparent importance of the contextual and structural variables on implementation success, there has been little empirical support demonstrating the importance of organizational variables on system success. However, this situation should not prompt us to abandon the study of macro variables, but rather examine and consider alternative strategies which might profitably be employed to improve research methods in the area.

## 4.1. Use a Longitudinal Technique for Data Collection

The time and resources needed to conduct a longitudinal study are well documented. The problem of conducting a longitudinal study is compounded when studying MIS by the systems development life cycle. It is difficult to find systems prior to installation and then to coordinate the measurement of the variables with the actual data of system installation. Nevertheless, longitudinal studies provide an extremely sensitive technique for understanding the dynamics of implementation.

## 4.2. Select Validated Instruments for Independent and Dependent Variables

While the use of “commercially” available instruments does not guarantee significant results, it does enable the researcher to concentrate on the research design. Validated instruments also contribute to the “rigor” of the study. Care must be exercised in the use of such instruments however, as there are instances where they may lead to abuses.

For example, the selection of a measure for system success is a particularly onerous task. There are validated instruments for system success but they are not suitable for all studies. Rather than develop new measures or revalidating existing measures, researchers are often tempted to force an instrument into their research design. The field is still agonizing over the dimensions of systems success and as yet there is no instrument which can satisfy the requirements of all studies. (See Sanders [42] for a review of the issues on measuring system success.)

Another problem related to the use of validated instruments for measuring system success is that they often cross levels of analysis. Subjects are asked to relate their perceptions of how the system impacts their job and also how successfully the system impacts the organization. This can seriously affect the interpretation of study results. Which leads to the next issue.

## 4.3. Select the Appropriate Level of Analysis

Closely related to the discussion of determining the appropriate success measure is the delineation of the level of analysis. In general the levels of interest might include society, the organization, the group or the individual. Research projects can be conducted at a single level of analysis or at multiple levels [8]. Most of the studies reviewed in this paper were conducted at the individual or task level of analysis. There are the examples which consider the impact of organization variables on system success at the organizational level of analysis. This is in part due to the problem of locating organizations which are implementing systems which have an affect on the entire organization. Considering the tremendous demand on resources and researcher time involved in conducting a longitudinal study at the organizational level, this is not surprising. Such a task would require years of effort. Is the effort worth it? We think so.

4.4. Consider the Organizational Form as the Independent Variable

We are proposing that a composite measure of the various dimensions of the contextual and structural variables be developed to study implementation success. For example, Mintzberg [39] has identified five different organizational forms: the simple structure, the machine bureaucracy, the professional bureaucracy, the divisionalized form, and the adhocracy. However, a more robust scheme has been accepted by organizational theoreticians for years, namely, the organic and mechanistic typology.

These structures result from dynamic interaction between contingency variables. The mechanistic form [4] is found where the task environment is stable, the technology employed is amenable to routine processes and, the organization is old and large. Behavior is very formalized and rules are an intrinsic part of the control function in such organizations. The organic form [4] is found in dynamic, volatile environments. Tasks are typically nonroutine, requiring a great deal of expertise for completion. Organic organizations tend to be small and young. Others have referred to these organization forms using different terminology. Thompson [44], for example, refers to them as closed and open systems, while Hrebeniak [24] calls them Type A and Type Z organizations.

From the point of view of MIS implementation research the two polar forms have different information requirements, require different information structures, and react differently to change and innovation.

## 5. Conclusions

Computer systems have and will continue to play an important role in organizations. When analysts design systems they are actually prescribing facets of how organizations should be structured. Designing structures is a complex, intricate task. Literature abounds on how to analyze and design complex organizations $[15,31,28,39,26,7]$ . The integration of concepts from organizational theory with research on what leads to the successful implementation of computer-based systems will assist analysts and designers with designing future systems and structures.
