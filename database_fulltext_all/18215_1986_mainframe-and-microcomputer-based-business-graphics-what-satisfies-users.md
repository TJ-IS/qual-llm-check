# Mainframe and Microcomputer-Based Business Graphics: What Satisfies Users?

John Lehman, Jay Van Wetering and Doug Vogel
University of Minnesota, School of Management, 271 19th Avenue
South, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

This paper profiles users of computerized business graphics in 200 organizations. It identifies differences between mainframe-based and microcomputer-based users. It profiles organizational and technological environment, motivation for selection and use, functional area use, end users, organizational responsibility and support, application profiles, impediments to increased use, and forecasts for the future. It identifies determinants of user satisfaction for both hardware strategies. In so doing, it provides empirical evidence for differing strategies of MIS involvement in the development of business graphics.

Keywords: Computer graphics, business graphics, personal computers, personal computing, end-user computing, user-acceptance, success factors.

## 1. Introduction

Users of computer-based business graphics have three basic choices for hardware implementation: mainframe-based, microcomputer-based, and mixed-hardware-based. Mainframe environments provide powerful software and relative ease of integration with organization information system. Microcomputer-based business graphics provide low cost, ease of entry, ease of use, and relative independence from the corporate information systems bureaucracy. The mainframe-only and micro-only examples appear to represent relatively pure centrally driven and user driven approaches.

The proliferation of personal computers in organizations gives rise to problems with the proper role of such hardware within an organization's information system. Almost every issue of data processing journals contains at least one article on the subject; very few with an empirical basis (at least with a sample size of more than one) [1].

The proliferation of business graphics in organizations also brings with it many questions. Who uses graphics? For what purposes? What impact do graphics have on the organization's information system in terms of management, technology, or support [2]?

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Description of Survey

The survey was intended to determine the current level and type of use of business graphics. After being presented on a sample of local organizations, it was sent to selected data processing managers in the United States. Approximately 200 (25%) responded.

Fifty-seven of the respondents had a business graphics capability using mainframes only. Twenty-four had business graphis capability using microcomputers only. The remainder used mixed computing resources. Summary descriptive data independent of micro or mainframe computers has appeared in [3].

## 3. Comparisons

## 3.1. Demographics

Respondents using the three hardware strategies were quite similar: predominantly managers, directors, and vice-presidents from MIS/IS representing corporate headquarters. Most respondents came from manufacturing or financial service organizations. The microcomputer respondents, however, were more diversified across industries and more evenly balanced. There was no significant difference between microcomputer and mainframe users in terms of size of the organizations represented or number of full time employees in the MIS/DP area.

## 3.2. Technological Capability

Comparisons between microcomputer and mainframe users in terms of organizational technological capability are illustrated in Fig. 1. Both groups are reasonably similar except for significant differences in terms of availability of database technology (p = 0.044), use of very high level languages (p = 0.031) and (not expectedly!) extensive use of microcomputers (p = 0.002).

## 3.3. Selection Process

Both groups are relatively new to business graphics as summarized in Fig. 2. The microcomputer group mean of 1.75 years is, however, significantly lower (p = 0.037) than the mainframe mean of 2.65 years. ## 3.6. Organizational Responsibility and Support

For the mainframe groups, responsibility for computer graphics within the organization resides primarily in the MIS/DP and Information Center areas. For the microcomputer group, responsibility for computer graphics within the organization is generally unspecified. Over one half of the organizations in the mainframe group have one or more support staff dedicated to computer graphics. ## 4. Satisfaction

The results of this section are based on contingency table analysis. The data collected in the survey was primarily nominal level data, or grouped by category. No implicit ordering or hierarchical relationship is present in such data. As a result, the major statistical test used for analysis is the Chi-square test of statistical significance. The results reported here are accompanied by two numbers. The first number represents the actual value of the Chi-square statistic as computed. The second value is the probability that such a value of chi-square would be obtained if the variables being examined were in fact independent. Another way of viewing this probability is in terms of error. A probability of .10 represents a chance that the reported relationship does not actually exist. The significant Chi-square statistic may have been caused by sampling error.

## 4.1. Determinants of Software Satisfaction

There was no significant difference in amount of user satisfaction with business graphics software between the different hardware configuration strategies.

No significant relationships were found between software satisfaction and other variables for organizations utilizing a microcomputer-based hardware strategy.

Determinants of software satisfaction for organizations which followed mainframe hardware strategy were:

1) whether ISSCO's Tell-A-Graf was in use,

2) centralization of processing for computer graphics in the organization

3) identification of the following as impediments to business graphics usage:

\* High cost of providing computer graphics

\* Integrating graphics with other systems

The respondents were asked to identify graphics packages which they used. Tell-a-Graf was the only software identified which was significantly correlated with software satisfaction (15.947, 0031). Of the organizations which used Tell-A-Graf, only 3.5% reported any dissatisfaction with their software. It should be mentioned here that none of the authors had any affiliation with ISSCO at the time of the survey.

The respondents were asked to indicate the processing mode (centralized, decentralized, mixed) that best described computer graphics in their organization. Where processing was centralized, 68% of the users were at least somewhat satisfied with the software. Decentralized mainframe environments showed a greater percentage of dissatisfaction with 43% of the respondents stating that they were at least somewhat dissatisfied.

The respondents were asked to indicate impediments to increased use of computer graphics in their organization. Four impediments were found significant with respect to software satisfaction: the high cost of providing computer graphics, the complexity of hardware/software selection process, a lack of support of computer graphics by the DP area, and difficulties in integrating graphics with other systems.

The high cost of providing computer graphics (7.75, 0.1009) is a significant determinant of satisfaction. 73% of respondents who did not perceive cost as an impediment for increased graphics usage were satisfied with their graphics software selection. Of those who did perceive cost as an impediment for increased graphics usage, only 42% were satisfied with their graphics software selection.

Sixty percent of the respondents perceived integrating graphics with other systems (9.61203, 0.0475) as an impediment to increased graphics usage.

Overall, the data reflecting software satisfaction for users in a mainframe environment show uniformly high satisfaction. Of the 56 respondents in this subgrouping, only 25% expressed any dissatisfaction at all with the software available. This suggests that other factors may be operating in a mainframe environment which have not been exposed by this survey. Possible candidates are response time, quality of graphical output, and ability to change/manipulate variables for graphical representation.

## 4.2. Determinants of Hardware Satisfaction

User satisfaction with business graphics hardware showed no significant difference between the different hardware configuration strategies.

The determinants of hardware satisfaction for organizations which utilized only microcomputers for business graphics were:

1) Use of graphics as a replacement or supplement for standard up reports

2) The availability of development assistance in the organization

3) The physical arrangement of graphics hardware devices in the organization

As the use of graphics as a replacement or supplement for standard DP reports (22.23, 0.1019) increased in ranking, so did the user's perception of hardware satisfaction. This may indicate that many users of microcomputers use them out of dissatisfaction with existing MIS facilities.

The availability of development assistance in the organization had a positive influence on perceived satisfaction for hardware (9.16, 0.0272). Two-thirds of the respondents whose organization provided development assistance were satisfied with the graphics hardware. By contrast, somewhat more than half of the respondents whose organization did not provide development assistance were satisfied with the graphics hardware.

The arrangement of the graphics hardware devices also contributed significantly to hardware satisfaction (12.305, 0.0555). However, the results are counter intuitive: satisfaction with the hardware increased with fewer devices and centralized locations. This may indicate difficulties with installation, maintenance, and control.

The determinants of hardware satisfaction for organizations which utilized mainframe processing only in their information systems were:

1) The perceived technical difficulties of providing graphics to users

2) The use of graphics for the informal communication of ideas

3) The availability of developmental assistance for graphics users

The technical ability to provide graphics to users is not a widespread impediment to increased use of computer graphics in a mainframe environment (15.947, 0.0031). Whether or not it is considered an impediment is significantly related to hardware satisfaction. Almost ninety percent of the respondents did not see it as an impediment to increased graphics usage. Of these, only 14.2% expressed any dissatisfaction with the hardware. The data for respondents who did consider technical difficulties of providing graphics to users as an impediment to increased graphics usage show 50% dissatisfied with the hardware in some degree.

As the importance of use of graphics for the informal communication of ideas increased, so did the satisfaction with the graphics hardware (35.322, 0.0185).

The availability of development assistance is associated with higher satisfaction with graphics hardware (9.341, 0.0531). Only twenty-six percent of the respondents who had development assistance for graphics expressed any dissatisfaction with graphics hardware.

## 5. Conclusions

At this time, microcomputer-based business graphics and mainframe-based graphics are not just technological alternatives. They are not integrated into existing information system to the same degree and their users report different advantages and disadvantages which mirror the distinctions between MIS-oriented and user-oriented systems or between organizational and personal computing [4]. We suspect that these distinctions are a transitory accident based on the way in which each type of system was introduced.

We expect that future studies would show fewer micro- or mainframe-only systems and increased integration with data exchange between mainframes and microcomputers. Mainframes and micros can expect to coexist and mutually interact, integrated within ever larger networks in which business graphics play a key communications role.

There are three major results of this analysis. One is empirical confirmation of the importance of information centers. The second is the existence of a relationship between hardware configuration strategy and user satisfaction with business graphics. The third is the existence of organizational strategies to maximize user satisfaction with each hardware strategy.

Information centers are an approach by MIS departments to facilitate end user computing. They may consist of micro labs, or some combination of micro and mainframe oriented tools. The need is illustrated by the apparent use of microcomputers to bypass centralized MIS organizations. Centralized support for user computer use is significant in terms of user satisfaction. Microcomputer users were shown to be more satisfied with central provision of facilitates. Users of mainframe hardware strategies were more satisfied when there was training provided. Both micro and mainframe users were more satisfied when there was development assistance available.

The relationship between hardware configuration strategy and user satisfaction appears to be complicated by organizational process; it is not clear which strategy produces higher satisfaction. However, users of different strategies have different profiles in terms of what contributes to their satisfaction.

Organizational strategies to maximize user satisfaction with each hardware strategy can be derived from this study. For a microcomputer based strategy, the important items are: the availability of development assistance in the organization and centralized physical arrangement of graphics hardware devices in the organization. For a mainframe only strategy, the important items are: software selection, provision of as many workstations as possible, support by central DP, developmental assistance, and integration with other systems. In addition, high costs and perceived technical difficulties are common pitfalls.
