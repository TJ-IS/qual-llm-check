# Acceptance of Management Science Recommendations: The Role of Cognitive Styles and Dogmatism

G. Chandrasekaran

310F Jacobs Management Center, School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA; tel.: (716) 636-3253

and

Peter J. Kirs

Canisius College, Buffalo, NY 14208, USA

The results of studies concerning the effect of personality variables on the successful implementation of management science recommendations have been equivocal. Although several studies in the past have shown significant effect of personality variables, recently a few studies have shown that lack of any impact due to these variables. Very few studies have considered the effect of dogmatism which has been shown in psychological literature as a powerful restrictor in entertaining new ideas and suggestions for decision making. Our study examines the effect of dogmatism and cognitive styles in choosing management science recommendations. The results show the need for special efforts required in choosing report format and style to suit different categories of individuals with varying cognitive skills and level of open-mindedness. Even subtle variations seem to produce the difference between success and failure of implementation.

Keywords: Implementation, Personality variables, Dogmatism, Cognitive Styles

There is a substantial body of literature in the MIS area which has addressed the issue of implementation problems (Harvey [4], Rubenstein et al. [11], Radnor et al. [8], and others) and personality variables for MIS success (see Zmud [14] for a thorough review of articles in the field). These groups of variables have been identified as important for implementation success: (a) organization related variables, (b) problem or task related variables, and (c) the personality variables of the manager and the MIS personnel. Although, recently a few researchers have questioned the usefulness of personality variables such as cognitive style in MIS research (Huber, [5]), equally powerful arguments have been made in supporting using such personality variables in MIS research (Robey, [9]). This paper is concerned with the personality variables which affect implementation success, especially cognitive style, which has been most often considered, and dogmatism, which has been examined to a much lesser degree.

The management scientist must also reciprocate by accommodating the heuristic cognitive style of the manager (A/H). The overall rejection rate of the management science reports in 64% for the high-analytic student group and 66.7% for the high analytic manager group. The low-analytic manager group had a similar rejection rate of 62.5%. The major deviation from this pattern seems to be within the low-analytic student group, which had a rejection rate of 35.7%. Generally, it would appear that irrespective of the cognitive style and the type of report, approximately one out of two of the students and two out of three of the managers rejected the management science reports.

It would appear than that an investigation of this rejection pattern might lead to a better understanding of the characteristics of the management science report and subsequently increase its appeal to managers.

Dogmatism has been viewed as a primary variable by Rokeach [10] in the adoption behavior - adoption of new products or new ideas. Dogmatism is the degree of open $\sim$ vs closed-mindedness to information or situation a person exhibits. A relatively dogmatic individual can ignore irrelevant aspects or information and take the relevant materials on its own merits. A highly dogmatic person, however, tends to be closed minded. It has been argued in the psychological literature that dogmatic mental systems represent a cognitive psychodynamic network of defenses against anxiety created by new products or new information. The psychologists, therefore, argue that the more persistently anxious or threatened the individual the more the individual manifests a closed mind. Schroder et al. [12] have noted this concept of dogmatism is also related to the integrative complexity in the decision process. They contend that in a not too complex decision making system the effect of dogmatism is bound to be even stronger.

One of the major applications of the dogmatism concept to decision making is in the study by Long and Ziller [7]. They use dogmatism as a factor in determining the extent of information search for decision making. They hypothesized a negative relationship between dogmatism and predecisional information search; their results confirmed the hypothesis with a very strong negative relationship. Consequently, they have concluded:

1. In decision-making situations, the nondogmatic person tends to delay decision or reserve judgment, and to search for and utilize additional information, whereas the dogmatic individual maintains the conceptual systems by limiting the intake of information.

2. The dogmatic individual defers an insecure self-structure by the expedient restricting of information input: that is, by controlling the source of data relevant to oneself and one's social conceptual structure.

3. Recognition of the existence of a problem is a necessary precondition for predecisional information search. The decision maker needs to accept the paucity of information and hence reserves opinion as a precondition to search for new information. Dogmatic individuals either fail to perceive or ignore the inadequacy of the environmental evidence.

Brightman and Urban [1] compare information processing strategies between high and low dogmatic subjects in the face of predetermined information per trial. The experiment is designed to parallel a Bayesian Information Processor with ambiguous and unambiguous sequences of events at each trial. The unambiguous sequence of events has more information content in the outcome of the trial; that is, if a red chip is pulled out of a bag containing a predominance of red chips, the conclusion could be that the bag is full of red chips. This situation cannot be assured in the case where the outcome is ambiguous. Their study reveals significant differences in the information processing strategies of high and low dogmatics in the ambiguous sequence of events since “high dogmatics tend to deal less effectively with ambiguity than low dogmatics.”

## 3. A Conceptual Model

These empirical studies indicate that dogmatism is a critical personality-variable affecting individual choice processes. The close-mindedness of the dogmatic individual may lead to rejection of additional or new information which could have helped in reaching a decision. Such an individual, however, perceives such information as a threat to the belief structure and hence has a tendency to reject it outright. The same logic may extend to acceptance of management science reports, regardless of their manner of presentation. Since this report may represent an intrusion into the belief structure, it is probable that the dogmatic personality will reject reports more readily than the low dogmatic personality. Our model has three innovations over the older one. First, it sees dogmatism as a perceptual filter where report rejection might take place because the personality traits of the very dogmatic person are not those needed for unbiased examination of any recommendations. People who are dogmatic reject recommendations in spite of their cognitive styles, type of report, or level of agreement.

We hypothesize:

HI: The rejection rate of management science reports will be significantly higher in the high dogmatic group vs. the low dogmatic group in a. the aggregate level.

b. each cognitive style group (analytic and heuristic),

c. each report type (analytic / heuristic),

d. all of the above, even when the subjects tend to agree with the conclusion.

Secondly, it seems that personality variables generally lead to agreement with the conclusions of the report. The acceptance of the recommendations for implementation may depend upon many conditions such as need for an expedient solution, top management pressure, etc. In other words agreement may be a precondition only when subjects are highly dogmatic, since the slightly or non-dogmatic individual may be flexible in experimenting with change. Consequently, the percentage of acceptances when subjects disagree with the conclusions may be significantly higher in low dogmatics compared to high dogmatics. This leads to:

H2: The number of individuals who accept management science recommendations even though they do not agree with the conclusion will be higher in the case of low dogmatics than in the case of high dogmatics.

Thirdly, this model visualizes a number of possible schemes in which acceptance of recommendations may take place. As outlined earlier, high dogmatics are likely to follow two strategies: (a) reject reports outright, and (b) disagree with conclusions and consequently reject reports. This implies that the rejection rate for high dogmatics is independent of cognitive style and report style. However, in the case of low dogmatics, cognitive style and report style may be a significant factor. Specifically, following Doktor and Hamilton's conclusions, the heuristic individual tends to agree with and accept recommendations if the report is analytic in nature.

H3: The rejection rate for high dogmatics will be the same for

1. analytic and heuristic individuals,

2. between those who received the analytic and general reports.

This paper describes an experimental study developed to test each of the above stated hypotheses.

## 4. Experimental Study

This study was designed to evaluate the extent of agreement with the report conclusions and acceptance of reports based upon two different styles of reports and the individual personality variables of dogmatism and cognitive style. The experiment was conducted using two MBA classes where students were taking a compulsory capstone course in planning systems. One section contained students who have had at least limited managerial experience. Furthermore, because of the nature of the course, the subjects were accustomed to receiving analytic reports for decision making.

The subjects were administered the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (Form G) to assess their cognitive complexity, and the Rokeach Dogmatism Scale (Form E) to determine extent of dogmatism. Once these personality inventories were completed the subjects were asked to read a simple business case and to assume responsibility for the outcome. To assist them in their decision, they were given, at random, either a general or an analytic consultant's report. At the end of the session, they were questioned about their agreement with the report, their acceptance of the recommendations for implementation, and their perception of whether the report they received was general or analytic in nature. The last question served to validate our perception of the reports distributed to the subjects. Additionally, a pretest was conducted where experienced evaluators were given both types of reports and asked to identify the differences between the two reports on a five-point Likert scale. A reasonable congruence was found between their analysis and our classification. In addition, in order to avoid necessarily equating analysis and quantitativeness, attention was given to choice of non-mathematical model oriented situations in the design of the cases and reports.

## 5. Results

The primary focus of this study is to evaluate the effects of a dogmatic personality in acceptance of management science recommendations, and also to evaluate its role along with cognitive style. Therefore, as a first step, a comparison was made between the rejection rate for high and low dogmatic personality groups. High dogmatics demonstrated a much higher rate (27%) of total rejection (five on a five point scale) than did low dogmatics (4.4%). This would seem to indicate that not only are high dogmatics individuals more closed to suggestions or new information, they also tend to be unequivocal in their decisions regarding these reports. Hence, it would appear that increased acceptance of management science recommendations by this group must be achieved through attitudinal changes.

As suggested by hypothesis 1, the rejection of recommendations in the high dogmatic group tends to be significantly higher than in the low dogmatic group.

As Table 4 indicates, a higher percentage of subjects who are high dogmatics, reject management science recommendations (a) irrespective of their cognitive style or the style of the report they received (in the aggregate). (b) within each of the cognitive style categories, and (c) within each of the report types. In other words, the acceptance or rejection of reports within this group cannot necessarily be improved by modifying the style of presentation. An analysis of variance (performed since the subjects were requested to accept or reject on a five point scale) indicates that the mean rating of the recommendations was 1.73 for the low dogmatics vs. 2.14 for the high dogmatic group. As shown in table 5, this difference is statistically significant at about the 3.5% level. Both the percentages and the statistical results seem to emphasize the significance of dogmatism as a variable in report acceptance.

Although the sample size is inadequate to perform any elaborate statistical analysis, the direction clearly indicates the need to develop implementation oriented reports in a style which suits the basic personality traits of the manager.

## 6. Conclusions

As many empirical studies have pointed out, personality variables play a pivotal role in the successful implementation of management science reports. This study contends that dogmatism acts as a perceptual filter through which implementation oriented reports may be rejected outright due to the close-mindedness of the manager. In addition to the above described effects of dogmatism, a two-way ANOVA for acceptance rating with dogmatism and cognitive style shows the effect of dogmatism overshadowing the effect, if any, of cognitive style. The conclusion of this study, therefore, suggests inclusion of personality variables such as dogmatism for explaining implementation success.
