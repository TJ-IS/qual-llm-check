# Issues in Information Systems Planning \*

Albert L. Lederer

Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA; tel.: (412) 648-1614

and

Aubrey L. Mendelow

School of Business & Administration, Duquesne University, Pittsburgh, PA 15282, USA; tel.: (412) 434-5153

Numerous difficulties face information systems managers as they develop IS plans. Three nominal group technique sessions used IS practitioners from different levels of management to identify specific difficulties. The findings indicate a wide variety of problems faced by these managers. Top IS managers were concerned most with learning the objectives of top general management. Middle IS managers were concerned most with adopting a methodology for planning. Operating IS managers were concerned most with understanding the perceived needs of the user. These results suggest the need for more sophisticated management techniques (or the better use of existing techniques) and the need for research to address the development, utilization and evaluation of such techniques.

Keywords: Information systems planning, management of information systems.

\* The authors contributed equally to the design and execution of this research project and paper.

Delta Airlines posted a loss of \$86.7 million for the year ending June 1983, simply because the company was “slow in recognizing the importance of computers to keep tabs on ticket prices in different markets” [7]. Otis Elevator put terminals into architects’ offices to enable it to participate in the design process and as a result picked up market share [24]. These are but two examples from a growing list of firms using computers and information systems to strategic advantage. Such strategic applications do not merely happen. They must be formally planned, their implementation carefully considered and their effect meticulously monitored to ensure that the desired benefits are achieved. With the very existence of the organization possibly at stake, it has become crucial to develop formal plans for the effective deployment of the information resource. However, recent research has shown that IS professionals consider improved information systems planning as the major issue of the decade [11]. This paper reports a study which examined the difficulties faced by IS professionals as they develop their plans.

## 1. Introduction

Albert L. Lederer is on the faculty of the Graduate School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. His PhD is in Industrial & Systems Engineering from the Ohio State University. Dr. Lederer spent over ten years in technical and managerial positions in industry in information systems. His research interests include the planning and implementation of management information systems.

## 1.1. Benefits of Information Systems Planning

The emergence of information systems planning (ISP) as an increasingly important issue in the information systems field has been recognized by both practitioners and academics. For example, an A.T. Kearney study reported that companies with integrated business and IS strategic plans financially outperformed those without integrated plans by a factor of six to one [3]. The authors explained that the existence of an IS plan shows that the development of information systems encourages change; information systems do not merely react to change. In addition, it has been suggested that a strategic plan for IS improves corporate productivity by reducing costs and increasing both control and revenue [16]. These views confirmed an earlier study which showed that 16 of 18 successful computer users (out of a sample of 36 users) had formalized plans for their EDP activities [21].

## 1.2. Making plans is not sufficient

However, the existence of formal information systems plans is insufficient. The plans must be implemented. To this end, McLean and Soden recognized the need for three distinctly different types of plans – strategic, management and operational – in the development of their planning model shown in Figure 1 [22]. This model served as a basis for the current study.

There is considerable usefulness of categorizing an organization's plans into three types. Strategic plans are concerned with the objectives of the organization, the resources used in achieving these objectives and the policies and guidelines which govern the acquisition use and disposition of these resources [2]. Management planning concerns the effective acquisition, use and disposition of the organization's resources while operational planning entails using the resources effectively and efficiently [2].

The McLean & Soden model shown in Figure 1 comprises eight tasks. The first six can be categorized as part of the generally recognized strategic planning process. Indeed strategic planning includes the use of information about the past, current and projected performance to enable management to determine company missions, basic purposes, objectives, policies and program strategies [27]. The management planning feature of the model is indicated by the block titled Medium Range MIS Planning (managerial). This planning phase deals with such concerns as determining priorities, projecting equipment acquisition and forecasting resource levels [22]; this fits the previous definition of managerial planning. Finally, operational planning (as indicated by the Short Range MIS Planning block in the model) involves developing schedules and timetables. These are necessary to plan individual work and procedures in order to achieve efficiency.

In 1969, Blumenthal indicated the desirability of a formalized planning process [6]. In the ensuring years numerous authors pointed out the necessary features of a formalized ISP. These have included:

● the need to link the ISP to the overall plans of the organization to ensure a consistency of IS goals with those of the organization [20].

\- the need to be able to alter the ISP in response to the organization's strategy [18], and

\- the need to acknowledge the potential strategic role of IS in the organization's strategic planning process so that this role could be incorporated into the organization's strategy [5,15,30,25].

Despite these and other prescriptions, a 1982 study found that the MIS long range plan itself, together with its integration into the business plan was a top priority with both professionals and academics [4]. Two years later a sample drawn from eight industries re-confirmed this [11]. Given these concerns, the current study was designed to identify the issues facing each level of information systems management as they seek to develop and implement information systems plans within their organizations.

## 2. Methodology

The lack of previous research formally identifying the difficulties of ISP and the likelihood that such difficulties are not easily quantifiable suggested the need to adopt a qualitative research methodology. In addition, an interactive approach to data gathering seemed applicable. Accordingly, the nominal group technique (NGT) [10] was adapted for the project and tested in a pilot study with six doctoral students. The NGT involved six phases; see Appendix 1 for its details.

The NGT presents several advantages to the researcher. It offers a well tested procedure which in one study was shown to be more effective at producing ideas than either the Delphi technique or discussion groups [29]. In addition, the nominal group technique has been used in a wide variety of contexts related to different types of planning and control. These contexts range from the uncovering of productivity problems [23] [operational control] to the identification of consumer perceptions of major pre-search problems [9] [management planning and control] to the development of corporate mission statements [18] [strategic planning]. The use of the NGT at all levels of planning and control confirmed that the NGT was suitable for use in this project because all levels of planning and control within the IS department were to be considered. Finally, the NGT requires little care on the part of the researchers to ensure that the participants are not influenced by the researchers' biases.

The NGT also has advantages to the participants. The opportunity to interact with peers regarding a topic of critical importance (such as ISP) is an attraction to professionals. Moreover, the subjects receive immediate feedback on their own ideas and those of the other participants. Finally, the NGT may offer the participant a new and interesting means of group decision making.

## 2.1. Research Design

McLean & Soden's planning model clearly delineates three levels of planning – strategic, managerial and operational. The prime responsibility for each type of planning rests with different levels of management. Strategic planning is the prime responsibility of top management, managerial planning and control rests with middle management, and operational planning and control is the responsibility of operational management. Therefore, it was decided to run three separate NGT sessions to ensure that adequate attention could be devoted to the planning concerns of each managerial level.

The researchers assigned the subjects to the groups during phone conversations as the participants identified their job titles and reporting relationships. Thus, managers whose primary focus dealt with project responsibilities (presumably short range) such as the development of single application schedules, manpower budgets and timetables along with general supervision were designated as operating managers. The middle management group was constituted from managers whose planning responsibility related to medium range MIS planning. These managers typically had project managers reporting to them and in turn they themselves reported to an executive with ultimate authority for the IS function in the organization and were designated as middle managers. Finally, the top management group comprised those who hold ultimate authority for the IS function in their organizations while still within the IS department. Thus three NGT sessions were held – one for each management level.

## 2.2. Sample Selection

Since the participants would have to assemble for a two hour session, they would need to come from the local business community. Therefore, members of the local chapter of an international information systems management association were informed of the project by letter. Letters were sent to members with managerial job titles. Subsequently, the researchers phoned potential participants to assure a greater likelihood of their attendance and to assess their managerial level for assignment to the appropriate NGT session. Likewise the researchers took care to insure that only one person would represent each organization during any given session. This assured that all participants could express themselves freely. In all, out of 51 initial phone contacts a total of twenty-four managers travelled distances of up to fifty miles to attend the sessions. The high proportion of participation substantiates the significance of the ISP problem. The composition and representation of each session appear in Table 1.

## 3. Results

Each NGT session produced a ranked list of the aspects of ISP which participants considered most difficult. The aspects for each group appear in Tables 3 through 5. The text of each aspect is a transcription from the original NGT flip charts and includes only minor grammatical improvements. Because the NGT required each participant to rank eight aspects, only the final top eight ranked items are reported. All aspects tying for the least difficult rank are reported; this accounts for the fact that 10 aspects are reported for the top management group. The mean rank (the sum of the ranks divided by the number of participants in the group) indicates the degree of agreement of the individual participants as to their perception of the most difficult aspect of IS planning.

In many instances, the aspects of ISP which each group found to be the most difficult bore some similarity. In order to compare the difficulties expressed by each group, the contents of Tables 3 through 5 were summarized into Table 6 to portray an overall picture. The key words of the aspects determined the basis of the summary and care was taken not to interpret any of the ideas too liberally. Nevertheless, the researchers acknowledge the subjectivity of the classification. While this may provide some useful insights, the reader is cautioned against drawing the conclusion that a given group perceives more difficulty with an aspect than another group does.

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Common concerns

Four broad concerns recurred in each of the three groups. The need to understand the requirements of the user was viewed as the most difficult aspect of ISP facing operating managers, while middle and top managers ranked it third and eighth respectively. This finding corresponds to general expectations because lower IS management levels probably interact more with users on a day-to-day basis developing and maintaining systems. Thus, as managers become further removed from this day-to-day interaction, other aspects of ISP may intrude more and appear more difficult. This progression is expected as the concerns of managers tend to take an increasingly global character as they rise in the organizational hierarchy [26]. Perhaps it is more interesting that the top managers recognized this problem at all.

Project prioritization was the second recurring concern in each of the groups. However there may be a relationship between management level and the type of prioritization which causes concern. Top management in this research viewed prioritization as a problem of coordination with the business plan of the organization. Middle managers and operating managers took a distinctly different view and focused on the criteria for prioritization. Thus it is evident that prioritization is both an important and a multi-faceted problem.

Environmental impact was the third recurring concern of each group of participants. Superficially, it may be surprising that the impact of the environment would concern operating management. Generally this level of management receives considerable buffering against the environment [28]. Because IS operating managers considered the impact of the environment as a difficulty, it appears that this buffering does not take place. The operating managers cited changes in government regulation and moves by competitors as examples. Hence the tasks of IS operating managers clearly are different from those of conventional operating managers.

The impact of changing technology was the fourth common concern of the three managerial groups. Operating managers wrestle with the immediate problems created by the introduction of new technology. ## 4.2. Enduring issues

It is rather striking that many of the issues uncovered in this study are not new. Indeed tools and techniques have been developed to overcome many of the problems expressed by the participants. Some examples include

\- structured analysis and design methodologies to formalize systems specifications to reduce uncertainty about users' needs [12],

\- prototyping to help understand the needs of the user [13],

\- Business Systems Planning and similar methodologies to help identify top management's objectives [14],

● data dictionaries to show the contents of existing systems to ease change and to plan the design and implementation of new systems [1], and

● application packages to speed implementation and reduce environmental impact [19].

Indeed, the list of techniques, methodologies and even software products which have come into use in recent years for simplifying systems planning, implementation and management is already very long and growing rapidly. Despite these advances, many of the issues mentioned by participants in this research closely resemble those considered when Blumenthal first identified the need for ISP in 1969.

These problems have continued to concern IS managers for various reasons. Speculation suggests that the accelerating demand for computing services in recent years has resulted in users with greater expectations and less understanding of the requirement for IS developers to learn their needs. Similarly today's growing reliance of organizations on computing may have increased the pressures on the prioritization process, on the need to understand top management's objectives, and on the impact of such environmental changes as government and competitor's actions. Finally, advances in hardware and software may have merely created more alternatives and thus complicated decision making. Regardless of the reasons, this research has shown that today's technologies for simplifying ISP have not eliminated the long-standing problems of ISP.

## 5. Conclusions

This study makes a first attempt at the systematic identification and classification of information systems planning difficulties in order to help improve our understanding of the management of information systems. Furthermore, the study has made several contributions to research in general and to the field of ISP in particular. The usefulness of the NGT has been demonstrated once again in a research setting. More importantly, the research has implications for both practitioners and researchers.

## 5.1. Implications for practitioners

The difficult aspects of ISP which surfaced in this study indicate the need for more sophisticated techniques in several areas. For example, prioritization techniques clearly need improvement. Likewise, new techniques for predicting the benefits of prospective projects should be sought. Improved communication with top general management would also be valuable in the planning process. The effective application of a good planning methodology might include these improvements; however, the participants in this research asserted that adopting such a methodology is a difficult task.

Furthermore, the study suggests the employment of buffering techniques to shield IS from the impacts of the environment. Success in this area might be achieved in diverse ways. Traditional buffering techniques such as application packages and use of contract personnel for peak periods might have to give way to new, technologically oriented ones. Software engineering techniques such as structured programming have long been advocated to address this problem but have not been completely effective. Additionally, non-technological methods could help achieve buffering. For example, increasing general management's awareness of the impacts of their plans on priorities and proposed systems could help protect IS from the effects of unnecessary changes.

Finally, the results offer practitioners immediate benefits. Practitioners could use the lists of issues to identify the extent to which they, too, are plagued by these concerns. We suggest that IS executives review the Operating Management Results (Table 5) with their project managers and senior analysts and review the Middle Management Group Results (Table 4) with their systems and area managers. IS executives should personally review the Top Management Results (Table 3). The reviews would help IS management determine the extent to which their organizations suffer from the problems and the efforts that they might apply to rectify the situation. This would help to pinpoint the severest problems and the greatest payoffs. Solutions may then be sought through further managerial and technological approaches.

## 5.2. Implications for researchers

This study has pointed the way for additional, meaningful research projects to address the concerns of practicing IS managers. For the first time the issues of ISP in the 1980s have been identified as a group. This is crucial to enable researchers to begin to understand the total ISP environment and to find the means to solve these issues. Specifically, this study points to the need to address such relevant problems as the identification of user needs, the development of prioritization techniques, the development of techniques for buffering the operational levels of IS from environmental impact, the development of an ISP methodology with the ability to communicate easily with general management, and finally, the identification of effective resource allocation techniques.

## Appendix 1

Steps in the Nominal Group Process

After an introduction detailing the purpose of the project and an overview of the steps in the NGT (phase 1), the participants silently generated their written responses to the carefully worded NGT problem (phase 2). For this project the participants addressed the following question: "What aspects of Information Systems Planning do you find the most difficult?" After the 10 minutes, each participant presented an idea on a round robin basis without discussion (phase 3). The ideas were recorded on a flip chart. After all the ideas had been recorded, the participants discussed and clarified the ideas;

some ideas were either combined, omitted or added at the group's request (phase 4). During phase 5 the participants silently and independently wrote the eight most difficult planning aspects from the flip chart onto $3'' \times 5''$ cards. Then they assigned the most difficult aspect a rank of 8. The least difficult aspect was then ranked 1. The next most difficult aspect was ranked 7. The alternate ranking process continued in this manner until each participant had used the numbers 1 through 8. This ranking system highlights the easiest and most difficult aspects [23]. The rankings by each participant were then totalled for each idea. The two hour session concluded with a brief discussion of the results with the participants (phase 6).
