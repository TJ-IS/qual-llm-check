# A Survey of Information Technology in the U.K. Service Sector

Chee Sing Yap and Geoff Walsham

Management Studies Group, Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge, Mill Lane, Cambridge, CB2 1RX, UK

Relationships between the use of information technology and organizational variables have been analysed using a sample of 638 business organizations in the service sector in the U.K. Data for the study were collected through a mail survey. Among the results presented here are the variation of the proportions of managers and information workers with size of organization, and the relationship between the use of computers and profitability. Discriminant analysis was used to distinguish between organizations using computers and those not using them. The study shows that organizations using computers tend to have more formality in communications, a higher growth rate and a higher proportion of information workers than organizations not using computers. The variation of computer capacity with size of organization is also examined.

Keywords: computer users, discriminant analysis, impact on organizations, information technology, organizational variables, service sector, survey

## 1. Introduction

There has been considerable interest in the impact of information technology on organizations in recent years as its use becomes more widespread. Information technology not only plays a supportive role in the operation of many organizations today, but it has also acquired strategic significance in an increasing number of organizations. Organizations are complex systems and the impact of information technology is complicated and varies from one organization to another. In fact, many of the effects are subtle and currently in the emergent stage.

One approach to examining the impact of information technology on organizations is through the use of case studies where a small number of organizations are selected and studied in detail. The conclusions drawn from these studies are specific to the particular organizations and may not be representative. An alternative approach is to survey a representative sample of organizations and analyse the impact through statistical techniques. The results are more general and this approach was used here. Data were collected through mail questionnaires which were sent to

## 2. Previous Studies

Many articles and books have been written on the impact of information technology. For example, Grayson [9] listed some 700 references in a bibliography on the social and economic impact of new technology. Some of the more recent work, both theoretical and empirical, was collected by Forester [7]. But the literature has provided few conclusive results. In a review article, Attewell and Rule [1] concluded that the effects of computing in organizations are more complicated and diverse than has traditionally been assumed. From amongst the large literature in this area, we look briefly in this section at two previous studies, one using the survey approach whilst the other was based on case studies.

Cron and Sobol [5] studied the relationship between computerization and overall firm performance using data obtained from a survey. Their sample consisted of 138 wholesalers in the medical supply industry in the United States. They found that computerization was positively related to the overall performance of medium and larger sized firms. Firms owning computers and making extensive use of them tended to be either very high or very low performers. These results were interesting, though it was difficult to establish causality.

A different approach was adopted by Buchanan and Boddy [3]. They used the case study method to examine the experience of six companies in Scotland concerning the effects of information technology on jobs and organizations. They found that reports of a rapid and wide ranging revolution in organizational and working life were exaggerated. Contrary to popular belief, none of the six companies studied made employees redundant as a direct result of introducing information technology. On the effect of information technology on performance, they warned against generalization because different types and levels of performance measurements gave different and conflicting indications.

These two studies illustrate the use of two different approaches to studying the impact of information technology on organizations. Although there have been a number of other studies in this area, the effects of information technology on organizations are still not well understood, let alone quantified. Our own study, aimed at identifying relationships between the use of information technology and organizational variables, was an attempt to provide further insight into this subject.

## 3. Design of the Study

The study focussed on the service sector of the economy which is undergoing rapid changes as a direct result of the information revolution $[2,4,8]$ . The sectors studied included Divisions 6, 7, 8 and part of Division 9 of the 1980 Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) for the United Kingdom. The actual grouping used for the survey was a variant of the official classification and consisted of five sectors:

1. wholesale distribution,

2. retail distribution,

3. transport and communication,

4. business and financial services, and

5. miscellaneous services including hotels and catering, repairs, and recreational services.

The sample was further stratified into three size groups on the basis of turnover, namely organizations with an annual turnover of less than £ 1 million, between £ 1 million and £ 5 million, and more than £ 5 million (see Table 1). The actual sample size for each of the fifteen strata was calculated from published statistics on numbers in each stratum, with more weight being given to larger organizations than a proportional allocation.

The questionnaire was designed after two pilot tests. The first involved personal interviews with senior managers from six local organizations, and the second was a mail survey aimed at 100 organizations in the U.K. The questionnaire was divided into two sections: the first section contained questions related to organizational variables whereas the second was devoted to questions on the use of information technology. We suggested in our personalized covering letter that the Managing Director or another senior manager would be the most appropriate respondent to complete the first section, whilst the second section could be best answered by a senior staff member with responsibilities in areas related to information technology. In both the design and implementation stages of the survey, many of the recommendations made by Dillman [6] were followed. For example, the questionnaire was produced in an A4 booklet format, a business reply envelope was enclosed with the questionnaire, and first class postage was used throughout.

The main survey was carried out in November 1984. The questionnaires were sent to the Managing Director of 3000 randomly selected organizations in the service sector in the U.K. The names and addresses of the organizations together with the names of the Managing Directors were obtained from a commercial agency. As suggested by Dillman, a follow-up postcard was sent to each of the organizations eight days after the questionnaires were sent out. A total of 695 completed questionnaires were received, representing a response rate of just over 23%. This compares favourably with other recent surveys on the use of computers in the U.K. [10,11,15], particularly since our sample was directed at organizations using computers as well as those not using computers. After eliminating organizations whose main activities were found to be outside the scope of the study, a final sample size of 638 was used in the analysis. The distribution of the sample by sector and turnover is shown in Table 1. No strong bias by size was detected in the response, but the miscellaneous services sector was slightly underrepresented. Another source of bias, inherent in any survey of this nature, is likely to be a higher response rate from users of computers and advanced information technology. While this could not be eliminated completely, every effort was made throughout the course of the survey to encourage non-users to respond. A summary report presenting some findings of the survey was produced and sent to the participating organizations [17].

## 4. Survey Results

We present and discuss a selection of the findings of the survey in this section. Section 4.1 is concerned with the variation of the proportions of managers and information workers with size. The remaining sections deal with results related to the use of computers, though the survey also covered the use of other information technology including telecommunications and word processing.

## 4.1. Managers and Information Workers

The workforce of an organization was divided for our study into six broad categories: managers, professionals, secretarial workers, clerical workers, technical support staff and all other staff. There are several detailed definitions of information workers in the literature [see, for example, 13, 14], but we chose to define information workers simply as secretarial and clerical workers. We also defined knowledge workers as managers and professionals, and materials workers as technical support staff and all other staff.

Table 2 shows the variation of the proportions of managers and information workers with the size of an organization. The figures shown are for the whole sample and included organizations both using and not using computers. Also shown in the table are the corresponding figures obtained from a U.S. study quoted by Strassman [16] in a paper concerning the use of information technology and productivity. The service sector of the U.K. economy is made up of mainly small and medium sized organizations and thus our sample contained more small and medium sized organizations than large ones. As such, figures for large organizations should be interpreted with caution.

Our data show that the proportion of managers decreases as the size of an organization increases. This result is consistent with that quoted by Strassman. Our survey also shows that the number of information workers in an organization is directly proportional to the size of the organization. For organizations with up to 500 employees, approximately 30% of the total number of people employed are information workers. This result is, however, not in agreement with the figures quoted by Strassman which show a consistent decrease for the proportion of information workers as the size of an organization increases. We can offer no definite explanation for the discrepancy. One reason could be that the definitions of information workers were different, though it was unclear from his published work which definition was used by Strassman. Also, the statistics compared were obtained from two different economies, namely the U.S.A. and the U.K. While our data were statistically valid for organizations with up to 500 employees, larger samples would be necessary before one could confirm the trend for larger organizations.

## 4.2. Manpower Profiles for Computer Users and Non-Users

Manpower profiles of organizations using and not using computers were analysed. The results, aggregated under knowledge, information and materials workers, are shown in Table 3. The comparison was limited to the smaller organizations since there were very few large organizations which were not using computers.

The trends are clear. Organizations using computers have a significantly higher proportion of information workers than organizations of a similar size which are non-users. About one third of the workforce of computer users were information workers, compared with less than one quarter for non-users. Further examination showed that the differences were mainly due to clerical workers rather than secretarial workers. Conversely, organizations not using computers had a much higher proportion of materials workers than organizations using computers. The proportion of knowledge workers was also significantly higher for computer users, with the exception of very small companies. To filter out the effects of sector, similar analyses were performed for each of the five sectors. The broad patterns described above were similar in each case. As the results for knowledge, information and materials workers are correlated, we discuss only the result for information workers.

The results could be interpreted in a number of ways. One view is that organizations increase the number of information workers, particularly clerical workers, as a result of computerization. When computers are used, more information workers are required not only to process existing paper-based information into suitable formats for transfer into computerized files, but also to process more information arising from technology induced demand. For example, when computers are available, more calculations and analyses are done to answer all sorts of “what if” questions. Porat [13] went even further to suggest that computers and communication technology encouraged the growth of bureaucracies and thus created jobs for low productivity information workers. The higher percentage of information workers observed for computer users does not necessarily imply an increase in the size of the total workforce. It could be the result of a relative increase in information workers due to a reduction in materials workers, which could be achieved through redundancies, natural wastage or re-training into information workers. The scope for this type of labour saving in the service sector is probably more limited than in the manufacturing sector.

A second possible interpretation is that those organizations which are using computers have always had a higher proportion of information workers. This assumes that some organizations are more information intensive and have more information workers, even in the same industrial sector. Because of the nature of their business, these information intensive organizations are more likely to use computers. It is implied that the higher percentage of information workers is a cause for the use of computers rather than a result of using computers.

Unfortunately it is not possible to establish causal relationships from survey data. Our first interpretation is consistent with the fact that the proportion of the workforce engaged in information activities in the economy as a whole has increased rapidly over the last few decades $[12,13]$ . It is probably also true that organizations which are information intensive are more likely to use computers than organizations which are less information intensive.

## 4.3. Use of Computers and Profitability

The profitability of an organization was measured by the return on capital employed, defined as profit before interest and tax divided by net assets. As profitability varied considerably from one sector to another, it was necessary to have separate analysis for each of the five sectors surveyed. Within each sector, organizations were classified into four profitability bands for both computer users and non-users. The numbers obtained in each band were compared to the values expected if there were no relationships between the use of computers and profitability. The Chi-squared statistic was used to test for statistical significance. The null hypothesis was that the distribution of profitability levels was the same for all organizations. The analyses showed that none of the test statistics was significant and hence the null hypothesis could not be rejected. Our survey data did not provide evidence to support the view that the use of computers improves profitability.

As our sample consisted of mainly small and medium sized organizations, this result was, to some extent, consistent with those of Cron and Sobol [5]; they found that computerization was related to the overall performance of medium and larger sized firms but not for small ones. It was suggested that the use of computers increased the total expenses of small firms and hence the potential for cost savings was limited.

We believe that the use of computers can often improve the performance of organizations, but the improvement may not necessarily be reflected on a single aggregate measure, such as return on capital employed. It is likely that a different measure might have given a different result, as found by Buchanan and Boddy [3].

## 4.4. Discriminant Analysis

The multivariate statistical technique known as discriminant analysis was used to distinguish organizations using computers from those not using them. In a two group discrimination, as in this case, the technique weights and combines some possible discriminating variables into a discriminant function that differentiates the two groups as much as possible. This is achieved by maximising the between-group variance relative to the within-group variance. The discriminant function derived is a linear combination of the discriminating variables and takes the form:

$$
D = d _ {1} Z _ {1} + d _ {2} Z _ {2} + \dots + d _ {n} Z _ {n},
$$

where D is the discriminant score,

$d_{i}$ $(i = 1,\dots ,n)$ are the weighting coefficients, and $Z_{i}$ $(i = 1,\dots ,n)$ are the discriminating variables.

In this analysis, eight variables were used as candidates for the discriminants. These were: percentage of information workers, return on capital employed, growth of business over the last three years, formality in communications, competitiveness of market, centralization/decentralization of decision making, routineness of work activities, and predictability of customers' requirements. Preliminary analysis showed that size would be the most important. As we were interested in trying to identify others, the size variable was purposely excluded. The discriminant functions were estimated using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) and summary results are shown in Table 4.

For the overall sample, the discriminant function derived included three explanatory variables: formality in communications, growth of business, and percentage of information workers. Overall, 68% of the organizations were correctly classified. If the organizations were randomly classified, the expected rate of correct classification for the sample would be 57%. Since we excluded size from the analysis, and the discriminant function derived was a relatively simple one, the rate of correct classification was considered satisfactory. Furthermore, as our aim was to identify organizational variables relevant in distinguishing computer users from non-users, the classification rate was not of particular interest. Results for the individual sectors are also presented in Table 4. Although slightly different results were obtained for each sector, the discriminant variables for each and for the overall sample are fairly similar.

Our results imply that organizations which use computers tend to have more formality in communications, a higher growth rate and a higher proportion of information workers. That organizations using computers are more formal in communications is perhaps not surprising. For efficient use of computers, data must be highly structured and standardised. This requirement means that computerization will tend to lead to more formalization in written procedures and communications. The next variable was the growth of business over the last three years. Organizations which had expanded were more likely to use computers than those which had stayed the same or contracted. This might be contrasted with the profitability level discussed in the last section. Discriminant analysis showed that while the growth of business was a good discriminant, the profitability level was not, except in the retail sector. This leads us to speculate that the use of computers improves the performance of organizations and puts them in a better position to grow, though the improvement may not be reflected in profitability in the short term. The third discriminant for the overall sample was the proportion of information workers.

Another variable which was included in more than one discriminant function for the individual sectors, though not for the whole sample, was decentralization of decision making. In wholesale distribution, transport and communication, and business and financial services, organizations with more decentralized decision making were more likely to use computers than organizations with more centralized decision making. This result might be associated with the trend towards distributed processing and decentralization in computer systems.

## 4.5. Computer Capacity

It is difficult to give a precise measure of the computer capacity of an organization. Technical measures such as memory size and processing power were avoided because they might be interpreted in different ways and discourage response. Instead, questions were asked on the make, model and the number of computers installed.

To give an approximate measure to computer capacity, a weighted score was used. The computers were classified into mainframe, mini and micro computers and the weights assigned to each of these types were 5, 3.5 and 1 respectively, but it was found that the results were not sensitive over a reasonable range of these weights.

The relationship between computer capacity and the size of an organization was examined using regression analysis. It could be deduced that the computer capacity of an organization increased approximately with the square root of the total number of employees. The estimated regression line explained about half of the variance in the data ( $r^{2}=0.49$ ). Similar results were obtained in separate analyses for the individual sectors. However, this further analysis showed that the computer capacity per employee in the business and financial services sector was significantly higher than that in the other sectors.

The results obtained for computer capacity could be contrasted with that for a more mature technology such as the telephone. Our analysis showed that the number of telephone extensions used in an organization increased approximately with the total number of employees. When computer technology becomes more mature and when computers are as widely used in organizations as telephones, the computer capacity of an organization might also increase approximately with the number of employees.

## 5. Concluding Remarks

A number of interesting relationships between the use of computers and organizational variables have been obtained. Firstly, organizations using computers tended to have more formality in communications, a higher growth rate and a higher proportion of information workers than organizations not using them. This is particularly interesting, since it provides no evidence to support the commonly held view that computerization leads to a reduction in the information workforce. Secondly, the relationship between the use of computers and profitability level was not significant. Finally, it was found that the computer capacity of an organization increased approximately with the square root of the total number of employees. These results were for organizations in the service sector in the U.K.; similar studies could be carried out to cover other sectors of the economy. The major limitation of this study was the survey approach. The results only provide a 'snap-shot' at a particular point in time and it is not possible, with mail surveys, to analyse issues in each organization in depth; the case study approach is needed to achieve this.
