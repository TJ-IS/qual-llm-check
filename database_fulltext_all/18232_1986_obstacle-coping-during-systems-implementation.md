# Obstacle Coping During Systems Implementation

K. Gregory Jin

School of Business Administration, Wayne State University Detroit, MI 48202 USA

Charles R. Franz

College of Business & Public Administration, University of Missouri-Columbia Columbia, MO 65203 USA

This paper presents a case description of coping with technical and behavioral obstacles that arose during the implementation of a computer-based budget information system. Two obstacle coping strategies are described as they occurred in two successive stages. The first stage describes coping with technical obstacles resulting from errors and omissions in systems design. The obstacle coping strategies, which were based on rigid communications and formal meetings, produced by dysfunctional behavioral outcomes that threatened cancellation of the system. The second stage describes how the stage I dysfunctional behaviors were coped with utilizing principles of organizational change. The case describes the role of a key change agent, who served as both a technical and behavioral process manager. Key implementation process strategies initiated by the change agent are described and analyzed in terms of their effects on behavioral changes and systems completion. Finally, the obstacle coping process is related to the Kolb/Frohman implementation model and defined as a three step process in which problems are diagnosed, confronted, and resolved.

Keywords: Implementation, Obstacle Coping, Kolb/Frohman Model, System Implementation, Organizational Change, Project Authority, Information Systems Development

This paper describes a three-step dynamic obstacle coping process, based on the Kolb-Frohman model of organizational change, as a means of properly dealing with major obstacles that arise out of unresolved technical and behavioral issues during the implementation process. A case is presented to illustrate the dynamics of obstacle coping. An outside consulting group designed a major budget system which contained serious omissions and design flaws. Attempts to redesign and correct the errors by technical analysts were based on achieving technical goals only. Formal and rigid communication structures were instituted to cope with the initial technical obstacles. No consideration had been given to the user's perceptions of information needs or attitudes toward the system's quality. When the problem solving perspective changed from technical to behavioral, and organization change strategies were initiated, dysfunctional behaviors disappeared and the system was successfully completed in a very short period of time.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The Kolb/Frohman model for implementing planned technical change in an organization is reviewed. Then coping with obstacles during systems implementation is introduced as a special application of the Kolb/Frohman model and a case is presented illustrating the obstacle coping process. Four obstacle coping strategies used in the case are discussed. Finally, the obstacle coping process is described as a three step process that is related to the seven phases of the Kolb/Frohman implementation process.

## 2. Implementation Process Model

The implementation process model originated out of the work by Lewin [16] and Schein [20,21]. This theory of social change is based on an iterative three-stage process of “unfreezing” and “moving” behaviors and attitudes toward a desired change objective, and then “refreezing” the new behaviors and attitudes. A test of the Lewin/Schein change model [22] gave it credibility for describing successful systems implementation.

The Kolb/Frohman implementation model attempts to more narrowly focus the Lewin/Schein model to the special problems of implementing technical change in an organization. Based on a model of the consulting process, an outside change agent (consultant) introduces change into the organization by leading the client through seven phases of planned change: scouting, entry, diagnosis, planning, action, evaluation and termination. The first three phases – scouting, entry and diagnosis – correspond to “unfreezing.” The next two phases of planning and action correspond to “moving” and the last two phases of evaluation and termination correspond to “refreezing.”

The descriptive power of the Kolb/Frohman model is documented by many examples in the MIS literature. In particular Lucas and Plimpton [18] used the model to evaluate the complex relationship between technical designers and users. Ginzberg [11] developed a test of the Kolb/Frohman model by developing a questionnaire for management scientists and users. Lucas [17, Chapt 4] summarizes various other studies related to the implementation process where the focus was implementing technical change.

Ginzberg [8] observes that more rigid structuring of roles is appropriate when applying the Kolb/Frohman model to the problem of technical change. He notes that the Kolb/Frohman model is consistent with the organization development literature with one major difference. The consultant introduces technology-based change into the organization by bringing high-technology knowledge and skills into the client problem area. Traditional organizational development literature describes the change agent as one who utilizes existing knowledge and expertise in the client problem area. Ginzberg [8] applied the Kolb/Frohman model as a prescriptive tool for guiding systems implementation in a state government setting. He concluded that the model proved successful in directing attention to nontechnical issues and in dealing with the issues at the proper time.

## 3. Obstacle Coping with the Kolb/Frohman model

When systems development does not follow the implementation model prescription, the Kolb/Frohman model can still be appropriately used to deal with behavioral obstacles that arise. Problems and issues often arise during systems implementation that pose serious threats to the successful completion of an implementation effort. These obstacles may be technical and/or behavioral. Technical obstacles can arise when the technical staff of programmers and analysts cannot correctly identify user requirements and correct and eliminate mistakes in the computer programs. When negative and dysfunctional behaviors follow, serious attitude and perception obstacles arise. The following case illustrates how the Kolb/Frohman model can be utilized to manage behavioral obstacles even when the systems implementation plan was initially prescribed by technical norms.

## 3.1. Organizational Background and Setting

This case takes place in a large county school system comprised of two hundred schools and a large bureaucratic structure (see Fig. 1 for organization chart). In the new budget application project, the major user was the Budget Office, consisting of the budget officer (department head), assistant budget officer, four budget analysts and two secretaries. The budget officer reported to the associate superintendent for business and financial services, who reported to the deputy superintendent of the school system.

The budget application team consisted of the planning analyst (temporarily assigned as project leader), an analyst/programmer, and a senior programmer. Later this team was managed by the new project manager of the financial subsystem projects (first author). The new budget application project was one of several major systems projects undertaken by the financial subsystems team. The financial subsystem project manager reported to the Assistant Director of Systems, who reported to the Director of Systems. The Systems department was only one of many other computer services departments for the School District. The Associate Superintendents of both Computer Services and Systems Development (hereafter called Computer Services), and Business and Financial Affairs (hereafter called Financial Affairs) reported to the Deputy Superintendent.

## 3.2. The Problem Situation

The budget application was initially developed by an outside technical service firm three years prior. It was intended as a tool to be used by the staff of the Budget Office to aid the school system in the timely and accurate generation of the operating budget. During the final implementation phase, numerous programming errors were discovered and extensive systems testing had to be repeated by the internal technical staff to locate the problem areas. By that time, the outside service firm was free from the service obligations under the contract.

In the course of systems testing, some design deficiencies were identified and had to be overcome by revising the original detailed design specifications. This required a considerable amount of detailed systems analysis of the budget operating requirements. During the problem period, about one year before the budget planning time for the upcoming fiscal year, very little progress had been made in analyzing and resolving technical problems and issues. The Budget Application Team encountered serious problems in making the technical progress necessary for the final implementation of the new budget application.

## 3.2.1. Stage I: Negative Obstacle Coping (January - June)

The Systems Department management staff attempted to resolve the persisting problems by establishing formal communications channels with the budget officer through a series of formal memoranda regarding the status of major outstanding tasks. These tasks needed to be completed before the new budget application could become operational for use in the upcoming fiscal year budget development process. There were also formal meetings among the budget officer, the assistant director of the Systems department, and the planning analyst in an attempt to complete the projects as planned.

The formal memoranda exchanges and formal meetings proved to be an ineffective coping mechanism for the resolution of problems and issues. Both the budget staff and the technical staff experienced considerable difficulty in communicating their needs and expertise through formal communication channels. In particular the technical staff needed more direct, rapid responses to questions. Memoranda sent by the Systems Department often went unanswered for weeks before a response was received from the Budget Office. For example, on January 30th, the Systems Department sent a request to the Budget Office pertaining to task identification and priority setting for the Budget System. A response was sent back February 28th by the Budget Office indicating that prioritization of operational items had been completed. In another instance a memorandum listing the status of major task areas was sent to the Budget Office on April 4th, but no response resulted until June 3rd in which the Budget Office reported its understanding of the status of seven major task areas. The budget officer displayed some concern about completing the budget application on time, but stated that he expected it to be fully operational for developing the upcoming fiscal budget.

By June various budget staff members and technical persons were pessimistic about the possibility of implementing the budget application because of the many persisting problems and issues they previously failed to resolve. Poor communication between the budget office and the technical staff of the Systems Department was becoming very apparent. The formal meetings and official memoranda exchanges proved to be an ineffective mechanism for the resolution of problems and issues. Rather than facilitate badly needed rapid communication and face-to-face interaction between the two groups, these formal communication channels further hindered any joint efforts between the two departments.

Furthermore, two memoranda, one from the Budget Office and the other from the budget project leader revealed a large gap between the understanding of the two offices regarding the current status of the budget application. The disagreement between the two memoranda seemed to indicate that the formal communications channels, memoranda exchange and meetings were further hindering implementation of the new budget system.

The preceding scenario of problems and issues and the method of coping with them represents a technical approach to solving implementation obstacles in which system goals receive highest priority over behavioral goals. Rather than considering the importance of user attitudes and commitment to the problem, a formalized communication structure was imposed upon both groups with no consideration of the ensuing consequences. The next stage illustrates the use of the Kolb-Frohman implementation model to describe coping with the behavioral obstacles arising out of Stage I.

## 3.2.2. Stage II: Positive Obstacle Coping (June - September)

The problems and issues related to the budget system testing and implementation persisted. The morale of both the technical systems staff and the budget office became lower. The technical systems staff began to blame the budget office personnel for the lack of active support and involvement in the project. Likewise the budget office staff became critical of the technical staff for the lack of ability to produce results on schedule and to provide the necessary technical support for the systems implementation.

The positive obstacle coping took place as a result of a new project manager (the first author) being hired during the first week of June. The new project manager had been trained in organizational intervention strategies and possessed extensive systems development experience. He began immediately to initiate a series of steps to correct the problems and issues plaguing the budget application. The following chronology details the problem solving steps.

3.2.2.1. Scouting During the scouting phase the nature of the user's problem is identified. Facts and knowledge of events leading up to the present problems are identified. The present commitments and motives of both parties are assessed to determine the attitudes and perceptions of both the technical and user staff personnel. This preliminary investigation work provides a foundation for the phases of entry and diagnosis to be carried out later.

Immediately after the new project manager had been hired, he was given a copy of memorandum prepared by the Budget Office indicating their view of the status of the budget application project. The new project manager immediately met with the planning analyst, who had been the previous project manager, and the analyst/programmer, who was testing all the budget application programs that needed to be corrected. The new project manager discovered that the perceptions of the status of various budget programming tasks differed considerably between the technical staff and the Budget Office.

The planning analyst and the project manager prepared a reply memorandum to the Associate Superintendent for Computer Services in response to the earlier memorandum issued by the Budget Office. The purpose of the reply memorandum was to state the technical staff's perceived status of each budget task area. A comparison of the two memoranda revealed a serious gap between the understanding of the Systems Department and the Budget Office regarding the current status of the budget application. The technical obstacle was becoming further complicated by negative attitudes and perceptions.

The project manager decided it was time to discuss the problem situation with the Budget Office management. It was clear that the Budget Office needed the budget system. They also recognized the need for the new budget system to be successfully completed. But the project manager did not yet know who would be the key budget person to champion the new budget implementation effort.

3.2.2.2. Entry After scouting, a formal entry point in the organization needs to be identified. The primary user department needs to be re-identified and a champion person selected. A psychological contract should be developed showing goals and expectations for the system. The method of approaching the design and the nature of the relationship between the implementor and user should be clearly identified. Since the problem consists of coping with implementation obstacles rather than identifying who the user department will be, the critical activity is to determine who will champion the new implementation efforts and specifically how the organizational change efforts will be carried out.

After perceiving the grave situation during the scouting phase, the project manager discussed with the Director and the Assistant Director of the Systems Department the critical need to take some immediate corrective actions. The project manager suggested that he take immediate control of the budget application project and be introduced to the Budget Office Director as a “strong man” who had the complete authority to implement not only the new budget application, but also all other financial systems applications such as accounting, payroll and transportation accounting. The previous project manager was not given sufficient authority to “get things done” and often had his decisions changed by other management other decisions.

The Director of the Systems Department introduced the new project manager to the budget office staff, including the budget officer, assistant officer and four budget analysts. The project manager was introduced as a person who had complete authority and responsibility for overseeing the developmental activities of the new budget application, and as a person who had considerable past experience with other financial systems projects. Thus, the technical expertise of the change agent was established as is recommended by the Kolb/Frohman model.

The budget officer indicated that the Budget Office had been receiving sporadic systems output, but no tangible, final results had been produced. He affirmed that the computer programs just needed to be corrected since so much time and effort had already been put into them by his budget analysts.

The project manager learned from the Systems

Department that the assistant budget officer was the defacto informal leader of the Budget Office. He was perceived by some of the technical staff members as a potential key “resister” to the new budget application. The project manager met with the assistant budget officer, asking his perceptions of the importance of the new budget application. The assistant budget officer replied that it was absolutely necessary to implement the new budget application successfully if the budget office was to meet the immediate and future budgeting requirements. He went on to offer his appraisal of events which included a perceived lack of communication between both the technical and budget staff, and excessive turn-around time.

The assistant budget officer was very receptive and enthusiastic to the project manager's idea of forming a joint systems group/budget office work team. This work team would meet weekly to solve various issues and problems and make progress reviews. Thus the entry point had been established as the assistant budget officer. At this point the project manager and the assistant budget officer had established an open communications. They began to diagnose what the assistant budget officer perceived as problems and issues preventing successful new budget implementation.

3.2.2.3. Diagnosis The diagnosis phase attempts to more precisely define and clarify the problem in greater detail than during the scouting phase. Discussions take place to develop a shared understanding of the problem and to identify and clarify departmental interdependencies [8] that are critical to the successful implementation of the project.

Diagnosis was taking place concurrently with much of the entry phase discussions. The assistant budget officer stressed the need for eliminating the excessive exchange of memoranda which he perceived were holding up much of the progress of the new budget implementation. The memoranda delayed systems progress by taking too much time and distorting the needs and concerns of the budget office staff. Nothing tangible was being accomplished.

Another critical problem that surfaced during this diagnostic phase was that certain required work sessions for problem resolution could not be held as needed due to scheduling and commitment conflicts. Frequently the budget analysts needed the analyst/programmer to explain certain unexpected conditions or errors. But often they would find him in a meeting on another project or in the computer room. Likewise, when technical staff members had questions on specific operating budget requirements, they found key budget office personnel away on special assignments or in a budget review meeting.

The assistant budget officer and the project manager agreed to plan and form the problem-solving work team the same afternoon to cope with these critical interdependencies. The project manager suggested that the budget office staff and the technical staff jointly agree to set aside a certain time each week for reviewing the project progress. Specifically, the work team would address various issues and problems encountered during the week, review necessary outputs, exchange ideas, and plan for specific necessary actions.

At this point the project manager and the assistant budget officer had defined and clarified the behavioral problems that were serving as obstacles to successful new budget implementation. More importantly the interdependency between the technical staff and the budget staff had been openly discussed. A plan was beginning to emerge that set a psychological contract for trust and collaborative problem solving. The assistant budget officer obtained full authority to be a project coordinator representing the budget office. Also he reached an agreement with the budget officer that “no progress reports or memoranda of any kind would be written until the jobs were done.”

3.2.2.4. Planning During planning, successful coping with obstacles during systems implementation requires that the change agent establish the relationship between the technical staff and the budget office staff. Also the methods that will be used and what people will be involved in making it happen should be clarified.

The plan called for immediately convening a meeting of the joint problem-resolution work team which consisted of two budget analysts, the systems planning analyst and the analyst/programmer. The assistant budget officer stressed the importance of the work team and requested "110%" effort from everyone to aid in successfully completing the budget implementation. The project manager confirmed that he and the assistant budget officer would act as coordinator in charge of weekly work sessions, setting group goals and guidelines. The participants all agreed that they would meet in the budget office conference room any time during the week whenever any obstacles or problems were encountered by either the technical or budget office staff. During the meeting the assistant budget officer, at the suggestion of the project manager, prepared and distributed to all work team members a written outline of action steps for the work team members to follow.

The project manager outlined further task directives to the technical staff. He emphasized the critical need to communicate with the budget office staff and to more actively interact with them. The programmer/analyst was to hand-carry any systems test output to the budget staff in analyzing the output and jointly search for solutions to any errors or problems. He was instructed not to leave the test output material with the budget staff and not to come back to the systems department without any feedback from the budget office.

The project manager also obtained top priority from top management in the use of computer test time. The technical staff members working on budget application programs were to be given as much computer test time as they needed during the day or night.

An important aspect of the diagnosis and planning was to open new communication lines between the technical and budget staff members. The interdependencies had been identified and clarified and a relationship that would promise clear and informative communications had been established by means of an action plan.

3.2.2.5. Action and Evaluation The action phase of the Kolb/Frohman model emphasizes the importance of determining whether the socio-technical solution that was planned in the earlier phase is actually working. When coping with obstacles, the expected trust and collaborative behaviors must be examined and evaluated for their effectiveness. In particular it is important to observe whether the anticipated changes are actually occurring.

At first the skepticism and mistrust on the part of the budget staff toward the technical staff did not decrease. Negative comments persisted about repeated computer output errors. The budget staff did not want to continue to review the other output which contained the same persistent errors.

As the technical staff interacted and became acquainted with the budget office staff, they started to bring more and better output to the weekly team work sessions. More positive behaviors were being displayed by the budget staff and they began to voluntarily visit the technical staff in the Systems Department. Communications became more direct, open and efficient. Gradually the budget office staff showed more commitment to use the new budget application for the upcoming fiscal year as they saw more of the correct and usable output results they had been seeking.

3.2.2.6. Termination The termination phase involves transfer of ownership and ensuring that new behavior patterns have been adopted by the users. It is important to “refreeze” the trust and cooperation created between the users and the systems development personnel during the action and evaluation phases. Finally the users should feel that they “own” the system and feel the commitment of responsibility for future systems enhancements and maintenance.

The new budget application was successfully implemented in September and transferred to the Budget Office with the necessary user instruction manuals. Information from the new application was used in time to prepare the upcoming fiscal year operating budget. The budget staff continued to maintain a close working relationship with the systems personnel during post implementation and maintenance, which later facilitated additional necessary enhancements and modifications. Both the budget staff and systems personnel felt free to voluntarily communicate and exchange information about post-implementation problems and enhancements. The regularly scheduled meetings on Wednesday were replaced by more informal meetings and discussions. The two group leaders continued to promote an atmosphere of trust, respect and mutual learning on the part of the budget office staff and the technical personnel toward each other. The “refreezing” was accomplished through the promotional efforts of the two change agents.

## 4. Analysis of the Obstacle Coping Strategies

The negative obstacle coping process, described in Stage I, did not contribute to the implementation of the new budget application. It led instead to worsening attitudes and perceptions for both parties. In contrast, the positive obstacle coping process, described in Stage II, achieved a constructive resolution of problems and issues, which led to the successful implementation of the budget system. In analyzing the reasons for success and failure, four obstacle coping strategies emerge: Establishing effective communications structures, delegating full project authority to the project team level, sanctioning a powerful change agent role by management, and integrating highly differentiated groups with secondary project structures. Each of these is discussed in the following sections.

## 4.1. Establishment of an Effective Communication Structure

In the negative obstacle coping situation a rigid and formal communications structure was established between the senior management of the user group and the systems development group. Formal media of information exchange, such as memoranda, were used.

The positive obstacle coping process, in contrast, was characterized by an intensive and informal communication and face-to-face interaction between the two groups who had the technical knowledge and information required for making necessary budget implementation decisions.

## 4.2. Delegation of Full Project Authority

The necessary knowledge and project decision authority were not located at the proper hierarchical position in the negative obstacle coping process. The influence necessary to achieve the required problem resolution was concentrated with upper management, not with the appropriate group hierarchies where the necessary knowledge and information existed to achieve such decisions $[15]$ . Too many levels of management authority participated in the resolution of problems and issues.

In the positive obstacle coping process full project authority to carry out necessary implementation tasks was given to the project manager and the assistant budget officer. A lateral relationship was established [7] which moved the level of decision making down to the working participants. Because they had full project decision making authority and detailed knowledge of budget application requirements, they could exchange information rapidly and directly without involving different levels of management hierarchies. (See [4] for a more complete discussion of IS project control and authority.)

## 4.3. Emergence of Powerful Change Agent Roles

In the negative obstacle coping process no change agent existed to integrate the two diverse groups and create an inter-group relationship built on trust and collaboration. The initial project leader lacked sufficient authority or influence, due to his low position in the organization hierarchy, to effectively initiate any positive changes.

The change agent roles played by the two group leaders in the positive obstacle coping facilitated changing the behavior of the project participants from reactive and passive to proactive participation. The new project manager was given the authority to create any changes necessary to successfully implement the new budget application. He identified the assistant budget officer as a critical entry point and successfully organized a joint problem resolution work team to integrate the two diverse groups.

## 4.4 Integration of Highly Differentiated Functions

A critical reason for the failure of the negative obstacle coping process was that no secondary project structure existed to integrate the highly differentiated budget office and the systems technical group. Project team participants and management did not emphasize the need for creating a problem resolution mechanism (the new method of communicating) or a secondary project structure (the work team) to resolve issues and problems constructively.

In the positive obstacle coping process the two co-leaders successfully created and sustained a secondary project structure, i.e. the problem-resolution work team, to address the urgent, critical problems and issues encountered each week. Informal personal relations were integrated with the secondary project structure. For example the budget analysts under the leadership of the assistant budget officer became part of the ad hoc work team and engaged in free and open discussion with the technical systems staff when resolving problems. The secondary project structure served a vitally important role of reducing perceptual differences between the two groups by encouraging the participants to question, clarify and verify certain preconceptions through intensive interactions.

## 5. The Obstacle Coping Process

Obstacle coping strategies used in this case progressed through three major steps: Diagnosis, confrontation and resolution. The diagnosis step corresponds to the scouting, entry and diagnosis steps of the Kolb-Frohman model. Entry in terms of the user department is most likely already established when the systems development has begun. However, as the case illustrates, entry at the proper level in the organization is critical. Furthermore it is critical to identify change agent(s) who will “champion” and “fight” for the cause [2].

Confrontation combines the Kolb/Frohman phases of diagnosis and planning and requires a mechanism or method whereby face-to-face interaction may take place so that differences can be identified and discussed. The confrontation interaction is necessary to promote a direct and clear communication that will permit discussion of problems and clarification of misperceptions. The confrontation can produce negative results if the coping mechanism is formal and inflexible. However, positive results can occur when it is set up at the proper organization hierarchical level where the information exists. Also sufficient authority and prestige must accompany the change agent(s) vis-a-vis the coping mechanism to ensure that design decisions will be carried out and not reversed at a later time [3]. This coping mechanism was discussed in the analysis section as a secondary project structure to integrate highly differentiated functions.

Resolution is the continual application of the Kolb/Frohman action and evaluation phases in which the mechanism is initiated and assessed for its results. As the planned actions are performed, they should be evaluated for their effectiveness.

Many iterations of action and evaluation may be required to take place to ensure that the coping mechanism is effectively dealing with the targeted obstacle. Most likely the plan will require modification before ownership and responsibility for maintenance are transferred to the users during the termination phase.

## 6. Conclusions and Implications for Management

This case illustrates a process of dynamic obstacle coping during systems implementation. The objective of the paper has been to illustrate some dynamics of organizational change in coping with major technical and behavioral obstacles during the implementation process. The obstacle coping strategies initiated in this case were based on the assumption that problems and issues arising during systems implementation may be most effectively and efficiently coped with by applying organizational change strategies. In this particular case technical obstacles were mis-handled during Stage I with coping strategies that placed technical goals and objectives as the highest priority. During Stage II dynamic obstacle coping strategies based on organizational change principles resolved behavioral issues, resulting in a rapid and successful completion of the Budget system.

An important conclusion from this case suggests the importance of creating a collaborative problem solving atmosphere based on trust and mutual respect [17, p. 109], [1]. The objective in such circumstances is to shift from the technical expert position to a psychological contract position. Initially in systems implementation projects the relationship between technical developer and the user is based on the “expert power” of the technical expert [17, Chapt. 5]. Often this power serves as the dominant basis for establishing the initial relationship between the user and technical departments. In the event that the systems implementation process incurs serious technical errors and problems, both behavioral/political as well as technical/rational views must be considered for successful obstacle coping [6]. To effectively deal with ad hoc technical and behavioral problems, Zand [21] advocates a collateral structure to solve special technical implementation problems when the bureaucracy becomes too “sluggish”. Implementing technical change with a temporary, secondary project structure, such as the joint problem-resolution work team in this case, represents a collateral problem-solving structure that provided rapid responses to problems and issues.

The collateral problem-solving structure utilized in this case closely parallels the “tactical” model approach described by Keen [13] to counter resistance incurred during systems implementation. He suggests, in part, establishing contracts, co-opting users, building technical systems personnel credibility and authority, and identifying key resistance to the implementation. Obstacles to implementation, however, do not necessarily arise from the overt resistance discussed by Keen, but may, as this paper suggests, result from poor communications between the two groups. In the present case management carried out Keen’s “tactical” modeling to improve communications by sanctioning the two group leaders to serve the role as change agents. They integrated two highly differentiated groups by creating a secondary project structure, e.g. the joint problem-resolution work team. The critical feature of the work team was that it had been set up to promote open, direct confrontation and interaction among work team members. The two group leaders influenced the group members to trust each other and work collaboratively. Also, the authority to make implementation decisions was sanctioned at a point in the organizational hierarchy where the detailed knowledge of the budget application existed. This locus of project decision authority in Stage II resulted in team members believing that their decisions and participation would count for something. During Stage I much confusion existed when upper management exercised authority to reverse decisions or dictate unplanned changes to the work team. It is important to recognize the role that organizational factors play during systems development, which affect user perceptions of their information system [5]. Additionally, organizational structures, such as the problem-resolution work team, can affect the political (behavioral) dimensions of systems development [19].

Jin [12] proposes that the dynamic obstacle coping process will be most effective when used as a means of anticipating certain implementation obstacles that arise during systems development phases. By understanding that certain issues, conflicts or obstacles may emerge during systems development phases, systems implementation personnel could analyze potential obstacle conditions (problems and issues) and design and implement appropriate action strategies. In this manner obstacle coping would function as a proactive diagnostic method to anticipate Ginzberg's [10] recurring key issues, in addition to serving as a "tactical" method for effectively countering the implementation resistance discussed by Keen.
