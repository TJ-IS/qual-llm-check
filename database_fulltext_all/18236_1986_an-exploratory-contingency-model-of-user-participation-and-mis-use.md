# An Exploratory Contingency Model of User Participation and MIS Use

Eunhong Kim

Kookmin University, 861-1, Jöngnung-dong, Söngbuk-ku, Seoul 132, Korea

and

Jinjoo Lee

Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), P.O. Box 131, Chongryang-ri, Seoul 131, Korea

A model is proposed of the relationship between user participation and degree of MIS usage. The model has four dimensions: participation characteristics, system characteristics, system initiator, and the system development environment. Stages of the System Development Life Cycle are considered as a participation characteristics, task complexity as a system characteristic, and top management support and user attitudes as parts of the system development environment. The data are from a cross-sectional survey in Korea, covering 134 users of 77 different information systems in 32 business firms. The results of the analysis support the proposed model in general. Several implications of this for MIS managers are then discussed.

Keywords: Participation, User Involvement, MIS Use, Participative System Design Strategy, Task Complexity, System Development Environment

## 1. Introduction

This study focuses on the influence of user participation on the process of implementing a Management Information System (MIS) as a determinant of system success. Owing to the theoretical and empirical nature of such research, the value of participation in facilitating change has been generally accepted $[10,13]$ . Thus authors in this field have commonly stressed the value of user participation. Empirical findings, however, have not validated its benefits. The inconsistencies of most of the previous findings on user participation and MIS success were reviewed by Ives and Olson $[11]$ ; these are the underlying imperative for this research. A contingency approach could be very useful in understanding the true nature of the participation-success relationship. Therefore, an integrated exploratory model is proposed, based on the results of the previous research findings.

## 2. A Conceptual Framework

The concept of introducing a contingency approach to the study of user participation is not new, though it is unusual and not systematically integrated. More than a decade ago, Powers and

![](/api/attachments/EGEM4RRX/fulltext/images/682fa32c6ec2bfbcc05ff73c916cef6e5ee8a1f226dc2700a74eec20f4c2956c.jpg)

Dickson [20] found that user participation did not always improve MIS project performance, but that “participation by operating management in design, formal approval of specifications, and continual review of projects” was positively related to user information satisfaction. These findings implicitly suggest the need for considering the participants, the time phase in MIS project, and the type of participation as contingency factors in a study of user participation. More recent research by Olson and Ives [18] adopted and extended such a concept and tested it empirically. The results of that research can be summarized as a significant correlation between the activity of user sign-offs at each stage of the System Development Life Cycle (SDLC) and user satisfaction. On the other hand, such researchers as Vanlommel and De-Brabander [25], Edstrom [6], and Zmud [26] have found that some stages of the SDLC (definition and installation) are more important than others.

Next, the type of system being developed has often been considered a factor in the participation-success relationship. Edstrom [6] divided the type of system into programmed and nonprogrammed. Doing so, he found a significant impact on the degree of user influence. Zmud and Cox [27] proposed well-definedness as a criterion for choosing the appropriate system development strategy. Schonberger [23] also argued that a higher degree of user participation is necessary for successful development of complex systems. In sum, the relationship between user participation and system success is supposed to be stronger for a less programmed, more unstructured, or more complex task.

Furthermore, the source of system origination was found by Powers and Dickson [20] to be a factor significantly correlated with system success. This factor was adopted as a contingency by Alter [1] who conceived the source of system initiation and user participation as key dimensions of system development pattern. He found that initiation by users improved the probability of success, even with low participation. These findings are consistent with the common wisdom of technological innovation: that “demand pull” innovations tend to achieve market success more frequently than “technology push” ones [17]. Conceptually, success of an information system is often compared to the market success of a new product or technological innovation efforts [22].

Previous research thus shows some factors to be considered in probing the relationship between user participation and system success. These can be classified into three parts: factors related to participation itself (participation factors), the characteristics of the task (task environment), and the source of initial stimulus (system initiator). All these are considered in the present research.

Additionally, the organizational climate or, more specifically, favorableness of the attitudes of the key members toward information systems is also considered as a contingency factor. Top management support or commitment has been commonly regarded as an important variable $[5,7,21,25]$ . Others have stressed the importance of favorable user attitudes in achieving successful MIS implementation [14,16] and in inducing more participation [15]. Top management and the user group may well be considered to be the major parties influencing the organizational MIS. ## 3. Method

Data for this study were drawn from a survey of the current status of MIS development and management in Korean business firms. The study covered 134 users of seventy-seven systems in thirty-two organizations. The samples were randomly selected from a population of about 200 firms with mainframe computers. The systems selected had been developed within the last two years. Users were randomly selected from the set of users of each system included. Table 1 summarizes the sample characteristics according to the industry type of the firm and the application type of the system.

Three kinds of questionnaires were prepared for the study, one for measuring the overall environment of the organizational information system, another for examining the process of performing the system development projects, and the last for measuring users' perception and behavior with relation to both the organizational system function and the individual system. Questionnaires of the first type were distributed to information department managers, those of the second type to the specific systems development personnel. Finally, the third type were delivered to the users. Measurement scales for major research variables, and the type of respondents are summarized in Table 2.

The degree of system usage is a frequently adopted measure of MIS success. As argued by Lucas [15], however, if it is mandatory, it may not be an appropriate indicator for success. From this perspective, system use was measured by considering both the frequency and the voluntariness of use. Each was measured on a single item sevenpoint Likert-type scale. Then, by multiplying the frequency by the degree of voluntariness, we could obtain a meaningful measure of system use. Scores obtained from this measure range from 1 which means “Much Less Frequent Use” and “Completely Mandatory Use”, to 49, “Very Frequent Use” and “Completely Voluntary Use”. The scale is normalized by taking a square root.

Stages of the SDLC have been classified in various ways, but most are similar in fundamental concept. Five stages were used in this study: feasibility assessment, information analysis, system design, program/procedure development, and installation/changeover [4]. The degree of participation by the user group in each stage was measured on a seven-point scale.

The measure of task complexity was largely based on works by Gorry and Scott Morton [9]. However, the “structuredness” of a task was divided into the structure of the procedure and the periodicity of the job interval. With the level of management activities originally defined by Anthony [2], each of three dimensions for task complexity was measured on a single item seven-point Likert-type scale. Source of system initiation, or system initiator was classified into either user group or nonuser group.

Top management support was measured by an instrument originating partly from Vanlommel and DeBrabander [25], but modified for this study. Two of the five items measure the attitudes of top management, and the remaining three measure the extent of top management involvement in the MIS planning and control process. Finally, overall user attitudes were measured by an instrument similar to that by Lucas [14]; it consists of five items for the measurement of user attitudes toward systems personnel and three for measuring attitudes toward the potential for the use of computers in their organization.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Reliability and Descriptive Statistics

Item analyses were performed with Cronbach alpha coefficients for all multi-item scale measures. The results show that deletion of any item decreases the alpha value, and thus all the items originally presented should be included in further analysis. Calculated alpha values for task complexity (three items) and user attitudes (eight items) were 0.79 and 0.84, respectively, all of which were measured from user respondents. For top management support (five items) measured at the organization level, an alpha value of 0.85 was obtained.

## 4.2. Statistical Tool for Analysis

Cross tabulation of contingency table analysis was employed for investigating the adequacy of the contingency model. As regards the level of measurement, sample size and exploratory nature of the research it was considered the most appropriate tool. For each variable measured at the ordinal level, the observations were divided into two groups, with the median value as the dividing point. Then, the degree of association was analyzed by constructing a contingency table between two or more dichotomized variables.

## 4.5. Other Contingencies and Participation-Use Relationship

Table 6 summarizes the influence of the other contingencies on the relationship between user participation and system use. It shows that user participation, especially in feasibility assessment, is more important when the information systems development is initiated by a nonuser group. Alter [1] has stated that “… the common wisdom (is) that the needs of users must be the driving force in developing systems and that users should participate actively in implementing them … (p.33).” He also found this to be valid in a case-oriented survey of 56 decision support systems. The findings from our cross-sectional field study are rearranged in a different format to be compared with Alter’s in Table 7. It shows that systems not initiated by the user group should involve them more actively in the development process than those initiated by the user group.

Other remarkable results are those patterns of influence by top management support and user attitudes. User participation in the definition stage is significantly related to the system use, when it is developed in an organization where top management does not strongly support activities of the information systems. The same pattern is found when the user group has unfavorable attitudes toward computer potential and specialists. In the case of user group participation at installation, however the contingencies act in the opposite direction. The result suggests that in less favorable conditions, participation by the user group should be exerted in the definition stage of SDLC, but that in more favorable environment, participation in installation should be highlighted.

## 4.7. Interaction Effects of Task Complexity, System Initiator, and Environmental Factors

A comprehensive analysis requires two separate five-way contingency tables, one for top management support and the other for overall user attitudes. Owing to the limited size of the sample in the study, results could not be interpreted with statistical confidence, though they show some positive evidences for the contingency model.

## 5. Implications for the MIS Manager

There are several implications from this work for the information manager. First, there are some stages of SLDC which deserve active participation efforts of the user group (in definition and installation). As many authors argue, the definition stage is very important, since it typically accounts for no more than 25% of the required resources, but has great effect on the user [4]. An information manager should help the user group allocate their participation efforts to the appropriate stages.

Third, the initial push to develop an MIS should be initiated by the potential user group. If a system originates from extraorganizational or other internal sources, user participation in the development stage is critical to the system success. User participation becomes far more important for more complex systems. MIS managers have the responsibility of training and developing users able to recognize their own requirements.

Finally, an information manager should make an effort to induce positive interest of top level management, as well as of the user group. If the organization is not supportive at the top management level, a participative system design strategy should be emphasized. In such a situation, participation as a motive of voluntary and frequent system use $[3,19]$ operates to a critical extent.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

To conclude, our findings imply that MIS managers should not always count on a participative system design strategy as the best policy but look at the contingencies. However, the results should be interpreted and accepted with caution, since this research has limitations: all the variables were measured at one given point in time, which made it necessary to rely upon the memory of the respondents. Though only recent systems were involved in the study, a relatively long-term period for project completion could distort responses. Also, some of the variables were measured by new instruments rather than by the widely tested ones. In spite of this, we believe that this contingency model and empirical findings will stimulate greater interest in this approach. Further research with a more comprehensive model in various settings is expected.
