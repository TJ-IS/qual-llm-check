# Strategy of Information Management (?)

Arnoud W. Zijlker

The Netherlands

In necessariis unitas
In dubiis libertas
In omnibus autem caritas
Petrus Meldenius, ca. 1626

Information management seems to be the “in” subject of the decade. However, it remains an elusive target. It is suggested that the problem lies in the assumption that we are dealing with information management, as of it were solely concerned with the supply of information. We argue that it would be more fruitful to think in terms of managing information technology, in view of its perceived increasing strategic interest to organizations. The subject is treated from both an historical and organizational perspective.

For the time being, the ideal and reality still seem to be poles apart. On the one hand, we find that, despite a serious lack of sound insight, information technology has already been absorbed by most organizations; on the other hand, it is clear that for today's strategy, simple extrapolation does not suffice. We are learning how to bridge the gap in understanding, so that information technology may be actively exploited to strengthen whatever other activities an organization seems to find important for its competitive advantage.

Keywords: Strategy, Information technology, Portfolio management, Added value chain, Organisation, Systems.

Arnoud W. Zijlker graduated at the technical university of Delft, Netherlands, in 1955 in Process Technology at the faculty of Applied Physics. He subsequently held various engineering and managereal jobs in the chemical industry and attended a Postgraduate Business School ("Stichting Bedrijfskunde") in Rotterdam in 1966/67. In 1970 he assumed responsibility for "automation" in one of the divisions of Akzo n.v. and in 1973 he was appointed as corporate staff director “computer affairs” for this multinational chemical group. In this capacity he was responsible for corporate policy setting and coordination of applying information technology in all its aspects. Since October 1986 he set up as an independent consultant on the basis of this experience. Zijlker frequently speaks at conferences and universities about the various aspects of this subject and has been on the editorial board of I&M since its inception.

## 1. Introduction

What do we mean by the phrase: Strategy of Information Management? It may imply either a misleading suggestion or a purposeful branch of the tree of knowledge in business studies. For the purpose of this article, a broad interpretation is assumed; it embraces all types of uses and devices of information technology: computers, communication aids, storage facilities, and other components that aid in strategy-forming or decision-making in organizations.

For about a quarter century, the information providers have succeeded in usurping the phenomenon of the computer as a major provider of their power base. The use of a phrase such as Information Management implies that the strategy of the provision of information refers exclusively to its collection, storage, and subsequent retrieval for an anxiously waiting manager/user. This is, of course, misleading because attention is diverted from the processes that occur in the course of human dealings: the generation of knowledge and making it available to third parties through mutual communication and transformation of data (or raw information) into meaningful messages. The influence of information technology is enormous. It must be considered of significantly greater importance than mere information management.

In this article, the subject will be discussed from two viewpoints. It will first be treated from an historical perspective: we attempt to show how the goal of strategy-forming is subject to change with technology. Then it will be projected against the three tasks that Drucker [1] originally prescribed to management, viz:

● Realizing the Corporate Goals;

\- Organizing work according to its underlying logic; and

● Handling the human resources in a socially responsible way.

Information manifests itself in many forms. Every simplification whereby information is regarded as an ordinary resource is therefore bound to fail. Information flows in any intercourse between people; it is not, nor can it be, excessive: the term information explosion does not relate to overabundance of meaningful information but indicates excess of paper or signals in which meaning threatens to become irretrievably lost.

Indeed, a better term for this unfortunate phenomenon is information pollution! In other words, neither more nor less information is automatically better: what matters is quality. Information is concerned with meaning; i.e., human interpretation implying human behaviour and the consequent reaction. Information cannot be confined to technology or technologists, as was suggested by Forrester and his associated [2], in imitating the optimizers or general systems theorists seeing the world as a feedback machine \*. They were acting in a similar “optimistic” way to the alchemists who missed the point of chemistry while appearing to be addressing early research issues, or the first researchers in thermodynamics, who were seduced into seeking the Perpetuum Mobile – the perpetual motion machine that has since been proven impossible. Without incorporating human behaviour, information technology is likely to be equally mislead! The technology may start to suggest solutions that spawn more problems instead of solving them and thus produce passive acceptance of systems when active learning and serious participation is needed. In brief: strategy of the utilization of information technology is the real organization issue. And this implies that new information systems and media require operational behaviour incorporated in them to be effective.

## 1.1. The Primitive Period

In Europe's Dark Ages, truth was determined by Rome, formulated in Latin and passed on to the populace in bite-sized-chunks by the monasteries and clergy. The system and its officers were infallible. The “dark ages” of information technology began to take shape in the 1960's. It used a computer as its center. But this machine was incomprehensible to most of its users, accessible only to an elite, and described in terms that made Greek more understandable to the layman. The image of the supreme robot arose in fiction and perceived fact, and the computer took on the terrifying image of an artificial being merely needing to be perfected.

In terms of hard reality, the computer was an expensive, clumsy, and unreliable machine, which could be given a useful role only with much effort and understanding of a problem by mathematically trained programmers. The technical limitations of the machine also meant that its applications were limited to executing well-defined calculations (scientific computations) and certain simple sorting algorithms (administrative data processing). For the former, the machine, thanks to its relative speed and tirelessness, was capable of performances which increasingly excelled those of the human; in the latter, useful effects were its replacement of costly labor.

There was a growing illusion that operations research was a means of providing scientific management, and that an integrated management information system (IMIS) would be a panacea. At that time, strategies were not really mentioned. While management was aware that the IMIS potentially might be a particularly important phenomenon (e.g., the combination of or difficulties in integrating computer centers was an important item on the agenda of merger talks), the IMIS was not understood and remained a source of distrust. Management participation was primarily along its traditional line of control. The manager feared the effect of the high machine costs and the need to put it in the hands of the relatively unbusinesslike computer experts.

Despite many claims to the contrary, there was little significant change in organizations due to the introduction of information technology (wage accounting was automated, transport paths and production programs were improved by linear programming, but that was all). But neither the objectives of the firm, nor the mode of organization of work, nor social relations were directly affected by the introduction of information technology. The phenomenon quickly spread an atmosphere of misunderstanding and fear, and automation became synonymous with loss of jobs and the devaluation of human ability; the threat of “1984” [3] was felt to be real. The computer seemed destined to be useful as an information supplier, and to belong in the information providers’ sphere of influence.

These phenomena, after twenty years, still exist and thus the carry-over from the Primitive period cannot be ignored when formulating today's strategies.

## 1.2. The Gothic Period

People can never be made to conform to a pattern for long. This was also true in the later Middle Ages, when the Church in Rome was gradually forced to come to terms with the populace. This occurred in both words and actions and some light was allowed to flow into the churches.

Similarly, signs of change in applying information technology began to appear in the seventies. Teleprocessing brought the computer out of the closet; this was followed by “distributed data-processing”, when it became clear that, at least in computers, economies of scale had their limits. As the hardware became cheaper, the computer also became more accessible through higher programming languages. Primitive forms of interactive use became possible. It became economically attractive to develop “systems” for many kinds of forms processing, such as ticket bookings and order-processing in sales, purchasing, and technical service organizations. In addition to cost control, organizational effectiveness became an argument to justify investment in information technology.

However, just as Rome had to cope with the expression of the desire for freedom amongst the people, so the automators in the seventies came up against limits of “systemizability”. In response, the “informaticisers”, proposed adaptation of the organization to system designers’ diagrams overcoming resistance to change became an integral part of their tasks. Some dilemmas occurred because of the rapid succession of their Primitive and Gothic periods. Questions naturally arose:

● Should order entry be regarded as an input to the IMIS or as a form of delegation whereby the process of handling orders could be made more effective by improved internal communication? [4]

\- Should orders be subjected to the rigid rules of information suppliers, or was it better (and thus profitable) to define a system in which the executing process was aligned on the more general needs of the users, letting it grow accordingly? [5]

● Was resistance to change a hurdle to be overcome, or a warning sign to be taken seriously?

\- Did the manager define the process by looking at its controllability or was it up to the user group to define the specific logic of the task?

Under the influence of the changing role of “automation”, strategy formulation became a necessity.

Perhaps because corporate objectives were, unlike the other two management tasks, still not being affected by information technology, this need was seldom considered in the past. Recognition was largely restricted to the dialogue between the works councils, the trade unions, and the social sectors of the company. As more employees were affected by automation, resistance grew to the allegedly unavoidable destruction of labour tasks and deterioration of the nature of their work. As long as the truth was decided in Rome and its translation from Latin was the prerogative of the Monasteries participation remained a hollow phrase.

## 1.3. Renaissance

In Gothic times, the art of printing and the spread of literature in the vernacular sowed seeds of the Renaissance and the individual creativity that sprang from it. In a similar way, the fruits of information technology in the eighties gives rise to fundamental change. Time-sharing (mainly in the technico-scientific sphere) and then the personal computer have an effect similar to that of the art of printing in earlier times: information technology becomes a toy, a creative tool, and a means of communication. Thus, it has evolved into a resource in the hands of the individual, who no longer needs the intervention of an expert, and who is no longer subject to the rigid rules of computer system controllers. New applications, which had been considered impossible or uneconomical earlier, become rapidly viable. Laymen discover that they can do their work in a different way and also attract new or different work from that of the past: the secretary learns to exploit her word-processor, the designer his graphics terminal, and the planner his decision support system. What is true for an individual is also true for parts of the organization, and thus ultimately the whole.

Thus, the corporate objective entered the sphere of influence of information technology. Bankers are now moving into wholly new fields of service; manufacturers are entering the market for expertise transfer, mass-producers are individualizing their product lines, distributors are relieving their customers of their stock management function, etc. In other words: Information Technology has started to change the way business competes [7]. It is no longer the question whether a strategy for the utilization of information technology is needed, but a question of how it can be developed. And, surprisingly, this is widely accepted in management circles.

## 2. Analysis

From this discussion, two important facts emerge:

● Information Technology is encapsulated in attitudes that are fast becoming obsolete; and

● Information Technology penetrates into all aspects of the company and all parts of the organization.

It appears therefore worthwhile to subject the group entrusted with implementing information technology to an analysis based on Drucker's model, as though it were a company in its own right.

## 2.1. Realisation of "Business-Goals"

The objective of the IS group is indicated in its name. Restricting their objective to information supply is to become a slave to the history of information technology. Such an attitude obscures the view on the quite different, and possibly far more important, potential of the phenomenon. Automation and systemisation are not the sole objectives anymore of the utilization of information technology, though they may be useful and profitable ways of doing so. Determining strategy now needs to be based on deciding what new opportunities information technology has to offer in relation to critical success factors of the organization [see 8 and 9 for discussion of competitive forces and the role of companies in the added value chain]. Even the exchange of information between companies and their environment is considered; emphasis is placed on strengthening desired relationships (e.g., supplier to customer) or on generating added value in the specific production process (e.g., individualization of product lines).

Strategy determination is aimed at maximizing the contribution of information technology to the competitive position of business; this argument is not limited to private enterprises, for it can be applied to governmental departments as well.

A method of implementing this idea is derived from the concept of portfolio assessment $[10]$ , in which the potential of businesses is assessed in terms of present profitability and growth potential. The McFarlan Grid was developed to assess the importance of present and planned implementation of information technology against the importance of specific and transient critical success factors. Essential to this technique is its accessibility for general managers and the creative stimulus it offers. But strategies do not end with the generation of good ideas: only when they are identified does the real work start.

## 2.2. Organisation of Relevant Work

The second aspect of strategy involves the organization of the work according to the logic of the task of the group entrusted with the introduction of information technology. In the Primitive period, this task was a simple one: programming the expensive machines according to the defined algorithm. The logic concentrated predominantly on the two scarcest resources: machine capacity and analytical/programming expertise. But as automation proceeded in the direction of systemisation, the need to integrate the task of the IS group within the organization required its decentralisation i.e., moving the locus of control towards operations management. This was delayed too long in many organizations through the inertia of the established order. The dilemma of deciding on a system for management information versus process organization and of organizational individuality versus infra-structural standardization have have hardly made the transition easy. The extent to which organizations were able to adapt to change depends substantially on the prevailing culture in the company. Micro-political power structures often proved too strong to allow for adapting to the new logic of the work. In addition to distributed automation and participative systemeering we now have supported do-IT-yourself. Increasing diversification of functional tasks, the changing economy of the hardware (from mainframe to PCs), and the increasing importance of networking for communication between participants (both people and machines) require coordination and demand a measure of insight that neither experts nor management have, as yet, been able to acquire sufficiently.

We begin to see a breakdown into:

● decentralized, front-line support, held together by

\- coordinated architectures, including design methods, training schemes, and quality control; and

● standardized infrastructure management.

However, as already remarked, antiquated attitudes, misleading task descriptions, and established power structures still form a not insubstantial barrier to this growing-up process.

The part least understood and most necessary is the concept of architecture, which may be subdivided into:

\- the typology of applications [11], such as true information systems, decision support systems, transaction processing systems, and knowledge or expert systems. Each to be examined in its specific roles and in its specific structure, and to be more-or-less mutually integrated and subjected to its own boundary conditions – such as security and controllability;

\- the physical structuring of applications – database, program modules, and man-machine interfaces; and

● the choice of suitable design tools for implementing these applications within the framework of a given infrastructure.

The strategic importance of these aspects of organising the IS-task according to its own inherent logic lies in the quality of its added value, just as the quality of operations, as in the Japanese model, has become of strategic importance in achieving the corporate objective in competition with others.

## 2.3. Social Accountability

By no means a minor quality criterion for the applications of information technology is derived from the social boundary conditions. All applications of information technology, including the most highly automated, are and remain manned. There is always the human element, which makes its contribution to the added value of the company. There is no doubt that such applications should be designed so that the employees can retain their motivation and self-esteem. It goes without saying that a measure of systemeering is necessary in any organization, but the way in which or the degree to which remains open for conscious tuning. Organization theory already offers more insight than is used in practice. Even so, systems with which the users cannot or will not live are like cars without trained or motivated drivers: at their best stationary, and at their worst a menace on the road. The strategy of utilizing information technology must therefore extend to the training and coaching of the users on the one hand and the training of experts in designing really user-friendly systems.

The best thing that has happened to the IS community (although to many as a too well disguised blessing), is that phenomenon known as the hobby- or personal-computer. The principle that applies here is: “The devil you know is better than the one you don’t” – unknown is unloved. For the first time, it has become sufficient to acquire a “driving license” for using computers and to participate personally in an experience that changes our attitude and allows us to enjoy the fruits of what otherwise would have remained in agonizing mystery. Thus in our strategy, account must be taken of the up-and-coming generation familiar with the computer, no longer willing to put up with the after-effect of concepts from our Medieval phase.

## 3. Pragmatics

With the absence of sufficient insight of many of those concerned, be they managers, experts, or users, compromise is inevitable. In practice, far-reaching decentralization of the I.S.-function notwithstanding its obvious drawbacks still seems the best way of avoiding major errors. Management, experts, and users at the frontline are still best positioned to know intuitively what is good for their part of the business. Errors are better found at the start than at the end and experimentation is less risky on a small scale. However, as a counterbalance to decentralization, more expert help is needed to ensure a consistent application architecture and technical infrastructure, to boost productivity for small-scale systems design and to allow for formation of networks to integrate those systems.

Renaissance style exploitation of information technology needs individual imagination and flexibility, if need be at the cost of some measure of standardisation and control. All things considered we may be back where it all began: discipline in an organisation should be induced by organisational means instead of being forced on organisations through the backdoor by automation. In the “primitive” and “gothic” periods of the recent past it was not unusual for computers and systems to be abused for this purpose. This weapon is now drastically blunted and management be better aware of this. They should recognise that implementing information technology may well be of too great importance to their business to be left to their IS professionals. Systems have to be conceived and organisationally implemented and managed by those who are accountable for their effect: the controllers for their management information systems, the sales managers for their order processing systems, the engineers for their (“computer aided-”) design systems, etc, etc.

It may be argued that the deeper that information technology penetrates into the organization and is integrated in it, the more organizations will be forced to restructure themselves to use it effectively. There are signs that this condition is already upon us! In the not so long run, the IS profession will play a background role, supporting a generation of managers who are fully familiar with this aspect of their work. For the time being, it remains necessary that the relatively few up-to-date experts should be allowed to play their part, helping to revamp and restructure their organizations so that information technology can be used to best effect. The way to this goal is still one of trial and error, but new generations will, hopefully soon, establish a new professionalism in handling this powerful technology, for real added value, which is more than “managing information”.
