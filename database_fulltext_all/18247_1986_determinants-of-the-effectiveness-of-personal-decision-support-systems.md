# Determinants of the Effectiveness of Personal Decision Support Systems

Sidney R. Snitkin and William R. King
Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh,
Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

The effectiveness of personal decision support systems is studied in terms of system usage, user characteristics, type of application and system characteristics. A system feature and function analysis was also conducted to determine how various system characteristics are valued by users.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems, System Effectiveness, Personal Support Systems, System Characteristics, System Usage, System Applications, User Characteristics, Systems Evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

The widespread application of “personal” micro-computers is widely acknowledged. However, little in the way of systematic empirical analysis has been performed concerning their use and effectiveness [30]. This study seeks to fill a portion of this void in the context of microcomputers that are used for personal decision support.

The term “decision support system” (DSS) has obviously become overworked. Such systems have traditionally been conceived as a means of attacking problems that are relatively unstructured [7]. However, there is evidence that, despite the widespread use of micro-computers for business problems solving, systems that truly address relatively unstructured problems are still rather rare [14].

Nonetheless, the idea of a “personal DSS” is becoming so widespread that the traditional DSS notion is being revised on the basis of an “in use” concept.

If a personal DSS is considered to consist of a general set of tools and a software package that permits the quick generation of a specific DSS that can then be applied to a specific problem $[24]$ , many of the business application software packages that are sold with PCs (and which are often marketed by vendors using the “DSS” terminology) may be considered to represent “personal DSSs.”

Unfortunately, there has not been much attention given to defining the specific features such a personal DSS should have or specifying which business problems it might be directed toward. This lack of specificity of function and features has been recognized $[24]$ and is considered in this study. In doing so, we are, in effect recognizing the validity of using the generic DSS terminology in this context (without being rigorous concerning the degree of unstructuredness of the problems to which it is applied) and of attempting to operationally define a “personal DSS” in terms of the features and applications that are actually “in use” and valued by users.

Such a use of the DSS notion may be thought of by purists as a perversion of the original intent, but it is consistent with common usage e.g., when a spreadsheet package is used with a PC as a DSS for financial modeling [8].

Moreover, the pervasiveness and importance of such applications of PCs to organizations cannot be ignored $[18,25]$ . In many organizations, the use of such personal DSSs is widespread, while the costs and benefits are still being defined and argued. Therefore, there is a great need for empirical evidence to complement the strong opinions and judgements that individuals inevitably make about something that is of such potential importance to businesses and of such potential threat to the traditional role of MIS departments $[20]$ .

## 2. Objectives of the Study

The primary objectives of the study are the:

● determination of the perceived effectiveness with which personal DSSs are being applied in real-world applications,

\- identification of the important determinants of personal DSS effectiveness.

A secondary objective is the specification of the features and functions that are perceived by users to be needed in such a personal DSS.

## 3. The Model and Measures

The model chosen for the study was simple for reasons of both parsimony and practicality. So little is known about the impact of personal DSSs on organizations that it was deemed advisable to use as simple a model as seemed to have the possibility of achieving the desired objectives. As well, the study methodology – a mail survey of users – precluded the use of an overly complex model.

The primary model used was:

Effectiveness

$= f(\text{usage, user, application, system})$ ,

(1)

i.e., the effectiveness of the personal DSS is presumed to be a function of the amount of usage, and characteristics of the user, the application and the system itself.

An alternative model might use usage as the dependent variable:

$$
\text { Usage } = f (\text { user }, \text { application }, \text { system }).\tag{2}
$$

Both models have bases in the literature [16,23] and both are tested in this study.

## 3.1. System Effectiveness

The overall effectiveness of the system was assessed as is typical in such studies, using a perceptual measure. The specific item was a five-point Likert-type scale that required respondents to describe the effectiveness of their systems as ranging from “very effective” to “not very effective” in “solving business problems.”

## 3.2. Usage

The level of system usage was assessed in terms of the estimated hours of use each week. The use of such measures is well established in cases where such system use is voluntary $[5,6,16,23,26]$ . The use of traditional DSS systems have been found to be highly voluntary $[24]$ , which would be expected to carry over to the personal DSS situation.

## 3.3. User Characteristics

The role of the user in determining the effectiveness of an MIS system has been considered a key element in many models of information systems $[11,31]$ . Many user traits have been suggested as being potentially pertinent to the user's ability to effectively utilize an MIS or a DSS $[1,5,11,27]$ .

The two measures for the user's characteristics that were chosen for analysis in this study were education and cognitive style. Education was measured in two ways - years of education and type of education [5].

Cognitive style can be measured by several different standard validated psychological tests [1]. Because of the constraints of the mail questionnaire methodology, a “short form” instrument developed by Vasarhely [28] was used as an indication of cognitive style in this study.

## 3.4. Type of Application

The importance of the type of application in determining the effectiveness of a system is also reflected in most MIS models. In the case of DSS systems, the unstructuredness of problems is probably the key reason for their development $[7,8,12,13,24]$ . Because of the presumption that “personal DSSs” (as well as many other systems to which the DSS terminology is applied $[14]$ ) may not well meet the traditional test of “unstructuredness,” the nature of the actual application base was assessed through a open-ended question that solicited the user to describe the most important applications for which his or her DSS is effective.

## 3.5. System Characteristics

The characteristics of the DSS are expected to have an impact on the system's effectiveness. This idea has pervaded MIS models [11] and is the basis for concern in improving systems. Typically, such concerns deal with the ease of user interface and system responsiveness [5]. In the DSS context, such characteristics as ease of use, flexibility, and adaptability have been emphasized [12,24].

For the case of personal DSS systems, the best embodiments of system characteristics appear to be the type of computer system, the type of software and the generic features of the system. In the case of MIS systems, such hardware and software features have been studied as determinants of success [19].

In this study, system features were identified based on vendor information, and assessed by asking users to rate each features or function in both descriptive and normative terms.

## 4. Methodology of the Study

The survey instrument was developed and pre-tested among informed users of personal decision support systems. It was mailed to 310 customers of a computer dealer who were known to have purchased the relevant hardware and software. The response rate was 18%.

A comparison of the responses of late responders (last 1/3rd) to early responders showed no significant differences. (The respondent anonymity requested by the computer dealer precluded an analysis of nonrespondents.)

## 5. Results of the Study

Various hypotheses relating usage, user, application and system characteristics and perceived effectiveness were tested in terms of both of the models described earlier.

## 5.1. Usage-Effectiveness Relationship

A chi-square test of the relationship between usage and perceived effectiveness showed the relationship to be significant at the 0.05 level, indicating the greater usage is indeed associated with greater perceived effectiveness.

This result might be taken to indicate that models (1) and (2), which differ only in their dependent variable, are equally good, since their dependent variables are highly correlated. Alternately, it might be considered to demonstrate the potential existence of a causal relationship between usage and perceived effectiveness.

## 5.2. System-Effectiveness Relationships

A chi-square analysis was conducted of the relationship between the type of system and perceived effectiveness. Three system types were used based on computing capacity, price and portability:

I - relatively small, low priced non-portable (e.g., Apple II, IIe, TRS-80, etc.)

II - portable (Osborne, Compaq, etc.)

III- relatively large, higher-priced, non-portable (Apple III, IBM PC, etc.).

There was no statistically significant relationship between the system type and perceived effectiveness. However, when a similar analysis was done for system software, a different result is obtained. When software was categorized into two generations using generally accepted definitions, a significant relationship was found – indicating that the use of “second generation” software, such as Lotus 1-2-3, is associated with improved perceptions of system effectiveness. Thus, these results suggest that improved software is a more important determinant of perceived system effectiveness than is improved hardware.

## 5.3. User-Effectiveness Relationship

Both years of education and type of education (“business, engineering, other”) were tested versus perceived effectiveness. Neither produced significant relationships. The cognitive style measure produced similar negative results.

Thus, these results cannot confirm that either the education or the cognitive style of the user is a significant determinant of perceived system effectiveness.

## 5.4. Usage as The Dependent Variable

System usage (hours per week) may also be used as the dependent variable as indicated in Model (2). Despite the high correlation of usage and perceived effectiveness, some interestingly different results arise.

When system type is related to usage, it is found that DSS with “low end” hardware (Type I) are significantly less used than are Type III systems. However, Type II systems (portables) are also highly used – a result that should be of significant interest to both vendors and those seeking to affect the ways in which PCs may be effectively used by organizations.

The relationship of system software to system usage was also significant with greater usage being associated with “second generation” software.

There was also a significant relationship between type of education and usage, with greater usage being made by those with business education. This, coupled with the fact that the years of education-usage relationship was not statistically significant may suggest a market for vendors.

Despite the fact that cognitive style did not significantly relate to perceived effectiveness, it did significantly relate to usage. The nature of this relationship can be interpreted to mean that those with an “analytic” style use their systems heavily, but are no more satisfied with them.

The feature receiving the worst rating was the ability to connect to external data. Although this feature is often highly touted, the actual use of such personal DSS systems appears to be on more independent activities that would not merit a great concern with this feature. The respondent subset that did favor this feature was made up of those interested in Stock Trend Analysis.

The highest rating among the functions was given to the need for basic statistical functions such as sum, average, etc. The other items with noteworthy ratings are those relating to basic financial functions such as NPV, IRR, etc. and the more advanced statistical functions of standard deviation, correlation, etc. The fact that these were rated above average supports the belief that personal DSS systems are being used in a somewhat sophisticated way. The lower-than-average ratings of data analysis and simulation tools, shows however, that the use of such systems is not in the management science and operations research contexts that are often envisioned for traditional DDS systems.

## 6. Conclusions

The study showed that, for the personal DSS sample analyzed, there was high correlation between usage and perceived effectiveness. Since these are both used as dependent variables in various studies, this suggests that either might be appropriate for this sort of voluntary use system.

However, specific analyses using each of these measures as dependent variables indicated interesting differences between the two. For instance, low end systems are used much less than more sophisticated ones. However, no such difference shows up in terms of perceived effectiveness. The portable category demonstrated relatively high use. Analysis of both measures suggests that 2nd-generation software is both more used and perceived to be more effective.

The educational characteristics of users were shown to be significant only in that those trained in business made significantly greater use of the personal DSSs than other categories. Since the number of years of education did not produce any significant results, this may suggest the core market for these systems.

The cognitive style measure was significantly related only to system usage. “Analytics” appear to use their systems more heavily, as might be expected, but they appear to be no more satisfied with them.

Overall, this study presents an interesting perspective on the evolving notion of a “personal DSS.” The evidence that is analyzed here suggests that the pragmatic notion of personal DSSs has meaning and that some determinants of the effectiveness of such systems can be identified. While such systems do not meet the formal criteria for DSSs as they were initially conceived, they are important to the everyday functioning of business organizations, and as such, it is important that this particular “in-use” DSS notion be identified and studied.
