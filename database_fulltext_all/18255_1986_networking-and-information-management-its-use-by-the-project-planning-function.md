# Networking and Information Management: Its Use by the Project Planning Function

Mark D. Matthews

Martin Marietta Aerospace, Titan Centaur Program, M/S G5705, P.O. Box 179, Denver, CO 80201, USA

The task of the project management function is largely that of integrating the wide range of issues that comprise the project. Information is the medium by which these issues are understood and managed; therefore, information management is also a critical function of project management. Networking, the processes involving the use of PERT/CPM and Precedence Diagrams, is a method to manage information. This paper provides some applications of networking in relationship to information management. The centrality of networking to information management is that of defining, structuring, and organizing information and the process of information feedback.

Keywords: Networking, Project Management, Information Management, Work Breakdown Structure, Network Diagram, Information Feedback

## 1. Introduction

Rob Thomsett, in an important monograph, People and Project Management, provides a prism for understanding a critical area of project management: “Project control is about people being supported by information systems that enable them to understand the complexity of issues characterizing the project they are attempting to control.” [14] In other words, the task of the project management organization is largely that of integrating the wide range of issues that are the project. Information is the means by which these issues are understood and managed; therefore, information management is a critical function of project management.

Networking, the discipline involved in creating and utilizing PERT/CPM charts, Precedence Diagrams, Earned Value, etc., is a tool and methodology for managing information. Networking has been used, with varying success, as a control or scheduling technique; yet, it has gone unrecognized, or at least not adequately recognized, as an extremely viable discipline for information management. The paper provides a brief outline of networking with respect to information management in a project environment.

## 2. Project Management and Information Management

In the project environment, managers are faced with a critical need for current, timely information to model day-to-day operations, identify and solve problems, explain trends, review and forecast performance, and describe the content of the project. Consequently, a smooth information flow must be built into the project as a basis for all planning activity [15]. This requires that the planning function establish an organizational methodology that:

1. Anticipates the way information will flow through the project;

2. Creates an effective review structure for evaluating information quality as it passes through the project;

3. Establishes controls, such that: all information used by more than one organizational component is controlled; that one approved version of the Master Plan/Master Schedule is being used within the project; and that all plans and schedules directly trace back to the approved version. [2]

Networking supports this process by providing a framework for information generation. First, information is the interpretation of data. Second, the same data may give rise to different interpretations (information) by different persons. The project environment encompasses personnel that possess a number of varying skills and disciplines. This creates different and sometimes conflicting views of the project. Therefore, one important function to be achieved is to manage the different interpretations of data within the project environment.

Third, information can only become information within the framework of a system. The method by which information is transformed creates and controls its various interpretations.

Through networking general information is transformed into project-specific information for the project. Information that has been defined, structured and organized through networking will be available consistently for appraisal of the project, at various stages of development, and against a range of criteria. Consequently, each organizational entity will be able to access the same data. A system is provided in which data is easily transported rather than requiring translation between participants.

The flows of information between Test Operations Planning on the Ground Support System of the Space Shuttle Program and other components are illustrated in Fig. 1. It shows the heterogeneous elements to which the planning function must respond and underscores the process of information generation and feedback between components. As an example, Test Operations Planning provides information to logistics on a test schedule. Logistics evaluates this; then determines that some cables, required for the test, will not be “on-dock” in time to support the scheduled dates.

The planning function is central to the flows of information throughout the project. The effectiveness of this function rests, in large part, on its ability to manage the different views of the project and the diverse information flows, as outlined in Table 1. As part of its function, planning must also insure the integrity of project management information. Its position in the process of information exchange mandates that a methodology is in place to insure information quality and that plans and schedules can be traced back to one approved master plan. It must also respond to the requirements of the varying levels of management, e.g. system level, tests within a system, and sequences within a test [8].

## 3. Networking - Defining Project Management Information

A project is dynamic and multi-disciplined. The dynamics of the environment are such that many teleological variables (such as the objectives, goals, tasks, functions and purpose) combine to impact the direction of the project. The variables differ both between organization functions and levels of management. Because these teleological variables often clash with each other, a method is required to respond to these differences and, simultaneously, to discipline the planning and direction of the project toward a single goal.

Networking forces a systematic delineation of project components and sequential continuity. Additionally, networking requires that the project be defined as a finite chain of activities that cannot be arbitrarily broken. Consequently, when used, networks require that the project be thought out from start to completion. Its effective use imposes a discipline on how the project is to be view and how activities are to be undertaken $[12]$ .

Networking relates to information management by first providing a method to define information. Phasing schedules and work breakdown structures (WBS) are used to systematically decompose the project into its functional components. A simplified example of a work breakdown structure of the Ground Support Systems of the Space Shuttle Program is illustrated in Fig. 2. Through the work breakdown structure, the project is decomposed into manageable levels of work. By delineation of project tasks/subtasks/activities/work packages, information requirements can be determined.

Fig. 2 shows the differing requirements of the varying levels of management. For example, top-level management requires information to understand the progress occurring in the different phases of the project, i.e. site activations, system testing and site operations. Mid-level management responsible for the ground system tests requires information on the conduct of all system tests such as when the tests are scheduled to start and complete; if any, what major problems the tests have uncovered; and what are the constraints to conducting the system tests. Lower level management responsible for the Hydrazine (N2H4) System is concerned with the conduct of the activities, equipment, manpower, etc. needed to support the testing of the Hydrazine System.

Forrester observed that an organization is a “complex interlocking network of information channels.” He concludes, “Every action point (activity) in the system is backed up by a local decision point whose information sources reach into other parts of the organization and surrounding environment.” [3] The work breakdown structure is a careful assessment of the basic activities comprising the project. When the work breakdown structure is constructed it is possible to specify information precedents and further data processing requirements; to provide the visibility of the processes that create the roles within the information generation process and the corresponding systems.

The information precedents of an activity identified in the work breakdown structure – Hydrazine (N2H4) System Functional Verification Test – are listed in Fig. 3. Upon delineation of the project tasks/subtasks/activities, a generic model of information precedents is established. ## 4. Networking – Structuring Project Management Information

The work breakdown structure has a number of important functions in the structuring of information, i.e. how data is to be broken out or summarized according to requirements at the varying levels of management. In addition to simplifying the complexity of the project, the work breakdown structure provides a method to insure that the level of detail of the information is consistent with the level of detail to which the project is to be controlled.

Structuring of information by level of control is essential in establishing the framework for integrative planning between levels of management. This may be the critical function of the WBS. Without this recognition, there is the probability of generalizing information requirements as if they were applicable to all levels of management when they are applicable only to a single level; generalizing information requirements as if they apply to one level when they are actually applicable to a different level; or emphasizing one level over other levels [16]. Lastly, the WBS provides information that serves as the basis for further detailed planning--in particular, the construction of project management network diagrams.

The construction of network diagrams corresponds to a new phase in the planning process. Strategic considerations, program objectives and specifications are processed into structured, detailed master plans and schedules. The significance of the network diagram in this phase is that it assists management by:

1. Providing a graphical representation of the project's desired behavior;

2. Identifying and describing information flows and decision-making processes, serving to direct and organize information flows;

3. Serving as a constant information feedback mechanism following the same principles of a regulating circuit. [13]

The network diagram is an infological model of the information-decision-activity relationships that comprise the project. Modeling the constraints and operations that reflect real-world situations, the network diagram provides an explanatory model of the processes and systems involved in the project. Consequently, through the construction of networks, it is possible to determine what specific information is required to manage the project's behavior. With completion of the work breakdown structure and network diagram, information has been structured such that there is a systematic and clearly defined association of individual project task activities with elements of information [5]. Finally, the use of networks and the WBS provide an order pattern which serves as the basis for the reporting system and are used to structurally arrange the data base of the entire project.

## 5. Networking – Organizing Project Management Information

The organization of information/data comprises the final phase in providing the medium for project management. Networking organizes the continuity of information usage. Information is organized into coherent patterns with non-contradictory action implications. Networking is a medium for feedback on the project centering on task activities, task interdependencies, planned versus actual comparisons and other associated elements of information $[1,11]$ . The establishment of information and feedback systems based on networking minimizes problems with inconsistent information. Project scheduling and control information – for scheduling work, facilities, equipment, procurement activities, etc.; for better coordination and synchronization of component parts of the project; to facilitate cooperation and communication of diverse project efforts by relating individual efforts to the total scope of the project; provide a tool to isolate and minimize trouble areas;

5. Instruction Information – for disciplining planning and task coordination toward a single goal; provide a “road map” of the project’s direction and processes it will take to produce the desired end item;

6. Information Feedback - for measuring the plan against actual performance; minimize deviations from the plan; support timely corrective action when deviations do occur;

7. Continuous Progress Information - for a dynamic portrayal of project status to facilitate a smoothly coordinated project and provide a framework to understand the future impact of present-day activity [9,13].

## 6. Summary

Networking provides management a methodology to define, structure, and organize information. The use of networking as a methodology for the management of information avoids the following problems:

1. Duplication of effort. Duplication and overlap occur in generating, collecting, analyzing and utilizing information because the information requirements have not been defined nor coordinated;

2. Loss of information integrity. Similar sets of information are collected by different functions at different levels within the organizations; consequently, the integrity of the information is degraded as different organizational entities issue similar but inconsistent information;

3. Decreased productivity. The organization's process of information exchange is clogged by irrelevant information, as managers have no real clarity on what information is required to assist in carrying out their tasks; the lower echelons of the organization are burdened with excessive demands for information from the higher echelons;

4. Higher costs. Excess information increases the amount of information processing and handling, consuming an increasingly larger share of the budget;

5. Bad decisions. Decisions devolve from outdated or erroneous information;

6. Loss of market influence. The organization assumes unnecessary risks and fails to take advantage of opportunities [6,10].

The method by which information is collected, stored, and distributed has a profound impact on the interactions within the project environment. Increasing access to relevant project management information is the essence of networking. Networking affects the availability and quality of project management information by achieving the following:

1. Defined, structured, and organized information on the project's status;

2. Definition of the role of the information resource-most significantly, information for measuring and controlling project behavior;

3. Information to increase the comprehension of the different interrelating jobs;

4. Well-defined basis for construction of information systems to support the varying levels of management; particularly relating information requirements to decision-making and problem-solving;

5. Management of the flows of information and the process of information generation along cybernetic principles;

6. Establishment of controls to insure that all of the information which has value is available, and that only that information which is of value is collected [9].
