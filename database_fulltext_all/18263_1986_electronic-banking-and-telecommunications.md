# Electronic Banking and Telecommunications

James C. Grant

Senior Vice President, Operations & Systems Dept., The Royal Bank of Canada, 180 Wellington Street West, Toronto, Ontario M5J 1J1, Canada

This paper presents a banking perspective of the major trends in electronic banking and telecommunications. Organized into four major trend issues, the paper provides some valuable insights into the telecommunications carrier business, into their impact on financial institutions and banking in particular, but also into free enterprise and regulated national systems, and effects that are caused by lack of competition.

Keywords: Common Carrier, Competition, Electronic Banking, Financial Services, Government, Issues, Monopoly, Post Telephone & Telegraph (PTT), Regulation, Royal Bank, Telecommunications, Trends.

## 1. Introduction

Even before becoming a nation, Canada had a leading edge communications capability. In 1846, about two years after Samuel B. Morse had demonstrated the electric telegraph in the United States, the first telegram in Canada was sent from Toronto City Hall by the Mayor to his counterpart in London, Ontario, a distance of approximately 100 miles. In 1847, the first Canadian telegraph service was connected to systems in the United States. The first submarine cable in North America was laid in 1852 between New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island and in 1866 the first effective trans-atlantic cable came into use for telegraph service.

Canada's telephone networks originated with the work of both Alexander Graham Bell, inventor of the telephone, and Guglielmo Marconi, the inventor of radio. Bell invented the telephone near Brantford, Ontario in 1874. Two years later the world's first "long-distance" call (approximately 5 miles) was made over telegraph company lines from Brantford to Paris, Ontario.

Early modes of communication in Canada were closely related to transportation and, it is not often noted that banking was closely related to both. Bank Branches spread across the vast Canadian West with the growth and expansion of the railway and telegraph networks. Together, communications, transportation and financial networks provided the basic infrastructure for economic and social development and were essential for the task of opening up a vast continent, building a nation state and bringing Canada to the world.

During the past century, the evolution of public telecommunications facilities and private electronic banking services - telephones and automated banking machines, microwave communications and on-line banking, computer communications and electronic payments systems - has taken place more or less independently of the available means of transportation. These telecommunications and electronic banking capabilities now constitute the central nervous system of an advanced industrial nation like Canada. Not surprisingly, communications in its many and varied forms have become a sensitive area of public policy.

In this paper I will present a banking perspective of the major trends in electronic banking and telecommunications. I will outline the issues as I see them and the implications of these issues on the ability of Banks to deliver timely and competitive electronic financial services.

## 2. Global Trends & Implications for Electronic Banking

2.1. The first major trend is a push by telecommunications carriers to globally integrate information access and communication.

Historically, the objective of the telecommunications industry has been to extend communications service on equal terms to as many people as possible. It offered universal access to standard communications devices in standard applications, the telephone being the obvious example. Today, there is a high degree of direct communication between the world's public telephone networks. In contrast to telecommunications, the computer industry has grown because it offered computerized solutions to individual problems. For competitive and entrepreneurial reasons, many computer vendor's systems have not been designed to communicate directly with other computer vendor's systems. While the telecommunications industry has to be more attuned to user's needs, the computer industry has to become more attuned to the need for direct communication among diversified vendor systems.

State and private telecommunications monopolies are using technological advances to modernize their older networks and switching equipment and offer new Public Data Network services. In the long term, they plan to exploit the capabilities and universal reach of future digital telecommunications networks to create a single Integrated Services Digital Network (ISDN). Their goal is to provide fixed and mobile digital communications to support a wide range of voice and data services. There will be built-in computer processing capability in the network, and local or national telephone exchanges may be able to act as computer bureaus.

In most countries, these monopolies have traditionally shaped the telecommunication supplier industry's development. They continue to impose wide ranging controls over what communications and computer equipment users can attach to their networks. Though a number of these monopolies have relaxed the rules over the supply of subscriber equipment to permit more competition between local manufacturers, most are aggressively defending their monopoly and control over the network and basic circuit capacity. In their view the provision of the national telecommunications infrastructure is a “natural” monopoly. However, it is becoming difficult to reconcile their policies with the goal of encouraging competitive high technology industries. The monopoly is appearing less natural and more artificial to the broad range of competitive suppliers and business users of telecommunications services, both in the home country and internationally.

Some believe that these monopolies will be forced to allow more private competition in the provision of “value added” computer processing services since they may find it impractical to maintain a monopoly over the wide range of services which technological advance is now making possible.

The Canadian telecommunications industry is different from its counterpart's situation in Europe and Asia. The fact that we have one of the best communications networks and state-of-the-art digital communications services in the world is to the credit of the Canadian private sector communications industry. Unlike the practice in countries where telecommunications are a monopoly controlled by the state, the Canadian telecommunications carriage industry consists of a mixture of private, government and joint private-government corporations and organizations. Canada presents a complex mixture of federal and provincial legislation, policies and regulation. The difference between the Canadian (and North American) Telecommunications industry and the rest of the world is rooted in our history and geography.

2.2. The second major trend is a drive by the financial services industry to provide enhanced electronic services through the use of telecommunications.

Competitive advantage in the financial services industry is shifting, partly because the medium of financial exchange is shifting from documents to electronic images and impulses. That shift is removing the traditional entry barriers to participation by a range of financial institutions, retailers and other business corporations in the payment system and other financial services.

The most dramatic and visible use of telecommunications networks by the world's banks in the last decade has been the emergence of The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT) which is neither a basic store-and-forward message switching network nor the total solution to bank communications needs. SWIFT is a value added network for international banking. The value added components are common banking standards, ease of banking communications, lower message costs and global coverage.

The major issue facing SWIFT is its role as a value added bank network in the context of a highly competitive international banking environment and the likely strategies of telecommunications monopolies. These monopolies want to control the international communications, switching and transmission networks for banks and they are exerting pressure through restrictive policies on tariffs and facilities to force users to use their new generation of public data networks. However, many banks, including the Royal Bank of Canada, have already invested in building their own internal network capability. For competitive and security reasons these banks need the choice of selecting the telecommunications facility that best suits their needs.

Canada has the potential to be a world leader in the delivery of enhanced electronic financial services. This is because we have a strong, long established coast-to-coast national banking system and an established telecommunications infrastructure. Canada also has leading edge software technology and we have organizations and institutions like the Canadian Bankers' Association, the Payments Alternative Communications Exchange and the Canadian Payments Association who bring the players together to discuss the issues.

The Royal Bank like many large international banks has an established electronic portfolio of enhanced Corporate, Commercial and Retail convenience related financial services covering Cash Management and Treasury based financial services; credit card and ATM network sharing activities and in technology applications like the “smart card”. All these provide a first class base of experience for developing the electronic service delivery infrastructure that’s needed. Canadian consumers appreciate convenience and know good service and value when they see it.

Direct debit at the point of sale (POS) will be an alternative payment mode by 1990. This trend is supported by increasing consumer acceptance of electronic banking and plastics including credit cards. Canadians have the highest per capita acceptance of credit cards in the world. The North American consumer in the 1980's is mobile, convenience oriented and time sensitive. After home and work, shopping malls are the third most frequented place. There is a trend away from specialty-type credit cards. Banks will move towards an identification and entitlement card as people want to carry less plastic while keeping the convenience and ease of access offered by card based services.

Countries that will lead in the provision of enhanced electronic financial services (and therefore reap the national economic benefits) will be those countries that have the right mix of an innovative and aggressive national banking system, national retailers, high acceptance of ATM's and plastics by consumers and a flexible, market driven, telecommunications infrastructure.

2.3. The third major trend is a focus by host nations on controlling the flow of international business information

Barriers to the movement of information internationally have posed some major issues for corporate management and public policy makers. The issues involved in the movement of information across national borders sharpened in the late 70's as computer and communications technologies converged. International banks have changed their operations to take full advantage of the economic and operating benefits of international networks to the point where they are dependent on their continued reliability. In today's age of integrated computer communications technology, the flow of information across international borders has become a marketable commodity. Public concern about new information technology has spawned privacy and data protection laws in Europe and the U.S. It has generated heated debate in United Nations organizations and industrial and third world forums over national sovereignty, cultural integrity and control of the media. More recently, the issues are becoming openly protectionist and economic in nature.

Business, particularly in North America, has always viewed telecommunications as a pipeline for the free flow of information. The domestic environment, in particular, was free of restrictions in the movement of information. When restrictions arose, technical or tariff solutions were implemented by the telecommunications monopoly to solve the problem. As a result, each new restriction tends to be seen as an isolated problem and not as a part of a broader trend towards a more restrictive environment with long range business implications.

The United States Government wants the Organization for Economic Corporation and Development (OECD) to consider the trade, social and economic implications of national policies governing: right of access to the network (the gateway); private leased lines; competition versus monopoly control in telecommunications; resale and shared use of lines; erosion of the telecommunications monopolies revenues; competition in providing customer-premises equipment; international satellite policy; and development of the Integrated Services Digital Network (ISDN). An OECD data flow survey of Multinational Corporations revealed two major complaints with foreign governments and their telecommunications monopolies. First, restrictions prevent firms from establishing the most efficient and least expensive communications routes, especially since monopoly rates are high. A second major restriction was that uncertainty over future restrictions inhibits planning and delays implementation of international communications applications. Specific major problems were cited in Japan, France, Sweden, West Germany and the Netherlands.

The ISDN as currently envisioned, will evolve from today's analogue, copper-wire telephone system. While the technical considerations are immense, major strategic questions need to be answered. Where will the so-called “intelligence” (automatic computerized functions) be located in the network? Who will control them? What effects will these automated functions have on business users?

The questions raise issues of national sovereignty, individual rights and privacy, economic and national security. The reaction of the world community and action by the International Telecommunications Union and other international organizations will determine the degree of success of the ISDN. To the ISDN, international boundaries would be transparent and data banks located within certain countries could service the entire network. The intelligence required to control the network within one country could be located in another. A major policy debate will centre on whether the public or private sector will control the network and the “intelligence”. In the future, we see Governments and their telecommunications monopolies fighting aggressively against private sector competition with their new generation of Public Data Networks (PDN’s), which they hope will be the forerunners of the ISDN.

2.4. The fourth major trend is the increasingly direct and visible impact of telecommunications regulation and policy on the cost and convenience of business services and corporate strategies.

The older public telecommunications networks are no longer viable solutions for today's age of electronic financial services. While new technology has made some of the regulatory rules on the older public networks transparent, it is also vital to the telecommunications monopolies' strategy of introducing a new generation of Public Data Networks. This new generation of public telecommunications networks have built-in computers which allow the monopoly supplier to electronically switch, store, transmit and route international business traffic in what the utility feels is the most optimal manner.

From a technology perspective, international banks such as The Royal Bank should adopt the business objective of ensuring that delivery of enhanced financial services to clients is not hindered by Government legislation or telecommunications regulations. Our vision for the public or private Telecommunications Network is that it is an electronic utility which allows us to deliver a variety of our enhanced banking and information services to our customers. Ideally, a business unit would only have to obtain access to an electronic workstation to hook up to this global capability.

The main components of our service infrastructure are: (1) an integrated banking communications network accessible to a large customer and user base offering cheap time, independent of distance; (2) a customer and user base possessing a range of compatible machines using standardized computer and communications capabilities; and (3) a diverse range of information providers feeding information to the network. The appropriate levels of data security and data protection must be built into this infrastructure to ensure the protection of our information network and protection of the confidentiality of our clients records worldwide. This is crucial if we are to operate and market a viable and credible global “enhanced service” capability for our clients. “Monopoly” limits are artificial in today’s environment and they must be defined carefully and strictly to allow maximum competition.

## 3. Conclusions

To survive in the highly competitive world of electronic banking, banks need the choice of utilizing public monopoly telecommunications services or competitive business telecommunications services. The application of tariffs, and restrictions on network attachments and network switching by telecommunications monopolies may stifle the ability of the banking industry to offer competitive financial services. Monopoly interests may hurt our customers. Business pressure and vigilance are needed to ensure that monopoly powers are kept in check.

Royal Bank executive management have taken a leading role in debating and influencing the political and economic issues of information flow and privacy. Today electronic financial networks and electronic communications networks are converging. They form the confluence at the centre stimulating the need for change in banking and telecommunications regulation.

Political and technological reality in today's hotly debated international environment is to actively shape and formulate business plans in view of government and monopoly informatics policy. This is particularly important in the area of telecommunications which is crucial to our plans for providing enhanced electronic banking services for our clients.

## Profile of The Royal Bank of Canada

The Canadian banking industry employs 150,000 Canadians. There are over 7,000 bank branches in Canada, which means one branch for every 3,500 Canadians. Canadian Banks total over 400 billion dollars in assets and the six major Canadian Chartered Banks have a significant market presence in leading financial centres around the world.

The Royal Bank is Canada's largest bank and North America's fifth largest with assets of more than 96 billion dollars (Canadian) and an international network of more than 240 operating units in 44 countries. Technology in The Royal Bank is of major strategic importance, in fact, overall technology related expenditures now account for 20 per cent of the Bank's total non-interest expense. The Royal Bank's systems handle an average of 4.1 million cheques, more than 560,000 credit card transactions and one million on-line transactions in a single day. Its coast-to-coast on-line banking links its 1,440 branches across 4 1/2 time zones in Canada.

A consistent technology leader, The Royal Bank has anticipated the increasing trend to decentralization of operations, networking and open systems. It is developing expertise in network integration by building a new generation of communications “gateways” and developing interfaces that standardize electronic communications between incompatible computer network and Public Data Network architectures. It is an acknowledged leader in its use of distributed technology.

The backbone of The Royal Bank's communications and information processing operations are the public and private telecommunications networks which link the Bank's processing centres across Canada and its international network of processing centres in 25 countries.
