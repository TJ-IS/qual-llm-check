# Integrating Systems into the Organization \*

Michael J. Samek

Management Consultant in Information Technology, 955 Lexington Avenue, New York, NY 10021, USA

Over the last thirty years, computer technology – hardware and software – has entered every phase of business and government. It has not been an easy or smooth transition, but much has been learned about the success factors of integrating systems into the organization.

This paper adresses those factors - other than technical ones - which facilitate successful systems, which are in the nature of organizational attitude, behaviour and style. The roles of various functions vis-à-vis systems management are discussed.

The paper concludes with some practical rules which have evolved with experience, intended to insure that systems are indeed useful and effective and therefore judged successful by the users and the developers.

Keywords: systems integration, organizational attitudes, success factors, success in systems management, transition to computers, useful systems, effective systems, new technology.

## 1. Issues and Definitions

Bringing new technology into an organization, be it a commercial enterprise, a manufacturing company or a government department, has always been difficult. We believe that there are two factors involved: the complexity of the technology itself and the change which is the consequence of the new technology.

However this may be, in order to make the new technology successful - computer technology in our case - it has to become a fully accepted part of the organization. I call this process “integration”.

In this paper - as in most places - the often used word “system(s)” means computer technology and alternatively, the systems organization, which in turn includes not only the systems development or software function, but the hardware, the computers and their peripherals, as well.

The word “organization” may mean a whole company or a specific unit in such a company, it may refer to a governmental body or a business unit. Finally the frequently used word “function” refers to an entity having clearly identified, distinctive tasks, i.e. the information systems department is a function, as is accounting or sales.

## 2. Managing Technological Change

Even though we have been occupied and living with the evolving computer technology since the early 1950's, it seems that the promise of the technology has always been somewhat ahead of the reality. Every hardware generation change, from vacuum tubes to solid state to integrated micro-circuitry, and every improvement in software, has come to us with the understanding that the shortcomings of the past have been overcome and that a great deal of new capability (and likely capacity) has been added. We the computer professionals, immediately translated this into new and expanded systems plans for our users. We frequently fell short of the promises made; often, amongst other reasons, the added capability swallowed the added capacity.

In my opinion, the shortcomings are nevertheless due in large measure to our inability to absorb changes - rather than the complexity of the technology itself. We need to fully explore and understand the nature and the impact of the changes on the procedures and purpose of the user organization on one hand, and on the people in the user organization on the other.

In the middle 1950's American business began to bring computer technicians into its organization to make the then new computers work, usually in the accounting/bookkeeping function. This worked quite well as long as the tasks performed were an extension of existing tasks done with accounting machines, or were more or less of routine nature. However, as the capability of computers increased (as well as the experience of the technicians) the technical people and the accountants began to find themselves in positions of conflict. It appeared because the interest of these two groups seemed to be different, one occupied with computer technical problems and the other with accounting principles. None seemed to have sympathy for the other.

Similar cases or incidents of conflict between the systems people and other functional groups, sales and manufacturing for example, started to appear as the role and the capability of the computer increased and systems development efforts began. These situations made integration of the systems group rather difficult.

Had such conflicts occurred between functional groups, other than systems, certainly senior management would normally have stepped in to resolve the issues. Typically, when the conflict occurred with the data processing function, management usually did not involve itself because the conflict was always blamed on technical issues. In reality, if technical issues were involved at all, they were of minor nature compared with organization political issues.

Thus, the first specific success factor in the integration of systems is:

Senior management of the organization must be prepared and willing to deal with the systems function like any other one: it must be managed! Technical complexity is not a reason for the failure to exercise management duties!

## 3. The Right Objectives

Mention was made of the problems experienced by the technicians who first joined the accounting departments back in the middle 1950's: the pioneers who knew the new technology and were ready to apply it. We soon discovered an unexpected problem. While we understood the vital importance of the technical people who were effective in dealing with the intricacies of computer hardware and software, we also believed that technical excellence was a primary objective. We had to learn that the objectives of the technicians were not the same as the objectives of the systems functions as a whole. We recognized that the job of the computer function was to provide services.

With this I give the second specific success factor:

While technical excellence is a vital ingredient to success, it is not the end objective of the systems function. The end objective is to provide services of superior quality to support the objectives of the total organization.

## 4. Being Part of the Management Team

The systems function, like every other organization, has an organization structure of its own, depending on size, with several levels of supervision and management. The levels range from totally technical to fully managerial, the latter providing the direction of the systems function.

To provide the needed direction in the fullest sense, the systems function manager must be able to deal with and understand business in general but particularly the business of the organization of which he is a part and which his function serves.

The systems manager must be familiar with the manufacturing, sales, marketing and financial functions – to name some – their organization, and of course, their aims and objectives. This leads to the third specific success factor:

The senior systems manager (whatever the actual title may be) must be part of the top management team, participate in its learning process and its decision making, to be able to direct the systems function in concert with, and for the benefit of all parts of the enterprise.

## 5. The Independence of the Information Function

Again, in the 1950's it was a foregone conclusion that the data processing function was part of the financial function and, most often part of the accounting group. The historical reasons are clear, the computer followed the electric accounting machinery and frequently was its replacement. It was therefore natural to keep things as they were, the systems function within the financial function.

Nevertheless, this seemingly rational arrangement created the seeds of problems which are still in existence, we are still grappling with the issue. As the capability of the computer grew and as other functions in the organization became aware of the computers potential – usually spurred on by the computer professionals – for tasks other than accounting and bookkeeping, the competition for the usually sparse resources in people and computers began.

The end effect was that those who controlled the systems function had their work done and had priority for new developments, to the detriment of others. A simple trait of human nature, self interest, often kept worthwhile computer applications in other departments from being pursued and fulfilled. I share a strong conviction that the information systems function must be a distinct and separate function on the organization chart in order to operate to the benefit of the entire enterprise. To ensure a reporting relationship which will avoid the appearance of parochial interest, a neutral spot, close to the top panagement level would do well.

The fourth specific success factor consequently is:

The requirement that the specific placement of the systems function in the organization hierarchy assure its independence. It should be responsible to top management and be in a position to serve all functions within the organization without undue pressure from any one part.

## 6. Communications as a Key to Success

These comments have dealt largely with organizational even political issues which surround the technology which intruded into the body of the organization. As important as it may be to recognize these organization political issues – and remember that technical people tend to deny interest or involvement in political issues – we have yet to address another subject dealing with the product, rather than the function.

In the development stage of a system, the designers - systems analysts, programmers etc. - base their design plan, usually called requirements specifications, on frequent and detailed information exchanges with the users. From the very beginning we have assumed that this interchange will produce the desired end results, namely the information needed to fully describe the proposed and desired system.

Unfortunately it took some time to learn that these vitally necessary communications did not take place as expected. It was assumed that the users would be able to communicate their needs clearly, describe how they perform their tasks, and that this information would be accurate as well as complete. We learned soon enough that our assumptions were wrong. It turned out that people, particularly those most familiar with routine tasks, are very likely to misrepresent them. Of course this is not done purposely but is due to so-called over-familiarity. This idea is not easily accepted, but there has been research done into the subject of over-training, which is closely related. It indicated that exhaustive training and consequently over-familiarity and over-confidence with procedures, lead to serious errors during emergencies - the very reason for the training. In any case, the unfortunate result of the poor communications is all around dissatisfaction at best, and an unusable system at worst.

This should not give the impression that the systems designers have no guilty part in this unhappy situation. Quite the contrary, they contribute more than their share to the problem. Frequently, particularly inexperienced designers will assume that they know as much as the user, or worse, believe that they already know what the user wants. Their interpretations of the user needs, and equally dangerous, their improvements on what they think the user wanted, creates vast problems.

As the system progresses and seems ready for implementation, the user (and sometimes the designers as well) find that the new, eagerly awaited system is not quite what they had hoped for or expected. The disappointment on everyone's part is great and the failure of the systems developers (as it will be perceived by everyone in the whole organization) will be communicated to everyone. The reputation and the functional role of the systems function is thus damaged, and this affects the progress of systems in the whole organization. Systems failure are very costly and disruptive to everyone, individually and to the whole organization. They may well delay the development of other systems and create fear of failure in the developers and even the users.

Thus, there is a fifth specific success factor: It is easy to correct a technical error but impossible to change the design direction of a system without totally re-doing it. The communications between systems designers and users, their openness and clarity are the key to successful systems. It is essential that everyone is cooperating on reaching full understanding and agreement on what the system is designed for and what it is to do - in the greatest possible detail.

## 7. Stylistic Fit

Probably least recognized, but nonetheless important is the issue that systems must not only function properly but function befitting the style and “organizational climate” of the user. We really need to be sensitive to this factor and indeed use our senses rather than hard analytical tools to accomplish this fit. It is even difficult to put into a concise description, however we characterize it as “the way the user usually does things” and that it “feels right”.

Thus, specific success factor number six: In addition to the obvious technical and operational quality characteristics of systems, it is important that they also fit into the style of the organization. Without this fit, integration of the system into the user organization cannot be achieved.

This paper is based on insights acquired through experience – usually the hard way. This learning mode can, and should be, supplemented by personal interchanges, professional meetings and peer groups, as well as perusal of the broadly available literature. Together, these should achieve a level of understanding needed to effectuate integration of computer technology into the organization for the benefit of and for better management of enterprises.
