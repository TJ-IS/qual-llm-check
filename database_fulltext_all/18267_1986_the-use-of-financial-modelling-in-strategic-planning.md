# The Use of Financial Modelling in Strategic Planning

G.M. Brooke and N.M. Duffy

Graduate School of Business Administration, University of the Witwatersrand, P.O. Box 31170, 2017 Braamfontein, South Africa

The focus of this study is the application of computerised financial modelling technology to the strategic planning function of certain industrial and commercial organisations. The purpose of the research was to establish whether a new generation of modelling technologies and concepts, as expressed by the notion of decision support systems, had been successful in resolving the problems experienced with previous generations of computer models. The research was based on a structured questionnaire completed by 51 selected users of packaged financial modelling systems. The results indicate that many problems have indeed been resolved by the new technology which emphasises the direct involvement in modelling activity of management responsible for the strategic management function and points to the key role of the “implementer” who provides the necessary interface. It is concluded that the management of financial modelling as an organisational discipline is now of increased importance.

Keywords: Financial modelling, Decision support systems, Strategic planning.

## 1. Introduction

Financial modelling, taken broadly as the application of computer programming techniques to corporate financial planning and decision making, emerged as a distinct phenomenon during the 1960s when third generation computers with high-level programming capabilities came into general commercial use in the United States and the United Kingdom [4]. Management scientists recognised the potential of the computer as a planning tool and began to develop programmed simulations of company operations; these simulations came to be known as “corporate models”.

A period of rapid growth followed but by the early 1970s the shortcomings of corporate financial models were becoming evident. They were large, cumbersome and inflexible; expensive to develop, maintain and run; required input and produced output in enormous volumes; and were neither understood nor supported by operational managers. At the same time computer and modelling technologies were changing. Cheaper, faster computers providing interactive programming facilities were becoming available, especially in the form of computer bureaux.

General-purpose computer languages were being replaced by purpose-built modelling and planning systems which vastly simplified the process of model development and made computer modelling accessible to non-specialist personnel such as accountants and planners.

The combination of new technology and new perceptions of how computer could be applied to managerial problems led to the coining of the phrase “Decision Support Systems” (DSS) to describe this new approach. This concept attempted to draw together everything that had been learned about managers, models and decision making, and to create a disciplined framework within which systems could be developed to provide “decision support”.

The basic purpose of the research presented here is to evaluate this new approach to computer modelling at the level of corporate finance. Given the nature of the difficulties experienced with the early generation of “corporate models” and the popularity of the DSS concept, it needs to be established whether DSS has overcome these difficulties.

The specific objectives of the research were to:

(1) Evaluate how successful the DSS approach has been in practice in encouraging the use of financial modelling for strategic planning purposes;

(2) Identify the problems experienced with the implementation of DSS technology; and

(3) Suggest how these problems, if any, might be overcome.

## 2. Definitions

The purpose of this section is to define key terms which will be used throughout the paper. Three key terms have been chosen – “financial modelling", "strategic planning" and "decision support systems". These terms are used to refer to systems and concepts that form the theoretical nucleus upon which the research is based. None of the terms is amenable to really precise definition although all are widely used both in the literature and in practice.

## 2.1. Financial Modelling

Few authors listed in the References attempt an explicit definition of “financial modelling”, or even use the term at all. There is a considerable range of phrases used to denote the application of computer programming techniques to corporate financial planning and decision making $[5,7,8,9,13,14,16,20,21,24,25,27,28,30,31,36]$ and no clear or systematic terminology is evident. For the purposes of the research undertaken here the definitions of Neild $[28]$ have been adopted. Neild proposes two definitions of a financial model:

(1) “Strictly speaking, a financial model is any system of written statements, mathematical or merely qualitative, which relates to the money flows of all or part of an organisation’s activities.”

(2) “…the term financial model will be applied only to mathematical models which are capable of assessing the effects of changes in the organisation’s operations on the balance sheet, profit and loss account, budget, cash flow or funds flow of the company or one of its divisions. Usually these models will be computerized.”

## 2.2. Strategic Planning

Anthony [1] gives this definition of strategic planning which is used as the standard for the present research:

“Strategic planning is the process of deciding on objectives of the organization, on changes in these objectives, on the resources used to attain these objectives, and on the policies that are to govern the acquisition, use, and disposition of these resources.”

The significance of this definition is that it delimits the application area of financial modelling for research purposes. This implies that the four decision areas mentioned in fact comprise the use of financial modelling in strategic planning.

## 2.3. Decision Support Systems

The term DSS is used extensively in the literature and by vendors of financial modelling systems. In essence a decision support system is an interactive, computer-based system providing easy access to decision models and information to support decision making [6]. Financial modelling systems are therefore the subset of DSS relating to corporate financial management.

## 3. Historical Review

Hayes and Nolan [15] provide a useful review of the history of computerised “corporate” modelling in the USA. Three phases of modelling are identified:

## 3.1. Phase 1: 1956 to 1963

This period was characterised by the development of operating models of specific processes using second generation computer technology. The modellers were operations researchers who, having developed successful operating models, attempted to extend modelling upwards (in “bottom-up” fashion) to corporate planning level. The failure of this approach derived from two basic factors:

(1) Planning models are not operating models.

(2) Technicians did not understand the managerial planning process.

## 3.2. Phase 2: 1964 to 1969

The arrival of third generation computer technology prompted a new “top-down” approach to model development – in other words a conscious attempt to simulate the operations of the total organisation as viewed from a strategic planning perspective. The modellers were still technically-oriented (management scientists and systems analysts) and tended to design monolithic models requiring vast amounts of input data and producing voluminous outputs. The Sun Oil model [7] was a classic model of this period. The failure of this second approach may be summarised as follows:

(1) Monolithic models are inflexible and expensive to run and maintain.

(2) Managers cannot comprehend large, complex models.

## 3.3. Phase 3: 1970 Onwards

During this period the availability of interactive computer systems and more user-oriented languages such as BASIC and APL enabled managers themselves to develop small ad hoc models for personal rather than corporate use using “inside-out” design (that is, model development proceeds randomly according to user priorities and understanding).

## 3.4. More Recent Developments

Hayes and Nolan [15] were writing too early to comment on the emergence of packaged modelling systems which appear to have become a noticeable phenomenon in the USA from the mid-1970s onwards. SIMPLAN, one of the first such systems to appear on the market, was first installed in 1974 [3].

In the UK, Grinyer [10] reports a very similar pattern although it appears that modelling systems had already become widespread by 1975 [12]. The impact of this trend from general-purpose languages to packaged modelling systems was to emphasise the characteristics of the “inside-out” phase, since computer facilities were becoming increasingly accessible to managers involved in the corporate planning process.

The next period in financial modelling history begins in January 1977 when a “Conference on Decision Support Systems” was held in San Jose, California [5]. The significance of this conference was that it marked the beginning of the DSS movement which attempted to draw together everything that had been learned about computer modelling systems and corporate decision making and to provide a theoretical basis for further study and research. DSS rapidly became a popular concept, generating a steady stream of journal articles and seminars, while vendors of modelling systems began to refer to their software packages as “Decision Support Systems”.

## 3.5. Financial Modelling Developments

A parallel development related to the evolution of financial modelling was the advent during 1978 of the microcomputer and VISICALC, the first "spreadsheet" package which provided an elementary modelling capability in the form of a display matrix with user-specified relationships between rows columns and elements. The importance of this development could be summarised as follows [4]:

(1) Personal computing for managers became much cheaper.

(2) The computing facility was entirely under the manager's control.

(3) There was no need to learn a programming language in order to use the system.

As the power and capacity of microcomputers increased, modelling systems similar to, but less sophisticated than, those available on mainframe computers and minicomputers began to appear on the market – including compatible subsets of mainframe systems. At the present time, therefore, financial modelling is progressing in three areas:

(1) In theory, via the DSS school of thought.

(2) In practice, via packaged modelling systems.

(3) Via “spreadsheet” systems such as LOTUS 1-2-3.

## 4. Decision Support System Overview

DSS represents an attempt to formulate a theoretical basis for the application of computer technology to managerial planning and decision making [18]. The concerns which gave rise to the DSS movement centred on the apparent failure of prior attempts in this direction to yield significant results. Views which exemplify this perception include the following:

"Top managers are not finding contemporary models to be useful in aiding the strategic planning process." [13]

"The high hopes and optimistic forecasts for computerised information systems in supporting decision-making processes have not been fulfilled. This is particularly true with regard to the top management levels." [29]

Even in the early days of modelling the fundamental reasons put forward to explain this failure were expressed as follows:

(1) The isolation of managers from the modelling process [24].

(2) The unstructured and unstable nature of the strategic planning environment [13].

Although many other factors are quoted in the literature, for example $[15]$ , it was these two which the DSS school addressed as being the primary obstacles to making computer technology useful to managers in their decision making activities $[18,19,20]$ . To overcome these problems it was clear that a new approach was required which would:

(1) Bring managers and modellers closer together.

(2) Be sufficiently flexible to adapt to the ill-defined and changing needs of the strategic planning process.

This new approach was DSS – an essentially technological solution in which the major emphasis was on improved hardware and software facilities rather than on organisational and human disciplines. Although some recognition is given to these aspects $[29,35]$ this was mainly because a DSS was regarded very much as a personal, rather than an organisational, tool $[18]$ . Even today Klein and Hirschheim $[22]$ go as far as to argue that:

"The challenge, it would appear to us, is to steer DSS development through the proverbial mine field of power and knowledge imbalances, collective vs individual interests, incongruencies, and consensus formation difficulties. The lesson to be learned is that DSS growth and development may be hazardous to organizational health."

The more DSS gets to grips with the needs of senior managers, the more problems of this type are emerging. The elusive nature of the true meaning of the word “problem” [23] and ongoing uneasiness with existing models of decision making [2] are only two examples of what this Pandora’s box is producing.

The research problem in hand is, therefore, to evaluate in practice the DSS approach in the specific area of financial modelling. To achieve this the following questions need to be answered:

(1) Do financial, or other, managers use financial modelling systems as personal tools?

(2) How closely are senior managers involved in the modelling process?

(3) To what extent is the use of financial modelling integrated into the strategic planning process?

(4) How successful has the DSS approach been in encouraging the use of financial modelling for strategic planning purposes?

(5) What problems, if any, have been experienced with the implementation of DSS in the financial area?

(6) Is it possible to identify solutions to such problems?

Against the proliferating use of financial modelling [26], the answers to the above questions will be a strong indication whether DSS is meeting some of its major objectives, at least in the financial area, or in danger of becoming yet another failed attempt at making computers directly useful to senior management.

## 5. The Research Approach

The following commercially available financial modelling systems with sizeable user bases were selected from the South African Computers Users Handbook:

FCS-EPS

IFPS

SIMPLAN

AS

MODEL

With the assistance of the vendors, responsible personnel within the user organisations were identified and the lists of these users then constituted the research sample. A questionnaire (see Appendix) was mailed to 75 individuals identified as users of financial modelling systems by the system vendors. In a few cases more than one user was identified within a single company. Some 69 companies were involved. A total of 51 completed questionnaires were returned, which represents a 68% response.

Some underlying assumptions and constraints resulting from this approach need to be mentioned:

(1) There is an implicit assumption that the organisations most likely to have used financial modelling techniques at the strategic planning level were those which were users of the modelling systems identified on the “short list” of selected systems. The disadvantage of this is that it excludes users of other computer-based tools from the scope of the research. Bearing in mind the fact that packaged modelling systems, or DSS generators [34], are specifically designed for computer-based modelling applications (and especially financial modelling applications) whereas other tools such as APL which could be used for modelling purposes are really general purpose programming languages, or DSS tools [34], the assumption does not appear unreasonable. There is also evidence to support the view that the impact of commercial modelling languages on the decision making process is greater than that of general programming languages [33].

(2) The assessment of the impact of financial modelling on strategic planning was by means of questions after the event unlike the ongoing observations of Scott Morton [32].

(3) It is possible that the respondents, being active users of financial modelling systems might have been more favourably disposed towards these systems than some other sample.

The method of analysing the collected data is non-parametric, that is there has been no attempt to take a random sample from a total modelling population and apply statistical tests to derive generally applicable results. This non-parametric approach is that used in all of the surveys mentioned in this paper except for the research of King and Rodriguez [21] which was confined to a sample of managers attending a business school programme.

In accordance with this approach, the results of the research are presented in summarised tabular form.

## 6.1. Background of the Respondents

The industrial or commercial sectors within which the 51 respondents operated were very wide-ranging with the main concentrations being in finance (8), manufacturing and distribution (7), motor (4), chemicals and oils (4) and mining (4). The respondents' companies ranged in size from annual turnovers of under R50 million (7) to over R2000 million (3). In ten cases the annual turnover was not disclosed.

## 6.3. Frequency of Use

Since the research is concerned with the relationship between the strategic planning function and the use of financial modelling, it was important to test the frequency with which the system was used (in conjunction with other tests) to isolate irregular or infrequent use which might indicate a lack of involvement or commitment to the system. This does not imply that strategic planning is regarded as being “regular and frequent”: the rationale is rather that the frequency of use would affect closeness of managerial involvement in the process.

From the data in Table 2 it is clear that very few respondents may be classified as “infrequent” users: 51% of them used their systems daily and 88% monthly. This test alone does not isolate strategic planning usage from other uses and hence we cannot conclude that the systems are used daily or monthly for managerial decision making (in contrast to other lower level tasks). The Hough study [17] found that of 130 managers who had heard of DSS, only 39 did not use it at all, 74 used it indirectly or occasionally and 17 used it directly or constantly – a somewhat weaker degree of usage, probably due to the higher organisational level of the respondents in the latter study.

## 6.7. Perceived Financial Modelling Benefits

The purpose of this section of the questionnaire was to establish the extent to which the benefits of the new technology had been realised in practice, according to users' evaluations and perceptions. The list of randomly ordered perceived benefits included both relevant and irrelevant categories of benefit in an attempt to reveal users' comparative assessments.

It should be emphasised that these are perceived benefits. On the basis of users' perceptions, however, it appears from Table 6 that the technology lends improved efficiency, rather than improved effectiveness, to the financial modelling function. These perceived benefits closely resemble the benefits identified in the Duffy and Hough study [6].

In addition, the involvement of management in the modelling process is perceived to be a low priority benefit relative to other factors.

## 7. Interpretation of Results

Considering the results in general, the following points may be made:

(1) Within the respondents' organisations, financial modelling is a widespread and frequently performed function which covers a range of financial applications and involves a range of personnel.

(2) Financial modelling is not a function which is limited to any particular financial application, nor is it performed by any particular person within the organisation structure. It appears to be a multidisciplinary activity which is adapted to users' functional requirements.

(3) The emphasis in financial modelling is on descriptive rather than evaluative applications, implying that financial modelling has as yet only limited application to strategic decision making.

(4) The involvement of management at the strategic planning level in the modelling process is not direct but rather operates indirectly through support staff who provide the key interface between the modelling system and management. These support staff are typically immediately below the strategic planning level and the bulk of financial modelling activity is performed by them.

(5) The new technology has had the effect of improving the technical efficiency of modelling, but it is uncertain whether similar improvements have been made in terms of the quality of the results. It is suggested that one of the reasons for this is that financial modelling has remained a technology rather than become an organisational discipline.

(6) It is clear that even with the new tools researched here, model development is still considered a time-consuming activity and hence cannot always respond to the exigencies of strategic management.

## 8. Conclusion

Amongst other things DSS aims to resolve the problems which have been experienced in applying computer modelling techniques to managerial planning and decision making. A basic method of achieving this was to develop a set of concepts and tools which would enable managers to become much more involved in the modelling process so that they could formulate and model their decisions directly on the computer system. The research resulted in qualified confirmation of the success of the DSS approach in encouraging the use of financial modelling for strategic planning purposes, but the role of the “implementer” is a key factor in determining how, and possibly whether, the DSS succeeds in practice.

Many perceived technical benefits are realised as a result of financial modelling systems and this has led to an increased demand for modelling resources and facilities as the range of applications has increased. The greatest need that has emerged, however, is for improving the organisational effectiveness of financial modelling systems and for ensuring that modelling is an activity which is integrated with other organisational functions rather than treated as a “standalone” process.
