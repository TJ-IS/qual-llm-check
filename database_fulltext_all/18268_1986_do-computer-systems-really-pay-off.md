# Do Computer Systems Really Pay-off?

T. Lincoln

Senior Consultant, IBM United Kingdom Ltd., 389 Chiswick High Rd., London W4 4AL, UK

Over recent years IBM-UK has worked with a number of other organisations to appraise the financial returns achieved from computer systems. Using a standard methodology called SESAME, approximately 170 applications across a range of industries have been reviewed to date. This consistent base of information allows senior executives to compare the financial performance of computer systems with other investments; to set reasonable financial targets for new systems development; and to judge the 'return on investment' from their computer systems with that achieved by other companies. This paper provides an overview of the SESAME methodology, discusses some results of individual studies and summarises the general conclusions emerging from this work.

Keywords: Cost-Benefit Analysis, SESAME, System Effectiveness, Post-Audit, Systems Benefits, Pay-off

After obtaining a Ph.D at London University, Tim Lincoln joined IBM-Hursley Laboratories in 1968. There he gained experience as a project manager and had two European assignments including planning manager of IBM's European Systems Research Institute. On his return, he helped establish IBM-UK's consulting function where he now works exclusively with senior executives concentrating on strategic planning, investment appraisal and IS effectiveness evaluation.

For this work he has gained many awards including the first Outstanding Technical Achievement award within IBM-Europe.

Tim Lincoln has been a Visiting Fellow of the Oxford Centre For Management Studies for 10 years, is a member of an IFIP technical committee, and has been a member of a UK government working party on computer systems appraisal. He is internationally recognised as an authority on the financial appraisal of computer systems and has consulted and lectured widely on this subject both in Europe and the Middle East.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the following scenario.

A Chief Executive rings his Financial Director:

"Ernest, I've just been reviewing the latest budget requests. Do you know the second biggest line item this year, about $15\%$ of the total, is related to information technology in one form or another. Why do we seem to be spending more every year when every day I read in the press that the price of technology is falling by $40\%$ p.a.? Tell me Ernest, do you really think we are getting value for money?".

At this stage there's a long pause as the Financial Director tries to remember the arguments put to him by his DP Manager.

“Well Frank, you have to remember that the hardware costs are only a small proportion of our total expenditure on technology. People costs are rising, the cost of the software is going up all the while, and of course we need bigger and bigger machines every year to cope with the growth of existing applications and the new requests which have come in from the users”.

The chief executive is not impressed - he can sense soft, woolly rationalisations.

"All of this may be true Ernest, but we can't just keep spending more and more. Before I agree to these new budget requests I'd like to be convinced that we really are getting value for money from our existing investment. I have a feeling that our DP Manager is obsessed with purchasing the latest technology toys. I would like you to investigate the situation and let me have a report before the next budget committee meeting".

Discussions similar to this have occurred in many companies over the last few years. Despite the wide use of cost-benefit forecasting to justify proposed system investments, executives remain sceptical of the level of benefits actually achieved. Partly this can be explained by the uncertainties inherent in the cost-benefit forecasting process. User's reluctance to commit to future savings, previous large cost overruns, arbitrary estimates of system life, risk, and inflation rates all erode the credibility of any forecast. However in addition management must bear part of the blame. All too frequently there is a lack of financial disciplines applied to the system development process; it is often unclear who is accountable for benefit achievement; it is rare to find a DP plan integrated with business plans; and user benefits are frequently declared as ‘intangible’ and rarely post-audited.

Such a situation reinforces suspicion in executive's minds that benefits from computer systems are speculative and probably marginal. Increasingly we see a reluctance to invest further until concrete evidence is available that existing system investments have produced reasonable financial returns.

This analysis is supported by a recent report sponsored by the UK Department of Trade and Industry [1] reviewing the barriers and opportunities associated with information technology. It concluded that “the main barriers (to the further use of information technology) are the lack of appropriate cost-benefit techniques and the need to consolidate previous investments”. Previous analysis of such barriers within individual companies in the mid-70's [2,3], led to the establishment of a long-term IBM-UK programme, termed ‘SESAME’ (Systems Effectiveness Study And Management Endorsement) designed to explore computer investment appraisal issues. This paper will describe the type of SESAME studies undertaken, review some of the results achieved, and will outline some of the overall conclusions which are now emerging.

## 2. The Contentious Comparator

A cost-benefit analysis is essentially a comparison between two states. Proposed new system costs and benefits are usually compared with those of current systems whether they be manual, partly computerised or fully computerised. Post-audits of established systems, however do not have such an obvious basis for comparison and it is essential to decide what the comparator will be before undertaking a study.

## 2.1. Measured Impact on Financial Performance

Perhaps the ideal comparison is that of the same organisation before and after the system was introduced. The question being asked in then “Has the system brought an improvement in overall financial performance?” Unfortunately, in the majority of cases studied, the environment changes sufficiently during system implementation to make a simple before and after comparison meaningless.

This is illustrated by a recent study of a hotel management system. Before implementation the hotel occupancy rate was approximately 68%. In the 12 months following implementation this rose to about 87%. However during the same period key exchange rates moved significantly, tourism increased by 20%, and other changes occurred affecting the management processes within the hotel. So it would have been absurd to claim the total occupancy increase as a system benefit. Attempts to compare occupancy increases across different hotels, some of which introduced the system and other which did not, merely demonstrated that each hotel is unique and responds to environmental changes in different ways. It is perhaps worth noting that even if the environment had not changed significantly merely comparing overall financial performance over one or two years would introduce the possibility of ignoring important implications which had yet to produce an measurable financial impact.

## 2.2. Comparison against an Alternative System

If then simple before and after comparisons are usually not helpful what other questions can be asked which may provide insights? An alternative approach is to ask “What would have happened if the system under review had not been implemented?” This implies an attempted reconstruction of the state of affairs assuming either an alternative system is in place or the activity was not undertaken.

This is by no means a straight-forward task and two fundamental questions immediately arise. The first concerns the basis for comparison and the options will be include the previous computer system, alternative technology, or manual systems. The second concerns the level of service that will be assumed and the options range from the same level of service provided by the current system to not undertaking the activity at all. The answers provided to these questions significantly effect the type of analysis and credibility of results.

Not all computer systems lend themselves to this type of analysis. Strategic systems are usually so interlinked with business decisions that to dissociate the two becomes virtually impossible.

Experience has shown that after reviewing the options and thinking though the implications, most senior executives require an initial analysis against an essentially manual alternative system with a level of service that could be expected from a manual system. This is most interesting since it suggests that many executives still query whether the use of computer technology is beneficial even after many years of computer usage. It might be thought that a more obvious comparison base would be the previous computer system. In this case the incremental investment decision is being tested. However many executives are content to leave incremental technology decisions to their computer management team while still retaining a concern about the use of computers for certain tasks.

While comparison against a manual base is not the choice of all executives, it does provide a number of positive advantages. It provides a bedrock comparison which is consistently applicable to most situations and can be viewed as the systems equivalent of zero based budgeting; it clearly highlights those computer applications (if any) whose objectives could be met at less cost by a manual alternative system; and it has the pragmatic advantage of having being used over a number of years in many different companies and has provided insights to senior executives which they found valuable.

The use of a consistent approach across a large number of applications also provides the capability of developing a statistical base. As can be imagined the availability of a data-base of financial results is of considerable interest to senior executives since it allows them to judge their performance against a wider population and to begin to set targets for improvement.

## 3. SESAME

## 3.1. SESAME Studies

The SESAME Programme started as a series of individual cost-benefit post-audits with the first study being conducted by IBM-UK and British Aircraft Corporation in the mid-70s $[4,5]$ . It has now evolved into a co-ordinated attempt to build a data base of proven financial returns gained from the use of Information Technology. A SESAME study focuses on systems that have been implemented for at least 12 months and identifies in considerable detail the full costs and benefits experienced to-date. Once these are established, projections will be made over the expected system life to a maximum of 5 years.

The approach adopted by an individual study will be tailored to the specific requirements. Where a ‘before and after’ comparison is valid this will be undertaken. In most cases however the approach has to be a comparison against an alternative system and SESAME is essentially neutral on the question of which comparison base is to be used. The approach merely requires that the sponsor of the study selects a basis for comparison with full awareness of the implications of his decision.

It worth perhaps positioning SESAME in relation to other evaluation techniques. SESAME is essentially a bottom-up approach, based on individual case-studies, as contrasted with top-down studies of the business impact of information technology [6,7,8]. It should be clear that SESAME does not attempt to evaluate the full implications of a computer system. There may well be social or organisational implications for example which a full and detailed evaluation would wish to consider. Such full evaluations pose significant difficulties as discussed recently by 'Hirscheim and Smithson' [9]. SESAME merely sets out to explore one dimension of the evaluation process in the hope that the results will be of some interest in themselves and will add to a fuller appreciation of the impact of computing systems on organisations.

## 3.2. Cost Allocation

A SESAME study seeks to identify all significant costs associated with each system under review. Cost categories typically include system development, operation and maintenance, user analysis, training, data input and data analysis; management time directly associated with system development and operation; and all related overheads.

There are of course situations where accurate records have not been maintained but it is usually found that base records (e.g. timesheets) and careful estimating enables an acceptable cost profile to be built-up. The difficulties of precise cost allocation are well known especially where a common resource is involved. This is particularly true if allocation within arbitrary boundaries (e.g.

organisation) is required. SESAME studies however are concerned with overall cost-effectiveness and it is usually possible for system boundaries to be defined which are meaningful to management and allow fairly straight-forward cost allocation. The objective is to produce a cost profile which is accepted by senior executives as realistic and comprehensive. To this end more recent studies include an audit of the cost profile by the internal audit function to ensure all reasonable costs have been included.

## 3.3. Benefit Identification

The SESAME approach identifies benefits by asking users to consider how they would meet their current objectives without the help of the system under review. The basis for comparison is defined before-hand and reviewed with senior executives as discussed in section 2. Extensive user interviews are held in which every attempt is made to ensure users seriously think through the implications of their answers. The user interviews employ questionnaires, based on a proven standard but customised to individual requirements, covering the following areas:

\- System usage/perceived importance, satisfaction

\- User development and operational costs

\- System problems – financial impact of problems

\- Costs/Benefits associated with alternative system

\- Expected growth

\- User requested enhancements of current system

\- Comments/quotes concerning system performance.

Interviews are documented and users asked to review the documentation, amend it as necessary, and to sign-off that it represents their considered views.

Experience has shown that it is very rare for users to be unable to identify with a reasonable alternative system – even if essentially manual – and to specify the impact on resources that such a system would have. Multiple interviews provide a check on individual assessment and reviews with senior management ensure that unrealistic estimates are challenged.

The resource implications of an alternative system depend of course on the use being made of the current computer system. In a few cases users are confident that they could switch from the current system to even a manual system with little increase, and sometimes even a reduction, in resources. In the majority of cases however users feel that the introduction of a manual system, if that is the basis for comparison, would require more people and would be accompanied by additional penalties. Typical examples of these are reduction of competitiveness, increased inventories, additional scrap etc. Users are asked to quantify the impact of these additional penalties (if they exist) separately from the headcound implications, using past statistics (e.g. inventory levels) wherever available and these estimates are subject to considerable analysis and senior management review before they are accepted.

## 3.4. Analysis and Presentation

Experience has shown that no single financial measure adequately describes the full financial performance of a computer application. SESAME studies generally quote the break-even point, mean benefit/cost ratio over system life and internal rate of return for all applications reviewed.

The output from a full SESAME study will typically be a presentation to the Board of Directors covering the following:

1. Detailed analysis of systems cost and benefits profile over the expected life of the system and under alternative growth scenarios as appropriate.

2. User assessment of the systems effectiveness with key problem and critical success factors analysis.

3. User requested system enhancements with "first cut" future costs and benefits forecasts.

4. Hardware and budget implications of predicted growth and future enhancements.

5. Management recommendations designed to optimise the return from their computing investment.

## 3.5 Studied Conducted

To-date SESAME studies have been run in 20 organisations. These can be classified into three major types covering 167 individual applications – see Fig. 1.

Type ‘A’ studies conduct a detailed review of all applications being run within a installation. Such studies typically will take four weeks to complete and will employ a team of 4–6 people. Type 'B' SESAME studies review very large stand-alone systems and examples include a library system within a local authority and a distribution system for a large brewery chain. These studies typically take 2–3 weeks with a team of 2–3 people. Type 'C' studies cover general facilities or services such as an information centre, personal computing and end user computing facilities. These studies typically take 1–2 weeks for 2–3 people.

## 4. Example of Cost Benefit Profiles

Within the limits of confidentiality many studies have been described in individual reports [10,11]. It is therefore not the intention here to illustrate all of the types of analyses which a SESAME study would produce. However, examples of typical cost benefit profiles are given in fig 2, 3, and 4.

## 4.1. Merchant Bank

Fig. 2 provides an interesting illustration of the dramatic growth in benefits which can occur for a personal computing application. The application in this case provides computer support to foreign exchange dealers within a Merchant Bank. The cost profile shows that a considerable period of “research and development” was required before the application was useable by the dealers. However, once implemented, the benefits grew very rapidly and exceeded the costs by a considerable margin.

The benefits in this case were overwhelmingly concerned with increased revenue and profit generation rather than cost reduction. By providing foreign exchange and bond dealers with decision-making aids to support market transactions, the bank's executives were confident that a dealer could transact more deals and that these deals would on average be more profitable. In addition it was felt that such dealing aids allowed dealing with new financial instruments (futures, options etc.) which, in their early life, tend to be more profitable than conventional instruments. Finally it was felt that the ability to deal profitably in a variety of markets brings further advantages when marketing the bank's services to prospective clients.

Even allowing for a comparatively short expected life of this application, the internal rate of return generated was approximately 400% per annum and the mean benefit to cost ratio was of the order of 15.

## 4.2. Integrated Newspaper Management System

Fig. 3 shows the cost benefit profile of an integrated set of applications concerned with the production of a daily newspaper. The applications covered sales, finance, production, editorial and location services. The company concerned was 'Eastern Counties Newspapers Ltd.' [12] who had implemented a comprehensive system based on computerised typesetting and web offset technology. The prime objectives of the system were flexibility, quality and ability to be tailored precisely to the needs of the business. The basis for comparison in this case needed to take full account of the production functions and it was not felt that a totally manual system would be realistic. However, a valid comparison could be made with a system based on paper tape photocomposition which was similar to their previous production system and still considerably in advance of most national newspaper systems. Other functions (e.g. finance) were compared with the best available noncomputerised method.

![](/api/attachments/HUHD4G68/fulltext/images/ad44f932b98ae0ea2cf774844d60c0271db450043903aa57ff2093f9fa2bff8e.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Cost-benefit profile - integrated newspaper system.

The analysis identified a base cost-benefit profile for existing systems and an additional cost-benefit profile associated with ‘direct input’ of advertising copy. The major benefits of the existing systems are associated with pricing, advertising accounts, commercial and advertising administration and production labour. The additional benefits associated with direct input are primarily in the production area but with significant additional advertising revenue.

While not as spectacular as the results shown in Fig. 2, this cost-benefit profile is still very healthy. For an overall investment of between 7.3 and 9 million pounds, the return on investment achieved is in the range of 30% per annum, for the existing applications, to 42% per annum when taking into account direct input. These returns show the significant impact that changes in working practices can have on financial returns and therefore on willingness to invest further.

## 4.3. Local Government

Fig. 4 provides an example drawn from a local government Borough. The Borough in question tends to be very motivated towards cost-effectiveness and computing is seen as a means of providing services at minimum costs consistent with management objectives. The applications in this case was developed initially to exercise control over housing rents but was extended as its value was proven.

At the time of analysis the system controlled a fortnightly collection service, managed the arrears process and controlled voids (empty houses), sales of properties and homeless persons. Planned enhancements included control of repairs and maintenance. The system is almost totally on-line with user department providing all input data.

The cost benefit profile shows a very healthy build up of benefits greatly exceeding projected costs. For clarity the effect on benefits of expected government grants are identified separately. The internal rates of return for this application were approximately 130% p.a. Without government grants, rising to 230% p.a. with government grants. The impact of such high returns on senior management's perception of computer systems was very significant and is described in some detail in a subsequent talk given by the DP Manager [13].

## 4.4. Winners and Losers

With such a wide range of applications reviewed with the SESAME technique it is possible to identify the best major investments and the worst. Fig. 5 provides such a breakdown.

It is noticeable that the same industry and organisation types appear as both winners and losers and it is clear that no one industry has the recipe for producing maximum returns from information technology. It is quite common to find in the same organisation, applications producing spectacularly high returns and applications which are a dismal financial failure. Almost always this can be shown to result from a lack of an effective financial appraisal and control process applied to computer systems. Management judgement, even steering committee consensus, is not a substitute for formal analysis.

## 5. Overall SESAME Results

It is only comparatively recently that sufficient SESAME studies have been undertaken to allow meaningful statistics to be produced. These analyses are shown in Fig. 6, 7 and 8 and they relate to the total population studied.

Fig. 6 illustrates the break-even point distribution found amongst the applications analysed. We see for example that approximately 2/3rds of all applications reviewed break-even in less than 1 year after implementation. Such rapid break-even points can come as a surprise to Financial Directors accustomed to the more leisurely break-even points of industrial investments.

Both analyses dramatically demonstrate the outstandingly good financial performance available from information technology. Average benefits exceeding costs by a ratio of 2–3 are very common. Internal rates of return in the hundreds of percent per annum are the norm rather than the exception.

When evaluating these figures it should be borne in mind that SESAME adopts prudent and cautions accounting principles, and users generally accept that SESAME results tend to understate the available return on investment. The results shown therefore in Fig. 6, 7 and 8 can be taken as base line performance. Fig. 9 summarises the overall financial picture drawn by SESAME studies and this picture looks exceedingly healthy.

Once sufficient statistics are available it is possible to look for correlation between key financial parameters. Fig. 10 illustrates this by plotting internal rates of return against development costs.

As might be expected the results are scattered and must be viewed with caution. In particular at the higher development cost range the sample size is small and conclusions must be drawn with care. However, it does appear that the vast majority of applications reviewed fall between two boundaries. The lower boundary occurs at about 25% per annum internal rate of return and is independent of development costs. The upper boundary however appears to vary with development cost as shown.

At lower levels of development cost, a high variability of internal rates of return is perhaps not unexpected. Applications developed in this range are primarily personal computing or end user computing applications. Such applications often access databases of operational data which have been developed previously to support 'core' systems and thereby 'add value' to them. It is not surprising therefore that very high internal rates of return can be generated under the right circumstances. However such applications are not in general kept under tight control and large variations in IRR can be expected.

At the other end of the development cost scale however we are dealing with very large systems which frequently break new ground. Research and development costs associated with these types of applications can be large and because of the size of the investment, project management is usually very thorough. Accordingly, where development costs are high, one would expect the risk to be reduced, possibly at the expense of limiting the overall returns available. Even in this range however, return on investment can still be expected to be between 40% and 100% per annum.

Over the period in which SESAME studies have been conducted, the author has tried to find similar financial post-audits against which these results could be compared. Individual application audits have of course been undertaken although these are rarely published, but no comparable statistical base has been identified [14]. However figures do exist originating from two very thorough cost-benefit forecasting techniques used by IBM consultants. The 'Application Transfer Team' approach has analysed very large number of applications over the last few years and in most cases predicted detailed costs and benefits. ## 6. Summary

The SESAME results described in this paper illustrate the very attractive returns on investment which can be achieved through the use of information technology. 80% of all applications reviewed were showing positive returns. In most cases, the return on investment was above 100% per annum and in some cases exceeded 1000% per annum. However, it should also be borne in mind that one in five of the applications studied were not showing sufficient financial returns to justify the original investment.

Analyses of this sort have proved very valuable for senior management. They enable them to feel more confident about the financial returns available from technology and thereby allow a more rational investment policy to be developed. Equally such analyses enable senior management to weed out those applications which are not financially viable and thereby enhance the overall return on investment available from technology. This success has lead to the recent extension of SESAME to the evaluation of the implications of complete computer failure $[17]$ .

In addition to evaluating existing applications, such results also enable targets to be set for new development. Hitherto standards have been available covering efficiency of computer applications, for example response time, availability, maintenance load etc. Now however, the opportunity arises to establish standard for effectiveness of applications in terms of return on investment. These targets can be ambitious compared with the returns available from other industrial investments.

The SESAME programme has shown that it is possible to post-audit the financial returns of computer systems. While many organisations do perform post-installation audits of major applications, it is unusual for these audits to evaluate thoroughly the benefits achieved in addition to the costs. The absence of such hard post-audit financial data has worked against the interests of both technology professionals and the organisations in which they work. Many discussions with senior executives have convinced the author that all major computer applications should have clear cost-benefit targets established as part of the planning process and should be automatically post-audited.

The database of financial results described in this paper will be enhanced by conducting further studies. The SESAME methodology will be used in these studies to ensure consistency of results and it is hoped that in this way standards will be developed to allow objective measures of financial success and failure. It is believed that the availability of such a database will answer some of the legitimate concerns of senior management who will thereby be encouraged to invest further in those areas where proven return can be achieved.
