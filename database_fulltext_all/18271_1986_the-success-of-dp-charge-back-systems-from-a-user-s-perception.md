# The Success of DP Charge-Back Systems from a User's Perception

François Bergeron

Section Systèmes d'information Organisationnels, Faculté des Sciences de l'administration, Université Laval, Québec, Québec, Canada G1K 7P4

The factors related to charge-back system success were studied in 53 large and medium-sized organizations in the United States. The 263 user-managers who completed the questionnaires identified important organizational and charge-back system characteristics associated with charge-back system success. Successful charge-back systems were observed when 1) the users were held accountable for results, 2) they were involved in the preparation of data-processing budget, 3) they had authority over data-processing activities, 4) they were charged in proportion to the services used and 5) their charges carried meaningful information.

Keywords: Charge-back system, chargeout, budget reports, information systems, behavioral accounting.

François Bergeron is an assistant professor of Information Systems at Laval University, Québec, Canada. He holds an M.Sc. in Economics and an MBA from Laval, and he received his Ph.D. in Information Systems from the Graduate School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles. His current research interests focus on data-processing charge-back systems, information centers and critical success factors.

## 1. Introduction

Data-processing charge-back systems are mechanisms aimed at controlling the allocation and use of data-processing activities and services in organizations. The ever growing use of computers in all areas of business justifies the implementation of such systems. Indeed, many organizations spend as much as three to five percent of their revenues on data-processing. In some cases, this represents a very large sum of money.

A recent survey [7], found that 58% of the organizations charged their users for data-processing services. The charge-back (charge-out) mechanisms varied from simple overhead allocation of costs to very detailed charges with a real transfer of funds from user departments to the data-processing center. Another study [3] conducted in 1984, revealed that 26.5% of large organizations charged their users on a frequent and detailed basis. Furthermore, 15.6% of the organizations not charging their users for data processing services expected to do so within the following year. Finally, the positive attitude of end-users toward charge-back systems in their computing environment [26,27] and the need to control this new sector of activity suggest that these systems will continue to be implemented in the future.

Various objectives of charge-back systems are mentioned in the literature. The most frequently cited are: cost control, cost recovery, demand control and resource allocation $[5,12,19,20,33]$ . User involvement in information systems design has also been mentioned. While in most cases data centers are organized as costs centers, this is not always the case and some of them are structured as profit centers $[25]$ . They charge their users for profit purposes.

Unfortunately, not much is known about the success of these charge-back systems. Indeed, the few studies that attempted to assess the effectiveness of charge back systems generally brought mixed results. Nolan [21] explained a very small portion of the variation in user attitudes toward information systems which he thought was related to the characteristics of their charge-back system while Olson and Ives [23] could not show the existence of a link between the quality of charges and user attitude toward the information systems personnel.

This study measures the success of a charge-back system in terms of user behavior upon the receipt of the charges (bills, budget reports) from the data-processing department. A successful charge-back system can be defined as one in which the users analyze their data-processing charges with the intent to control the spending of data-processing resources. It is only if this activity is carried out that the charge-back system can bring some benefits. This is in opposition to charge-back systems in which charges are ignored by users who do not try to control their data-processing costs. The aim of this study is therefore to determine the data-processing charge-back and organizational characteristics related to the success of the data-processing charge-back system in terms of user behavior with respect to the charges (budget reports).

## 2. Research Approach

## 2.1. The research model

The research model used to assess the charge-back and organizational characteristics related to charge-back system success is presented in Figure 1. The success variable has been measured in many ways in the literature. User satisfaction [13], user behavior [8] and user performance [18] are typical measures. Recently, Srinavasan [30] underlined the importance of using these measures in comparable environments. In this study, the success variable is based on a behavioral measure: the extent to which the user charged for data-processing services analyzes the charges and investigates variances, upon the receipt of data-processing charges and after, with the purpose of meeting the data-processing budget. The success variable is the dependent variable of the model.

Fig. 1. The Research Model.

Five independent variables are expected to be positively related to it. Each of them is the object of a specific hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1: User accountability for meeting the data-processing budget is positively related to charge-back system success.

Long ago, Drucker [6] identified accountability as an important dimension of the budgeting system. Later, accountability for results was also identified as a strategic aspect in information and control systems [16]. In this research, accountability is defined as the process, the need and the obligation that organization members provide management with a reasonable justification for any action related to the administration of their budgets. An accountable user is asked to explain the variations in data-processing budget and to report actions to correct any problem.

Hypothesis 2: User authority over data-processing activities is positively related to charge-back system success.

It is important that the user have the necessary authority to control the data-processing activities for which he is billed. In the information systems field, Glennon [10] explained the importance of giving users equal levels of authority and responsibility over information systems development projects. A lack of authority on information systems expenses would impede the user from adequately controlling his budget. In this study, user's authority is the user's power to decide on the quantity and type of data-processing services to be received from the data-processing department.

Hypothesis 3: Variability of costs is positively related to charge-back system success.

There is an abundance of literature on the different pricing schemes that can be used to charge users. Basically, for practitioners, several methods exist for charging users $[5]$ : actual costs, standard costs, and direct or marginal costs. Costs can also be allocated on an overhead basis, on a resource rate basis or on a cost per unit basis $[24,23]$ . However, whether they are of an engineering type $[11]$ or of an economic type $[15]$ , no optimal solution has yet emerged. The problem is due to the peculiar economic environment of computing services, where the notion of marginality of cost is difficult to apply. Although there is no generally accepted method for charging users, all methods are based on one common principle: costs charged to users should vary in proportion to use. The measure of cost variability used in this study corresponds to the level of proportionality of the charges with respect to the services received.

Hypothesis 4: The meaningfulness of information contained on the bills is positively related to charge-back system success.

The quality of information produced by an information system is an important aspect of information systems $[9,28,34,17,1]$ . Swanson $[31]$ used it as a predictor of use of information systems. More specifically, Nolan $[21]$ suggested that understandability of the charges was critical to the effectiveness of a charge-back system. More recently, meaningfulness of information was pointed out by data-processing managers as a strategic dimension of data-processing charge-back systems $[29]$ . The meaningfulness of information is the extent to which the charges received by the user are understandable, insightful, comprehensible, complete and so on.

Hypothesis 5: User involvement in data-processing budget preparation is positively related to charge-back system success.

User involvement is a well known construct in information systems for its association with information system use $[2,16,31]$ . It is expected that a similar relationship will be observed in a data-processing charge-back system environment. User involvement is the extent to which the user-manager participates in budget preparation, prior to budget approval. It should be associated with charge-back system success.

## 2.2. Methodology

The participants in the study were user-managers charged for data-processing services. The questionnaire used to test the five propositions gathered separate sets of data on two different types of costs: 1) development and maintenance and 2) operations costs. The hypotheses were tested separately for both types of costs.

The accountability, involvement, cost variability and success scales were five-point Likert-type scales whereas the authority scale was a seven-point Likert-type and the meaningfulness scale was in a semantic-differential format. The accountability, involvement and success scales were adapted from Swieringa and Moncur [32] whereas the authority and cost variability scales were devised by the author. Finally, the meaningfulness scale was based on a factor analysis of qualitative characteristics of data-processing budget reports made by Bergeron and Boyer [4]. The exact scales used in this research are presented in Appendix 3.

Scale reliabilities were tested using the Cronbach alpha technique. The number of items in each scale along with the Cronbach alpha measures for development costs and operations costs respectively are: accountability (6, 0.89; 6, 0.89), authority (7, 0.84; 6, 0.78), cost variability (4, 0.81; 2, 0.87), meaningfulness (7, 0.94; 0.7, 0.95), involvement (5, 0.91; 5, 0.91) and success (6, 0.84; 6, 0.83). These are considered to be high levels of reliability [22].

## 3. Results

The target population of this study were large and medium-sized organizations in the United States charging their internal users for data-processing services. A sample of 1,246 organizations were first contacted to inquire about the existence of a charge-back system in their organization and their willingness to participate in the study. A total of 1,093 questionnaires were then sent to 115 organizations that charged internal users for data-processing services on a frequent and detailed basis. From these organizations, 54 distributed the questionnaires to their user-managers and 263 completed questionnaires were received for a net response rate of 24.1% (263/1093). The reasons provided by the 61 organizations that did not distribute the questionnaires were as follows: business pressure (10), recent modifications in charge-back system (4), does not apply (17), no special reason (13) and no answer (17).

## 4. Discussion

All variables presented in the research model were significantly related to charge-back success. This means that successful charge-back systems were observed in organizations showing the following characteristics: the users were held accountable for results; they were involved in the preparation of data-processing budget; they had authority over data-processing activities; they were charged in proportion to the services used; and they received charges containing meaningful information. On the contrary, unsuccessful charge-back systems were observed in organizations not showing the above characteristics. These results were expected from the research model.

These variables had various degrees of importance in explaining the variations in the level of charge-back system success. The results of the regression showed that for both types of costs, accountability, involvement, and meaningfulness were the important variables whereas cost variability was important for operations costs only. Given these results, it is first suggested that the organizations hold their users accountable for results. For that purpose, the organizations should follow organizational policy with respect to user accountability for other kinds of budget, i.e., non-data-processing. The level of accountability of the user for the data-processing budget should correspond to the usual level of accountability for meeting any budget in the organization.

Secondly, efforts should be made to involve the user in the data-processing budgeting process. It is to be expected that holding the users accountable for results should encourage them to participate in budget setting activities. If users are held accountable for results, a thorough evaluation of their needs before the budget is finally approved should help them meet the budget thereafter.

Thirdly, attention should be paid to the meaningfulness of the information transmitted to users. Basically, users should be able to identify actions to be taken to correct variances from their data-processing budget. The information should be complete and presented in an understandable and instructive format. In practice, this could mean that the charges are organized on a project (system) basis. Simple algorithms of computation could also be used so that users can understand the relationship between the detailed and the total charges.

As far as cost variability is concerned, this variable explained variations in charge-back system success for operations costs only. It is less important for development costs. This may be due to the nature of operations costs versus development costs. Once approved, the operations budget cannot be modified very much. Users can hardly decrease the amount of computer services they planned without harming the normal functioning of the organization. When it is time to make some slight adjustments in the amount of operations services used, it is therefore important that costs for operations activities vary in proportion to the services. This is different for development costs since systems with different degrees of sophistication can be developed within the same budget. Furthermore, it is certainly difficult for the users to judge the variability of development costs because the quantity of services received, at least in terms of output, is a fluid notion.

Finally, the user's authority over data-processing activities did not help to explain variations in charge-back system success. This could be explained by the fact that authority is a necessary but not sufficient condition for charge-back system success. In this study, the sufficient conditions were accountability, involvement, meaningfulness and operations costs variability.

## 4.1. Limitations

It is appropriate to point out some limitations of this study. Firstly, the respondents to the questionnaire were all charged on a monthly or weekly basis. It can therefore be assumed that they received their charges on a timely basis. Different results could be obtained if organizations charging their users on a quarterly or annual basis were surveyed. Secondly, the data used in this study is based on users perceptions. The collection of objective data could have brought different results. Finally, the population of respondents come from large and medium-sized organizations in the United States. The external validity of the results is therefore limited to this type of organization.

## 5. Conclusion

This study proposed a model explaining data-processing charge-back system success. All hypotheses concerning the importance of specific organizational and budget report characteristics on charge-back system success were found to be significant in explaining the success of these systems. Some variables were also found to have a greater influence than others. Further research should concentrate on the policies and mechanisms useful to create the environment needed for successful implementation of charge-back systems.
