# An Assessment of Alternative Application Development Approaches

Mary R. Sumner

Department of Management Information Systems,

School of Business, Southern Illinois University at Edwardsville, Box 1106, Edwardsville, IL 62026-1106, USA

The purpose of this study was to determine what development approach would be selected by MIS managers to develop applications with various characteristics. Managers of information systems representing St. Louis based organizations were asked to select from three development approaches, including traditional, user-development, and microcomputer development. The findings showed that these MIS professionals had concrete ideas on what development approach was most appropriate. When the number of users and number of workstations were four or more, respondents felt that traditional development would be used. Where data security, system audit, data recovery, and data retention requirements were necessary, traditional development was also preferred. User and microcomputer development were most likely in cases where on-demand, unstructured reports were needed and where systems were departmental or personal in scope.

Keywords: User development, application development, microcomputer development, fourth generation languages.

## 1. Introduction

One of the issues arising from the rapid growth of end user computing is how to determine the most effective development approach for a given application. Now that users have tools to develop their own applications, they may be taking on projects which should be developed by using traditional methods. Software development groups sometimes find themselves completely re-designing a system which has been developed by a user on a microcomputer because that system hasn't been developed according to the firm's systems development methodology and lacks proper documentation.

## 2. Objectives of the Study

The purpose of this study is to determine what development approach is most likely to be selected by MIS managers for applications with specific characteristics. Information centers are becoming central control points for users to seek direction on appropriate development approaches, and, in turn, to find the tools and resources to implement their applications. An analysis of the most appropriate development approaches for various types of applications will provide helpful direction to both information center analysts and user managers.

## 3. Review of the Literature

A number of development approaches, including user-development, prototyping, and the use of software packages, are designed to improve application development productivity. The first approach is to have users develop their own systems. In their study of user-developed applications, Rockart and Flannery discovered that 35 percent involved report generation and inquiry/simple analysis, and 50 percent involved complex analysis. Fifty-two percent of these systems were departmental in scope and 31 percent were personal. Thirty-six percent of the data used for these applications was extracted from production data files, or copies of existing data bases [13].

On the question of the impact of user development, Robert Rosenberger argues that end-user computing consists mainly of the ill-defined, "one-shot" types of personal and departmental applications which do not significantly impact the backlog of larger, production system projects handled by DP professionals [14].

The limitations of user-developed applications are recognized. They lack documentation, procedures for validating data, audit and control, security, and backup and recovery [4]. In a study of end user computing within 21 firms, Tor Guimares found only two firms planning to establish quality assurance mechanisms for user applications, even though most of the MIS executives interviewed expected these applications to be technically deficient in comparison with systems developed by MIS personnel [7].

A second approach development is prototyping. Here, a systems analyst works with a user to build a quick system using a fourth generation language. The system is continually revised and refined as it is being used and until it is acceptable to the user [11].

Without question, the prototyping approach has benefits. A number of companies report that systems developed using this approach cost 25 percent less than those developed using the traditional approach. Prototyping is of great value when the user is uncertain about what the proposed system should do and can specify his requirements as a result of the iterative, participative process which prototyping employs [6].

A third approach to overcoming systems development bottlenecks is using software packages. These can be implemented in multiple installations and offer economies of scale in development. Modifying a package for local needs is more cost-effective than designing the system from scratch. However, if system requirements are so unique that the cost of modifying the package approximates the cost of in-house development, the latter approach should probably be used [6].

User-development, prototyping, and use of packaged software offer time and cost savings in application development. Increasingly, companies are allowing users to choose how to have their applications developed. If information systems departments do not offer guidance on this issue, users will explore all sorts of alternatives- buying packages, experimenting with fourth generation languages, and developing systems on personal computers. ## 4. Procedures for the Study

A random sample was made of St. Louis based organizations. These organizations included both large and small employers representing a variety of industries and government agencies. A questionnaire was developed and sent to the director of information systems within each organization. The recipients were asked to complete the questionnaire themselves or give it to a senior analyst who was familiar with the types of information systems being developed using fourth generation languages, microcomputer-based software, and traditional methods.

Respondents were asked to provide background information about the current application development and maintenance backlogs and methods being used to improve productivity, such as the use of fourth generation languages and microcomputer-based software. In addition, they were given a listing of application development characteristics and asked to check the development approach most likely to be used. They were given a choice among Traditional Development, User Development, and Microcomputer Development, using the definitions developed by Shomenta, Kamp, Hanson, and Simpson in their Application Approach Worksheet [15], see Table 1.

In addition, the respondents were given an opportunity to report the impacts of using fourth generation languages and microcomputer-based tools on application development productivity, including quantitative and qualitative benefits and limitations.

## 5.4. Application Development Approaches

In cases where they felt criteria in the worksheet would not be used to select a development approach, the respondents checked N/A for not applicable.

Number of Users. The first category, number of concurrent users, related to the number of people using the system at one time, either for entering data or inquiring into the system. See Table 9.

In cases where there was only one system user, microcomputer development was favored, as compared with cases with four or more users, when traditional development was favored.

Number of Locations. The number of locations referred to the number of geographic locations or sites requiring access to the system (Table 10).

About one-third of the respondents favored traditional development in cases where there were two or more geographic locations.

Number of Workstations. The number of workstations referred to the number of terminals or printers requiring access to the system (Table 11).

Processing Dynamics. Processing dynamics refers to the range of output formats. One system may use reports which are consistent and well-defined, while another may require reports that vary. See Table 13.

Respondents checked traditional development for systems with well-defined output requirements and preferred user development and microcomputer development for systems requiring on-demand reporting and “what if” types of analysis.

Data Recovery. The data recovery requirement refers to the degree of necessity for recovery of data in the event of system failure. In one situation, up-to-minute data recovery to the failed transaction may be necessary. In another system, manual recovery may be accomplished by re-creating any lost data. In another situation, automated recovery to the last backup may be sufficient. See Table 14.

In systems in which automated recovery to the failed transaction was necessary, and in cases where recovery to the previous day was needed, the respondents selected traditional development. In cases where users were responsible for manually recovering their own data, user and microcomputer development were chosen over traditional development.

## 5.5. Impacts of the Automated Tools on Productivity

One of the benefits of using fourth generation languages and microcomputer-based tools in application development is their impact on productivity. Respondents indicated that they were using tools such as NOMAD, LOTUS 1-2-3, and DBASE II in requirements definition and in systems design. A variety of report generators, such as DATATRIEVE, REPORTER and EASY-TRIEVE, were used to automate coding and testing, and various text processing tools were used to develop documentation.

When asked to estimate the approximate percentage of time saved in various phases of the systems development process as a result of using automated tools, seven of the twenty-two organizations surveyed provided estimates, while the others could not make an estimate (see Table 27).

The greatest impact on productivity was evident in the programming and testing phase. Overall, the percentage of time saved in coding and testing averaged 36 percent; the time saved in maintenance averaged 17 percent for the firms responding to this question.

Fourteen of the firms agreed that improvements in development time had resulted from using fourth generation languages and microcomputerbased software. Five respondents noted that less time was being spent on documentation. The qualitative improvements described were better understanding of users' requirements, better quality systems design, and less maintenance (see Table 28)

## 6. Summary and Recommendations

All of the firms participating in this study had some experience using fourth generation languages and microcomputer-based software. Overall, 21 percent of the data processing professionals used fourth generation tools, compared with about 13 percent of the users.

The data processing professionals surveyed had fairly concrete ideas on what development approach would be used for systems with certain characteristics. Where the number of concurrent users, number of locations, number of workstations, and number of users concurrently updating data were four or greater, respondents said, traditional development would be used. When one user in one location was maintaining data, microcomputer development was most likely.

When output was scheduled, report well-defined, and immediate access to data necessary, traditional development was the most likely approach. User and microcomputer development were used to develop on demand, unstructured, unscheduled reports.

Where data recovery, systems audit, data security, and data retention requirements were necessary, traditional development was most likely. This may reflect the attitude that user developed systems are poorly documented and lack quality assurance.

When systems were organization-wide in scope, where their data were used by multiple departments, traditional development was the approach used. Departmental and personal systems, it was felt, would be developed using fourth generation tools or microcomputers. User developed systems relied on data extracts from production data bases. In contrast, systems with high data volume, higher complexity and greater life expectancy would be designed using traditional methods.

The major impact of using fourth generation tools was felt at the programming and testing phase of systems development projects, respondents noted. The qualitative benefits of using these tools included better understanding of user requirements, less maintenance, and increased user responsibility for maintenance. The problems of using fourth generation languages in application development were high use of machine resources, lack of data integration, and programmer resistance.

The overall responses indicate that DP professionals make clear distinctions between the types of systems which should be developed using traditional methods and those which should be designed by users with fourth generation tools and microcomputer software. Those projects which have corporate wide impact, support multiple user access and concurrent updating of data, require automated recovery to failed transaction, and have high transaction volumes, it was felt, should be developed using traditional methods. User development was not an acceptable approach for systems having to meet data recovery, security, audit, and retention requirements.

Most of the managers surveyed acknowledged the need to increase the use of fourth generation languages and microcomputer-based software in application development, mainly because machine resources were ultimately going to be less expensive than people costs. Increasing user involvement in application development and in maintenance, several noted, improved user understanding of data processing.

## 7. Issues and Recommendations

The information center should provide a clearinghouse function and assist users in identifying the best development approach for their applications. When user requirements are less certain, information center analysts should use prototyping as a method to involve users in specifying their needs. These analysts should be on the look out for “common” systems which can be developed in one user area and transported to others. When commonality occurs, special attention should be paid to documenting the application.

Quality assurance of user-developed applications is an issue suggested by the findings. At present, many user systems are poorly documented. The information center should establish quality assurance guidelines covering documentation, data dictionary, backup and recovery, system security, and data retention requirements and encourage user management to promote adherence to these guidelines. Finally, data processing management should establish standards for user systems that may evolve into applications using mainframe based facilities and corporate data.

The move toward using fourth generation languages and microcomputer based tools is inevitable. In MIS organizations, there is not enough manpower to do the work. Users, equipped with automated tools, are becoming increasingly involved in application development. Yet, the expanding use of computing resources by end users must achieve some balance. The respective roles and responsibilities of DP professionals and of end users in application development must be defined so that investments in time, effort, and resources can be maximized.
