# An Investigation of Microcomputer Policies in Large Organizations

William L. Harrison
Oregon State University, USA

and

Mary Ellen E. Dick

Software Access International, USA

Most large organizations are already integrating microcomputers into their information processing activities. As this integration matures, we should expect to see fully-developed policy statements which encourage the productive use of these versatile new tools. This research, based upon extensive interviews of managers in charge of microcomputing in large organization, measures the extent to which microcomputer policies are in place and classifies the nature of the policy statements themselves.

Keywords: Microcomputers, Microcomputer policy, Organizational policy.

## 1. Introduction

Since that time, the use of personal computers in large organizations has grown dramatically, though the principal players are now the IBM PC $^{3}$ and Lotus 1-2-3 $^{4}$ . The number of personal computers in use in business organizations was in excess of four million units at the end of 1984 and was expected to be in excess of six million units by the end of 1985 [4]. Some 20 million users are estimated to have regular access to these micros.

The scale of these purchases and the anticipated level of use of microcomputers within these organizations suggests an environment within which control might be very important.

The magnitude of these numbers suggests that the acquisition and use of microcomputers within large organizations provides a fertile area for research. This effort was designed to ascertain the extent to which large organizations have developed policy statements to control the acquisition and use of microcomputers and to study the nature of those policy statements.

## 2. The Purpose of Organizational Policy

The formulation of organizational policy statements is part of an effort to control the behavior of members of the organization. It is dynamic. Policy statements are stimulated by problems which are perceived as important enough to warrant special treatment. Paine and Naumes [13] state that:

Policies may be described as broad guides for managers, supervisors and other employees for the achieving of objectives.

Policy deals with issues which (1) have wide ramifications; (2) have a long-time perspective; and (3) use critical resources toward perceived opportunities in a changing environment.

Presumably the acquisition and use of microcomputers fulfills organizational policy. The ramifications of their acquisition and use are wide. Estimates of the average level of use of microcomputers by “knowledge workers” range from 25 percent to something in excess of 50 percent. Given that these machines potentially give access to the information base, the ramifications of its misuse are wide and frightening.

It is hard to prove that microcomputers have a long-time perspective: they have been used in organizations for only about 5 years. But there are no obvious substitutes yet in the market place and virtually no one seriously expects them to disappear. It would seem, therefore, that microcomputers will be used in the long term.

The acquisition and use of microcomputers in organizations obviously consumes organizational resources: money, time, and the talent of many professionals. Their use again involves these resources and has a potentially major impact upon its information.

## 3. Microcomputer Policy Concerns

The acquisition and use of microcomputers requires an organizational policy, but the environment within which the policies will be implemented is quite unsettled. Some problems are:

1. In what ways will the traditional roles of the information processing group change as facilities are distributed throughout the organization?

2. How will new communications technologies be utilized?

3. What will be the method used for office automation?

4. In what ways will traditional information processing, communications and office automation be integrated?

5. What will be the role of end-users in the development of applications?

The development of microcomputer policies depends substantially on the answers to these questions. As a result, microcomputer policies may be expected to change significantly with time.

The Diebold group [5] identifies four primary areas which must be addressed by microcomputer policy statement: data security, data integrity, hardware and software compatibility, and cost justification. Omand [11] provides a somewhat more detailed set, as follows:

1. Data management: the accessibility, consistency, compatibility, and security of data;

2. Product proliferation: the problem of sharing data with noncompatible systems, technical support for a variety of systems, and the potential duplication of development effort;

3. Acquisition practices: the ability to leverage prices and the protection of the user from uninformed purchases;

4. Level of user maturity: protection from obsolescence and the stage of growth of the user organization:

5. Shift in skill sets: the development of applications and the integrity of that process when performed by the user organization.

Hughes [8] identifies only three questions:

1. What are appropriate uses for macros?

2. Who approves acquisition of microcomputers?

3. What are the relative roles of users and the systems group?

A set of microcomputer policy statements which attempts to cover all eventualities will probably be considered overly restrictive, because many users moved toward a microcomputer environment to take advantage of the increased freedom it promised. Policy which is excessively restrictive will be a source of frustration. Diebold [5] characterizes the challenge for policy-makers as one in which they must decide whether they should adopt a “control mentality” or a “permissive, even encouraging, environment”.

Our research was undertaken, in part, to determine the extent to which organizations have risen to the challenge. We would like to be able to measure the extent to which organizations have produced restrictive, permissive, or supportive policies. Unfortunately our measuring tools have few facile metrics with which to work; we were forced to make a subjective judgement based upon the interviewer's assessment of responses to questions. Appendix A contains an edited version of the questionnaire which guided the interviews.

## 4. Microcomputer Policy Elements - Previous Research

Keen [9] describes the philosophy of microcomputer policy as one which tries to “encourage cost-effective applications of microcomputers; allow end users every opportunity to acquire hardware and software; and coordinate the recommended software and hardware with education, consulting support and sharing of experience and applications.” He describes a set of steps for the acquisition of microcomputers. These entail: the appointment of a microcomputer coordinator; the selection of up to three approved hardware configurations and an approved list of software by the coordinator; user-initiated requests for purchase; and the assumption (by the user) of responsibility for legal, audit, security, integrity, and ethical requirements. Many of the policies reviewed in our study contain elements from this list.

Goren [6] reports on a study of microcomputer policy within organizations in the public sector. His work covered the microcomputer policies of five city governments. The major results of his study were:

1. purchases were limited to specific vendors;

2. compatibility with the organization's mainframe was required;

3. technical specifications were standardized;

4. specific operating systems were required;

5. necessary programming languages were identified;

6. an upper bound was placed on total system cost;

7. a purchase approval procedure was stipulated. The results of our study seem to confirm this.

## 5. Research Methodology

Our results were obtained from a study of organizations selected from a sample that included the Fortune 500, the Forbes 500, the Fortune Service 500, the largest US educational institutions, and large agencies of government. Organizations were selected at random from the sample. Within each organization we tried to find the person whose function included that of “microcomputer manager”. Questionnaire-based interviews were held with approximately 100 such managers. Seventy-five interviewed were by telephone and twenty-five in person, during the last quarter of 1984.

The questionnaire itself was extensive (see Appendix A). It was pre-tested, and, in its final form, contained over 30 questions. Many of these questions were in support of other research efforts by Software Access International and were not released for publication here.

## 6. Research Results

We first present an analysis of the time frame within which organizations have developed microcomputer policy. Next, we detail the policy elements common to most comprehensive policy statements. Then, we discuss the variety in schools of thought of microcomputer management evident from our results. Finally, we present an assessment of the nature of the policies.

## 8. Common Elements of Microcomputer Policy

The policy statements varied greatly. One of the more interesting dimensions was that of formality of the policy statement. Some organizations reported a quite formal microcomputer policy. Others had no formally written policy provisions. In spite of this, we tried to extract a set of policy provisions common to many organizations. Five provisions were common to many policy statements.

Justification of Need: Many organizations require completion of a formal justification of the need for the system to support business processes. Generally the justification could be a brief description of the intended purposes to which the machine would be put; they were (fortunately) quite different in scope from the justifications for any other size computer system.

Our study indicated that 46 percent of the companies interviewed had policy statements which required those acquiring personal computers to formally justify their need for the systems (Fig. 2). In 19 percent of the companies, there was no such requirement. The response from the remaining 35 companies are not specific enough to allow classification and are, therefore, shown on Fig. 2 as “uncertain.”

Centralized Control: Most organizations exerted some control over the acquisition process through approval by some central authority. In some, this was exercised by a manager in the information processing organization, while in others the implementation depended upon a more broadly-based committee. A centralized approval was required for PC purchases in 52 percent of the companies (Fig. 4). Such approval was not required in 28 percent of the companies and 20 percent of the responses was “uncertain.”

Standardized Hardware and Software: The most common mechanism for constraining purchases was the publication of an approved list of hardware and software. Users were “encouraged” to purchase only from the approved list. Sometimes, users were told they could not buy unapproved items or that there would be no internal support or training. Occasionally, control was through the source of funds; e.g., corporate funds could be used to purchase items on the approved list while other purchases were only funded through departmental budgets. The hardware and software responses are illustrated in Fig. 5 and 6 respectively. Fig. 5 shows that 29 percent of the companies required that hardware be purchased from an approved list while an additional 22 percent provided a recommended list with a variety of incentives to encourage “proper” purchase behavior.

IBM Compatibility: Many organizations resorted to a specification that any acquired microcomputer systems must be compatible with the IBM PC. This requirement varied from “must be IBM” to “IBM compatible” to “must be able to communicate with an IBM.” These results were not tabulated.

## 9. Schools of Thought: Restrictive, Permissive, or Supportive

There appeared to be three distinct opinions on the way to effect a beneficial environment for the use of microcomputers; there was evidence of each. The restrictive school believe that users must be protected from themselves and the organization must be protected from their uninformed purchases. Organizations with such policies typically had a rigid list of allowed standard products. Any other purchases incurred a risk: no support could be expected from the support organization.

The second felt that a restrictive policy was not flexible enough. While they supported a standard list of products and encouraged users to buy from it, they also assumed other needs. They, were therefore, willing to help users of nonstandard purchases, if they were justified in advance.

The third group seemed to be just a service organization. They felt that their role was to help users acquire, use, and maintain whatever products they wanted.

It is not possible to deduce which of the policies might be most productive. A restrictive policy may be best in one organization and a permissive one in another. It seems unlikely that we will ever have the ability to measure such a fit.

An informal, subjective analysis showed very little evidence of the fully supportive school; concerns of compatibility, maintenance, support and training seem to make a laissex faire purchase policy hazardous.

The predominance was therefore with restrictive policies. We should expect that increased standardization, less diversity of products, and increased capabilities of support groups will eventually allow more permissive policies.

## 10. Conclusions

Most large organizations had microcomputer policies in place by the end of 1984. Predominately, these policies were designed to be restrictive. The restrictions included formal justification of need, formal justification of cost, centralized approval of purchase, and required or recommended hardware and software acquisition lists.

Developments in microcomputer policy can be expected to parallel the development of end-user computing. Couger $[2]$ describes the use of “soft” controls by information services departments to guide end-user activities. Microcomputer policy might also benefit from such control efforts.

With time, we can expect microcomputer policy statements to become more comprehensive (as loopholes in existing policies are discovered and closed) and less restrictive (as standards and interconnectivity reduce the potential risks). Further research will be needed to test these claims.
