# Management-by-Exception Reporting: An Empirical Investigation

John A. Ricketts

International Consulting & Education Company, Houston, Texas, USA

and

R. Ryan Nelson

Decision & Information Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, Texas 77004, USA

Management-by-exception (MBE) reporting is among the oldest and most-widely implemented techniques in business systems. Yet research in this area is scant. The laboratory study presented here concludes that decision makers differ in their preference for exception reporting on the basis of dollars, per cent, or both. In addition to large deviations, some decision makers chose to investigate small deviations from normal budgets, and all deviations from high-risk budgets. Contrary to expectations, Weber's Law of Just-Noticeable Differences did not describe how decision makers identified exceptions. A better description was provided by an Exception Frontier Model.

Keywords: Management of information systems, Human aspects and social impacts of computing, Management-by-exception reporting, Decision making, Information requirements analysis, Information retrieval.

## 1. Introduction

It is often suggested in the literature that an information system's effectiveness is more often affected by the style of information use than by the technical characteristics of the system [1,7]. "Far too often, data-for-the-sake-of-data has been the objective, and too little attention has been paid to the ways people use information (style) and the ensuing effectiveness of the result" [15]. At the practical level, one common style of information use is "management-by-exception (MBE) reporting." It is based on the notion that only variances, both favorable and unfavorable, should attract decision makers' attention, particularly if they were assessed as being significant. Hence, it provides a basis for allocating, or rationing, managerial effort [6,10].

\- opportunities and problems are identified.

While many control systems use the exception principle, MBE reporting is underutilized [20,28], and there is paucity of research on the subject, except in the accounting literature [e.g. 16]. Only one study of a purely is nature was discovered; in that study, Judd et al. ## 2. Previous Research

## 2.1 Individual Differences and MBE Reporting

Individuals differ in the rate, extent, style, and quality of their information processing. Chervany and Dickson [8] found managers tended to be more comfortable with detail information, even though their decision-making performance was better with summary information. Benbasat and Schroeder [4] found that managers unfamiliar with their job tended to ask for irrelevant information. In a call for empirical research, Zmud [27] stated, "The variations in cognitive behaviors attributable to individual differences as well as the documented limitations inherent to human information processing and decision behavior provide ample opportunities for designers to provide is that extend the decision making capabilities of organizational members."

Psychological studies into the nature of human information processing indicate the limitations of humans as information processors and their inability to recognize those limitations $[11,19,21,24–26]$ . Furthermore, when integrating psychological and is research, one must not overlook the application Weber's Law, which “was originally developed to apply to judgments of physical stimuli such as the brightness of light, the loudness of sounds, and the heaviness of weights” $[14]$ .

It states that for each individual, just-noticeable differences are a constant proportion of physical stimuli. Essentially, in a budgetary context, this means a larger difference is required to distinguish larger figures. Whether this Law applies to more than the perceptual component of MBE reporting is controversial. Cleveland believes that it applies to perceptual tasks, but not cognitive tasks (such as decision making) [9]; however, others believe it applies to both [e.g. 14].

In 1975, Dickhaut and Eggleton reported on experiments that examined comparative judgments of numerical information, specifically accounting [14]. They concluded that (a) their results appeared to be consistent with Weber's Law and (b) their subjects developed decision rules for handling numerical information and tended to apply them throughout the comparative judgment task. Based on this, we believe that similar behavior patterns might exist in MBE reporting, particularly with budgetary information.

## 2.2 Information Presentation and MBE Reporting

MBE reports can differ in terms of format, style, and color. For example, within a budgetary context, they might (a) represent data as percentage deviations, (b) depict exceptionally large deviations in red, or (c) use a graphical format. For over a decade, the IS literature has paid particularly close attention to the effects of information presentation [e.g. 3]. As Dickson et al. [15] state: "It is suboptimal to continue developing information support systems without serious consideration of alternate forms for the information provided, the level of detail incorporated into ensuing reports, and the transmission media employed".

## 3. Research Methods

This laboratory study is a systematic replication of Judd et al. [17]. In direct replication, research methods are the same, but in systematic replication, research methods extend the earlier study. Thus, it tests prior findings and adds new ones [23].

## 3.1 Research Questions

The following questions drive this research:
1. Do decision makers use dollars, per cent, or a combination thereof to evaluate (i.e. select and rank) budget variances for action?

Past research describes budget evaluation methods in the aggregate, without showing whether individuals actually employ those methods. This research focuses first on individuals' methods, then on aggregate descriptions.

2. Does size of budget variance play a role in the method of evaluation chosen?

Do decision makers use the same evaluation method when budget variances range between 1–11% as when they range between 1–25%. For example, some decision makers might rely on dollars when variances are small, but per cent when they are large.

3. Do decision makers evaluate budget variance tables differently than equivalent bar graphs? Decision makers' preference for, and belief in, graphic information presentations are demonstrated by rising sales of graphics hardware and software [18]. However, research into the relative effectiveness of graphs and tables is inconclusive, in part because the tables and graphs used in some past studies were not equivalent or of poor quality [13].

4. Does Weber's Law predict decision makers' evaluation of budget variances?

Are they as concerned about \$5,000 (or 10%) variance for a budget of \$50,000 as they are in a budget of \$100,000? Noticeable differences may not be actionable differences.

5. Does Weber's Law apply to both tables and graphs?

Since tabular and graphic stimuli have different properties, perhaps Weber's Law does not apply to them equally. For example, tabular information may require more cognition than perception.

## 3.2 Variables, Measurement Instruments, and Subjects

The first independent variable, Size of Budget Variance, was implemented by adopting existing instruments without modification as shown in Figs 1 and 2. We judged these treatment levels to be sufficiently different from each other for a reasonable test. These are tabular reports of 24 budgets, actual expenditures, dollar variance, and per cent variance. Actual expenditures exceed budgets in all cases. Rows within each report are randomly ordered, so budget variances are evenly distributed.

The second independent variable, Form of Information Presentation, was implemented by creating two additional (graphic) reports as shown in Figs 3 and 4. These contain the same information in the same order, same size (8.5 by 11 inches), and same color (black on white) as the tabular reports. Bar graphs were chosen because they are common in business and therefore facilitate generalization. In addition, the bar graphs used in this study satisfy many of Cleveland's principles of good graphics:

1. they rely heaviest on the most accurate of all elementary perceptual tasks: Judgment of position along a common scale

2. budget and actual expenditures are superimposed in the left graph, but the differences are displayed separately in the other graphs

3. tick marks and legends are outside the data areas

4. reference lines extend across the graphs without hiding data

5. zero points are shown for each scale, yet resolution is good.

One principle that could not be satisfied was to sort cases by magnitude rather than label. This was not feasible because several incompatible sorting criteria were available and any one would have biased subjects toward viewing the data according to that criterion. The effects are left for future research.

The dependent variables, Budget Evaluations, are lists of budget cases that subjects selected and ranked as worthy of action. These lists are recorded by each subject on ranking sheets for each budget report. Other dependent variables were derived from Budget Evaluations. Evaluation Method is the apparent method used by each subject to arrive at Budget Evaluations. Evaluation Methods include Dollar Variance, Per Cent Variance, a combination of dollars and percentages (to be defined later), and Other (undeterminable) methods.

Of 108 subjects, 70% were employed full-time and attended an MBA program part-time. The remainder were full-time MBAs with part-time employment. All subjects had completed an accounting course that included budgeting concepts and were enrolled in IS courses that included decision-making concepts. These groups did not differ in age, sex, or budget evaluation profiles. $^{1}$ Therefore, their data was pooled for further analysis.

## 3.3 Experimental Design and Procedures

The experimental design was two by two: report form (table versus graph) and size of budget variance (1–11% versus 1–25%). It was conducted under sufficiently realistic conditions to expect generalizability beyond the laboratory (i.e. test effects are unlikely to have contaminated the findings). Each subject initially received either Tabular Reports or Graphic Reports for evaluation. Written instructions on a cover sheet read:

As an executive of a large organization, you requested a report of the managers who exceeded their budget. This report is summarized on the following pages. Please indicate which (if any) of these budget discrepancies you would want to check into.

Having chosen cases for investigation, subjects were given a separate ranking sheet and instructed to rank those cases. Evaluations required five to twenty minutes. No time limit was imposed. After at least one week elapsed, each group received the other information presentation for evaluation. This period between treatments was sufficient to eliminate all short-term and most long-term memory effects. During debriefing, the handful of subjects who said they recognized the information as the same on both occasions also said that this did not affect their evaluations. Subjects were asked not to discuss the experiment between treatments; and, so far as we were able to determine, they complied with this request. Tables-first and graphs-first budget evaluation profiles did not differ statistically; therefore the data was pooled for further analysis. $^{2}$

## 4. Data Analysis

The analysis was conducted as follows. First, we used the same criteria as Judd et al. and confirmed their findings. Second, we improved the criteria and obtained substantially different results. Third, we examined the range of applicability of Weber's Law to MBE reporting and found that it did not hold beyond the perceptual level. Finally, as an alternative, we devised an Exception Frontier Model.

## 4.1 Preliminary Classification Criteria

Judd et al. used two criteria to deduce the evaluation method used by their subjects. First, all selected budget cases had to have higher ranks based on either dollar variance or per cent variance than those not selected. Second, all selected budget cases had to be ranked in descending order based upon either dollars or per cent. $^{3}$

Using these criteria, Judd et al. found that only 11% of their subjects used dollar variance and only 12% used per cent variance to evaluate the tabular budget reports. For the remaining 77% of their subjects, they defined a new evaluation method, Decision Impelling Difference (DID), as “dollar variance times per cent variance.” DIDs fit the average budget case ranks quite well ( $r^{2}=0.97$ for Small and for Large Variance Reports).

Using the same criteria to analyze our data for the tabular reports yielded nearly identical results: 10% of our subjects used dollar variance, and 13% used per cent variance. For the remaining 77% of our subjects, DIDs fit the average budget case ranks reasonably well ( $r^{2}=0.78$ for Small Variance Report, $r^2 = 0.83$ for Large).

Therefore, when we used Judd's classification criteria, our data confirmed their findings. However, we see two problems with this analysis. First, the criteria used to deduce the Dollar and Per Cent methods are much stricter than the criteria for the DID method. Second, fitting DIDs to average budget case ranks creates a model of aggregate subject behavior without showing whether any individuals actually employed DIDs.

## 4.2 Final Classification Criteria

Rather than letting DID be the default category, if the criteria laid out for Dollar and Per Cent methods are also applied to the DID method, then substantially different conclusions result: DIDs were used less than either dollars or per cent alone. Only two of our subjects used it for the Small Variance Report, and one used it for Large Variance. This reduced the subjects in our DID category from 77% to 3% and shifted the remaining 74% into an Unknown Methods category.

Human beings are imperfect information processors. Possibly some subjects intended to employ one of these methods, but a single slip moved them into the Unknown Methods category. Maybe subjects did not view some differences as material. For example, if a subject were scanning for differences greater than \$1000, then Cases 15 and 20 in Fig. 1 would be interchangeable in rank to the subject, but noninterchangeable to the criteria employed to deduce the method.

To permit some interchangeability while excluding major slips, we relaxed the classification criteria. First, we computed the cumulative absolute deviation between perfect ranks based on dollars, per cent, and DIDs (Table 1), and each subject's ranks. Second, we chose the method category with the smallest cumulative deviation. Third, we assigned empty lists, ties, and individual deviations exceeding three ranks to the Unknown Methods category. $^{4}$ These revised criteria did increase the number of classifiable lists, but 43% remained in the Unknown Methods category. Fortunately, subjects themselves provided clues during debriefing.

The classification criteria are based on the assumption that large budget variances are all that is important. Though large variances are typically most important because they usually indicate immediate problems, they are not perfect indicators. For example, small budget variance can indicate that monies were spent (perhaps poorly) so that under-budget variance would not appear. If budgets are adjusted according to past spending, a “spend it or lose it” attitude may prevail. In addition, some budgets that are on target now may be on target because harmful expenditures have been delayed to later reporting periods. Conversely, adherence to budgets can be contrary to other goals. One subject said: “if you aren’t hurting us [with some budget overrun], you must be hurting our customers”. Thus, some subjects said they ranked some of the smallest budget variances, too.

In addition to overlooking immediate problems, reliance on large budget variances can also fail to indicate future problems. One line of reasoning proceeds as follows: Large budgets are assigned to departments or projects that are large, complex, or critical. Thus, if a large budget has small variance, it is more likely headed for problems than small budgets with comparable variance. Furthermore, the greatest harm (measured in dollars) may occur when large budgets go out of control. Thus, some subjects ranked large budgets with small variances.

Based on these secondary factors, we inserted a prescreening step. It deletes all ranks for budget cases with small variance (those in the lowest third), but records which subjects used this method. We call this a secondary evaluation method because small-variance and big-budget cases usually were not among the most-highly ranked. Prescreening reduced the Unknown Methods category from 43% to 30%.

## 4.4 Applicability of Weber's Law

Weber's Ratio is $D$ divided by $B$ , where $D$ is the just-noticeable difference between two stimuli, and $B$ is a base measure of them. Weber's Law says this ratio is constant over all $B$ for a given subject.

The treatments in this study were designed to reveal subjects' Budget Evaluation Methods, but the data also can be used to examine the applicability of Weber's Law to MBE reporting. If it does apply to more than perception, then a ratio akin to Weber's Ratio ought to be the same for Small and Large Variance Reports.

Such a ratio can be computed from each subject's data. Whereas the numerator of Weber's Ratio is a just-noticeable difference in a perceptual sense, the Exception Threshold (smallest budget variance chosen by each subject) is a just-noticeable difference in a cognitive sense because smaller variances are noticeable, but not actionable. The budget corresponding to each subject's Exception Threshold is analogous to Weber's base stimulus, but in this study the stimulus is complex. $^{6}$ Thus, the Exception-Threshold-to-Budget Ratio was obtained by dividing dollar variance by dollar budget, per cent variance by 100, or DID variance by dollar budget, depending on the Evaluation Method deduced for each subject. Since Weber's Ratios vary across subjects, paired observations are required (i.e. only subjects using the same Evaluation Method across both tabular reports, or across both graphic reports, are suitable for analysis).

Table 3 shows descriptive statistics for Exception-Threshold-to-Budget Ratios according to Size of Variance, Report Form, and Evaluation Method. Contrary to Weber's Law, the ratios are significantly smaller for Small Variance Reports than for Large Variance Reports. $^{7}$ Therefore, Weber's Law does not explain how subjects established their Exception Thresholds. In a budgetary context, even slight differences may be noticeable, but not necessarily actionable.

## 4.5 An Exception Frontier Model

Since Weber's Law did not explain how subjects established their Exception Thresholds, we developed a simple model that fits the data. The mean Exception Threshold for the 1–11% variance reports was about 9%, and for the 1–25% variance reports about 16%. If X is the largest budget variance in a given report, Y is the corresponding mean Exception Threshold, and we assume a linear relationship through our two data points, then the Budget Exception Frontier is $Y = 0.035 + 0.5X$ . In other words, Budget Exception Thresholds were one-half the largest budget variance in each report, plus 3.5%.

This Exception Frontier Model is depicted in Fig. 5. Line A is the Exception Frontier (smallest actionable budget variances). It is defined by the data points at the intersections of the dashed lines. For reference, line B shows largest actionable budget variances. Region 1 is Actionable Budget Exceptions. Region 2 is nonexceptions. Though the units in the figure are per cent, translation to dollars or DIDs is straightforward and does not alter the model significantly.

Of course, we cannot test this model with the data used to derive it. Such testing must be done in subsequent studies. We believe, however, that the model is both plausible and parsimonious. As with any empirically-derived model, extrapolations beyond the bounds of the data should be made cautiously, if at all. Beyond the lower bound of our data, the largest budget variance and corresponding exception threshold are equal at 0.07. Thus, when the largest budget variance is less than 7%, the model predicts that subjects will not identify budget exceptions because no variances exceed their thresholds. Whether this is truly a floor for Budget Exception Thresholds or the relationship is nonlinear are issues for future research. Similarly, we cannot now say whether the Exception Frontier Model applies when budget variances are not uniformly distributed. Finally, since it is based on mean values, it describes subjects in the aggregate rather than individually.

## 5. Discussion

Do decision makers use dollars, per cent, or DIDs to evaluate budget variances for action? Yes, 70% of the subjects used these primary budget evaluation methods, but 30% used unknown methods. Contrary to Judd et al., DIDs did not dominate the other methods. Furthermore, many subjects also identified exceptions based on secondary methods (small variance or big budgets). Therefore, users appear to prefer MBE reporting with enough flexibility to offer several concurrent exception criteria. In order to identify evaluation methods that are presently unknown, future research might ask subjects to articulate their criteria, rather than attempt to deduce them later.

Does size of budget variance play a role in the method of evaluation chosen by decision makers? Yes, this is significant. When budget variances are small, decision makers use dollars to identify exceptions more than twice as often as percentages or DIDs. When budget variances are large, decision makers use DIDs to identify exceptions more than twice as often as dollars or per cent. Future research could investigate other budget variance magnitudes, including under-budget variance.

Do decision makers evaluate budget variance tables differently than equivalent bar graphs? No, report form apparently did not affect the evaluation methods used. This finding agrees with DeSanctis' tentative conclusion that graphs are not better than tables for many tasks. However, we cannot say how far this finding can be generalized. Future research might investigate other graph forms, including those that violate more of Cleveland's good-graphics principles.

Does Weber's Law explain decision makers' evaluation of budget variances? No, Weber's Law did not describe how subjects determined actionable budget exceptions. A better description is provided by our Exception Frontier Model. It says that actionable budget exceptions are a function of the largest budget variance. For example, suppose budget group $A$ is equivalent to budget group $B$ , but group $A$ 's largest budget variances are smaller than group $B$ 's. MBE reports based on Weber's Law would use the same cutoff point for both groups' variances because the budgets are equivalent. The smallest variance reported would be the same for both groups, so more budget variances would be reported for group $B$ . On the other hand, MBE reports based on our Threshold Frontier Model would use different cutoff points for each group. The smallest variance reported for group $A$ would be smaller than the smallest variance reported for group $B$ , but more variances still would be reported for group $B$ .

Does Weber's Law apply to both tables and graphs? No, as described above, beyond the perceptual level, it applied to neither. However, our Exception Frontier Model applied to both.

## 6. Conclusions

Since the Exception Frontier Model better describes how decision makers in our study identified actionable budget exceptions, if MBE reports are intended to emulate personal judgments, they should contain budget variances on or above the Threshold Frontier. In addition, since some decision makers are suspicious of small budget variances and prefer to monitor large budgets most closely, automated MBE reports should also show cases that satisfy these secondary criteria. Future research should investigate other points along the frontier with research designs that separate size-of-budget-variance treatments from size-of-budget treatments.

Whether automated MBE reports should emulate manually-prepared MBE reports is a question that can be raised, but not answered here. Arguments favoring emulation are that exception methods are straightforward to implement, and at the same time, those methods are readily understood. The strongest argument against emulation is that the power of the computer is not fully exploited. For example, statistical sampling techniques might do a better job of avoiding information overload when the number of budgets is large. Similarly, trends drawn from historical databases may reveal problems that would escape point-in-time exception analyses. Whether a particular method of exception identification is characteristic of good managers is another question.

Finally, practitioners and researchers should recognize that MBE reporting is not as well-understood as many believe. Additional benefits may yet be squeezed from MBE reporting systems, if we continue to refine them. For example, this study suggests that the design of MBE reports should be based on the size of budget variances and individual decision maker preferences for exception criteria, but will not necessarily benefit from graphics instead of tables. Further examination of the relationship between decisions, the decision makers, and information systems might expand the benefits of MBE reporting while weakening the constraints.
