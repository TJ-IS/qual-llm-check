# Information Systems Planning: Lessons From Strategic Planning

Ellen M. Hufnagel

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, 241 Mervis Hall, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, U.S.A.

IS planning has changed considerably since its inception as an operational planning tool, expanding its scope to address key business objectives and forging a link with strategic business planning. A great deal of attention is currently focused on identifying and developing strategic information systems. Strategic planning has also become more broadly-focused over time, as simple forecasting techniques have been replaced by a variety of adaptive approaches that emphasize industry structure analysis and environmental dynamics. However, strategic planning has come under fire recently for failing to live up to its promises. As these two disciplines become more closely aligned, IS planners can learn from the experiences of strategic planners and, hopefully, avoid a similar crisis of confidence.

Keywords: Information Systems planning, Strategic planning.

## 1. Introduction

Over the last two decades, researchers and practitioners in the field of information systems (IS) have placed increasing emphasis on the need for more effective planning of IS resources. During this period, the definition of IS planning has been expanded as computer systems have become vital to the operation of many firms and have taken on a strategic role in others. In this same period, strategic planning has also been developing as a discipline, with the early narrowly-defined forecasting-based approaches yielding to more adaptive views concerned with the dynamics of the environment and the search for opportunities to create sustainable competitive advantage. Today, information and its related technology are regarded as competitive strengths by companies that have advanced to the point of successfully integrating IS planning with strategic business planning.

A closer look at the development of these two types of planning suggests some similarities in the problems encountered to date and some possible lessons for IS planners.

## 2. Levels of Managerial Activity

Gorry and Scott Morton [10] suggested that a framework of planning and control systems proposed by Anthony [1] be used as an aid in understanding both the development of IS activities and the potential problems and benefits associated with this new technology. This same framework, which classifies planning and control activities in terms of three levels of managerial activity, provides the basis for this review of IS planning systems development as it highlights their increasingly broad view of the role of IS in the organization.

In this framework, there are three levels of planning activities: strategic planning, management control, and operational control. Although it is generally recognized that certain activities may not fall clearly into a single classification within this scheme, this taxonomy is nonetheless a useful way of looking at developments in IS planning.

## 3. Approaches to IS Planning

Computers first gained widespread acceptance as a technological response to the need for faster and more efficient ways to process growing volumes of routine transactions. Is planning was minimal during this period as “the pressure for planning on other than an ad hoc, one application at a time basis could not have occurred until available technology, and an accompanying body of experience in its successful use, permitted a more comprehensive approach” [2, p. 2]. Planning tended to focus on justifying large expenditures for computer hardware, based on labor savings, improving reporting capabilities, and maintaining some element of control over development activities. Concern with is planning as a more formal discipline began to develop with the realization that the value of isolated applications could be enhanced through integration into larger, more complex systems.

## 3.1 Operational Planning Approaches

Operational planning and control activities deal with scheduling and controlling work according to some pre-established rules. The first is planning approaches can be viewed as operational in nature as they were concerned with specifying procedures for making trade-offs between competing projects and adjusting priorities. Lyles [21] characterized these planning approaches as reactive project-oriented efforts having near time horizons and little-input from top management. Development of these plans was frequently a bottom-up endeavor which focused on defining the specific needs of each potential user group and then consolidating departmental plans into an overall time table for implementation.

## 3.2 Management Control Approaches

As is planning efforts became more widespread, a number of planning frameworks that emphasized the importance of management input were developed $[5,16,23,25,26,35,42]$ . These top-down approaches were concerned with how best to incorporate business objectives into the IS plan. Emphasis was on ensuring information (resource) availability and the efficiency and effectiveness of the systems that produced this information.

Several methodologies were suggested to guide IS planners. The best known are IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP) [41] and Critical Success Factors (CSF) [33]. While somewhat different in orientation and scope, these “top-down” planning approaches were distinctly more proactive than earlier methods, calling attention to the need for greater management participation in the IS planning process. Although issues of resource allocation and development priorities were of great concern, they were clearly subordinate to and guided by management’s view of the future direction of the business enterprise. Perhaps the most significant contribution of these methodologies was the elevation of IS to a new level of importance.

## 3.3 Strategic Planning

Rather early in the development of IS planning, Kriebel [17] suggested an approach that added a strategic dimension to the possible outcomes of the IS planning process. He identified three stages of decision-making by top management:

1. Determination of specific objectives for systems development and performance criteria for evaluating progress.

2. Establishment of policy with respect to environmental issues, including organization structure, planning and growth time frames, and management's commitment of resources to be applied in developing systems.

3. Appraisal of computer systems potential, including an assessment of the company's current position with respect to competitive advantages and limitations.

King [14] developed the notion of a reciprocal link between strategic business planning and is planning in outlining a methodology for translating the organization's strategy set into a set of strategic IS requirements. Through detailed analysis of organizational goals and their translation into IS design strategies, greater interaction between IS and business planners was anticipated. A feedback link was suggested as a means of further increasing the probability of IS success.

The potential contribution of IS technology to business strategy has since been elaborated by a number of researchers ([4,7,22,28,34,39,40] and others), though not in the context of a formal planning process. Drawing primarily on Porter's [29] techniques for competitive analysis, these authors argue that information technology can be effectively utilized by many companies – to differentiate their products/services from those of the competition and to strengthen relations with buyers and suppliers. The evidence presented to date is predominantly anecdotal, however; Clemons and McFarland [4] point out that most companies that have successfully used information technology to achieve competitive advantage have done so more by chance than as a result of careful planning. The argument in favor of a more formalized approach is nonetheless appealing and suggests that a bidirectional relationship between strategic and IS planning can be beneficial to the entire organization.

King [15] presented an expanded model which helps to define this relationship in combining strategic Planning for Information Resources (SPIR) with Information Resources Assessment (IRA). The objectives of this SPIRA model are twofold:

1. to ensure that the information systems of the firm are consistent with the overall business strategy, and

2. to enable the firm to make full utilization of the resources at its disposal in implementing strategic decisions.

SPIRA begins with an evaluation of the organization's strengths and weaknesses with respect to its competitors and the specification of strategic objectives. This business strategy set must then be translated into information resource terms to develop a definition of the goals for strategic management of information systems. The second phase consists of identifying ways in which information can contribute to the firm's success by providing competitive advantage.

Rackoff et al. [31] also described a formal planning process for developing strategic information systems, using a framework that considers both the strategic target (suppliers, customers, competitors) and five strategic thrusts (differentiation, cost, innovation, growth and alliance). These strategic thrusts are viewed as the fundamental link between business strategy and information technology as they suggest opportunities for investing in new information-based assets as well as for leveraging existing information resources.

These planning approaches are representative of the range of viewpoints presented in the IS literature. According to Lucas and Turner [20], such approaches to IS planning produce three types of information systems, depending on the level of integration of information systems and business strategy:

1. Independent systems, resulting from an operational control approach, are not directly linked with business strategy. Their primary objective is operational efficiency.

2. Policy Support systems reflect a management control focus and include decision-support tools and other systems that contribute to the development of business strategy but are not a part of it.

3. Fully Integrated systems are a reflection of the formalized strategic planning approach which views information systems as critical resources with the potential to create new products, open new markets or set new directions for the firm.

This brief synopsis provides an overview of the development of IS planning. While the development process has not proceeded linearly from the operational to the strategic, the general thrust over time has been in this direction. However, a survey of actual IS planning practices of firms today indicates that a variety of approaches are, in fact, being used [6]. Just as there are proponents of various planning models, there are multiple theories to explain why firms choose a particular planning model.

## 4. The Choice of Planning Methodologies

In the first version of their stages-of-growth model, Gibson and Nolan [8] associated the various approaches to planning with the firm's stage of development, defined in terms of growth in the is budget. They postulated that after a period of learning (Stage 1: Initiation), firms would exhibit rapid, unplanned growth and steeply rising costs (Stage 2: Contagion), culminating in a budgetary crisis. This crisis would then cause the firm to establish control over growth and spending (Stage 3: Control). Viewed as a turning point with respect to the firm's use of is resources, this third stage heralded the introduction of formalized planning as firms moved away "from the early cost reduction applications of initiation and early growth toward projects aimed at improving operations, revenues and the quality of unprogrammed and strategic decisions" [8, p. 85].

In a later version, Nolan [27] noted that it is not until firms reach Stage 4 (Integration) that planning takes on a management control perspective, with emphasis shifting from internal data processing concerns to external user concerns. Still later in Stage 6 (Maturity), alignment of the applications portfolio and the larger organization begins to occur. Because the last two stages were only vaguely defined, the impetus for such a move remains less than clear. While intuitively appealing, the stages-of-growth model is not built on a strong theoretical foundation [13] and attempts to empirically validate it have yielded mixed results [6,19].

McFarlan et al. [24] related the range of planning activities engaged in to the firm's developmental stage; like Gibson and Nolan, they defined a continuum along which a firm moves as it assimilates its technology. During the first phase, Identification and Initial Investment, planning activities are expected to be minimal as the firm seeks to acquire new technology and to develop an understanding of its potential uses. These activities continue into the second phase, Experimentation and Learning, as attention is concentrated on teaching users how to apply technology. Planning begins to receive more attention at this stage, as the need to prioritize and sequence projects becomes crucial. Technology planning is also heavily emphasized. In Phase 3, short-term control and efficiency considerations take precedence as management attempts to contain the growth of expenditures. Phase 4 is characterized by more widespread acceptance of technology and a more forward-looking orientation as managers consider long-term trends. They further contend that a company's approach to its planning should be a function of the extent to which existing applications have strategic impact and the potential impact of applications in the development portfolio. When the IS department is at a relatively low level in the organization and IS planning activities are minimal, computers tend to function in a support role and have little strategic importance. If a company is heavily dependent on IS for support of current business activities (as in a factory environment), but has no plan to expand the role of IS in the future, planning efforts will focus on shorter time frames and be operations oriented. Companies that are not dependent on the uninterrupted support of existing computer systems will display more distant planning horizons if their development portfolios include applications that are crucial to the achievement of strategic objectives. In those few companies where both current and planned applications are of strategic importance, considerable planning is required and input from top management is essential. Thus, “a company’s placement in this matrix not only influences how IS planning should be done but also has implications for the role of the executive steering committee, organizational placement of IS, and the appropriate type of IS management control system” [24, p. 152].

Sullivan [37] studied IS planning efforts at 37 major US companies, noting that companies whose computer systems had had a significant strategic impact were more likely to engage in IS planning. The rate of diffusion of IS technology throughout the company was also correlated with IS planning. He suggests that the choice of IS planning techniques is a function of the level of infusion or strategic importance of existing systems and the degree to which the use of computer technology has disseminated throughout the company (Fig. 2).

In Sullivan's assessment, firms that have experienced minimal infusion and diffusion can be expected to follow a stages-of-growth approach to planning. Where infusion is high but diffusion is low, the Business Systems Planning approach is more likely. In decentralized environments, where technology is scattered throughout the organization, a Critical Success Factors approach is applicable as long as infusion levels are low. Companies that have experienced high levels of both infusion and diffusion are more complex in terms of their planning approaches and may use some combination of techniques. This assertion was corroborated by Drury [6], who attempted to validate Nolan's expanded stages-of-growth model. He found that “consistent with progress in systems sophistication, more planning and control techniques have been introduced, but that the mix at any point remains a source of continuing difficulty” [6, p. 67].

These theories offer alternative explanations of how and why the IS planning function develops over time. Each is based on a somewhat different notion of the organizational characteristics that lead to increasingly sophisticated planning systems. Despite the diversity of viewpoints and some contradictory empirical results, however, there seems to be agreement that some level of IS planning is essential and that the extent to which a company plans will increase with the complexity of the IS environment. There is also evidence that the future will bring with it new ideas about and approaches to IS planning as companies become more sophisticated in their use of information technology and as the technology becomes more complex.

## 5. Approaches to Strategic Business Planning

Strategic planning as a formal discipline has also undergone a number of significant changes over the last few decades, some quite similar to those exhibited by is planning. Chakravarthy [3] described the evolution of strategic planning systems in terms of three phases. The first, centralized planning, began with functionally organized control systems emanating from the top of the organization for the purpose of monitoring performance compared to budget. During this period, the diversification of many large companies led to their restructuring into several operating divisions. In the second phase, as the number of products and markets increased and technological complexities demand greater sophistication, strategic plans were developed at the division level, then evaluated and integrated into an overall business plan. A third phase began to emerge in the 1970s, emphasizing the portfolio approach to strategy planning. Although a number of models were utilized, all were concerned with assessing the strategic position of the various business units in an effort to control the resource allocation process.

Gluck et al. [9] describe a similar pattern of strategic planning development, adding a fourth phase: strategic management. They argue that this phase emerged when the top management of some firms became unsettled by the realization that strategic decisions were being made at the business unit level. In response, these executives increased their investment in strategic planning in an attempt to integrate strategic thinking into the day-to-day management of the organization. Thus, the major difference between strategic management and the externally oriented planning of Chakravarthy's third phase "is not the sophistication of their planning techniques, but rather the care and thoroughness with which strategic planning is linked to operational decision making" [9, p. 16].

## 6. Parallel Planning Developments

The first two phases in the evolution of strategic planning parallel those in IS planning, as both disciplines moved past their initial short-term orientation and limited focus to recognize the need for more widespread participation across all levels of the organization. As the complexity and organizational impact of IS issues increased, top management became more involved in the planning process, resulting in a shift from bottom-up to top-down planning; in strategic planning, the movement was reserved as divisions charged with implementing the plan were given more responsibility for its development.

The Phase 3 portfolio approach to strategic planning has its counterpart in the strategically-oriented is planning systems that are currently emerging. To better integrate planning into the mainstream of organizational activity, both models focus on a broader scope of activities than their earlier counterparts, attempting to develop a better understanding of the industry environment and the competitive strengths of the firm, thus increasing the relevance of the planning results.

The strategic management phase appears to have no parallel in the area of IS planning. However, as information and its associated technology become more and more vital to the achievement of strategic objectives, we might expect top management to experience some discomfort with the range and scope of decisions being made in this area and attempt to integrate IS thinking into day-to-day management activities.

Rhyne [32] described strategic planning systems in terms of a continuum, from rudimentary to sophisticated, based on the planning horizon used and the degree of openness to external factors and environmental change. As planning systems become more sophisticated, he argued, not only do they attempt to look farther into the future, but they also place increasing emphasis on qualitative objectives, the firm's internal capabilities, and the effect of social, political, and technological trends.

While Rhyne operationalized the construct of planning openness in strategic planning terms, it seems that is planning systems that take a long range view and concern themselves with a wider variety of external factors are more likely to contribute to the strategic direction of the firm than are those with narrower planning horizons and an internal focus. Companies may, in fact, be forced to expand their planning horizons to include longer time frames and a broader range of environmental issues, as industry boundaries and the competitive environment within many industries are significantly altered by the use of information technology for strategic gain.

## 7. Strategic Lessons for IS Planners

A study by Steiner and Schollhammer [36] suggested that the acceptance of techniques for long range planning by firms in the 1960s and early 1970s was not without problems. Some of the primary pitfalls reported were a tendency for management to focus on current problems at the expense of planning for the future, failure to develop a foundation of business goals, and top management's failure to become directly involved in planning.

Each of these difficulties also has its close parallel in the development of IS planning systems. For example, in a study of some IS planning paradoxes perceived by IS managers in 20 firms, Lederer and Mendelow [18] identified the consuming nature of daily “fire fighting” and the lack of clearly stated business goals as serious impediments to successful IS plan development. There is also evidence [30] that, in organizations that view IS as important to corporate strategy, planning effectiveness is dramatically increased when few levels exist between the IS manager and senior management. Thus, when the organizational distance between IS management and top management is great, top management’s involvement in the IS planning process is presumably minimal, leading to less effective IS plans.

In looking at a firm's ability to cope with such planning problems as it gains experience, Higgins [11] identified three phases in the planning systems development cycle: conceptualization, design, and maturity. Through an empirical study of 86 Fortune 1000 companies, he determined that companies with mature planning systems were better able to deal with integrative problems than were companies where conceptualization and design issues remain to be adequately resolved. On the other hand, many companies with mature systems were grappling with the fact that planning had become routinized over time, eroding corporate enthusiasm for the resulting plans.

While the difficulties reported in the 1970s related to the ability of firms to integrate the strategic planning process into existing organizational structures, James [12] suggests that the 1980s have evidenced ‘cracks’ in the business strategy function itself. He describes a series of problems which have arisen in recent years in connection with strategic business plans, including: failure of strategists to adapt to the changing business environment, a tendency to narrow the focus of planning to one key dimension or competitive strength at the expense of a balanced and integrated approach, misapplication of strategic concepts, and ineffective strategy implementation. He argues that realignment and restructuring of strategic concepts and functions is required if business strategy is to regain its credibility.

The lesson for IS planners is clear. While current concerns focus on integrative problems, on getting sufficient support from top management and building on a base of corporate business plans, care should be taken to ensure that, as these issues are resolved, the IS planning process does not become so routine that organizational enthusiasm and imagination is lost. Furthermore, efforts should be made to develop IS plans that recognize the dynamic nature of the competitive environment and that utilize information resources creatively in response to changing opportunities. As these two types of planning become more closely aligned, IS planners will need to better understand strategic concepts and, similarly, must help educate strategic planners about IS concepts. Finally, IS must actively seek the participation of managers throughout the organization in planning activities to increase the likelihood of successful implementation – for even the best laid plans are of little value without their commitment during implementation.
