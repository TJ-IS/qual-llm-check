# Structure and Function of Information Centers: Case Studies of Six Organizations

David P. Christy

Division of Management Science, College of Business Administration, Penn State University, University Park, PA 16802, USA

Clinton E. White, Jr.

Department of Accounting, College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716, USA

The information center (IC) is a concept advocated by many information systems professionals for effectively supporting end-user computing. Detailed interviews with the managers of six ICS and with users of IC services indicate that the structure and organizational location of the IC may effect its staffing, service mix, and vision of the future.

Keywords: End user computing, Information center, Managing information systems, Organizing computing resources, Organizational structure, Training, User education, User support service.

## 1. Introduction

The Information Center (IC) has been advocated by firms and information system professionals as an effective approach for supporting and managing end-user computing (EUC). This is important, because end-users often attack problems with direct cost consequences in many cases [4]. This is in contrast to Management Information System (MIS) personnel, who generally perform data collection and organization tasks as a staff function, which means that it is difficult to evaluate the MIS effectiveness. It is clearly in the firm's interest to enhance the productivity with which end-users perform such analyses.

In this study we define end-users as managerial personnel who use computer hardware and software to support decision making. While data entry and wordprocessing activities must be linked and compatible in future office automation (OA) schemes, probably managed by the same organizational unit, the focus here is decision makers as end-users (for a contrasting view, see $[5]$ ). This study examines organizations that may have mainframe end-users in addition to typical personal computer (PC) users; in several organizations, the use of mainframe tools is increasing.

## 2. Information Center Background

The IC structure generally consists of a centralized group of user-oriented personnel that provides support and training. Martin [4] describes the roles of the IC as encouraging, training, supporting, and managing EUC. The final category includes, by implication, varying degrees of both accountability and control. Henderson and Treacy [3] suggest that there is a “life cycle” of IC development that begins by creating an environment of encouragement, and proceeds to a more control-management model in mature organizations. While this model may have some appeal, an alternative hypothesis is that the different emphases in existing ICS may be motivated by different organizational goals and objectives rather than this maturation process.

We postulate two roles characterized by their approach to promoting and encouraging EUC. First, the “helping hand” design would require a marketing orientation of the IC and a quick response, user-oriented service that would not attempt to restrict operations or data access. The second role, that of managing and controlling the EUC environment, is likely to focus on evaluation of hardware and software requests, matching applications to appropriate configurations, and controlling data distribution. The time required for each transaction between the client end-user and the IC, the accountability criteria employed, and the operation of the IC as an overhead or chargeable service are additional distinctions between these two models.

## 3. Six Organizations

Extensive interviews were conducted in six organizations that had established information centers. Each organization had a reputation for EUC support, and was referred to us by an independent MIS consultant. The institutions included a bank holding company (FININST), an energy resource exploration and distribution firm (NGAS), an electric power utility (ELEC), a power systems engineering research organization (NUC), a manufacturer/retailer of consumer products (CONPROD), and a manufacturer of industrial equipment and machine tools (INDPROD). Interviewees included IC managers, IC staff, and end-users.

Information collected in these interviews, in a structured interview format of common questions and open response replies, included the organizational position of the information center, the philosophy and goals of the IC, the service mix provided, critical success factors, and comments on the future of end-user computing.

During the course of the research, several hypotheses were developed about the ICS in the subject organizations:

H I. The organizational position and immediate superiors of the IC determine its focus and its relationship to traditional MIS activities.

H II. The organization's philosophy and goals for EUC direct the relative mix of services offered.

H III. Evaluation criteria and stated success factors depend on whether the IC role is primarily to “promote” EUC or to “manage” it.

H IV. Conjectures regarding the future of EUC are shaped by the organizational location of the IC.

## 4. Effect of Organizational Location

The six organizations in this study had positioned their IC in one of two locations. Three firms (FININST, NGAS, ELEC) gave the IC relative autonomy with respect to the MIS group. In these three, the IC was either independent from or part of an applications group that was a peer to MIS, where both the IC and MIS manager reported to a Vice-President of Information Services. The remaining three firms (INDPROD, CONPROD, NUC) had the IC manager reporting to the MIS administrator. This distinction appears to be significant.

The MIS peer ICs are staffed by generalists who, in virtually all cases, did not have systems development backgrounds. They are “knowledgeable” users and facilitators whose common characteristic is their EUC orientation. The IC units in these organizations have distinct identities and career paths. They do not provide services that are similar to or competitive with those of MIS. In no case did these ICs report participation in actual systems development projects; rather they favored problem solving activities in support of truly end-user applications.

The MIS subordinate ICs exhibited a much wider diversity of staff backgrounds with a significant number of personnel recruited from the systems development groups. They were more likely to have been involved in systems development projects and user applications development. They were more actively involved in direct application development. The MIS peer ICs were more likely to function in a client-consultant relationship. The more technical orientation of the MIS subordinate IC staff members and their relative choice of tasks appears to be an attempt to maintain credibility with the MIS organizational superiors.

## 5. Senior Management Support and Participation in EUC

Support and promotion of end-user computing at the highest level of the organization affected the flow of resources into the IC, but did not appear to threaten its existence. Our sample includes firms with enthusiastic senior management who actively support EUC, cautions senior managers, and one firm that has no champion of EUC above the division level. In this sample there is no consistency between this presence or absence of enthusiasm and the organizational location of the IC, or for that matter, the participation of senior management in training programs or EUC activities. The three firms studied that engaged in significant engineering-based activities (INDPROD, NUC and ELEC) had senior personnel who are either active end-users or have participated in IC sponsored training sessions. This is no doubt due to the predisposition and technical background of the managers.

## 6. Information Center Goals and Objectives

Gerrity and Rockart [2] have concluded that a stated strategy is an important criterion for successful implementation of the IC concept. In general, our findings support this.

The existence of formal strategies, policies, and procedures indicates a “managing” approach to information centers. In ELEC, NGAS and FININST, the IC was clearly used as a structure to manage end-user computing. This is not to imply that they did not provide encouragement and training, but in each case these activities represented a smaller commitment of staff and resources. They set their own agenda; activities in which the IC would participate were clearly defined and did not overlap in any way with the functions of MIS. Each had developed policy and procedure documents. Each set standards for hardware and software supported by the IC and established justification and acquisition procedures. In each case, terminology such as “cost-effective” and “controlled” occurred in mission statements.

It is not clear that these ICs that seek to “manage” EUC are doing so as a result of some developmental life cycle. None of these three organizations had a longer history of end-user computing or a longer established IC than the other three firms. We believe that these ICs manage EUC rather than focusing on encouragement and support activities because of the existence of clear organizational objectives that evoke the responsibility to manage and control organizational resources.

The same three firms (NGAS, FININST, and ELEC) had evaluation procedures based upon their IC objectives. NGAS procedures were largely anecdotal but complete. FININST performed detailed cost/benefit analyses with end-user groups as a part of a needs audit; they had also initiated a series of questionnaires in an attempt to document the benefits of the IC. ELEC had developed a formal statement of objectives, but was just beginning to develop evaluation procedures; this indicated that they had some standards by which the effectiveness of the IC could be evaluated. The other three organizations had done no evaluation of IC activities, except when evaluating whether to start up the IC. The focus of these ICS was on faster data access without MIS involvement, including vague statements regarding productivity improvement. They had no formal statements of objectives.

## 7. The IC Service Mix

The initial services offered by the IC depends upon the source of enthusiasm for end-user computing. If the source is the end-users, then that population tends to dictate the focus of operations. For example, Benson [1] notes that mainframe end-users frequently require support in electronic mail or data capture activities. PC-based users, on the other hand, tend to be more involved in analysis and computer graphics. Because each of the ICS studied here has been in existence for several years, these initial requests should not bias the current mix of services.

Services offered can be organized into four general categories: (1) training, (2) problem solving, (3) "hot lines," and (4) administrative/technical support. Training activities were dominant in the ICs that identified supporting EUC as their major function; as was previously noted, training represented less of a commitment in the "managing" ICs. Four ICs conducted formal classes for small groups (six to ten users). The nature of the training depended upon the degree of common interests of the participants. INDPROD and ELEC constructed training groups with some common interests and tailored applications specifically to them; NGAS conducted training solely on a one-on-one basis, with training keyed to a user-identified application of immediate importance. CONPROD had no full-time IC staff, but relied on a peer training network for informal instruction. All IC managers agreed that the biggest obstacle to effective training was lack of available hardware for use immediately after the training session. Three of the six ICs provided portable PCs on a short term check-out basis in an effort to alleviate this problem.

Problem solving activities varied according to the software supported and depended on whether end-users used micros, mainframes, or both. All six managers cited database modeling as a special problem area requiring support. Mainframe users have a greater requirement for applications development, whereas microcomputer systems tend to have better documentation that encourages “self-help” and independent development.

The objectives and mission of the IC are quite evident in the approach that each takes to telephone “hot line” support. In the ICs that seek to “manage” EUC, the degree to which resources are dedicated to hot line support is limited. NGAS, for example, had no hot line service as it was seen as a less cost-effective use of staff time than alternative IC activities. FININST, on the other hand, provided hot line services, and this was considered part of their mission of supporting remote users within an urban area. This was, however, viewed as a major source of staff burnout and lowered productivity, and it was therefore continuously under review. In contrast, NUC staff members devoted virtually all of their resources to “hot line” activity. In each case where telephone support was provided, the IC manager cited it as an area where demand for the service exceeded their ability to provide it.

INDPROD did not provide “hot line” support to all end-users. Corporate objectives required that EUC services be distributed in such a way that there could be no impediment to their use. The IC developed a “key user” concept, in which each department supervisor identified one individual to serve as a first line of support in lieu of the IC. This permitted remote manufacturing facilities to have support similar to that available at corporate headquarters, and enabled the IC staff to develop personal relationships with a smaller number of individuals. In addition, it raised the level of sophistication of questions that were directed to the IC in an organization where the IC had wordprocessing support as a part of its charter.

In IC units that seek to encourage and support EUC, on the other hand, “hot line” support and rapid response are considered crucial elements. However, effective mechanisms to achieve this goal still elude most organizations. In one interview, the then IC manager explained that his estimate of the client base served by the IC was the product of the number of users and the number of software items each user employed, because a single user familiar with three software packages was essentially three potential “hot line” clients.

The service category of administrative support includes such tasks as maintaining mainframe user identifications, processing requests for data extracts, developing macros and job control statements, and installing and repairing hardware. All ICS facilitated the creation of production data extracts from the MIS department. Other administrative support functions depended in part on the relative use of mainframe and microcomputers and, in part, on the extent to which alternative procedures were encouraged. INDPROD used their key user concept to distribute the cost of IC support to the departments that required it. FININST had a log of IC staff support time for distributed cost allocation. By comparison, three other organizations considered the IC to be a nonchargeable service.

## 8. Critical Success Factors

Each IC manager was asked to identify the factors that were critical to the successful implementation of the IC concept in their organization. Their responses can be categorized as follows:

\- Establishing hardware and software guidelines to insure compatibility, reduce the number of hardware configurations and software products supported, and evaluate mainframe software for EUC support.

\- Characteristics of IC staff and users to provide quick response time to user requests, to establish a positive working relationship with MIS, and to identify and nurture champions of EUC among users.

\- Management philosophy, including “establishing clear lines of responsibility” between the IC staff and users, and “eliminating the control philosophy of the MIS department.” One firm stressed that the potential benefits of EUC must not be oversold, emphasizing that productivity of users may actually decrease during a learning phase.

\- Insuring cost-effectiveness to provide a formal justification process and thereby increase the potential for IC success, and to demonstrate to management the cost effectiveness of specific software and systems.

Problem areas most frequently mentioned by these same managers included the speed of technological change and the difficulty of providing advanced training to experienced users, increasing problems with providing user access to the quantity and quality of data that they request, user database development problems, and problems with computer capacity.

## 9. The Future of End-User Computing

In Social Psychology of Organizing [6], Karl Weick discusses the tightening of “organizational dyads” that occurs when the buffers that permit autonomous action are removed. Organizational lines of authority, ownership of hardware, systems, and data, and compartmentalization of tasks are examples of the buffers that have been reduced or eliminated by EUC. This concept of increasing interdependence in organizations provides a framework for the opinions on the future of EUC that were recorded in our interviews.

An important impetus for the development of the IC concept was the creation of a more independent environment for computer-based decision support. Prior to IC, requests for timely computer support required lead times and approval cycles that made ad hoc decision support difficult. One purpose of the end-user approach was to establish a buffer between decision makers and the MIS department, thereby reducing the direct demand for MIS services.

However, end-users have become more sophisticated consumers of data and software products. End-users now request more database access and sophisticated fourth generation languages, which will require that a closer relationship with MIS be reestablished. It is likely that the IC will assume this role, and that the relationship between the IC/MIS dyad will indeed tighten (see Henderson and Treacy). The challenge to the IC will be to negotiate the terms of this new relationship in such a way that the opportunities created by EUC are not compromised. An autonomous IC with clearly stated goals and objectives and established procedures will be in a stronger position to negotiate the terms of these agreements.

## 10. Conclusion

This research involved extensive interviews with IC managers, staff, and end users. The data suggested four hypotheses with the following generalizations:

1. The organizational position of the IC and the background of its staff are important variables in developing an autonomous IC department dedicated to the management of EUC.

2. Firms that have a clear mission for the IC have a greater ability to insure that the mix of EUC support services offered reflect the mission of EUC in the organization. Lack of such a focus results in a service mix that frequently fails to rise above the routine training and encouragement of EUC; it seldom creates a competitive advantage.

3. Evaluation of the IC services and the effectiveness of EUC is unlikely to occur in laissez-faire IC organizations.

4. Managers of information centers who truly manage independent information support operations view the future of EUC within the context of organizational dynamics. Managers of ICS that primarily support and encourage EUC are concerned about new products that will require training and support, but do not express concerns about the broader implications of EUC on the organization.
