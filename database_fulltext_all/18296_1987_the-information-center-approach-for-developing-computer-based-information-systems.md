# The Information Center Approach for Developing Computer-Based Information Systems

Charles R. Necco, Carl L. Gordon, and Nancy W. Tsai

MIS Department, School of Business and Public Administration, California State University, Sacramento, 6000 J Street, Sacramento, California 95819, USA

Organizations which have used computers for a number of years are well acquainted with the software maintenance problem and the new-application development backlog. The first exists because computer programs require various types of changes throughout their life. The second has been created because so many systems development staff have to be assigned to software maintenance, and because many computer users have recognized the value of additional computer applications in their organization.

It is believed that the concept of end-user computing has been used to relieve the new application development backlog in many organizations. In some, information centers have been implemented partly to support the development of computer applications by end-users. A survey was conducted to determine to what extent the information center approach is being used. Over one-half of the responding organizations reported their use. The survey included an identification of the functions provided by information centers to end-users and the responsibilities of both information center consultants and end-users. Recommendations are presented.

Keywords: Information center, Software maintenance problem, New application development backlog, End-user computing.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background

Organizations have been using computers to perform data processing tasks for over 30 years. During this period, computer hardware and software technology has changed dramatically. The approaches used to develop computer-based information systems (CBIS) have undergone changes, also. The major purpose for the use of a computer in an organization has been to perform necessary data processing and to provide useful information in a cost-justified manner.

The use of computers in organizations has met with varying degrees of success, and some organizations have encountered major problems. Among these are the software maintenance problem and the users' unmet demands for additional service. These have been fueled by the rapid technological improvements and consequent hardware cost-reduction in the computer industry.

The software maintenance problem exists because changes are generally required throughout the life of a computer program. Some of the more common are to:

1. Correct an error which was previously undetected.

2. Accommodate changed laws or governmental regulations. For instance, the effect of changes to tax laws on payroll programs.

3. Support new requirements of users. As a user gains experience in using computer output, additions or modifications seem necessary.

Many requests to change are very complex and time consuming, and such maintenance may require extensive effort on the part of both systems analysts and programmers.

Over the years, the inventory of installed computer programs has grown. Today, large organizations have thousands of operational programs written in procedure-oriented languages, such as COBOL. These require periodic maintenance. The required program maintenance is even more difficult when the programs are unstructured and poorly documented. In some organizations, the software maintenance problem has reached such proportions that one-half to three-fourths of the systems development staff are assigned to this work $[7,8]$ ; thus, there are less personnel available for new application development. It has been estimated that many organizations have a backlog that ranges from several months to five years $[2,5]$ .

It has also been suggested that many users become so frustrated over the inability to the traditional department to satisfy their requests that they have become reluctant to make more [8]. Various techniques have, therefore, been developed to resolve such problems:

1. Structured programming; to make programs more maintainable and to improve programmer productivity.

2. Better management of the system development life cycle; to be responsive to user requirements.

3. Structured analysis and design; to develop CBIS which are better and require less maintenance.

4. Automated support for systems analysis, systems design, and programming; to improve the productivity of developers.

The proper use of these techniques should improve the situation. However, it is too soon to estimate the magnitude of their impact, especially since users have become too frustrated and impatient to wait for the traditional management information systems (MIS) department. Instead, the concept of end-user computing has arisen. In some organizations, an information center has been installed partly to support end-user computing.

## 1.2 Information Center Approach

The information center approach was developed by IBM Canada in 1974 to promote end-user computing; they were experiencing a software maintenance workload which absorbed about 75 percent of their system development personnel. Their initial effort was focused on helping end-users to access databases on mainframe computers and to write simple programs to generate information needed to manage the organization [4]. Subsequently, information centers have been developed to manage end-user computing on both standalone microcomputers (personal computers) and via terminals or microcomputers which interface with larger computers. Furthermore, MIS researchers have recommended that information centers support end-users by helping them select applications and by providing standards and training to assist them with developing, testing, and documenting end-user applications in a satisfactory manner [1,6,12].

An information center is normally separate from the traditional MIS department. It should be staffed with technical specialists and consultants who assist end-users in the selection and use of hardware and software to perform tasks in their functional areas. There are various ways an information center can be organized, various types of computer usage that can be supported, and differing responsibilities for information center consultants and end-users.

The MIS literature provides examples of why and how the information center approach to develop CBIS should be used $[5,10]$ . Research was deemed necessary to determine:

1. How many and what types of organizations are using the information center approach?

2. What other approaches to develop CBIS are being used in conjunction with this approach?

3. What is its organizational location?

4. What specific functions does it provide?

5. What are the relative responsibilities of the information center consultants and end-users?

## 2. Results

## 2.1 Research Method

A questionnaire was developed and mailed to a sample of 950 randomly selected organizations listed in The Directory of Top Computer Executives [3]. The survey was national in scope, and 97 properly completed questionnaires were returned by responding organizations throughout the United States, for a return rate of $10.2\%$ . The data from these usable questionnaires provide the basis for the results, analysis, and discussion presented in this paper.

## 2.2 Users of the Information Center Approach

Of the organizations which responded to the survey, 50 indicated that they were using the approach. Table 1 presents their industry backgrounds. Table 2 provides a summary of the characteristics of those organizations using the information center approach.

This suggests that organizations who have installed information centers have more employees and have used computers longer than those others who responded to the survey. This seems consistent with the thoughts that organizations who have used computers longer will have a larger software maintenance problem and that organizations with more employees are likely to have a greater unmet demand for new computer services. Therefore, these kinds or organizations would be more inclined to install information centers.

The responders, also, provided data on their approaches to developing CBIS. Table 3 provides some insights into how organizations are developing CBIS today and to what extent the information center approach is being used in conjunction with other approaches.

Apparently some organizations currently have multiple approaches to developing CBIS. Furthermore, the information center approach is being widely used with all other approaches, but most highly by organizations that provide both an automated and prototyping approach. The automated approach needs computer-based tools to support systems analysis, systems design, and programming to facilitate the development of CBIS. The prototyping approach often utilizes fourth generation languages with a database management system to develop a model of the desired system. Both approaches require relatively new hardware and software. Therefore, it appears to be reasonable that such organizations would install information centers which, also, require newer hardware and software.

## 3. Conclusions and Recommendations

## 3.1 Conclusions

The research described in this paper was performed to gain insight into how information centers are being used by organizations today to support end-user computing. Results from a sample of a broad cross-section of organizations throughout the United States:

1. End-user computing is being done by over two-thirds of the organizations.

2. Over one-half the organizations reported using the information center approach to support end-user computing.

3. Organizations are using multiple approaches to develop CBIS in their organizations today.

4. In the majority of the organizations that have an information center, the manager of the information center reports to the manager of the MIS department.

5. Information centers are widely supporting the functions of office automation, personal computing, end-user computing via a mainframe, and decision support systems.

6. The responsibilities for the performance of the major tasks required to develop a CBIS in an information center environment are assigned to end-users, information center consultants, and shared to varying degrees in the organizations.

## 3.2 Recommendations

Organizations should recognize that the software maintenance problem associated with traditional MIS departments is likely to persist and will only serve to heighten the problem of unmet user demand. End-user computing has arisen to meet these demands, but it should be managed by using an information center approach. Therefore, computer-using organizations should be considering using this approach if they do not currently have an information center. Furthermore, organizations that have one should evaluate the results obtained through its use.

The following should be considered when an information center is initiated; they should also be considered when it is periodically evaluated.

1. Recognize that end-user computing and the information center approach are merely alternatives for developing CBIS. Therefore, a formal strategy for their use should include:

a. How end-user computing, supported by an information center, complements the CBIS development efforts of the traditional MIS department.

b. How the information center will be supported in terms of a budget, staff, and other resources.

c. How the responsibilities for the acquisition and use of the appropriate hardware and software technology will be allocated.

d. How the performance of end-users and the information center staff will be measured.

2. Be aware that when end-users develop CBIS they can repeat all the mistakes made in traditional MIS development. Therefore, use the information center approach to plan and control end-user computing and avoid experiencing:

a. Uncoordinated acquisition of microcomputer hardware and software; it may be incompatible with other hardware and software in the organization.

b. Technology that does not improve personal productivity and support the attainment of organizational goals; it is not cost-effective.

c. Development of computer applications in a non-standard manner, so that:

(1) The requirements of the application are not clearly and formally specified, so that the end-user cannot determine if the requirements are met.

(2) Improper systems analysis is performed, so the automation is of an awkward, inconsistent manual procedure.

(3) The application is difficult to maintain and can produce reports which are inconsistent and inaccurate.

(4) Inadequate testing allows omissions, inconsistencies, and inaccuracies in the system, so that erroneous output may not be detected and yet may be used to support decision making.

(5) Documentation is inadequate and hinders application of the system, making maintenance more difficult and time consuming.

(6) The task of integrating independently developed applications into other operations will be impossible.

d. Development of duplicate computer applications and related databases in different functional areas leading to inconsistent information and the inefficient use of resources.

3. Ensure that the information center approach is used to manage end-user computing by:

a. Developing procedures that evaluate requests for computer services and determine whether the development work is more appropriate for the traditional MIS department or end-user computing.

b. Writing standards for the development and maintenance of end-user computer applications.

c. Describing the responsibilities of end-users and information center consultants and ensuring that they receive the appropriate training.

d. Developing guidelines for estimating the costs and benefits of end-user computer applications.

e. Requiring that implemented end-user applications be audited to determine whether user requirements are being met and that the benefits were realized.

f. Establishing a top management steering committee that devotes time and energy to oversee the use of computers in the organization by end-users and provides the necessary support for the information center approach.

g. Ensuring that information center consultants have both the technical skills and orientation necessary to provide end-users with effective support.
