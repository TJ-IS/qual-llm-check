# What Does Information Technology “Do” to Business Communications?: Two Empirical Studies

Douglas E. Durand

Professor of Management Information Systems, School of Business, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

Rex O. Bennett

Professor of Marketing, Chapman College, School of Business Orange, CA 92666, USA

Samuel Betty

Associate Professor of Communication, University of Colorado at Denver, Denver, CO 80202, USA

According to recent surveys, communication skills are among the most important areas of managerial performance. With the widespread use of information technology, end users are producing large amounts of written communications. Yet surprisingly little empirical research has been conducted to determine the effects of information technology on business communications. Reported here are two studies, which focus on the effects of information technology on the structure of written communication, and the time required to read end-user generated versus traditionally generated compositions.

Two views of the writing process and the role of information technology in it are the mediationist perspective (technology permits the text to be polished until it is of the highest quality) and the extensionist perspective (technology encourages open, natural composition which is conversational in tone). Our findings supported the latter view.

Its effects on end-user composition include more composition time and document length. Thus, organizational efficiency (overall time savings) could decrease through the extensive use of end-user generated written communication.

Keywords: Communication, Business communication, End-user computing, Word processing.

## 1.1 Two Views of Word Processing Technology

The literature on effects of word processing technology is diverse; we classified it into two fundamentally different views.

## 1.1.1 Mediationist Perspective

The first view is the mediationist perspective. Theorists who take this position stress the technology's contribution to working and reworking a written piece until it is polished to the writer's maximum ability. The word processor encourages changes and rewriting (revisions) because it is easy to make alterations to the layout, sequence, and content of a written document. Once the content has been entered, average persons can craft the work until it is polished to a point which, because of practical limitations (e.g. turn-around time) was heretofore beyond their grasp. A writer can also easily try various phrases and word orders and select the most appropriate.

From this viewpoint, the computer permits a high degree of control over the writing process, resulting in a more complex and polished product. Persons using other methods simply would not be able to accomplish such written quality in a similar time frame.

## 1.1.2 Extensionist Perspective

The extensionist perspective is a second view of the technology. It holds that the computer encourages a more open and natural flow of words. The resulting output is more direct, less stilted, less complex, and exhibits less self consciousness. It is more like verbal conversation than is conventional written communication.

The extensionist argument suggests that less formality and less abstraction of expression are likely hallmarks of text generated through word processors. The speed of the equipment encourages a casual stream of consciousness. Consistent with this view, many hardware manufacturers claim that it is faster to enter text on a keyboard than to dictate it. If writing with paper and pencil results in word patterns which are different from normal speech, then using an alternate technology (i.e. word processor) could result in communication different from that generated by hand: a more conversational communication.

## 1.1.3 Word Processing Technology and Style

Empirical evidence to support the validity of either perspective is very limited and flawed. The perspectives may not be mutually exclusive, word processing may encourage a mix of stylistic and structural effects. Lacking economical support for either perspective, the researchers focused on the extensionist perspective because of its parallel to spoken communication. This parallel is supported by the extensionist emphasis on fluency, spontaneity, casualness, informality and conversation. The literature defining the differences between speech and writing points out that speech is wordier, syntactically less complex, more personal, more repetitious, more monosyllabic, and less varied and simpler in vocabulary than writing $[1,2,4,5,6,11,16,21]$ . If the extensionist perspective is valid, word processing prose will have more of these characteristics of speech style.

## 1.2 The Studies

Two laboratory experiments were conducted. Twenty-two dimensions of writing style and length were analyzed. In addition, the time required to compose and read both word processing generated output and conventionally written compositions were compared.

## 1.2.1 Hypotheses

To guide these studies, several hypotheses were developed. They were founded upon the belief that word processing technology encourages a more informal, natural, or conversational style. Because each subjects' writing sample was an analysis of a business case, the style was expected to have an informational or business report orientation.

1.2.1.1 General Hypothesis Written analyses composed using word processing technology will have have a more informal and conversational writing style than those which are composed using the conventional technology.

1.2.1.2 Supporting Hypotheses In comparison with “handwritten” compositions, writings composed on the word processor are hypothesized to be, have, or use:

A. Conversational

1. more redundancy, more word repetition

2. longer sentences (long-winded)

B. Informal

3. a more informal vocabulary

4. more contractions

5. unorthodox use or spelling (less pure)

6. low verb quality (low-key style)

7. less punctuation

C. Information Orientation

8. fewer first person references

9. more consistent sentence length

10. longer paragraph length

11. more consistent paragraph length

D. Direct

12. more monosyllabic, simple sentence construction

13. more coordinate sentence structure, more assertive

14. fewer prepositions

15. less abstraction (more concrete)

16. less argumentation (assertive and confident)

17. less language energy (quiet or muted)

18. more total words written

E. Reading Speed

21. longer to compose

## 2. Method

## 2.1 Study 1: Discussion of a Case

## 2.1.1 Subjects and Procedure

Seventy-nine students in an introductory information systems course served as subjects for the first study. Their average age was 23 years. Eighty-one percent of the subjects were employed in business at the time of the study. For the majority, their work frequently required written communication. Thirty-seven percent of the students were female.

The subjects were randomly assigned to one of two groups. The experimental group $n = 41$ used a word processing module (Enable). The students had used the package for several weeks; they displayed proficiency in its use through completion of several assignments. Each of the participants using word processing indicated that they possessed sufficient typing skills to permit them to touch-type (without watching the keyboard). Students, who had been originally selected for word processing use but did not know how to type were assigned to the conventional writing group $n = 38$ . This helped insure that poor keyboard skills would not limit the written responses of the word processor group.

## 2.1.2 Task

Everyone analysed a “case”: The Dashman Company. This is a classic in the management field; it is short (one and one-half page) but provides a rich number of issues for discussion. To guide the subjects’ responses, four questions were given to help stimulate thinking and to focus on specific issues, thus reducing the variability in the analysis.

All subjects were given a maximum of ninety minutes to complete their written analyses; they could stop when they felt they were finished. The procedure placed a reasonable, real, time pressure on completion of the task (to simulate a work deadline).

## 2.1.3 Analysis

Each of the written outputs was analyzed using Quintilian Analysis [22], a computer program specifically designed to analyze writing style and content. It is widely used to detect and analyze stylistic patterns in written compositions, based upon grammatical rules and statistical norms of writing patterns found in twentieth-century American and English writing. Quintilian analysis measures, among other dimensions, those characteristics of style identified in Section 1.1.3 as indicators of word processing effects. The advantage of such a program is its ability to produce an objective rating of the writing style, and thereby eliminate variability. Quintilian's output are measures of writing style on twenty dimensions (see Table 1).

## 2.2 Study 2: Reading the Material

A second study was conducted to determine the time needed to read the compositions.

## 2.2.1 Subjects and Procedures

The subjects were business professionals. They were, on average, 29 years old with five years of work experience.

Each read a pair of written case analyses. The selections were those generated in Study 1. Both types of documents were retyped in a common format; the style and format was thus the same for both types of communication. To the degree possible, each pair had the same length. The word processing and (now typed) handwritten discussions were alternated to reduce any ordering effect (one-half read the end-user word processor document first). Subjects were instructed to read at a speed reasonable for comprehension. The time was recorded.

By analyzing a variety of responses, the procedure avoided the methodological problems associated with a fixed effects design, where each subject is given a single type of input. In contrast, the random effects design, used a variety of inputs (multiple word processor, pencil, paper and typist analyses) to determine the average effects of the technology on a group of end users. This more closely resembles on-the-job conditions; it provides information across a range of examples.

## 3. Results

## 3.1 Study 1

Summary results of the analysis are shown in Table 2. Here, a pattern emerges; it strongly supports the hypothesis that word processing technology has an extensionist effect on the writer. The pattern of the eleven statistically significant findings support this.

Most of the remaining hypotheses received directional, if not statistically significant, support; only three of the 21 hypotheses were not in the direction predicted.

## 3.2 Study 2

Table 3 gives the results of the reading speed test. Compositions produced on the word processor are read significantly faster than those composed using paper, pencil and typist (2.75 vs. 2.99 minutes, p < 0.10), while writers (on average) devoted 38 percent more time when using the word processor. Thus, reading speed increased while composition speed slowed.

It should be noted that the absolute lengths of word processed communications were substantially longer (total words) than those composed conventionally. However, due to the increased document length (12.7 percent more words), it took longer to read the word processor generated compositions. Thus, end-user efficiency of word processing technology may be negative.

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Implications for Management

The implications are significant and far reaching. First, word processing technology causes changes in the writing. People are likely to change the length, style, and complexity. And this can occur without knowledge of the writer. Such changes may be desirable or undesirable, but they occur: technology is not neutral.

For managers, the use of word processing technology should be a conscious decision. Some occasions may lend themselves to the use of word processing technology, while others may not.

Regardless of the personal preference, the match between task and technology should be made for maximum effectiveness. Managers who fail to recognize that technology changes the communication are limiting their personal effectiveness, as well as that of their organization. Study 2 showed that the time to read the output from word processors is less than that of handwritten text, if the selections have the same length. The simpler writing style seems to be faster, if not easier, to the reader.

The increased reading speed is mitigated by the finding that persons who used the word processor wrote more. More total time was required to read the output from word processors because more words were composed.

The additional composition time and document length associated with word processing appears to be a function of both the speed with which prose can be composed and entered into the system and the tendency of writers to spend more time writing. James Martin has characterized the technology as “user seductive”; persons frequently become so involved in the writing process that they continue to use it.

Productivity can be divided into two segments: efficiency and effectiveness. Both studies suggest that end-user word processing communications result in different output than those produced by conventional means.

From a strict efficiency viewpoint, serious questions have been raised regarding the desirability of end-user generated compositions. Such writing takes longer to compose and requires more time to read. Organizationally, productivity will decline unless the communication is more effective. From an effectiveness standpoint, end-user generated written communications have a more verbal and conversational structure and style than traditionally generated written communications. Does the writer's communication style affect the manager's confidence in decisions? Thus, important questions remain regarding the effectiveness of communications technology.
