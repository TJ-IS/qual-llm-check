# An Attribution Model of Decision Support Systems (DSS) Usage

Dr. Cary T. Hughes and Dr. Michael Lucas Gibson
North Texas State University, P.O. Box 13677 Denton, Texas
76203, USA

The effort to utilize Dss in the corporate sector has largely involved the implementation of a decision based only on strategic corporate advantages with little, if any, regard for behavioral issues. Businesses are social organizations and must consider behavioral influence on the success of implementing decision results. The application of attribution theory provides a means of preemptively incorporating behavioral considerations in the decision making process. By incorporating attribution theory in Dss models, the decision maker is able to increase the probability of success in the implementation of a selected decision alternative. This paper discusses why it is important to consider attribution theory in deriving a Dss model and suggests a simplistic means of applying this potentially powerful theory.

Keywords: Attribution theory, Behavioral science, Causal factors, Cognitive processes, Decision support systems, Effective decision making, Rational decision making.

## 1. Introduction

When a theory fails to model the phenomena it purports to model, it is not the phenomena, but the theory that is wrong. DSS model building in business is usually predicated on certain relationships holding true; e.g. the notion that an $X\%$ change in variable costs has a $Y\%$ impact on profit. But underlying these relationships are the behaviors and decisions causing the cost variation. But, the best formulated plans of decision makers frequently fall short of their expected outcomes because of behavioral difficulties in implementing the optimal results of the decision process. Thus business organizations can no longer afford to allow decisions to be made without preemptive consideration of the behavioral influences on the outcomes of decisions.

Depending on organizational goals, incentives, control systems, and inferences about subordinate behavior, decision makers exhibit strong preferences for certain outcomes. Once committed, decision makers are seen as susceptible to biases in both the acquisition and processing of information [15]. These biases usually surface as the decision maker arduously responds during the decision making process. However, most DSS systems do not include a provision for embedding desirable biases in the models designed to aid in decision making. Attribution theory provides a means by which certain biases are preemptively applied with a DSS.

## 2. The Place of Rationality

In studies of executive decision making, a consistent finding is that only a few of the issues faced by executives can be modeled by rational problem-solving techniques $[4,13,14]$ . In this sense, rationality is defined as a process beginning with a thorough canvassing of alternatives and some steps later ending with detailed plans for implementation $[7]$ .

However, managers seldom make decisions in this context. For example, in his study of General Motors, Kotter found that managers frequently made decisions, not based on the facts of the situation, but on how well the manager had performed on similar tasks in the past [11]. As a result of studies like this, the rational model has been under sustained attack in business and economics for over 30 years [5,17].

What has been established relative to rationality is that many, if not most, decisions in organizations are apparently subject to irrationality. This is true even without considering factors that lead to strange behavior: such as power and politics [1, 8, 16]. It is wrong, however, to assume that since there are many instances in which individuals do not conform to the rational model, they are not motivationally driven. It is more productive to first ask: if individuals do not behave as the rational model posits, are there other useful models explaining how and why they behave as they do? A DSS model influenced by attribution theory presents one such model.

## 3. Attribution Theory

Due to the social nature of business, it is necessary in designing effective DSS to discern the underlying rules and patterns that people use to understand and model the behavior of others. Broadly defined, attribution theory is not a theory but a set of theories about how people ascribe cause to the behavior of others. A single generally accepted definition of attribution theory does not exist. Some of the better ones are:

1. The cognitive process through which individuals infer causation from observed behavior [2].

2. The perception or inference of cause [10].

3. The study of the causal interpretations that

persons give to events in their environment [3]. Thus, Attribution Theory is a cognitive process involving perception and inference to deduce causation about observed behavior or events in the environment. Less precisely, it is the study of the rules of how people come to “know” what they see about other people and situations.

## 3.1 Kelley's Model of Attribution

Kelley proposed a covariance model of attribution for answering how people “know” or make attributions about the behavior of others:

“...(I)ntention is inferred according to the principles of uncommon effects: the intention governing the action is indicated by those of its consequences not common to the alternative actions, and the fewer such uncommon effects, the less ambiguous is the intention [9].”

In other words, the less problems have in common, the easier it is to distinguish between them and arrive at a solution.

The relevant causal factors for many problems are persons $(P)$ , stimuli $(S)$ , and times $(T)$ with respect to four modalities: distinctiveness, consensus, consistency over time, and consistency over modality. Distinctiveness is the extent to which others do not respond to the same stimuli in a similar manner. Consensus is the extent that others respond the same way. Consistency over time is the extent that the same stimuli elicit the same response at different times. Consistency over modality is the extent that an object elicits the same response in varying settings.

Crittenden [3] provides an excellent example of an interpretation of these four modalities. Given that a friend likes a movie, the observer's task is to ascertain whether it is a function of:

1. the friend's attributes (person),

2. the attributes of the particular movie (stimuli),

3. the attributes of current conditions (time).

The modalities are used to decide which possibility is more likely.

The modality of distinctiveness may be exhibited in the friend's assessment of the movie. This distinctiveness may be due to the distinctiveness of the:

1. friend's current self esteem (person),

2. the movie (stimuli), or

3. current conditions (time).

Consensus favorable reaction to the movie may influence the friend's judgement because:

1. the friend tends to follow the crowd (person),

2. the movie actually is well made (stimuli),

3. of the climate of the time period, such as initially popular period movies which do not stand the test of time (time).

The friend's judgement of the movie may exhibit consistency over time since this movie:

1. is the type of movie the friend consistently likes (person),

2. is consistently assessed over time as a good movie (stimuli),

3. is of the type whose assessment is consistently influenced by current conditions (time).

The final modality refers to consistency of evaluation across the other three modalities. The friend likes the movie because:

1. he likes it (person), regardless of whether it is distinctive, others like it, or the conditions under which it is viewed,

2. it is a well made movie (stimuli) and may or may not be distinctive, may or may not be a popular movie, and is always going to exhibit the quality of its production, or

3. of when and where it is viewed (time) regardless of whether the movie is distinctive or not, others like it or not, or whether or not this is consistent with assessments the friend makes over time.

The individual does not “logically” analyze a situation, but achieves much greater economy of thought by ascribing attributions about distinctive properties of the situation. The situation is evaluated more effectively if an iterative process is available. A DSS provides an iterative process for evaluating and reevaluating the situation. The inclusion of attributions in the DSS model prescribes that the model conforms with how decision makers actually analyze a situation.

## 4. Attribution Theory Model of DSS Usage

Fig. 1 represents a model of how attribution theory affects a manager's use of a DSS system. The manager starts out with a problem: a decision to make. A decision is reached by the use of the

DSS generator. Note that other factors, contextual variables such as organization level and the type of decision, influence the decision process. Also note that attributions on the part of the manager also affect the decision at this point.

Maier [12] suggests that the manager look at two factors before deciding whether to implement the decision or backtrack through the process again:

1. anticipated quality factor and

2. anticipated acceptance factor.

Maier proposed that an effective decision is determined by the formula:

Probability of = Probability \* Probability of
Effective Decision of Quality Acceptance

$$
P (E D) = P (Q) * P (A)
$$

where the normal mathematical rules of probability apply, e.g.

$$
\begin{array}{l} 0 \leqslant P (Q) \leqslant 1 \\ 0 \leqslant P (A) \leqslant 1. \end{array}
$$

If the probability of an effective decision is below the level of satisfaction for the manager, the manager goes back through the process and reevaluates the problem to arrive at another solution. This process is repeated until the manager arrives at a decision whose $P(ED)$ “satisfices” [17]. It should be noted that Maier’s formula is not a part of Attribution Theory. However, it can be incorporated into the overall model.

The important questions in this process are where and how the application of attributions on the part of the manager affect the outcome of the decision making process. During the first iteration through a problem situation, managers derive attributions affecting the outcome of the decision. In other words, some account must be taken of how people are likely to react to the decision outcome. However, these assumptions are not normally defined in a DSS model.

It is operationally simple to incorporate Maier's formula into the DSS model and have managers enter values for $P(Q)$ and $P(A)$ for each iteration until a “satisficing” $P(ED)$ is attained. The $P(Q)$ is a separate issue whose quantitative factors are usually included in construction of the DSS model. What is of concern, with respect to incorporating attribution theory in the DSS model, is how the manager arrives at the $P(A)$ .

Since the $P(A)$ is in a large part dependent on the way the manager views the people who will implement the decision, attribution theory offers an appropriate means for assessing $P(A)$ . More specifically, the attribution modalities become intricate components for deriving $P(A)$ and are incorporated into the DSS model. For example the following function represents the generic derivation of $P(A)$ :

![](/api/attachments/785NPXAV/fulltext/images/8a9e850fba1b0e4d06c4f2077409546cf3a9ce05625618039114cd3a0451a800.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Attributional model of DSS.

$$
P (A) = f (D, C, C T, C M),
$$

where

$$
D = \text {   Distinctiveness   }
$$

$$
C = \text { Consensus }
$$

$$
C T = \text { Consistency   over   Time }
$$

$$
C M = \text { Consistency   over   Modality. }
$$

One practical quantitative derivation of $P(A)$ is that it be locally linear:

$$
P (A) = a D + b C + c C T + d C M,
$$

where $P = a + b + c + d \leqslant 1$ , and a, b, c, d are relative weights assigned to represent the importance of each modality used in deriving $P(A)$ for each participant.

Having $P \leqslant 1$ bounds the weights of each manager and ensures that the weights of each participant are on the same scale. Thus, one person's set of weights do not dramatically impact the solution more than it should. The assignment of these relative weights and subsequent inclusion in the DSS model represents the preempting of the decision process with an attribution-based solution.

## 5. A Case Example

Suppose that a manager has a project to implement and that $P(Q)=0.8$ has been exogenously determined while $P(A)=0.4$ has been derived using the locally linear expression for $P(A)$ , as a result $P(ED)=P(Q)*P(A)=0.32$ . The resultant $P(ED)$ may be considered too low by the manager. Reevaluating estimates for $P(Q)$ and $P(A)$ , the manager reluctantly concludes that it is necessary to improve the acceptance factor; e.g. $P(A)$ .

In recalling similar instances, it occurs to the manager that rarely did anyone rebel against doing something; more often, failed projects had not been pursued enthusiastically. The manager therefore begins using distinctiveness, consensus, consistency over time, and consistency over modality to find the most plausible explanation for the lack of enthusiasm. After considering the situation, the manager derives the formula: $P(A) = 0D + 0C + 0.2CT + 0.2CM$ to be used in calculating the $P(ED)$ . The rationale is more important than the values: the manager decided that the problem was not distinctive, with the group resisting actions disrupting the established work flow (a = 0). Second, the group did not exhibit consensus of behavior relative to similar situations (b = 0). Rather, three particular persons in the group were usually quite vocal in their complaints and their comments tended to dampen group enthusiasm. Therefore, the key to attaining acceptance is the relationship between the consistency over modality and time. The group resists any new idea that disrupts the established work flow. In the first iteration, the manager believes that it is necessary to look into the actions of the three complainers in more detail.

Applying the four attribution variables to the issue of the extent to which each of the three complainers willingly accept the idea, the manager arrives at the following distributions:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (A) _ {1} = 0 D + 0 C + 0. 2 C T + 0. 2 C M \\ \text {(Complainer 1)} \\ P (A) _ {2} = 0. 3 D + 0. 4 C + 0 C T + 0 C M \\ \text {(Complainer 2)} \\ P (A) _ {3} = 0. 3 D + 0. 4 C + 0 C T + 0 C M \\ \text {(Complainer 3)}. \end{array}
$$

Reconstructing issues systematically, the manager discovers that the second and third individuals are not consistent over time or modality: they only object when the first person objects. The manager also notes that apparently the probability for acceptance for the group is the same as that for the first individual. When the first person complains about changes in working patterns the other two agree, with their complaints affecting the rest of the group.

The manager recognizes that while the attributions are subjective, further improvements come from active testing. The manager formulates a plan to counter the first individual's objections. The manager carefully watches how well the attributions match the individual's actual behavior and iteratively reevaluates the attributions via the DSS model until:

1. the attributions match the perceived behavior and/or

2. the desired result is achieved.

Only then is the decision actually implemented.

## 6. Implications and Conclusions

If the true purpose of a DSS is “Executive Mind Support,” as suggested by Wagner, then it should allow a manager to deal with the “people” aspects of an issue as well as the “thing” aspects [18]. Or as Maier’s model highlights, ignoring the human aspects of a problem can be a prescription for failure: a zero value for acceptance results in a product of zero. The attribution theory DSS model outlined in this paper allows the DSS user to address certain behavioral issues even if the user does not have a behavioral science background.

The model, based on thoroughly researched concepts [6], starts simply and builds iteratively. The DSS user can alter the depth, breadth, and specificity of the analysis. Individuals with a strong behavioral science background may make more accurate initial attributions, but it is possible that those with a weak behavioral science background benefit the most. This is true because it forces those uncomfortable with the “people” aspects of a problem to make attributions and initial attributions are corrected through the iterative process.

For those uncomfortable with people issues, the application of the DSS model is private. The model does not require the user to “psychologize” about subordinates, it merely asks the DSS user to state the attributions already made in a systematic fashion. In other words, rather than attempting to change the behavior of others, the model merely assists the user to plan actions, taking into account the behavior of others.

Finally, the Attribution Theory DSS Model is simple to operate without being over-simplistic and is based on one of the most powerful and thoroughly researched new areas in Social Psychology.
