# Security and Integrity Controls for Microcomputers: A Summary Analysis

J.L. Boockholdt, Ph.D., CPA

Department of Accountancy and Taxation, College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, Texas 77004, U.S.A.

This article addresses the impact of microcomputers on information system integrity and security and suggests modifications to system controls appropriate to both standalone and networked microcomputers. Common business applications are identified and associated threats described. Appropriate control measures are discussed, including ways of creating an environment for microcomputing and methods of achieving data integrity and security.

Keywords: Data security, Data integrity, Microcomputers, Personal computers, Internal controls.

Yet this growth has created new problems. Security and integrity controls that are successful for mainframe systems may be insufficient for microcomputer systems.

## 2. Microcomputer Applications

Microcomputers may be used by business in two ways: single or multiple standalone micros each dedicated to a specific application, or micros in a network (either with a local area network or a telecommunications link to a mainframe system).

## 2.1 The Standalone Micro

Another survey [10] of 533 firms disclosed that 70% of those using micros performed office automation functions, including wordprocessing; 69% used them for decision support activities, such as modeling and statistical analysis; 45% used them in support roles, including maintenance of personal files and schedules. The most popular software employed on a standalone micro [2] were spreadsheet packages (90%), wordprocessing (64%), general accounting (37%), and graphics (36%). Spreadsheet applications included cash management, cash flow forecasting, financial analysis, and financial modeling [14].

Standalone micros may be found in organizations of all sizes. Networked micros, on the other hand, are most commonly used in larger business to supplement the mainframe. This requires the use of a link.

## 2.2 Micro-Mainframe Links

As users become more experienced, they recognize the potential of linking micros with the mainframe in order to obtain data for spreadsheet applications from the corporate database. The connection can be accomplished in several different ways. Each provides some advantages and its own threats to data security and integrity. A technical description of these links can be found in $[4,5]$ .

## 2.2.1 Data Entry

Micros are often used as data entry devices. A data entry clerk will enter accounting data to a file on the mainframe. Because the micro is programmable, it may perform data validation.

## 2.2.2 File Serving

In such applications, data files are routinely stored on a mainframe and downloaded as required for processing in the micro. This has the advantage of local processing at the micro with file security and integrity provided by the mainframe.

## 2.2.3 Information Downloading

This application will probably be most in demand. Using information downloading, a manager may create a micro file from a mainframe file; this then becomes input to a program on the micro. However, applications of this type will generally be implemented by active information extraction. The desired data will often be obtained from the mainframe via its database management system.

Microcomputer systems encounter many of the control problems of mainframes although they also create some unique difficulties $[8]$ . The typical micro user has neither the time, the skills, nor the motivation to implement the data safeguards normally found in a mainframe data center. Furthermore, the processing power in a micro allows the user to make changes that are beyond the influence of the mainframe controls.

## 3. Threats to Data Security and Integrity

In order to identify appropriate controls for micro systems, it is necessary to analyze the control risks, the threats to system security and data integrity, that they create [9]. The risks are in two forms. The easiest to control are those that arise from physical vulnerability. The more difficult and dangerous risks are those resulting from the vulnerability of data.

## 3.1 Physical Vulnerability

Micros and their peripherals, like calculators and adding machines, are subject to theft. Because micros are small, they can also be located in many user work areas; this exposes them to environmental damage, a threat to which calculators and adding machines are less susceptible. However, precautions that have long been employed in mainframe systems can be taken at reasonable cost.

## 3.1.1 Restrict Physical Access

Microcomputers should be located in areas which restrict unauthorized physical access. Not only is the micro protected from theft and vandalism by this practice, but also its data is less vulnerable; e.g. a private office may be locked and a locked container made available for storing diskettes. A desk or filing cabinet will often be adequate for this, but a safe may be required for confidential data. Physical restrictions to access become very important whenever a microcomputer is used to process confidential information.

## 3.1.2 Safeguard the Hardware

It is advisable to attach a computer physically to its workstation; cables and locks can be obtained for this purpose. Other devices (e.g. surge protectors) are useful in protecting a micro from fluctuations in the voltage of its power supply. Standby power supplies may be worthwhile for critical applications. These may contain rechargeable batteries and allow the micro to be used for several hours during power outages.

## 3.1.3 Plan for Contingencies

Organizations with mainframe systems have learned from sad experience the need for contingency plans; unfortunately this has not generally translated into similar practice for micros. Micro systems are more likely to be exposed to damage from fire, flood, or vandalism. They are also more frequently exposed to failure from smoke, dust, and temperature variations. Fortunately, because of their cost and portability, contingency planning is also much easier.

An adequate plan means that hardware and software backup exists. Spares should be on hand for every kind of microcomputer. A riskier policy is to purchase a spare whenever a primary machine goes down, but rapid change means that a new spare may not execute all applications that run on the older machine. Even a state-of-the-art micro is of little use under these circumstances.

Other contingency controls include: back-up site agreements, off-site storage of critical files and programs, and adequate insurance coverage. However, these require the cooperation of users who may view them as unnecessary. Providing an appropriate control environment for micros can help to alleviate this motivational problem.

A microcomputer is normally subjected to more threats than is a mainframe. But steps can be taken at reasonable cost to counter them.

## 3.2 Vulnerability of Data

The popularity of microcomputers has resulted in a dispersal of computerized data throughout organizations. Such databases are often beyond the influence of established security and integrity controls. Furthermore, the threats which exist in traditional teleprocessing systems are greatly increased in those networks that incorporate microcomputers; data becomes vulnerable to threats of loss, theft, corruption, and misinterpretation.

## 3.2.1 Loss of Data

Mis managers working in a mainframe environment have learned procedures to protect the files of a mainframe computer. The typical micro user is, however, unconcerned and unskilled with file backup practices routinely employed on a mainframe. At some time, such a user will probably erase a file or write over data.

## 3.2.2 Theft of Data

Adequate security measures are seldom used with micro systems. A standalone micro frequently becomes an employee's personal system. Uncontrolled copies of data can be stolen, or unauthorized access to data can be attained through micros left unattended. In a network, the micro can also be used to circumvent the mainframe security system; e.g. automatic logon facilities allow users to retain the mainframe userid and password in the micros for easier access. This may also allow unauthorized access.

## 3.2.3 Corruption of Data

Mainframe systems normally go through extensive testing before being accepted for production use. Mainframe data entry programs usually incorporate validation procedures; microcomputer systems, on the other hand, are frequently developed by users neither familiar nor concerned with this type of practice. Such systems often consist of database or spreadsheet routines that have undergone no detailed testing and that provide little validation of data upon entry. Erroneous data can be introduced or erroneous processing can occur through programs with unknown bugs or incorrect logic.

Such a threat becomes more ominous when micros are linked to a mainframe. This allows corruption of data on the mainframe; e.g. data may be modified at the micro and uploaded to the mainframe database with inadequate checks and audit trails. Errors can also occur if managers have the ability to make changes to data files used in spreadsheet analysis.

## 3.2.4 Misinterpretation of Data

A final threat arises from improper use of data. Managers use accounting and statistical data from mainframe systems when performing analysis on their micros. Micro–mainframe links make it convenient to do so. However, mainframe data is subject to misuse.

Misinterpretation may arise because of a lack of knowledge of the source of data. For example, a manager may choose to use an allocated cost as input in making a decision, while not realizing that its existence will not be affected by the decision. Other misuse may occur because of mixed time frames. Data produced by an accounting system is gathered for periodic reporting, and may no be appropriate for decisions that do not conform to the reporting cycle. For example, this can be a problem when evaluating the results of an advertising campaign. Overaggregation also is a problem, because much data is so summarized that it is not useful for many managerial decisions. For example, an accounting system typically records equipment operating expenses in total; this does not allow a manager to determine the cost savings when disposing of a specific piece of that equipment. All forms of misinterpretation are characterized by the fact that they lead to bad decisions, which may be very costly to an organization.

## 4. Creating a Control Environment

Ultimate responsibility for creating an appropriate climate for integrity and security lies with top management [15]. Their involvement in creating a control environment should be evidenced in three ways, as will now be discussed.

## 4.1 Responsibilities for Integrity and Security

Some responsibilities for data security and integrity are summarized in Fig. 2 for various segments of a typical business. Because most segments are users, most are involved. Top management is concerned with defining an appropriate organizational structure, allocating resources, and establishing policies. In conjunction with these duties, it should establish two offices. The Director of Security reports to top management and has organization-wide responsibility for all security matters; the Data Security Officer is administratively appointed to the MIS department and is specifically concerned with integrity and security of data. Most businesses have an MIS advisory committee that contains representatives from almost all user departments. This committee should have concern for the maintenance of adequate integrity and security.

## 4.2 Microcomputer Support Group

This group, created by top management, should consist of employees from both user departments and MIS; they should be knowledgeable in both micros and their applications. By advising users concerning their problems, the support group can gain acceptance for company-wide standards. It can also create an awareness of practices among users who are otherwise unconcerned with these issues. These responsibilities may be assigned to a corporate information center, whose duties may encompass all end-user computing [6]. A summary of their duties is proposed in Fig. 3.

```txt
Top Management
- provide an appropriate organizational structure
- establish security policies and approve standards
- allocate resources
- require periodic reports on security and integrity

User Department Managers
- ensure training of users in security and integrity procedures
- enforce top management's policies and standards

- screen employees before hiring

- assess adequacy of security and integrity safeguards
```  

## 4.3 A Corporate Program for Micro Integrity and Security

The microcomputer support group provides a source for advice and direction of all micro users. Once this group is intact, it can establish a corporate program. This is an on-going process that identifies required measures for all computers in the company.

## 4.3.1 Define the Population

The support group must first identify the segments of the organization that are using microcomputers. This will require company-wide survey, which may possibly be performed in conjunction with an internal audit. This will provide an inventory of all micros, showing their locations, brands, operating systems, applications, and major data files. Any existing links to the mainframe will also be described. This list then identifies the population for which integrity and security measures must be developed.

## 4.3.2 Define the Risks

After completing this inventory, the support group determines the associated risks. For each desktop system, the physical and data vulnerability are assessed.

In assessing physical vulnerability, the support group determines the extent to which the system is subject to theft, sabotage, damage from the environment, or destruction. In assessing data vulnerability, they determine the susceptibility to loss, theft, corruption, and misinterpretation of data. These then become the basis for selecting appropriate integrity and security controls.

```txt
Sensitive Data
- major threat is theft
- proprietary information and data subject to privacy restrictions
- includes salaries, personnel information, trade secrets
- concern is both security and integrity

Critical Data
- major threat is loss
- data required for day-to-day operations
- includes shipments, billings, purchase orders
- primary concern is integrity

Noncritical Data
- not subject to threats
- data loss would be inconvenient, not harmful
- includes memos, analyses, and newsletters
- neither integrity nor security a concern
```

At this time, it is advisable to develop a classification scheme for all corporate data and to characterize each micro system. In some organizations, the use of two data classifications (“critical” or “noncritical”) may be adequate. A third classification, “sensitive”, may also be desirable. Different classifications imply different control procedures. One classification method is shown in Fig. 4.

## 4.3.3 Identify Integrity and Security Strategies

The support group must select appropriate physical safeguards for each micro system. These may include: locks, cables, safes, locked offices, etc. Integrity and security policies are developed governing each of the classification of the data. These are submitted to top management for adoption.

## 4.3.4 Implement the Program

Perhaps the most difficult task remains – that of gaining acceptance and adoption of the policies by users, whose cooperation is essential for its success. A properly constituted support group is consultative and advisory. The sales effort will be continuous, but as users become dependent on the group for technical help and advice, they will accept the policies.

A corporate program provides a framework for the adoption of microcomputer integrity and security practices.

## 5. Methods of Providing Integrity and Security

In order to control the threats, top management must create an organizational environment that fosters concern for data integrity and security. Within this environment, control techniques can minimize the vulnerability of data. Though researchers have identified many of the controls, little guidance has been provided to link them to the threats $[13,18]$ . The support group must choose a combination of techniques that is appropriate for the threat and the risk level.

## 5.1 Data Integrity in a Micro Environment

Loss of data is prevented by policies which require periodic copying of all data files. Corruption of data is minimized by proper testing and validation of new applications, and by limiting their ability to modify existing live data files in applications.

## 5.1.1 Standalone Micro Applications

Micro users who are unaccustomed to a system can easily erase or forget to save their data files. Furthermore, such users are often unaware of methods for recreating data when errors occur. Consequently, most techniques require training users in the system, appropriate data backup procedures, and monitoring methods. Those are most important for data classified as critical or sensitive.

Backup procedures are routinely used on mainframe systems: their use should also be common on microcomputers. Management should ensure that whenever a hard disk is purchased, a streaming tape device should also be provided. Then users can easily make a backup copy at daily or weekly intervals. Some copies should be stored off-site. Backup procedures for floppy disks should be equally simple. One approach is the use of two floppies, one labeled “MWF” and the other “TuThSa”. At the end of each workday, each user makes a copy of that day’s floppy, and another employee collects all the copies.

Regardless of the backup procedure, steps must be taken to ensure that they are followed routinely. All micro users should have training in the applications software and backup management procedures. Supervisors should perform periodic audits. And finally, the Data Security Officer should be available to consult with users.

Another problem is the threat of data corruption. Micro systems seldom have the kinds of system test and validation controls found in mainframe systems. Therefore they are more likely to produce misleading reports. Some managers argue that all micro applications should be controlled as though they ran on a mainframe; others argue that this would limit the usefulness of the micros. A more reasonable approach is to apply a cost/benefit test: the cost of a control should not exceed its benefits. Then the control for an important application, such as data entry or strategic planning, should be greater than that exerted over less far-reaching applications [11].

All important micro programs should be subject to independent verification and certification. The test procedures should then be documented. Both user department managers and internal auditors have performed such functions $[1,3]$ .

Other controls over the databases can help to prevent data corruption. To the extent possible, micro applications should be written to perform simple validation upon data entry. Databases created on a micro should contain date and time fields which show the last time that they were modified. This can show unauthorized use or suggest that the data is obsolete. Reports show both the date of production and the date of last database change.

Improper restoration of data files from a backup file may be a source of error if the wrong backup copy is used. This may be prevented if only one specific individual is authorized to restore a specific data file. This person should maintain a written record of all such activities.

Data files on a standalone micro may never be as reliable as those produced on a mainframe. Nevertheless, appropriate use of these policies will reduce the probability of lost or corrupted data.

## 5.1.2 Networked Microcomputers

When microcomputers are used in a network with a mainframe, its backup procedures may be utilized. However, corruption of data on the mainframe is a real threat introduced by the micro-mainframe link. This threat can be reduced by providing appropriate limits when micros access the mainframe. Applications that allow full exploitation of the link require extensive controls.

Many organizations allow only one way data transfer. Data may be downloaded from the mainframe to the micro, but cannot be uploaded. This may be enforced by allowing read-only access by micros. A simpler version requires the user to request data in advance; it is then downloaded to a floppy disk by MIS personnel and the disk is handed to the user.

For those applications in which one way access is unacceptable, two way data transfer may provide both downloading and uploading of data. This allows for data entry, file serving, and complex spreadsheet applications.

The support group should be authorized to set standards that control data uploading, often based on existing practices governing updates on mainframes. If data is uploaded to the mainframe by communications software, file backup policies that exist for the mainframe then protect the micro user from loss.

Organizations should establish policies governing the use of microcomputer data in decision making. These policies can help to prevent dissemination of reports containing corrupted data. If all data is validated when uploaded and then downloaded only as needed, no data used in a report is more than one generation removed from validated data. This decreases the risk of data corruption. An alternative policy requires that all mainframe data be marked with the time and date of its creation. Any report produced by a micro is required to show this date and time stamp.

Another feature is implemented in certain software packages used for active information extraction. This is a “check-in check-out” facility, which notes data downloading and disallows access to the data again until it has been checked-in. This feature presents data corruption similar to that of concurrent update, where one user accesses a data field, a second changes it, and the first then overloads this change.

Two way data transfer on a link increases both the threat to mainframe data files and the benefits to users. Users will be more tolerant with policies if they understand the threats that exist to data integrity.

## 5.2 Data Security in a Micro Environment

Security is important for both critical and sensitive data, but more extensive procedures may be needed for that which is sensitive. The analysis of security requirements involves classifying the controls into five categories: overall security, system access, data access, monitoring and network access [7]. The first of these is concerned with security administration and the communication of security concerns.

## 5.2.1 System Access

For standalone micros, controls include restrictions on physical access. With networked micros, system access should also be restricted by the use of user-ids and passwords. Effective procedures for password administration have been developed for terminal-based teleprocessing systems and they can be extended for use in micros.

## 5.2.2 Data Access

Many controls that prevent access to data files and software are similar to those employed in mainframe systems. Every sensitive file should have a designated custodian. Each file should be stored either on a specially colored floppy disk or a clearly marked removable hard disk. These should be locked in a cabinet or safe after use. Storage of sensitive data on a fixed hard disk should be avoided, though it may be encrypted before being written to the disk.

In a network, data access is restricted by establishing user profiles and assigning read, write, or execute permission. However, it is best to limit the storage of sensitive data.

## 5.2.3 Monitoring

It is necessary to record and investigate usage of computer systems and data to detect unauthorized access. Some organizations maintain an activity log that is reviewed by the manager in charge. However, this practice will not disclose unauthorized and unrecorded accesses. Physical access restrictions are thus very important. In a network, the mainframe operating system should log any unauthorized attempt to access data, execute sensitive programs, or use systems utilities to copy, modify, or access files. These should be investigated by the data security officer.

## 5.2.4 Network Access

Many companies provide dial-up access to a mainframe. The vulnerability of these systems has been widely documented. Such systems are especially vulnerable to penetration by those who have knowledge of the system. It is therefore critical that the data security officer implement appropriate restrictions over telephone lines, modems, and the use of passwords. As a minimum, software should be installed to break the connection after a certain number of invalid passwords are submitted. Automatic callback software is also sometimes worthwhile.

## 6. Conclusion

Three decades ago, many observers predicted that computer systems would revolutionize the way managers make decisions. They suggested that the computer would become an integral part of these processes. Evidence indicates that this may be true. But the proliferation of micros has created legitimate concerns about data integrity and security.

This article has identified the major threats in the microcomputer environment. Some measures have been suggested for controlling them. Procedures have been described for creating an organizational climate in which controls can be effective.
