# Validating and Applying User Satisfaction as a Measure of MIS Success in Small Organizations\*

Louis RAYMOND

Department of Administration and Economics, Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières, P.O. Box 500, Trois-Rivières (Québec), G9A 5H7, CANADA

Measurement of user satisfaction is a methodological problem crucial to information systems research. As the impact of an MIS upon organizational effectiveness is basically unmeasurable, measures of user satisfaction provide the most useful assessments of system success. This paper investigates the measurement of user satisfaction in the specific context of small organizations. An analysis of the potential impact of this specificity upon user satisfaction served as a basis for the selection and adaptation of a previously validated instrument. A modified version was developed and pretested; results of previous research were then replicated and extended by administering the instrument to 464 financial managers in small firms. The data obtained was analyzed and discussed in terms of reliability and validity. The validated instrument is applied to demonstrate its potential uses.

Keywords: MIS success, user satisfaction, small organization, system evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

To insure greater relevance and impact of information systems studies, research hypotheses frequently include MIS success as the critical dependent variable. Organizations need mechanisms to evaluate MIS projects and MIS effectiveness after implementation. Thus, operational measures of MIS success are needed by researchers and practitioners alike. One can find in the empirical literature basically four types of surrogate measures of systems success: 1) user satisfaction (e.g. [5]), 2) level of systems usage (e.g. [13]), 3) user decisional performance (e.g. [4]) and 4) organizational performance (e.g. [23]). Some researchers have used more than one type of measure within the same study (e.g. [2]).

With respect to the measurement problem, researchers indicate that an approach based on user satisfaction, i.e. on the user's subjective judgement, is preferable to an approach based on objective measures of usage and performance [8,15]. In fact, apart from controlled laboratory experiments, problems such as the delayed effect of usage upon performance (individual and organizational learning), the difficulty of specifying acceptable measures of performance, and the necessity of controlling factors other than usage which affect performance, render the second approach much less attractive from a methodological and practical standpoint. Thus, previous methodological studies have attempted to define the user satisfaction construct and develop a valid and reliable standard measure $[10,16]$ .

This paper addresses the question of measuring user satisfaction in the context of small organizations. Prior work is replicated and extended by selecting, adapting and validating a previously developed instrument. Potential uses of the instrument are then demonstrated by applying it to identify specific organizational and individual MIS success factors.

## 2. Measuring User Satisfaction in Small Organizations

## 2.1 Measurement Criteria

The specific characteristics of small organizations have been examined by researchers from the various administrative sciences, e.g. [7,9,20]. In the information systems field, the unique organizational context of the small enterprise has been posited to have an impact on implementation success [6]. In turn, various aspects of information systems that apply to small business have been analyzed [19]. Thus, the empirical literature as well as the author's own experience suggest the following criteria for the measurement of MIS user satisfaction in a small business context:

1. The instrument should not only measure user attitudes toward the MIS product but also toward MIS services. Previous research has shown that smaller firms tend to be much more dependent on external resources and expertise in terms of systems development, operation, and maintenance. For example, the two principal problems cited by small firms in one study [3] were inadequate programs and poor service from service bureaus, while neither of these problems were mentioned by the larger firms. Thus, the level of service surrounding information systems usage in small organizations has a more direct impact on user satisfaction, because such organizations have no slack resources to act as a buffer between the user and the system. A consequence of the preceding criterion is that the instrument would be usable by practitioners as a diagnostic tool of user satisfaction.

2. The instrument should be oriented toward the implementation of the organizational MIS rather than the development of specific subsystems. As small business managers must shoulder a broader decisional burden, horizontal (finance, marketing, production) and vertical (strategic planning, operational control), their usage of the MIS is rarely restricted to a single subsystem. It is thus more appropriate to measure attitudes toward all the computer-based support, including transactional, reporting and decision-support subsystems or applications. For instance, this would exclude and instrument oriented toward a specific type of application such as a DSS success measure [21].

Small organizations also tend to implement MIS in a non-development manner by using consultants and standard software packages from vendors and service bureaus. This is due to the fact that limited intra- and extra-organizational resources prevent the small firm from having its own MIS development staff $[18]$ . Thus, an instrument which measures success factors of the classical MIS project, as in $[17]$ , does not fit in with the context of small organizations.

3. The instrument should be usable across organizations. Since the total number of MIS users in a small organization is minimal, researchers must sample many organizations to obtain statistically significant data. Each organization differs in terms of the nature of its organizational information system, it is thus impossible to have an instrument tailored to one organization, as in [14].

4. The instrument should be short and easy to answer. The personal and situational characteristics of small business managers make them particularly averse to anything that smacks of red tape or bureaucracy [1], these managers are also usually less experienced with computer-based systems. Thus, to insure a sufficient level and validity of response, the length and complexity of the instrument must be kept to a minimum.

## 2.2. Selection and Adaptation of the Instrument

A decision was made to evaluate previously developed user satisfaction instruments to determine if one could be used in a small systems context, albeit adapted. This was motivated by the desire to replicate and extend the previous work, promoting the use of a generalized measure. With this in mind, the Pearson and Baily instrument as modified by Ives et al. was chosen as best possible candidate.

The latter undertook to assess the reliability and validity of the former's measure and to improve it for use as a standard measure. Having sampled 200 production managers, they eliminated 6 scales from the original 39-scale instrument which utilizes the semantic differential technique; the number of adjective-pairs (items) per scale was also reduced from 4 to 2. Finally, a 13-scale short form of the instrument was produced. With respect to the present study, the weaknesses of the modified Pearson and Bailey instrument are: 1) no evidence of test-retest reliability, 2) inconclusive evidence on construct validity and 3) that it is still quite lengthy and includes scales that are not appropriate in a small systems context.

The strategy used to adapt the instrument was thus to eliminate scales that would be less relevant in small organizations (e.g., chargeback method of payment for services), and scales which ranked low in terms of their importance to the overall user satisfaction measure as determined by the two previous studies (e.g., volume of output information). This process produced the 20-scale instrument used in the present study; Table 1 presents the scales retained in the small systems version of the user satisfaction measure. Each scale is presented to the respondent in the form depicted in Fig. 1. The overall satisfaction rating is obtained by calculating the mean of the scores over the 40 items (20 scales, 2 items per scale); each item is given a score of 1 (dissatisfied) to 7 (satisfied) or equivalent.

Note that all 13 scales of the short form have been retained in the small systems version, thus insuring a primary level of validity and comparability. On the other hand, the vendor support scale, eliminated by Ives et al., was reintroduced in the small systems version. Whereas this aspect of MIS service is rendered quasi-invisible to large system users, it is of the utmost importance to the small business user-manager directly in contact with the vendor or service bureau.

A measurement study was undertaken to investigate the reliability and validity of the small systems version of the instrument. After a pretest with 27 users, the measure was administered as part of a mail survey of financial managers in the 3699 small manufacturing organizations of the province of Quebec, Canada. In this context, small means firms with 20 to 250 employees. This cutoff point is used by the Small Business Administration of the United States for the manufacturing sector, while the smallest firms were excluded to increase the probability of sampling computerized firms (it was not known in advance whether a firm was computerized or not). Because of the preponderance of accounting-related applications in small businesses, the financial manager (vice-president of finance, controller, or chief-accountant) is generally the main user of the MIS. The adressee was instructed to return the questionnaire unanswered if the firm did not benefit from computer-based support.

## 3. Validating the Instrument

## 3.1. Reliability

Reliability refers to the accuracy or precision of a measuring instrument [11] in terms of its stability over the time (test-retest) and error of measurement (error variance). The main objective of the pretest was to verify the test-retest reliability of the instrument as neither Pearson and Bailey, nor Ives et al. had attempted to assess it. Amongst the 27 users who participated in the pretest, 5 responded in the presence of the author to verify that there was negligible effect of ambiguity or lack of understanding of the instructions, and of the measurement scales themselves. The retest was administered one month after the test. Table 1 presents the product-moment correlations between the scores of the test and retest. Only 4 of the 20 scales had non-significant correlations; however, the overall measure of user satisfaction had a highly significant correlation $(\mathrm{p} < 0.001)$ . Thus, the instrument seems to exhibit sufficient temporal stability.

The error in an instrument is obtained by applying Cronbach's alpha test to the individual scales and to the overall measure. The reliability results for the actual measurement study involving

464 user-managers are also presented. The inter-item reliability scores range from 0.81 to 0.95 for the individual scales, with the overall user satisfaction measure having a reliability coefficient of 0.91. The internal consistency of a test is deemed to be acceptable when the reliability coefficients surpass the 0.80 level.

## 3.2. Content Validity

An instrument possesses content validity if its scales are representative of all aspects of the attribute being measured. Evaluating content validity is basically a question of judgement; i.e., judging each scale for its presumed relevance. We can first refer to Pearson and Bailey and to Ives et al. The rigorous manner in which these authors developed both a complete and short list of scales plus the results of their analyses lend credence to a claim of content validity. Scales that were eliminated in the small systems version were removed for sound reasons: non-relevance to the context and/or non-importance to the measure.

## 3.3. Construct Validity

Construct validity is an attempt to understand what property or properties can account for the variance of the instrument. The most powerful approach to construct validation uses factor analysis [12], which is a method to determine the number and nature of the underlying variables (factors) among larger numbers of measures (scales). The factor loadings indicate the extent to which each scale is associated with an underlying factor. In their study, Ives et al. found a 5 factor structure, with 22 scales loading at the 0.50 level.

The factor analysis conducted in the present study found a 4-factor structure with 14 scales loading at the 0.50 level. Table 2 presents the factor loadings and a comparison with Ives' results. The first factor draws from scales related to product output quality. The second contains scales related to the relationship between the user and the system, such as the user's understanding, participation, and access to the system. The service aspect includes a support factor related to delays in receiving output and developing or modifying applications, plus a fourth factor representing the user's attitudes towards the EDP staff. The last column shows that the results of the present study are quite consistent with those of Ives et al.; there seems to be a logical structure among the scales which confirms the multidimensional nature of the user satisfaction construct and which indicates that the instrumental definition of this construct is valid.

## 4. Applying the Instrument

Having shown the measuring instrument to be reliable and valid, it can be applied as a diagnostic and research tool for evaluating the success of information systems in small organizations. As an example, Table 3 presents the mean scores on the various satisfaction scales for the 464 user-managers sampled in the present study. 195 of these managers accessed their MIS only through printed reports, while others also interacted through a terminal; the mean scores were broken down for batch and online users.

## 4.1. The Instrument as a Diagnostic Tool

The systems development life-cycle always leads to an evaluation process in which the organization attempts to gauge its MIS effectiveness. One way for information systems practitioners to accomplish this is by identifying the particular aspects which cause users to be satisfied. One can see that small business managers are found to be most dissatisfied with time required for systems development, processing of requests for system changes, and training provided users.

These results indicate that it is support that causes most problems for small organizations. These organizations are more sensitive to the service aspect of their MIS, as this is provided mainly by external rather than internal sources, particularly in terms of software development and maintenance, and user training. Thus, a possible solution would be to increase the level of MIS autonomy in small organizations by more user-developed applications, with personal computer tools such as database management systems and spreadsheet packages [22].

## 4.2. The Instrument as a Research Tool

Information systems researchers must verify, through field studies, their theories and experimental findings in real systems design, a crucial aspect of which centers on the man-machine interface. Past experimental research has focussed on interactive systems as being more effective than batch systems for managerial decision support. The results confirm that interactive systems enhance acceptance of MIS. The on-line user-managers were found to have a significantly higher level of user satisfaction than the batch user-managers (on a two-tailed t-test, p = 0.001).

The scales where this difference was most significant were convenience of access, currency of output, and timeliness of output. On-line users also perceived themselves to have had a greater participation in the MIS. The user-managers were thus well able to perceive the specific advantages of interactive usage, confirming the necessity for more active information support based on man-computer dialogues, including interrogation and analysis capabilities, instead of a more passive type of support based only on periodic or exception reporting.

## 5. Conclusion

This paper has extended previous work in the development of a standard measure of user satisfaction in both research and practice settings. Building upon an instrument previously developed by Pearson and Bailey, and improved by Ives et al., the present study has broadened the domain of reliability and validity of these authors' measurement approach. The measurement tool developed here can also contribute to a better understanding of the specific aspects of small organizations in regards to information systems. This has become important with the advent of microcomputers as more and more small organizations have implemented an MIS and as empirical research in this environment is presently lacking. An increased knowledge of the factors of MIS success in small organizations is necessary, if organizations are to respond effectively to the unique problems and opportunities which confront them.
