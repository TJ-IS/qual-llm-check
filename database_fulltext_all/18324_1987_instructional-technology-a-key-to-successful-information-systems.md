# Instructional Technology: A Key to Successful Information Systems

Karen L. Medsker

Learning Resources, Indiana University-Purdue University at Indianapolis, Indianapolis, IN 46223, USA

and

Larry R. Medsker

Department of Computer and Information Science, Purdue School of Science, Indianapolis, IN 46223, USA

Success and failure patterns of information systems development are widely discussed. However, the potential of education and training to address these problems has not received adequate attention. Managers and other information systems professionals need to be aware of the best technologies both for development of information systems and for the associated training of developers and end users. The best way to produce effective, efficient training is through instructional technology. Although derived from system science, as are information system development methods, the processes and techniques of instructional technology are often unknown to information systems professionals. This paper discusses the parallels between these two disciplines and points out where each could benefit from the other.

Keywords: Information Systems Development, Instructional Technology, Instructional Systems, Systems Analysis, Education, Training, Software Engineering, Systems Science.

Karen Medsker is Director of Instructional Development at Indiana University-Purdue University at Indianapolis. She received her Ph.D. in Instructional Systems from Florida State University under the direction of Robert M. Gagne. Previously at AT&T Bell Laboratories in New Jersey, she was an instructional technologist and technical education manager responsible for course development in software engineering, VLSI technologies, marketing, and project management.

Dr. Medsker's current interests include faculty development and computer-based and videodisc instruction.

## 1. Introduction

![](/api/attachments/B2A7KZNC/fulltext/images/82d3e0409e49223cce3bc7c710800a892fa2d91a6105dc91b54da569ef3aae71.jpg)

The implementation of an information system (IS) in an organization may be disappointing, or actually fail, for a variety of reasons. Among these are poor technical development, misapplication of information technology, and misunderstanding of the organization that will use the IS [34,49]. Another significant factor is ineffective or inefficient training – for the developers and/or the end users, who need to operate the IS properly. Training is a critical component in the success of an information system.

Excellent training of IS developers, managers, and users, then, can significantly increase the chances for success of information systems. What are the critical attributes of excellent training? Excellent training is

\- Valid: it meets the needs of people for whom it is designed and the requirements of their work.

\- Reliably effective: a consistently high percentage of trainees achieve the performance goals.

\- Efficient: it consumes the least possible time and the fewest resources, consistent with validity and effectiveness.

Larry Medsker is an Associate Professor of Computer and Information Science in the Purdue School of Science in Indianapolis. He was formerly a member of technical staff at Bell Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey and was on the faculty at the New Jersey Institute of Technology. Dr. Medsker received his Ph.D. from Indiana University. His current research and teaching interests include models of decision making, expert systems, decision support systems, database design, and software engineering.

Excellent training can best be ensured by a systematic approach that incorporates established principles of instructional science and proven methods of instructional technology (IT). (For a concise comparison of the systems approach and traditional instruction, see [25]).

Information systems professionals routinely employ systematic methods in technical development but typically are unaware that similar approaches can, and should, be applied to training. This paper demonstrates the parallels between IS technology and IT and highlights the benefits that accrue from the latter. We also suggest ways in which IS professionals responsible for training can, by transferring their own technology to training applications, improve instructional development practices.

The ideas expressed in this paper can be useful to a wide range of readers, including IS managers, developers, professors, students, and users. Most IS managers make training decisions. Requirements for user training/acceptance must be established early in an IS development project, and the quality of the training/acceptance plan is critical. In addition, IS developer training must be created or acquired when making transitions to new development environments, technologies, or application areas. To make informed decisions, IS managers (not just training managers) should be aware of the costs and benefits of relevant new technologies, including IT. For university faculty in IS and related disciplines, awareness of IT can assist them in teaching the right content and skills in the most effective ways, thus producing more competent graduates and enhancing the reputations of their institutions. IS developers, users, and students should be well-informed consumers of the training or education offered. Armed with knowledge of the critical features of quality instruction, students and trainees can more critically assess strengths and weaknesses, beyond superficialities such as entertainment value or speaking ability of the instructor. User demand is an effective way to drive instructional quality upward.

This paper evolved from theoretical discussions of IT and IS similarities and from empirical observation. We have worked both in universities, where relatively few attempts at systematic course development occur, and in corporate training environments, where IT applications are more common. In both educational and industrial settings, we have used the systems approach to analyze, design, and evaluate courses, workshops, and curricula. We conclude that many more organizations could benefit from IT applications. The purpose of this paper is to describe the essence of IT and indicate who can, and how they can, benefit and under what conditions. Further, we indicate some areas in which IS technology has transfer potential to IT.

## 2. The Impact of Training on the Success of Information Systems

Training can affect the success of information systems in both direct and indirect ways. To understand the impacts of training, we need to review the problems associated with large software systems and identify how training addresses these problems. We then demonstrate the usefulness of instructional technology for improving training.

## 2.1. Problems in Large Software System Development

Pettijohn [36] defines quality as “fitness for use”, which involves affordability, inclusion of desired features, ease of installation and operation, performance to customer expectations, reliability, durability, documentation, and ease of repair and maintenance. Given this definition, the opportunities for quality to suffer are many, and the costs of achieving quality are great. Often-cited deficiencies include:

\- Schedule overruns

● Failure to match user requirements

● Non-acceptance by users

● Difficult use or maintenance

Costly as these deficiencies are, prevention is also costly. Placing greater emphasis on systematic development methods, tools and languages, user-friendly design, and user training and acceptance strategies, for example, can add to development cost and time.

The problems in software system development are the focus of a current project at the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation (MCC). To develop a precise understanding of complex systems development, they surveyed 19 major corporations involved in large software development projects. Preliminary results [43] have revealed the following six top problem areas:

1) Scarcity of application-specific technical knowledge - shortage of subject matter experts, few people who can quickly learn salient information, and lack of organized, concise bodies of knowledge on application topics;

2) Effect of change and uncertainty in user requirements – time and effort wasted modifying designs and coding due to misunderstanding of clients' needs or due to new requirements not revealed early in a project;

3) Ineffective communication and coordination among subgroups of analysts and developers – lack of informal network that allows sharing of problems and solutions among subgroups of developers;

4) Deficiencies in available tools and techniques for analysis and design representation - need for the development of aids that allow analysts to work more effectively with clients to produce more accurate requirements, specifications, and designs;

5) Technology transfer problems in attaining the most productive and efficient development environment – blocks to the use of new tools and techniques due to lack of understanding of capabilities and value of new technologies and due to cost from delays for retraining and retooling;

6) Special problems of documentation, certification, etc. associated with government projects – procedures and paperwork, lost time, and management complications due to increased number of agencies and regulations that are peculiar to government projects.

The costs associated with these problems are schedule slippages, reduced quality of software, and higher development costs. Much attention has been given to the potential for reducing time and effort through technical improvements $[6]$ . Reduction in staff needed for each project, earlier entry into the market, and future sales to satisfied users have been cited as ways to obtain cost savings or added revenues $[11]$ .

Other observations support the importance of technology transfer problems. For example, new development tools that could improve productivity frequently become available, but the time away from work and expense of retraining can block the decision to make the transition to a new environment. A current example is the Ada language, which has great potential, but, as pointed out in [5,12,47], the effectiveness and success of Ada requires carefully planned education and training. Another technology transfer challenge is to improve the performance of systems analysts. Research is being conducted to reveal how effective systems analysts solve problems [48]. Ways are needed to transfer these problem-solving skills to others.

A visible indicator of information systems failure is user dissatisfaction. This is a common problem for IS departments serving large companies [39]. User attitudes are important determinants of success in the implementation of information systems [4]. Users need to be oriented toward new IS products and trained effectively to avoid initial resistance and improve chances for acceptance. Early and effective interface design, including online help and documentation facilities, is a critical but often unknown or neglected means for ensuring user satisfaction. Even with user-friendly design, training development plans are often not integrated into the project life cycle. The continuous collaboration between interface designers and training developers, which could prevent user acceptance problems, often does not occur.

Often problems in IS development can be attributed partly to management $[5,43]$ . First, managers may not be well versed in the best system development techniques. If technical and application-specific knowledge is lacking at the management level, poor decisions may result. On the other hand, managers are frequently promoted because of prior technical achievement, and they may lack generic project management, people management, and communication skills they need to be successful $[15]$ .

## 2.2. The Contributions of Training in Information System Development

Training can mitigate directly or indirectly most of the problems and costs mentioned in the previous section. This section focuses on those areas in which training has a direct relationship to performance improvement.

To reduce the total cost of quality assurance, more emphasis must be placed on prevention and proportionately less on appraisal and correction of defects. Thus, quality assurance processes must be applied to all phase of product development projects, and quality becomes everyone's job. The concepts, tools, and techniques of quality assurance must be taught to everyone in an organization so that defects can be prevented at all steps.

Surette [46], in describing AT&T's new Integrated Quality System (IQS), says that in order for quality to extend to every step of the Product Realization Process, education and training is a "key foundational element". Not only must the company teach employees in all job categories the tools and techniques needed to achieve quality, but also each employee must adopt the attitude that quality is strategically important to the company and that the quality of his or her contribution matters. Quality is "people intensive", and continuing education and training are critical to its achievement. For example, Fowler [16] points to the central role of training in the technology transfer process for software engineering methodology. Fagan [14] demonstrated that design and code inspections reduce errors in systems. Training is necessary, though probably not sufficient, to ensure that inspections are implemented in large development projects [16].

Most IS personnel are not experts in the application area. The first author conducted an informal survey of managers at AT&T Bell Laboratories who were responsible for systems analysis activities for switching systems. These managers were asked to identify the most critical skill and knowledge gaps of their employees, especially new hires. The supervisors identified knowledge of switching technology, not the methods of systems analysis, as the biggest deficiency. Subsequently, a set of courses tailored to these employees was developed. Typical solutions to application-specific knowledge deficiencies include on-the-job “immersion” or “going back to school” for one or more comprehensive courses in the subject matter. The first is less than optimally effective, both are usually inefficient. Training tailored specifically to the needs of the IS developers is ideal and can save costs in the long run.

Technology transfer for new tools and languages is addressed by instructional technology in two ways. First, the analyst determines whether training is the most efficient or effective way to transfer the technology. Well-designed documentation may better and less expensively support user performance [22,26]. Second, if training is required, it can often be streamlined to reduce cost without sacrificing effectiveness. Evidence is cited in Section 2.3.

The need to enhance advanced problem-solving skills (as for systems analysts) is a complex issue. An important step is to identify specific behaviors involved in effective problem solving. As Vitalari and Dickson [48] suggest, these behaviors have implications both for training and for recruitment and selection. Individual effectiveness in problem solving is partially determined by genetic and personality factors not influenced by training [3]. Although it is possible to teach specific problem-solving skills via short-term training, both general and subject-specific problem-solving capabilities are believed to result primarily from long-term, cumulative problem-solving experience in a variety of contexts [3,20]. Thus it appears to be desirable to improve pre-college, college, and graduate instruction to include more guided and autonomous practice in solving novel problems. The tendency to teach cut-and-dried information and to require only recall performance on tests is a well-known and much-lamented problem in education [45]. Those in formal educational settings must demand higher-order thinking from students, and let them work on tough, real-world, open-ended problems.

User acceptance can also be facilitated by training. If user characteristics are taken into account in the early stages of system design, training needs will be reduced, and the system will be more readily accepted. For required training, planning and development should occur early on, not as an afterthought at installation time. Training is not only for skills and knowledge transfer, but can also be used effectively for attitude development. Desired attitudes should be specified $[29]$ and deliberate strategies should be designed to establish them during training programs. (In fact, most technical skills training has a surprisingly large attitude component.)

Managerial skills, both generic and specific to IS development, can be enhanced through training. In addition to learning application-specific information, managers need continually to learn about new development technologies and their potential and limitations. In this way, they can make informed decisions about when and how to make the transition to a new technology [5]. Information system project management skills are also needed, such as phase planning, facilities planning, generating resource requirements, and selecting organization structures. One such effort is the Software Project Management Workshop program at AT&T Bell Laboratories [40]. Generic management training is needed to help technical people enhance people skills, such as interviewing, giving assignments, motivating, evaluating, and running a meeting.

Education and training, then, are crucial elements supporting the development and use of information systems. Instruction is central in the array of technologies that support human performance. The systems approach (instructional technology) has been established as the best available approach for achieving effective and efficient instruction. In the following section, we describe the origins of instructional technology and cite evidence of its effectiveness.

## 3. Better Instruction Through Technology

The field of instructional systems (or instructional technology) gained impetus during and immediately after World War II. Histories of the field can be found in $[38,41,42]$ . Major advances in research and development related to military training were made when high levels of expertise in psychology, communications, and operations research were brought together. What began as an emphasis on the use of audiovisual media to improve learning gradually broadened to encompass systematic research and development of teaching and learning processes based in general systems science, and behavioral, social, and cognitive psychology.

Branson [7] cites numerous research studies and applications conducted by military organizations and Human Resources Research Organization (HumRRO) that demonstrated clearly the value of the systems approach. Initial investments in analysis and design activities for various military training courses were cost justified by enhanced learning and retention and decreased learning time. Also, the effects of appropriate media choice and delivery systems, and systematic evaluations of on-the-job versus special school training, were shown to make significant differences for decision making.

As implications for industrial training became apparent, further investigations were carried out by private companies and by the American Institutes for Research [8,9,10,23]. The Bell System was a leader in these efforts during the 1960's and 1970's; studies demonstrated dramatic improvements in learning effectiveness and efficiency (resulting in huge cost savings) through systematic application of empirical methods. In a classic study reported by Markle [31], instructional engineering methods reduced training time from 10 hours to 7.5 hours and greatly improved the effectiveness of a first aid course meeting Red Cross certification requirements. Between 1970 and 1975, 2.5 million Bell Systems employees were trained with this course, so the initial investment was more than justified. Shoemaker [44] describes several other cases in which empirical methods proved cost effective. A well-designed, self-paced electricity course was compared experimentally with an existing group-paced course led by a super-rated instructor. Achievement was greatly improved by allowing individual learning time to vary, showing a “ceiling effect” (scores bunched toward the highest possible score), as compared to the normal curve achieved with the group-paced course. Other training on installation, repair, and long distance operator skills developed using the AT&T Training Development Standards demonstrated improved job performance, earlier self-sufficiency on the job, and reduced training time. A course for long lines craftspersons was reduced from 45 days to 9 days, saving the company \$ 137 million over a five year period.

Dramatic effects of IT have also been documented in public education. For example, a new educational system for Korea was developed and empirically tested during the 1970's [50]. The curriculum for grades 1–9 was modernized based on a consensus of learning outcomes consistent with Korean culture and national goals. New instructional materials and delivery systems were developed stressing oral and written communications, analytical and creative thinking, problem solving, and independent study skills. In a controlled experiment comparing the new educational system with conventional instruction, subject matter achievement improved as much as 38.7% (problem solving in mathematics), and the percentage of students achieving 80–100% mastery of all subjects was 43.8%, compared to 8.9% of the control group.

Instructional systems is now a relatively common field of academic study, sending graduates into military, industrial, and academic settings. Instructional technologists do research, design curricula, develop self-instructional programs (e.g., multi-media or computer-based), or serve as consultants to subject-matter experts who develop/teach courses. Gagne [19] provides a current perspective of the research base, tools, applications, and potential of the field.

Instructional technology is used by many high technology companies today. AT&T, Digital, IBM, Texas Instruments, Control Data, Motorola, and National Semiconductor, to name a few, all employ professional instructional technologists and systematic processes for some or all of their internal technical training. Some companies consider the quality of customer training to be even more critical than that of employee training, and so use IT resources more heavily for user training associated with particular products.

## 4.1. Information Systems Development

The process of instructional development, having origins in general systems science, is very similar to the conventional approach to information systems development. As shown in Figure 1, the first step in developing an information system is to identify and describe precisely the problem to be addressed. Often there is an existing system whose performance is deficient in some way. These shortcomings are analyzed in relation to the ideal (perhaps derived from opinions gathered), and requirements are set for the new system (see, e.g., [21]).

The structured design of an information system involves the decomposition of the system into functional modules arranged hierarchically and represented by a structure chart. The system may be partitioned functionally into hardware, software, and human components, with interfaces specified. User training requirements should be specified at this stage. From the design, the system is developed, tested, and validated to demonstrate that it performs the required functions. The final phase includes installing the system in an organization, maintaining performance, and improving its functions and adding new features.

## 4.2. Instructional Systems Development (ISD)

In this section, the phases of ISD are defined and described. (ISD refers to the total project life cycle in which IT is applied). To illustrate, we describe the systematic development of a Structured Systems Analysis (SSA) course at AT&T Bell Laboratories. Part of a larger curriculum in system development technology, this course was geared to practicing professionals with advanced degrees and varying levels of experience. It was developed using a structured team approach, involving a content expert, an instructional technologist, and a media specialist. The resulting course was measurably effective, well-documented, modular and easily modifiable. This work has been reported elsewhere $[2,32]$ .

## 4.2.1. Problem Definition

The first step in instructional system development is to identify the problem to be solved. Just as a computer system is not the answer to every technical, management, or organizational problem, human performance problems have a variety of causes, only some of which are addressable by training. Therefore, instructional technologists employ “front-end analysis” or “performance analysis” to pinpoint actual, or anticipated, performance problems, their causes, and the hypothetically best solutions [30]. The solutions may not involve instruction. For example, human performance problems may best be solved by better incentives, job redesign, new procedures, improved working conditions, or expert systems. Training is prescribed only in some instances of skill or knowledge deficiency.

SSA Example. The SSA course was part of a System Development Technology curriculum. The general problem addressed by this curriculum was that new information systems did not always meet user requirements. A common complaint was “we need a Volkswagen and you delivered a Cadillac.” As in most companies, the challenge of improving software productivity was also felt. The curriculum was one of several solutions addressing these issues. Other strategies involved incentives, tools, and organizational development.

## 4.2.2. Analysis

Once instruction is identified as an appropriate solution, the requirements for the instructional system are analyzed, from overall requirements, to detailed levels [28]. Learner needs and wants, constraints, and available resources are considered to establish requirements. Task analysis [33] and a variety of other techniques [51] are employed at this stage to specify ideal learner behavior. The outputs of this phase are system requirements (what the instructional system must do, stated in observable terms) and the criteria for acceptance of the system (how well the instruction must perform in order to be usable). It is primarily through careful problem definition and analysis that validity is achieved.

SSA Example. An extensive survey of software development supervisors in the company was conducted. Respondents were asked what knowledge and experience their groups had currently in SSA techniques, and they were asked to estimate the size and extent of their training needs for each skill area. Interviews with expert developers and analysis of several leading textbooks brought additional focus to the definition of course outcomes.

## 4.2.3. Design

The design stage addresses how the system requirements will be met. A typical first step in instructional design is the decomposition of complex skills into component subskills and/or hierarchically related prerequisite skills. Resulting products are learning hierarchies and learning maps. These methods are supported by the theories of Gagne [18] and others. They ensure the inclusion of critical learning prerequisites or steps and the exclusion of nonessential material. Based on the instructional analysis results, modules of instruction and their necessary interrelationships are identified.

For each module, behaviorally-stated objectives (specifications) are constructed. Then strategies, based on the nature of the content and the types of knowledge, skills, and attitudes to be learned, are designed for accomplishing those objectives. An instructional strategy is an algorithm or step-by-step plan for teaching/learning experiences. Media are selected to facilitate the strategies. Just as pseudocode and flow charts provide guidance in software coding, instructional methods and media specifications communicate from the design phase to the development phase. They are “blueprints” for the actual “manufacture” of instructional materials.

An additional design activity is establishing test specifications. These serve as standards for judging success of individual modules and of entire courses. Test items, attitude surveys, and other performance indicators (all based directly upon the objectives) are identified. These specifications are later used to develop pretests, posttests, and follow-up measures, which assess retention and transfer. Key references on instructional design include $[13,17,37]$ . Careful design, and faithful implementation of that design, are the key contributors to reliable effectiveness and efficiency.

SSA Example. For each major course objective, a learning map was derived. A sample map is shown in Figure 2. Because the final desired performance is a problem-solving skill, a top-down analysis was used. Such learning tasks can be hierarchically decomposed into prerequisite rules and concepts. The correct learning sequence is from bottom to top (simple to complex).

Next, detailed objectives were developed (samples are shown in Figure 3). These six objectives reflect the left side of the learning map (see Figure 2), working from the first prerequisite skill (1) to the terminal skill (8). Each map and its corresponding objectives became a course module. The next step was to design lectures, examples, and exercises to teach the objectives. Frequent, active participation of the learners was required to ensure skill development. Pretest and posttest items were designed to measure learner achievement of key objectives.

## 4.2.4. Implementation

Unsophisticated trainers start at this phase by writing lecture outlines and pulling together content and material for presentation. This approach is equivalent to coding before an information system has been analyzed, specified, and designed. Unfortunately the code-first syndrome, all too common in practice, can lead to ineffective and inefficient training. When the number of learners is large and/or high learner performance levels are required (e.g., in IS user training), the results of poor instruction can be costly or disastrous.

Implementation is a critical step, requiring both technical competence and creativity. Expertise in subject matter, technical writing, media production, and computer-assisted authoring are often needed. But implementation is easier and more effective when 20–40% of the total project effort is devoted to analysis and design. Further, systematically engineered courseware is by necessity well documented, allowing standardization and replicability.

SSA Example. Development of the SSA course consisted mainly of producing student materials (notebooks with handouts and exercises), an instructor manual and overhead transparencies. These media were widely used in the culture, quick to produce, and easily modifiable.

## 4.2.6. Maintenance

Installation problems occur with new instructional systems. Adaptation to local needs, addition of new features, improvements, repair of previously undiscovered faults, and updating to reflect advancing content are possible reasons for maintenance. Because of its modularity and documented design, courseware produced using ISD is more easily modified. Maintenance activities, if monitored carefully, can also generate ideas for new systems.

## 5. Applications of Information Systems Techniques to Instructional Technology

The quality of IS training can be greatly enhanced via current instructional systems methods, as summarized above. Conversely, training development technology can be advanced through creative adaptation of some IS techniques. IS personnel responsible for training are ideally positioned to accomplish such technology transfer. We suggest below some areas that warrant further exploration.

## 5.1. Conventional IS Approaches

## 5.1.1. Structured Systems Analysis (SSA)

SSA is a traditional analysis technique for modelling existing systems to identify deficiencies and for generating alternative configurations for new systems. Concise representations (e.g., data flow diagrams) facilitate flexibility of thought, clear communication with users and managers, and efficiency for the final specification document.

There are at least two ways that SSA principles can be applied to ISD. First, SSA could facilitate the analysis and reconfiguration of macroscopic instructional systems – for example, a company's instructional management system, or the course development system of a training department. Second, SSA may be useful, during needs assessment, in analyzing specific jobs for which training is to be designed. Especially for work that is conceptual rather than procedural (e.g., systems analysis), data flow diagrams and data dictionaries would better represent the work than would traditional task lists and flowcharts.

## 5.1.2. Modularity and Reusability

Because of rising software production costs and shortages in personnel, software engineers have lately emphasized the concept of “component assembly” – that is, constructing programs from previously written, small, single-purpose modules. By drawing upon module libraries, less of the work for new systems has to be done from scratch. The economies achieved in this way are said to offset the increased initial cost required to make the components reusable in the first place.

Both training developers and learners could benefit from a similar approach to courseware. Small modules that effectively and efficiently teach single concepts or skills could be combined as needed to design a particular course, thus shortening development time. For example, a module that teaches the programming structure “if-then-else” could be reused in different programming language courses. The benefits of modularity could be increased if the principles of minimizing coupling between modules and maximizing coherence within modules were applied.

## 5.1.3. Walkthroughs

Walkthroughs (or inspections) are reviews of requirements, specifications, designs, code, or test data for early identification of errors. Walk-throughs are conducted with peers only, without managers or users present. One goal is the “ego-less” team approach to software development, in contrast to the traditional way in which individuals work alone on a “personal” product. Team approaches are often favored now, because objectivity and synergy result in greater productivity.

While it has long been recognized that review by subject matter experts and representative learners should be done early and often in the course development process, peer reviews have not been common. Walkthroughs can be an extremely useful, supplementary source of formative evaluation data. They were, in fact, successfully used in the design of the SSA course described above and have been continued with succeeding courses at AT&T Bell Laboratories [16]. Products that could benefit from walkthroughs include need statements, learning maps, scripts, and tryout data. Benefits for courseware development include early, and relatively inexpensive, error removal; fewer design errors in future products; and improved morale and job satisfaction.

## 5.1.4. Phased Development

Phased development means completing part of the total system while other parts are still under development. If delivery is also phased, certain modules or features may be delivered to the user while some other components are being designed, coded, or tested. This approach is valuable in that earlier use can be made of the completed components. Also, the project team can avoid making the same errors on subsequently-developed features through user feedback on the early releases. The system developers' morale may also benefit, because they are able to see something through to completion quickly, and their work will be more varied.

Phased development and delivery have been successful with instructional products. Particularly for inexperienced development teams, the learning that occurs from testing early modules of a course can be used to advantage in later modules, resulting in better first drafts. People who need the instruction urgently can begin using the materials.

## 5.2. Current IS Research Areas

A number of research and development areas, including potential alternatives to the traditional systems development life cycle, may offer concepts that can be carried over to IT. Although presently in only limited use, non-traditional IS methodologies are the subjects of much discussion and research (see [35] for an overview and analysis of current work).

Some new tools include prototyping [1] and the Role Construct Repertory (Rep) Test [24]. Prototyping, for example, may help identify instructional requirements by giving learners and trainers early experience with the system before major design decisions are made. Developers might collect learner preference data on formats and delivery style.

Another area of current interest is the automation of the development process through the use of computerized tools. A research goal is to be able to produce software directly from system requirements that have been entered into the computer. Beyond a general computerized tool for instructional systems developers, automated development tools could be of even greater significance in conjunction with authoring systems for computer-aided instruction.

## 6. The Decision to Use Instructional Technology

Information systems professionals and managers can benefit by requiring the same systematic approach to instruction as they do for information systems. Excellent training for developers and managers is one key to high productivity, whether the training is developed in house, or whether it is purchased from a vendor. User training is equally critical, since an effective installation may depend upon it. Specifically, the systems approach ensures that:

\- Instruction is used only as necessary; non-training solutions are recommended where appropriate;

\- All essential instruction is included, resulting in higher learner performance;

\- Excess instruction is omitted, assuring minimal learning time;

● Evaluation is facilitated by clear success criteria;

● Thorough documentation allows easier transport, replication, and maintenance of the instruction

\- Managers have better tools for planning, monitoring, and evaluating training personnel and products.

Even acknowledging the benefits of systematically developed instruction, one may ask: do the benefits outweigh the extra development costs? There are two ways to answer this question. First, the systems approach need not necessarily cost more. Since systems thinking is more an attitude than a set of activities, even a quick, informal application of its principles can yield significant results. For instance, an hour's reflection about “what do students need from this course and why?” can be a vast improvement over no needs assessment at all. Beyond this level, there are varying degrees of being systematic. Additionally, designing instruction correctly in the first place often is, in the long run, less costly than the trial and error method.

A second response to the cost effectiveness question is that it depends upon the situation. A full-blown ISD project with detailed application of all available technology may be warranted when any one of the following conditions obtain. Some combination of factors can also make a strong case.

\- The target population of learners is large. The development cost per learner obviously decreases when cost is spread over a larger target population. (Care must be taken to ensure that all learners, in fact, have roughly the same needs or that the instruction is versatile enough to accommodate differences.) If the subject matter is relatively stable, use of the product over time can effectively create a large total user population.

\- Direct transfer to job performance is required. Systematically-developed instruction is engineered to match job requirements. The content, examples, practice exercises, and tests match these standards. Thus, if enhanced job performance is the major goal of instruction, this approach is indicated.

\- High learner achievement is critical. In traditional education, we assume learner achievement will be normally distributed (the bell-shaped curve). Systematically-developed instruction typically produces a distribution dramatically skewed to the upper end of the achievement range. Where situations demand high performance, as with astronauts, nuclear power plant operators, airplane pilots, medical personnel, etc., systematic approaches to training are often used.

\- Replicability and/or exportability are required.

Systematically-produced instruction is carefully structured, well documented, and validated through trials with representative learners. Therefore, results are more likely to be reproducible with other learners and in other locations. Courses are less dependent upon particular instructors and less variable in the outcomes produced.

It appears that these conditions often hold for IS developer training. Corporations, and some university educators, need to prepare people in job-relevant skills. High achievement levels are usually desired, and numbers are often large. Thus, systematic approaches to instructional development are indicated. If training is purchased from a vendor, those familiar with the systems approach to instruction are in a better position to assess their needs and evaluate consultants and courses in relation to their own requirements. The payoff can be the production of higher quality information systems. Reduced training times and high effectiveness are important also for achieving greater market share by earlier entry.

For end user training, direct and immediate transfer of skills to job performance and replicability of results are desired features. Within a firm, users of a new information system need to minimize the time away from normal duties and learn the new system well enough to ensure higher levels of productivity in their work. For sales to outside users, proper implementation and convenience in the transition to the new system are important for customer satisfaction and future sales. New products have better chances of acceptance when users' initial experiences with the IS are not confusing or frustrating.

## 7. Summary

The education and training of information systems professionals and users has not made sufficient use of instructional technology. In most instances, we are still “coding first” when it comes to course development. Those concerned with obtaining or developing effective and efficient instruction should be aware that methods and expert personnel are available to aid in the systematic engineering of courseware. Training developers and instructional technologists need to study other disciplines that follow a systems approach, such as information systems technology and software engineering, to gain new insights and methodological improvements for the practice of instructional development. We believe there is a high potential for improving the practices of both information systems and instructional systems development through communications between these two systems sciences.
