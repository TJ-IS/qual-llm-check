# Treasury Management Information Systems and the Contribution of Information Technology

Michael J. Earl and Eng S. Ong
Templeton College, Oxford OX2 5NY, United Kingdom

Treasury management is a relatively new function in large corporations. It is a data-dependent activity requiring information systems support. Thus, many IT-based products are available to treasurers. An exploratory study has revealed that corporate treasurers are concerned with five core activities in which they exploit a rich set of information sources. They appear to rely on personal and informal information processes as much as use more formal systems and IT products. Paradoxically, most computer systems have focussed on external data collection where treasurers generally have developed their own effective intelligence networks. Conversely, internal data collection, which appears to be problematic in most corporations, so far has benefitted little from IT. Treasurers' usage of IT has been mainly confined to small, local or personal systems. These have been developed or acquired with scant involvement of in-house MIS departments. Better understanding of treasury managers' work and preferences therefore is a prerequisite for successful treasury MIS.

Keywords: Treasury Management, MIS, Information Technology.

## 1. Introduction

1. Treasury operations are clearly information-dependent as treasurers strive to establish the financial needs and positions of their organisations and to keep informed on, and analyse, financial markets.

In many large companies, the treasury management function has established itself only in the last decade. Consequently it has been a relatively late adopter of information technology (IT). However, the continuing stream of professional journals and conferences on treasury management information systems suggests that interest and experience are growing rapidly. This may be explained by three related reasons:

2. Financial markets are becoming highly automated and IT has become the delivery mechanism in the financial services industry at large.

It is thus timely to examine patterns of success and failure in treasury information processing in order to guide both the users and suppliers of treasury information systems in the future. Indeed since treasury management is essentially a new function, it may be instructive to build a reliable picture of treasury operations in practice, and of treasurers' actual information needs and uses, in order to place the potential contributions of IT and management information systems (MIS) in a realistic context.

The literature on financial management and treasury operations has rarely addressed the information problem. In foreign exchange risk management, writers have occasionally referred to the difficulties of collating adequate information $[5,11]$ while normative texts are appearing on how to embrace IT in the Treasury $[7]$ . Nevertheless there is no reliable reference material on treasury management information systems. The research project upon which this paper is based therefore was an early, exploratory examination of all forms of information processing in treasury operations. The investigation comprised three stages. Pilot interviews were conducted with three corporate treasurers of large UK multinational, multidivisional companies in order to establish the state of the art of IT in treasury management and elicit what seemed to be key issues. This stage proved to be very illuminating, started to fill in many of the gaps in the literature and suggested most of the findings confirmed later. Next a programme of semi-structured interviews with treasurers of eleven large UK companies in different sectors were conducted to probe the areas suggested by the pilot study. Finally, more structured and statistically analysable data was collected through mailed questionnaires sent to treasurers who were members of the Association of Corporate Treasurers and represented a diverse set of large companies by both sector and location. The response rate was 21% producing 16 responses. Although this is relatively low, the results tended to confirm the impressions gained through interviews.

The statistical data presented in this paper are derived entirely from the questionnaire survey. The discussion and analysis is heavily influenced by the interview discussions. In aggregate therefore the study is based on the views of 30 corporate treasurers. The findings therefore might be regarded as tentative. However distinctive trends seem to emerge, suggesting where attention should be focussed for development of more effective treasury management information systems. In particular current practice appears to fall short of common claims and rhetoric, informal information processing seems at least as important as formal systems and internal information needs may be more challenging than external intelligence.

## 2. A Possible Vision

Today, corporate treasurers can take advantage of four major IT developments: external information services, treasury workstations, decision support systems and the like using personal computers, and internal information systems delivered by the increasingly integrated technologies of computing, office automation and telecommunications. A vision of the modern automated treasury, not too remote from the propositions of popular articles and conference presentations, might comprise the following. The cash flows and balances, financial positions and commitments and funding needs of a firm's worldwide subsidiaries continuously update a central treasury database through a mix of micro, mini and mainframe computers connected and fed by communications networks. External information services, such as Reuters or Telerate terminals, might provide the treasurer with up-to-date market rates of relevant facilities and instruments. A bank's electronic banking package may provide to the treasurer's terminal detailed data on the firm's balances, positions, transactions history, and exceptions, perhaps compared against limits and targets. The treasurer, before matching external facilities with internal needs, then accesses a terminal or personal computer to enquire, analyse and model past experience and current possibilities using spreadsheets, database utilities and decision support systems.

This vision of integrated, timely, accurate, complete and analysable information to optimise treasury management does not seem implausible. However, it is a vision not necessarily grounded on what treasurers actually do, their actual information needs, their information processing abilities and preferences and the realities of information systems and IT hitherto.

## 3. Treasury Operations

In order to understand the context, needs and character of information systems and IT in treasury management, both the range of, and the degree of treasurer involvement in, treasury activities were ascertained. Treasurers were asked whether and how they were involved in the five core activities discovered by Davis and Collier [4] in their 1983 Survey for the Association of Corporate Treasurers. The results are summarised in Table 1 and confirm that most corporate treasurers are concerned with banking, borrowing, investment and foreign exchange. The degree of direct involvement they report suggests that they possess the authority to match their responsibility for these activities. Half the respondents were engaged in the fifth activity of working capital control, but with varying degrees of involvement.

Besides these core treasury operations, Davis and Collier [4] noted that in aggregate treasurers' activities were quite diverse. Table 2 lists the respondents' involvement in other areas. The variety of ancillary activities is apparent, although there may be some variation in definitions and terminology adopted by respondent companies and certain tasks may be part of the five core activities. No other dominant operation emerges, however, while a number of activities require only a partial contribution from the treasurer. The determinants of each treasurer's activity set seemed to be the nature of the firm's business, the current economic and financial environment and the treasurer's own choice an initiatives.

Two propositions are suggested only by this descriptive analysis of treasurer's activities. Treasury management information system requirements will be largely a function of the five core treasury activities. Second, the complete set of requirements will vary for each corporate treasury as the aggregate set of activities is diverse; for there is no universal model of corporate treasury operations.

However, to understand what would be relevant and appropriate information systems and IT solutions for the treasurer, it is essential to analyse information flows in the treasury department.

## 4. Information Flows

Clearly the treasurer relies on external sources for market information and internal sources to assess company positions. In principle a variety of channels or media is available. The early interviews with treasurers suggested three recurring themes. First, despite the fact that most IT products for treasurers focus on external (bank and market) information, internal information needs and sources were held to be equally important in treasury management. However, internal information was reported, paradoxically, to be more difficult to obtain than external information. Internal information systems, it seemed, often did not provide timely cash flow, currency denominated, forecast and treasury oriented data across the group. This may be because they are actually accounting-based rather than cash flow and commitment-based and because they are control-oriented rather than decision focussed. Thirdly, treasurers apparently invested in personal contacts because informal channels are highly valued and utilised in obtaining relevant information from both external and internal sources.

Building on these themes and using the activity classification adopted earlier, the nature of treasurers' information flows was surveyed and is summarised in Table 3. The formal information category includes regular accounting and financial reports and is largely quantitative. The informal category comprises telephone enquiries, meetings, gossip, trading interactions and personal contacts with peers and subordinates. External information from financial markets is seen to be the dominant need in banking, borrowing, investment and foreign exchange activities.

Internal information, as might be expected, is the dominant influence in working capital control and other activities. The majority of external information is obtained informally, despite the many formal information products available from banks and information service brokers. Working capital control, presumably being a routinised affair, relies predominantly on formal information, but other activities rely more or less equally on both formal and informal sources. It is clearly important to understand the relative advantages of informal and formal information sources in treasury operations.

Treasurers therefore were asked which information media they used and in what proportions. Table 4 demonstrates the diversity of media employed, which probably arises because sources are diverse. However Table 5, which takes the averages of responses in Table 3, further indicates that in internal information gathering informal channels are almost as significant as formal ones. Whereas other information studies have shown the appropriateness of informal systems even in internal information processing [3,6], this tendency is perhaps surprising for what is essentially collection of mostly quantitative data of a financial nature required routinely for relatively structured decisionmaking. The puzzle is accentuated by the responses recorded in Table 6 on information gathering difficulty. Over half the respondents find that internal information is more difficult to obtain than external and, perhaps more surprising, no respondents found external information more difficult to collect than internal information.

A particular difficulty reported is the problem of internal information float. Indeed 88% of respondents reported that their companies suffered delays in collecting internal information for treasury management. As indicated in the cross-impact matrix of Table 7, this study indicates that one means of overcoming float is again use of personal, informal sources. They may be more accessible, dependable, responsive and cooperative than most administrative procedures and systems. Unfortunately, the more complex and dispersed the organisation, the more difficult it may be to cultivate and maintain informal networks.

From this analysis of information flows two sets of propositions emerge for companies' information systems functions and third party IT providers. Personal and informal mechanisms for gathering internal information for treasury management are probably cultivated to compensate for the lack of, or inadequacy of, formal systems. Thus, if efficient and effective IT systems could be developed to replace them, they might find ready acceptance by treasurers of large companies. Second, informal systems nevertheless seem well suited to the diversity and subtlety of external information requirements. Further development of IT solutions in treasury management might well focus therefore on internal data collection, but are likely to be firm-specific. For external intelligence, any IT solutions must offer distinctive comparative advantage over the preferred personal mechanisms. They are unlikely to address difficulty of source or timeliness, leaving perhaps only ease of access and use or mode of presentation as areas for improvement.

## 5. IT Usage

The picture of information processing in the treasury function derived by analysing information flows contrasts sharply with the vision of the automated treasury painted earlier. The research study also attempted to map and explain current IT usage to see if the vision was at all realistic now or in the future. Adoption of different IT systems was measured and is reported in Table 8. Most treasury departments are found to use micro-computers and to subscribe to external information services. Usage of other information technologies is fitful and in total there is quite a mix of IT usage. Reasons for low usage of some facilities were discussed in interviews and usually centred on complexity of use, cost exceeding benefit, and unsuitability for the task. It is interesting to note that the high usage technologies are those that are both “stand-alone” and “user-friendly”. Exploitation of larger and more central or utility services is limited, suggesting that mainstream information systems and corporate IT functions have not yet addressed treasury operations.

Hardware and service trends however reveal little about information system types employed by treasurers. Adapting the classification used by Alloway and Quillard [2] in their survey of computer-based MIS in general, use and usefulness of information system type are cross-tabulated against core treasury activities in Table 9. Monitoring and exception systems are found to be most frequently used for the more external activities. These are, of course, the commonest type of formal and computer-based systems provided and used in organisations at large. Data collection systems are rated as useful for the internal task of working capital control, where information gathering was in principle held to be most difficult. Analysis and enquiry facilities are particularly valued for foreign exchange management, presumably in hedging decisions, and used quite often in investment and borrowing decisions. Such decision support systems tend to be highly rated in most management activities, as Alloway and Quillard found. Thus in the other core treasury activities, their perceived low usefulness may mean either that they are inappropriate or not appreciated and understood. Likewise the low usage and usefulness reported of most systems for working capital control may mean that they are inappropriate, or irrelevant if reliable internal data is lacking, or require complex and costly IT infrastructure.

Given the apparent local and ad hoc IT practice emerging for treasurers, the degree of the corporation's Data Processing function's involvement in treasury management information systems is of interest. Table 10 indicates low involvement in developing, implementing or supporting IT systems in the treasury. Several reasons may explain this. Treasurers' preferences for small and local technologies may have been better or more easily satisfied by external suppliers. Treasury information systems hitherto may have been low on corporations' applications priorities. DP departments may have been slow to appreciate or understand treasury information needs. Existing computer-based information systems may have been a poor foundation from which to satisfy treasurers' needs. However, if formal information systems are required to satisfy internal data collection requirements and if existing IT products and services in the treasury need to be integrated at all, it is clear that treasurers and DP specialists will have to work together in the future.

From this analysis of IT usage in the treasury three further propositions emerge. Small, local and user friendly IT facilities are preferred by treasurers. Second, treasurers have determined their own usage of IT, just as they often have pioneered many treasury management practices in general [5]. Third, treasurers have worked out what types of formal MIS are appropriate for their different activities; monitoring and exception for external actions, data collection for internal positions, analysis and enquiry for foreign exchange – and to a degree – financing decisions.

## 6. Conclusions

Information processing is central to treasury management, but MIS and IT usage are in a relatively early stage of evolution. Most investment in developing treasury MIS has been made by external third parties providing IT-based products such as treasury work stations and decision support systems. However valuable they may be, continued sophistication of these applications can only solve some of treasurers' information needs. The treasurers' information processing world is richer and more complex than is commonly recognised. Success in development of MIS and exploitation of IT for treasurers is more likely to be gained if the nature and preferences of treasury information processing are better understood. This study has made a start and four directional suggestions emerge:

1. Information systems requirements can largely be determined by analysing the five core activities of treasurers, but there is a variety of ancillary operations which may be significant in some firms.

2. As the typical treasurer very much acts as an information system collecting data from diverse sources and supported by a mix of information services, it is important to recognise that personal and informal information processes may be as important as IT-based and formal mechanisms, especially in gathering external intelligence.

3. The greatest information challenge in large MNCS appears to be collecting data on internal positions. Thus priority in treasury MIS development and exploitation of IT perhaps should be given to internal data collection.

4. Focus on internal information systems may require greater involvement of corporate DP departments, development of quite large systems and use of mainframe computing and corporate networks. This is a departure from treasurers' apparent preferences hitherto for small scale, local and personal information technologies. It is also a challenge which most treasury IT products in the market place do not address and which most banks and information service companies will not be able to tackle.

These propositions for success and for avoiding misplaced investment and disappointments in the future are derived from interviews with 14 UK treasurers and from 16 questionnaire responses. Further research across a wider sample might increase our understanding of treasury information processing to aid MIS practitioners. It also should help develop our knowledge of the new and rapidly changing treasury management function.
