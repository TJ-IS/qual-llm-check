# Managing Information Systems for Effectiveness and Humanity: Applying Research on Organizational Behavior

James C. Brancheau
Visiting Professor of MIS

and

Thomas R. Hoffmann

Director, WestBank Computing Services and Professor of Management Sciences

Management Sciences Department, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, 271 19th Avenue South, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455, USA

A key problem of is managers is determining how to manage their professionals for both effectiveness and humanity. Discussion begins by taking a close look at the nature of the problem, to sort out its causes; then it is turned around and viewed as an opportunity. Drawing on recent research in organizational behavior, several strategies for change are recommended. The concept of a psychological contract is introduced as a new perspective of the relationship between the is organization and its employees. This suggests that, within is, the only way to achieve long-term effectiveness is by managing with a sense of humanity toward employees.

## 1. Introduction

Due to increasing competitive pressure and the increasing strategic importance of information systems (IS), the call for greater productivity and effectiveness among IS professionals is loud and clear. In the past, it seemed that greater effectiveness could be achieved by harder, more disciplined, work and by adding new systems and procedures. IS managers have implemented broad-based governance systems, service request procedures, systems development controls, and structured analysis, design, and programming techniques. Through such efforts, some progress has been made in improving productivity.

Unfortunately, is managers can no longer be certain that strategies that were successful in the past will be successful in the future. It has become apparent that focusing on traditional indicators of effectiveness (largely financial and technical) at the exclusion of the human side is a tactic which can lead to short-term effectiveness at best, and long-term disaster at worst. A key problem is determining how to manage is professionals for both effectiveness and humanity.

## 2. The Problem

## 2.1. Managing for Effectiveness

Professional managers have grown up with a long and deep tradition of maximizing financial and technical indicators of effectiveness. In the early part of this century, managers were taught that human resources could be treated as simple extensions of production machinery. The principles of scientific management suggested that organizations could achieve maximum efficiency by dividing production into successively smaller parts. In this way, humans could carry out the work with little training or thought. Efficiency (short-term cost reduction) was equated with effectiveness (achievement of long-term organizational goals). Efforts were clearly focused on producing goods with minimum waste, expense, and effort. It was assumed that cost savings from more efficient production would result in increased profit. While information systems is a relatively new function in most organizations, IS managers have been heavily influenced by scientific management and its focus on efficiency as a surrogate for effectiveness.

The bureaucratic form of organization evolved from similar concepts. Max Weber, the German sociologist, described it as a social machine. In such a bureaucratic or machine model, rationality and predictability were sought by emphasizing:

\- a division of labor based on functional specialization;

● a well-defined hierarchy of authority;

\- a system of rules covering the rights and duties of employees; and

\- promotion based on technical competence.

In this model, organizational effectiveness was related to smooth functioning of the bureaucracy, and assumed to be the result of appropriately trained employees who were unquestionably obedient to the operating procedures described by management.

Bureaucracy has long had a poor reputation. Most is executives would not seriously suggest modeling their organization as a pure bureaucracy, but close examination reveals that the typical is organization resembles Weber's machine model in many ways. It utilizes division of authority, hierarchic levels of management, and formalized rules and procedures. Of course, some modifications have been made through decentralization to the divisional level, matrix management, and other progressive features, these are enhancements to the bureaucratic form, not a replacement for it.

As a result of the long tradition of scientific management and bureaucratic organization, is managers often equate effectiveness with strict financial and technological measures, with achievement of goals related to the number of systems on time and within budget, the size of the applications development backlog, the firm's total processing power, etc.

## 2.2. Managing for Humanity

In the late 1920s, managers and researchers began to realize that the world was not made up of organization men who fit nicely into narrowly defined roles unquestionably carrying out the order of the day. Failure to treat workers as human beings came to be regarded as the cause of low morale, poor craftsmanship, unresponsiveness, and reduced productivity. Management began to feel that it had to make workers feel important.

Employees in an organization are thinking, feeling human beings, since even though only part of a person's capacity may be needed to perform a given job, it is the whole person that comes to work each day. As a result, employees bring their own goals and ambitions with them. Unlike the ideal situation, individual goals are not always in accord with organizational goals.

An IS manager has a highly educated and highly trained work-force. As a result, the individuals in the organization have a need to express themselves on the job. They tend to become intellectually immersed in their work and expect more involvement, participation, and autonomy [2].

The combination of high expectations by employees, escalating competitive pressures, increasingly sophisticated technologies, and rapidly changing environments have created a need to tap the creativity and knowledge of all is employees rather than just a few key managers. Unfortunately, due to internal pressures within the firm, is managers continue to manage toward short-term financial and technical objectives. It seems reasonable to question whether it is possible to operate is effectively without satisfied and highly motivated employees.

## 2.3. Conflict

A major problem of IS management arises because the policies and practices designed to ensure organizational effectiveness often leave individual needs unsatisfied. In many cases, corporate or departmental practices create problems for employees. Employees may become alienated, insecure, and bitter if the organization fails to fulfill their minimum needs for security, self-esteem, growth, and development $[9,12]$ . The basic problem is determining how to permit some reasonable matching of human and organizational needs so that IS managers can be good to their employees and motivate them to work well and hard.

## 3. The Opportunity

The key proposition is that: it is possible to maintain both the perspective of the employee attempting to use the IS organization to fulfill individual needs and the perspective of the IS manager attempting to use human resources to fulfill organizational needs. It is argued that, in most cases the only way to achieve long-term effectiveness is by maintaining the appropriate balance between organizational and individual goals. In order to understand this, it is useful to consider why people do their work.

## 3.1. Why People Do their Work

Much of the research in organizations is concerned with finding out why people do their work. In a detailed review of the organizational behavior literature, it appears that expectancy, goal setting, and job characteristics theories are among a handful that have both scientific validity and practical application $[10,11,14]$ .

Expectancy theory goes beyond job context and seeks to explain the process by which people are motivated. The theory states that motivation is a combined function of the individual's perception that effort will lead to performance and that this performance will lead to desirable outcomes [6]. These components are depicted in Fig. 1.

On the job, it is maintained that performance is a function of ability and motivation. Individuals are depicted as rationally evaluating their various courses of action and choosing the course which maximizes the perceived value of the outcome. An implication of the theory is that managers should show individuals that their effort will lead to performance and then take steps to make sure that such performance is rewarded appropriately.

Goal setting theory has its roots in expectancy theory but is more applied in its formulation. In this theory, there are two determinants of behavior: values and goals. Values are things that an individual acts to gain or keep; goals serve to guide

Job characteristics theory provides an additional explanation of why people do their work. It suggests that non-material rewards are essential for satisfaction and high quality performance. According to this theory, certain characteristics of a job contribute to motivation. The theory proposes that high levels of satisfaction and motivation are dependent on the employee achieving three critical psychological states [4]:

1. The employee must experience the work as meaningful, worthwhile, or important.

2. The employee must feel personally responsible and accountable for his or her efforts.

3. The employee must receive regular and reliable feedback on his or her performance.

Hackman and Oldham developed a Motivating Potential Score (MPS) for a job based on the amount of skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback in the job. In general, the greater the degree to which a job has these characteristics, the greater its motivating potential. According to this theory, high motivating potential leads to high levels of satisfaction and performance. The basic components are illustrated in Fig. 3 (adapted from Hackman [5]).

Of course, not every employee is driven by the same basic needs. Accordingly, the effect of core job characteristics is moderated by the individual's characteristics. The primary moderating factors include the degree to which the employee has the necessary skills to perform the job, the degree to which the employee is motivated by the need to grow, and the degree to which the employee is satisfied by the contextual and environmental factors.

## 3.2.Strategies

People therefore work to achieve the rewards which accrue from their activity. Broadly speaking, rewards can be classified as material or non-material. Material rewards are well recognized and include pay and fringe benefits. Non-material rewards include stability, a sense of affiliation, challenge, and prestige.

Theories of motivation suggest that (1) non-material rewards are important and (2) each employee needs to be considered as an individual human being. Research suggests that a number of strategies are effective in helping to meet both individual and organizational needs.

Realistic Job Preview: One strategy that can be used by IS managers is the realistic job preview (RJP). RJPS attempt to provide job applicants with an accurate description of the job before they decide to whether accept it. Both positive and negative elements of the job are discussed before a decision is made to accept a pending offer. The preview often involves allowing the prospective employee to speak with current employees in similar positions. This is in contrast with traditional recruiting practices, which focus primarily on the positive aspects of the job.

By providing applicants with a more accurate description of the job, those who accept it will have more realistic expectations and thus be more satisfied with the job [1]. RJPs may also communicate an air of honesty to prospective employees and start new employees off with the right attitude. In addition, they may lead to improved self-selection by job applicants, since those who do accept the job are less likely to resign.

Within IS, RJPS may be effective for people when first entering programming and analysis positions (i.e. those not previously holding such positions). In such cases, the job applicant is not likely to have realistic information about potential work pressures. Since individuals applying for programming and analysis positions often believe that they have many other job opportunities, and RJP may also reduce cognitive strain after the individual has joined the organization. This should lead to greater commitment to the organization.

Path-Goal Leadership: Leader behavior has long been assumed to be a major factor in motivating employees. The path-goal theory of leadership has its origins in expectancy theory but focuses on leader behavior. The theory suggests that managers can improve employee performance by (1) identifying rewards that are valued by their employees, (2) administering appropriate rewards for attainment of organizational goals, and (3) clarifying paths to rewards by setting appropriate expectations and reducing barriers to the employee's attainment of rewards. Such actions will help clarify employee expectations, reduce their frustration, and increase their opportunity for personal satisfaction through effective performance.

Substitutes for Leadership: While hierarchical leadership has received the most attention, substitutes for leadership should not be overlooked. Research suggests that in some situations, hierarchical leadership may be ineffective [7]. When goals and the means to attain those goals are clear, a manger's attempts to direct employee activities may be seen as redundant and as imposing unnecessary, close control.

Substitutes for leadership are particularly abundant within 1s. Research suggests that within 1s, programmers, analysts, and managers possess a high degree of professionalism [2,3]. The employees' professional orientation can often serve as a partial substitute for leadership, since professionals typically cultivate horizontal rather than vertical relationships, give high credence to peer review processes, and develop important referents external to the employing organization.

Job-provided performance feedback provided by the work itself is another characteristic which can partially substitute for leadership. At least in the case of programming tasks, the work itself (program development and testing) provides immediate and concrete feedback about performance. Is managers should take advantage of this situation by reducing controls and increasing delegation, participation, and use of work groups.

Goal setting: Another strategy that can be used to motivate employees is goal setting; this is a simple concept, but its simplicity has led many managers to think that it can be used without planning. Research and experience suggest otherwise. The best results occur when the following steps are taken [8]:

1. Make the goal specific rather than vague; e.g. "decrease the applications backlog by $10\%$ " is better than "try to decrease the application backlog."

2. Set a time limit for goal accomplishment where possible; e.g. “complete successful testing of module ABC in 10 days.”

3. Make the goal challenging yet attainable. When goals are perceived to be unattainable, they will not be accepted and will not lead to better performance.

A related issue is whether goals should be designed for individuals or for groups. Some researchers argue for group goal setting on the grounds that it promotes cooperation and team spirit, while others argue for individual goal setting to promote individual responsibility. In 18, the determining factor should be the amount of interdependence involved in the work being performed. For example, on systems development projects where the degree of interdependence is high and individual performance is difficult to separate from group performance, group goal setting may be preferable to individual goal setting.

If goal setting is to work, IS managers must ensure that their employees will accept and remain committed to the goals. Simple instruction backed by positive support and absence of threats may be the best approach to ensuring goal acceptance. In addition, IS managers must take steps to ensure that the necessary support elements are available. IS managers must also make sure their employees have sufficient knowledge and ability to reach their goals; through training and development. In addition, employees must be provided with feedback on their progress so that they can adjust their effort accordingly.

Job Design: Couger and Zawacki's finding that is professionals have high growth need strength suggests that is managers take a careful look at job design as another means of managing for effectiveness and humanity [2]. Job design is an application of job characteristics theory.

A critical step in the design of any job is the decision about the way work should be distributed among the people who do it. Technological factors, individual training and experience, and equity of work loads are often used to decide how to distribute work [8]. Unfortunately, such considerations tend to result in rather specialized and fragmented jobs.

Job characteristic theory suggests that, wherever possible, natural work units should be created.

Jobs designed around natural boundaries can increase employee ownership of their work and improve the chance that employees will view it as meaningful and important. Different but related tasks may be combined into larger units of work to increase the variety of skills exercised in a job. Similarly, establishing direct relationships between technical personnel and the end-users of the technology can improve jobs in several ways: feedback increases because of additional opportunities for employees to receive direct praise or criticism; skill variety may increase because of the need to exercise interpersonal skills; and, autonomy should increase to the degree that individuals are given responsibility for deciding how to manage their user relationships.

Consider the area of system maintenance. Job assignments can be made so that one employee is given responsibility for all analysis and programming tasks for a maintenance service request. This expands the scope of the job to include a variety of technical and interpersonal skills. A single employee is made responsible for working with uses on determining new requirements, for designing, programming, and testing the changes, and following the changes through acceptance testing and sign-off.

Job design also suggests that jobs should be vertically loaded. In vertical loading, the intent is to start to close the gap between doing and controlling various aspects of the job. Vertical loading can be accomplished by delegating responsibility for deciding on work methods, providing increased freedom in time-management, and providing increased control over budgetary matters. Vertical loading increases the level of autonomy present in a job.

Finally, both goal setting and job design suggest that additional feedback channels should be opened to help employees learn how they are performing. Job-provided feedback is generally superior to manager-provided feedback, because it is more immediate and more private and increases personal control over the work. Establishing direct user relationships, placing design and programming walkthroughs in the hands of programming teams, and providing standard summaries of official performance records directly to employees are strategies for opening feedback channels. Organization Design: Writing on design alternatives for organizing IS, Zmud [15] recognized the need for a new approach. He suggests that IS organizations will not be able to meet the challenges which lie ahead unless they move toward a more flexible and dynamic structure. Traditionally, IS has been divided into subunits responsible for systems development, operations, technical services, and administration. These have been organized along functional lines and have utilized primarily mechanistic processes.

Zmud suggests that a much finer grained and dynamic organization structure is needed and that separate subunits should be created for each of the major functions of the IS organization. For example, different subunits should be responsible for operations, systems development, research and development, technology diffusion, planning, and administration. Coordination mechanisms should be largely informal and structures should be dynamic and overlapping, disintegrating when they are no longer needed. The predominant organizational processes should be based on the organic model, based on interdependent and flexible tasks, and adaptive, flexible role performance. A network structure of control and authority is used to facilitate communication in the needed directions. Distinctions between superiors and subordinates are blurred or non-existent; knowledge centers are identified as dictated by the circumstances and located wherever found. Even though the organic model may be difficult or impossible to achieve in its pure form, it offers a guide to direct managerial efforts within IS.

Self-Managed Work Groups: Organizational contexts outside is have shown that dramatic movement can be made toward the organic model. Consider the success some companies have had with self-managed work groups [13]; such groups represent a serious attempt to provide individual employees with a high degree of control over their jobs, by focusing attention on the group instead of the individual. Self-managed work groups are made up of relatively small teams of people with group-level responsibilities. Individuals are responsible for selecting their leader, assigning work responsibility, and evaluating performance. Serious attempts are made by the organization to provide regular feedback.

Self-managed work groups have been found to promote esprit de corps, task motivation, information sharing, and a sense of ownership of group outcomes. Plants utilizing this concept have operated very effectively according to traditional effectiveness measures. Self-managed work groups have been successful in bringing both organizational and individual goals together for mutual benefit. This can also be applied with is.

Organizational Climate: Overall, what is needed is a new climate within the IS organization. This does not lend itself to legislation through departmental policy, nor is it simply the additive effect of managerial strategies, such as providing realistic job previews to prospective employees, increasing participation, redesigning jobs, or setting specific goals. It is reflected in the day-to-day behavior of managers; i.e., in their attitude and conduct toward their employees. Since many non-material rewards are based on managers' day-to-day interaction with their employees, it is managers who are largely responsible for creating organizational climate. In IS, managers must work to promote trust and openness and the must be receptive to change.

To generalize the discussion thus far and raise it to a more conceptual level, it is useful to introduce a concept called the psychological contract.

## 4. The Psychological Contract

A psychological contract implies that there is an unwritten agreement operating at all times between workers within the IS organization and the various managers that represent the organization [12]. The contract is made up of the expectations of each of the parties. Many of these expectations are implicit and involve a person's sense of dignity and worth. Employees expect organizations to treat them humanly, to provide them with an opportunity to grow and learn, and to give them feedback on their performance. Schein suggests that violations of the psychological contract may be responsible for most employee problems, even though publicly they appear to be due to pay and other material rewards.

On the other side of the contract, the IS organization also has a set of implicit expectations. Managers expect their employees to enhance the image of the organization, to be loyal, and to do their best. Some of the greatest disappointments of managers arise when a valued employee is unwilling to work hard for the organization.

Over the long-term, the psychological contract must be renegotiated. The needs of both the employee and the organization change. Employees are likely to have different needs when they are young than when they are old. Young employees may need to be provided with challenges to allow them to grow and see that they can make an important contribution. Older employees may have more need for security. Organizations too, have different needs at different times. An is organization expects more loyalty during periods of heavy pressure, such as during the final stages of a critical system development project than it does at other times.

The psychological contract also provides a new way to look at effectiveness. Long-term effectiveness can be considered a systems-level criterion. As every organization has multiple functions and goals, a system's effectiveness can be defined as its capacity to survive, adapt, maintain itself, grow, and satisfy its stakeholders.

The technological and organizational environment surrounding the IS organization is changing rapidly. Effectiveness depends on successful adaptation to change, which requires highly motivated employees with the desire and ability to do their job, and flexibility and creativity in management with the willingness to change when necessary. Successful adaptation requires an internal climate of support and freedom from threat. It requires the flexibility of continuously redesigning the department structure and its jobs to be congruent with its goals and tasks.

## 5. Conclusion

Achieving effectiveness at the systems level is not an easy taks. Despite the difficulties involved, some guidelines can be offered.

First, the is organization must build a system for hiring and socializing employees to make them feel wanted, secure, and positively committed to organizational goals.

Second, if the IS organization expects its employees to be committed to its goals, it must provide rewards and conditions consistent with this level of involvement: more than handing out a larger paycheck, non-material rewards must also be offered, including offering a sense of belonging, the opportunity to grow, the autonomy to work creatively, and genuine responsibility for individual and group work. Admittedly, such rewards need to be tailored to individual needs and capabilities. However, they must be fulfilled if employees are to be expected to treat organizational goals as their own.

Third, the is organization must develop programs that obtain motivation and commitment in an integrative manner without destructive competition between subunits that leads to faulty intergroup communication, inflexibility, and commitment to subunit instead of organizational goals. Since it is very difficult to undo group conflict, it is better to avoid it.

Fourth, to remain healthy, the IS organization must stay flexible. In effect, IS managers must view organizational design as an essential coping mechanism. The organic model provides an ideal upon which IS managers can structure their organizations.

Finally, the chief is executive must develop a sense of identity by making the mission and goals clear. But goals should not be imposed upon employees, for it is far better that the goals be developed through employee participation. In this sense, leadership is best considered a function of all members of the department rather than a trait of individual managers. Good leadership and good membership blend to form an effective organization.

It has been argued that organizational effectiveness hinges on good communication, flexibility, creativity, and genuine psychological commitment. The argument is not based on the assumption that it would be nice to manage for humanity, but rather, it is based on the assumption that managing for humanity is the only way to achieve long-term organizational effectiveness.
