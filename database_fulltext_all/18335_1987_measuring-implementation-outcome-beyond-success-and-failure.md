# Measuring Implementation Outcome: Beyond Success and Failure

Edward G. Cale, Jr.

Assistant Professor

Babson College, Wellesley, Massachusetts 02157, USA

and

Kathleen Foley Curley

Associate Professor

College of Business Administration, Northeastern University, Boston, Massachusetts 02115, USA

This paper proposes a framework for measuring the impact of a computer-based application in an organization. Recognizing the complexity of the implementation process, the framework is multi-dimensional: it seeks to measure the outcome of an implementation effort on both the people and tasks affected as separate from user's definition of "success". The framework can be used to support either the quantitative or case study methodologies used in implementation research. Finally, the authors describe a field test of the framework to demonstrate its usefulness in an organizational setting.

Keywords: Implementation, Success, Failure, End-user Computing, Systems Implementation, Office Automation.

## 1. Introduction

The difficulties associated with implementing computer-based applications have been recognized for decades. As early as 1963, a McKinsey and Company study indicated that, of thirty organizations studied, few had been able to successfully implement the majority of their computer-based projects [18]. The plethora of implementation failures described early in the literature (see Drake [7], Ackoff [1], and Woolsey [25] for a sample) documented the substantial costs, both in terms of resources expended as well as opportunity costs associated with the failure to adopt potentially valuable applications.

Two recent dramatic changes in the environment of information systems have helped renew the interest in and need for implementation research. The first is the explosive proliferation of microcomputer-based systems and the second is the increasing presence of ready to use software packages $[12]$ . As a result of these two changes, the following trends have increased the difficulty of the implementation process.

1. The sheer number of sites implementing computer-based applications has increased significantly [9]. Professional resources available to support the exploding number of implementation sites are being hard pressed to keep up with the demand.

2. The direct users of computer-based systems are increasingly non-technical, white collar professionals [3,14]. These new users require substantially more support than did their predecessors in implementing new applications.

3. The users of software packages have little, if any, contact with the system developers. This separation is a violation of long held theory that successful implementation requires a close relationship between developer and user [6,15,17,19].

The overall implication of the above trends is that it is becoming increasingly important to develop both theory and practice to understand the process of implementing computer-based tools in a functioning organization. However, a review of existing implementation literature reveals few consistent conclusions or recommendations $[13]$ . The authors believe that there are two major reasons for this lack of coherence in implementation research: (1) the lack of an agreed upon and objective measure for capturing the outcome of an implementation effort, and (2) the development of two somewhat divergent research methodologies, case studies and quantitative analysis, whose findings have not necessarily contributed to each other to advance our understanding of the implementation process. Although these two approaches share certain data gathering methods in common, their insights have not always been adequately exploited in a complimentary fashion to provide a sense of building momentum or synergy in the literature. In this paper, the authors suggest and test a framework for capturing the results of an implementation effort which focuses on objective, measured outcomes, and at the same time can be used with both methodological approaches.

## 2. Measurement of Implementation Outcome

The most commonly chosen dependent variable in the research has been implementation “success.” However, just what is meant by success, how it should be measured, and who should determine its level, are extremely thorny issues. The concept of success is so value-laden and subjective that it is difficult to reliably evaluate. Measurements of implementation success have ranged from the procedural (“was the formal development cycle completed?”) to the behavioral (“is it used?”) to the emotional (“how do users feel about it?”) to the organizational (“has it changed the way the organization functions?”) to the strategic (“has it affected our competitive position?”).

To avoid the pitfalls of trying to measure a value as illusive as success, the authors decided to focus on the actual, measurable post-implementation impact which the application has on people and tasks in the implementing organization. Our goal was to obtain a comparative measure for implementation outcome which would reflect what had happened independent of prior expectations of users and, as much as possible, independent of external competitive pressures. To the extent that the outcome exceeds or falls short of expected or desired results, the user will consider the effort a success or failure. However, to the researcher, the outcome consists only of the impact of the effort, and would be comparable across all implementation efforts which yield the same results.

## 3. Competing Methodologies

Implementation research constructs have developed, for the most part, from their roots in one of the two widely used traditional methodologies, each with specific strengths and weaknesses. The first, a case study approach, has provided managers and researchers with rich detail and useful insights into the complexities of managing organizational change. However, the anecdotal nature of such studies and the vast wealth of subjective and objective data typically collected, makes summarization difficult and generalizations questionable. The findings tend to be so situational that it is difficult to transfer them into a different environment, or to build from them a sense of developing theory. Although researchers frequently attempt to generalize their findings on the basis of specific case studies, such generalizations are often assailed on the grounds that the site specific factors overwhelm the objectivity of the data gathering process.

On the other hand, the more quantitative approaches, which are narrower in scope, generally drive towards the collection and analysis of objective data to rigorously test hypotheses. The results of this approach often produce statistically supportable generalizations, which can be transferred and tested across sites. However, the nature of the analytic techniques often reduces the complex implementation process to a single outcome or independent variable: usually implementation “success” (with the associated problems already discussed.)

We sought to develop a pilot approach for capturing the results of an implementation effort which would combine some of the richness of the case approach with the objectivity and portability of the quantitative approach. The authors also intended to develop a framework which would recognize the inherent complexity of the implementation process and yet could be used with either case study or quantitative methodologies.

## 4. The Impact Framework

Our goal, then, was to develop a framework which would allow us to capture the outcome of an implementation effort in an objective, repeatable manner while, at the same time, retaining a perspective which recognizes the multi-dimensional nature of the process involved. In developing this framework the authors were guided by the experience of other researchers in the fields of MIS, office automation, and decision support systems, but not necessarily by research singularly focused on implementation. A number of researchers in different fields have wrestled with capturing the impact of an innovation on an organization. In this process, they have consistently focused on two fundamental questions in situations of innovation:

1. Who in the innovating organization has been affected by the new technology, and

2. How have the jobs or roles of those affected actually been changed?

Thus, to develop a framework for assessing the outcome of an implementation effort, the authors wanted to ensure that our approach would capture the answers to these two questions across a broad range of situations. However, for operational parsimony, the framework would not be so broad as to include those individuals external to the implementing organization such as customers, suppliers, and competitors. To answer the question of who within the organization is affected by an application, a slight modification of the classical Anthony [2] management hierarchy was used. To examine how these individuals are actually affected, findings from such seminal works as the Gorry and Scott Morton [11] framework for MIS management and Nolan's [21] stages theory were borrowed to develop a measure for the range of activities which could be supported by an application. Merging these approaches for answering the two questions, a two-dimensional framework is developed to capture who is affected by the new technology and in what way the jobs or roles of those affected have actually changed.

4.1. The Anthony Management Hierarchy: Who is Affected?

In 1965 Robert Anthony first described the management personnel of an organization as falling into three levels, which he labeled Operations Management, Management Control, and Strategic Planning. Operations Management consists of those short term, task oriented management activities responsible for the direct production of goods and services; Strategic Planning consists of those long term, environmentally oriented management activities concerned with charting the future course course of the organization; and Management Control consists of those intermediate range management activities concerned with translating long term plans and policies into executable schedules and budgets to be passed on to Operations Management, and the review of Operations performance and corresponding changes to plan.

Anthony's hierarchy provides a framework for examining what functional level (i.e. who) within management has been affected by an application. Following on the example of Ein-Dor and Segev [8], the authors added a fourth level, Operations Execution, to account for the large range of clerical and worker activities which are not a part of management, but are directly concerned with the production of goods and services, and often are impacted by computer-based systems.

This four level model of an organization provides us with a metric for separating the individuals by describing the functional nature of their positions. As Anthony described his hierarchy, the job characteristics of each level differ significantly from the other levels in such terms as planning outlook, time horizon, scope of activities, sources of critical information, and required mental attributes. By allowing us to separate the organization into distinct levels, this models allow us to examine who, from a structural perspective, has been affected by an application.

4.2. Range of Activities: How have jobs / roles actually changed?

Gorry and Scott Morton, as well as Simon [22] described management information systems as supporting a wide range of managerial decision making, from the highly structured decisions of operations personnel to the unstructured decision making of strategic planners. A number of other authors, including Carlisle [4], Lodahl [16], Nolan [20], and Strassman [24] have used similar constructs to examine the range of use, or set of activities that information systems are designed to support. Using this literature as a basis, the authors developed a definition for segregating this range of activities. Our definition consists of the following five categories: (1) handling data, (2) processing data, (3) reporting data, (4) communicating information, and (5) making decisions. In handling of data, such activities as the capturing, editing, sorting, retrieving, and copying of data are included. In processing of data, activities such as calculating, analyzing, and graphing data are included. Reporting of data, on the other hand includes activities such as answering inquiries, developing and filling documents, and reviewing documents. By communicating information, the authors mean the dissemination of data, information, and decisions. In making decisions, such activities as organizing ideas, asking “what if”

questions, implementing a course of action, and comparing performance to plan are included.

Clearly, the activities enumerated above do not exhaust the uses which an application could support under each of the five major categories, but they serve to illustrate the concepts involved. They also roughly match the range of decision making activity described in the literature and as such are firmly grounded in the history of MIS research.

## 4.3. Synthesis

Combining our expansion of Anthony's hierarchy with the range of supportable activities leads to a two-dimensional framework for examining the impact of an application implementation (see Table 1 below). Each cell in the framework describes an activity which an application can support at a given functional level of the organization. In addition, a row and column have been included to allow for summarization of outcome.

Operational use of the framework requires two steps. First, prior to actual implementation, a definition of maximum potential impact of the application would be determined. In other words, given the specific application, which activities in each of the twenty cells could reasonably be supported by this application. Second, after the subject organization has attempted implementation, the researcher would examine, again on a cell by cell basis, the degree to which the implementing organization had successfully mastered the potential uses of the application.

The determination of both potential and actual impacts can be accomplished with a range of evaluation approaches. At its simplest, a researcher could merely check off cells where the application has impact. At a greater level of detail, the researcher could describe qualitatively both the potential and achieved impacts. Finally, in its most detailed use, quantitative measurements could be determined for each cell of the framework.

## 5. Field Testing the Framework

This framework evolved out of extensive field work in one major implementation effort. The following case summary illustrates how our approach was used to describe the outcome of this effort. Nineteen subunits of a forest products organization recently implemented a powerful, computer-based planning and budgeting package. The application could support the collection of data on inputs and outputs associated with potential field projects, and the examination of budgets and outputs associated with various collections of projects.

Users could employ the application to support an array of activities. As its simplest, the application could serve merely as an electronic filling and display system, used for storing and retrieving information on individual projects. At the other extreme, once project data were entered into the system, the sophisticated user could employ linear programming techniques to study alternative groupings of projects based on a range of constraints, select a “best” package of projects, and use the system to convoy choices and alternatives to both subordinate and superior units within the organization. Each of the 19 implementing sites was required to make some attempt to use the application, but they were given considerable latitude as to exactly what use they would make of it.

Prior to any attempted implementation, the researchers engaged in in-depth discussions with the developers of the system and with potential users from all four levels of the organization. A consensus view of the capability and potential of the system emerged from these discussions. The participants concentrated, in turn, on each cell of the framework, identifying what impact a “100 percent” implementation would have for each of the 20 framework cells. This list of cell specific tasks then became the standard by which implementation outcomes would be measured.

The following example of how one cell of the framework was evaluated in examining an actual implementation effort is useful in understanding the operation of the overall framework. Prior to the actual implementation effort, the authors, together with the developers of the application, prior users of the application, and potential users of the application, studied each cell of the framework. For example, it was agreed that, at the Management Control level, the application could potentially support the making of four relatively independent and significant decisions. It was agreed that one of the four decisions was deemed to be fundamentally more important than the other three. A consensus was reached that, for decision making at the Management Control level, the most significant decision was worth 40%, with each of the other three decisions worth 20% each. After the implementation effort, each participating site was visited and the Management Control personnel, among others, were queried as to their use of the application in making the four decisions.

One site was determined to have relied very heavily on the application in their “40% decision”, and they were awarded the full 40 points. They never really used the application for making any of the other three decisions, although they tangentially addressed one of the “20% decisions” by using reports from the system. They were awarded 5 points as partial use of the system for this second question for an overall outcome of 45%. At a second site, the application played no role in any decisions which were made at the Management Control level; thus, that site received a 0% for that cell. In a similar manner, the maximum potential and actual achievement were evaluated for each of the other cells in the framework.

While the process for determining the maximum potential of the application for each cell of the framework involved a degree of subjectivity, it did provide a measure which, by consensus, was deemed to be reasonable, and, more importantly, which could be applied across all implementing sites to provide a consistent measure of outcome. Even though the 45% site mentioned above had hoped to achieve more, the application had a demonstrably larger impact on them than it had on the second site.

## 6. Conclusions

Given a consistent determination of maximum potential impact $^{1}$ , the framework could be used to examine the impact of a given application either within one organization or across a number of organizations implementing the same application. Within a single organization, the framework could be used as a diagnostic tool to indicate areas for further study. With its ability to identify who within an organization is affected and which category of activities are changed, the framework allows the researcher to quickly identify areas of interest. Used across a number of organizations implementing a given application, the framework would yield a different impact profile for each implementing organization. Comparison of profiles, through an approach such as PLUS analysis or simple table summary calculations, would allow the researcher to gain valuable insight into the range of application impact across the implementing organizations. With a sufficiently large number of implementing organizations, the framework could be used to capture the outcomes produced by differing implementation strategies, thereby providing an effective method for evaluating competing implementation approaches.

With its intertwining of human, technological, and organizational elements, the implementation process is extremely complex. One of the major strengths of the twenty cell framework is that it provides a rich view of both the people and the processes affected and can be used as an accompanying measurement tool with a traditional case study approach. For instance, each cell identifies an area (people and tasks) which needs to be studied and described to capture the impact of the implementation effort. However, use of the framework does not preclude the reduction of implementation outcome to fewer than twenty cell values. While losing richness and requiring either an implicit or an explicit assumption about the relative cell weights, columns could be reduced to summary values indicating overall implementation impact on activities, and rows could be similarly summarized to determine overall impact by organizational level. Finally, while surrendering all the richness of the framework, both columns and rows could be collapsed to provide an overall outcome level for the application at a given organization. Places for these summary values are shown in Table 1 as Row Summary and Column Summary. One could place the table summary value at the junction of the two summaries.

The summary values shown in the table are simple averages of the rows, columns, and total table values. This summary assumes equal value or weight for each cell. In situations where a researcher had reason to weight cells differently (e.g. increasing weights for cells deemed to have greater financial significance), the summary values would be different, but the use of the framework would remain the same.

In using the field sites to evaluate the impact framework, it was determined that the framework must meet two tests. First, it must work as a means of categorizing implementation impact; and second, it must overcome most of the shortcomings discussed earlier on measurement of implementation “success.”

The framework clearly met the first test. The differences in outcome among sites, as measured and displayed with the framework, were quite large. At the low end of the range were units which were only using the application to store and retrieve project information (the Operations Execution/Handling Data cell). At the high end of the range were several units which were using the application to support all five activities, with all four levels of the organization being involved in at least one of the activities. Thus, as a means for collecting and categorizing data on the implementation of a computer-based application, the framework appears to be quite successful. It visually, quantitatively, and qualitatively displayed a rich spectrum of differences among implementing sites.

To compare the framework approach to the more traditional “success” approach, the authors queried individuals at each of the sites on whether or not they felt the application had been successfully implemented at their location. Opinions of success varied greatly from individual to individual within each site – whose opinion should be accepted? Opinions of success contrasted with actual achievements also varied greatly between sites:

some units were making little use of the application, but were content with their situation and judged the implementation a success; at other locations, expectations for the application had been extremely high, and, despite moderate to high use across the organization and activities, the application was viewed as only partially successful. Thus, measurement of user opinion of success was, at best, flawed: it all depended on who was queried.

On the other hand, when shown the framework which had been filled in to detail the impact of the application on their site, there was substantial agreement among users that it did accurately display their achievements. Users might disagree as to whether their achievements were adequate or not, but there was little argument about actual implementation impact as measured by the framework.

The lack of relationship between achievement and feelings of success is a strong endorsement for the need for a more objective and activity oriented framework, and is consistent with the findings of Srinivasan [23]. This framework dispassionately shows what an organization has achieved. The users' opinions about their success more closely reflect how their achievements match up with their expectations. Additionally, when shown the framework profiles for all 19 units, the executive who had mandated and overseen the implementation in all sites expressed that the profiles did, in fact, accurately portray the differences among implementing sites.

## 7. Summary

In summary, the implementation impact framework proposed in this paper and displayed in Table 1 is useful to capture and record the results of implementing a computer-based application in an organization. The framework does a reasonable job of separating user achievement from user attitudes, overcoming a shortcoming common in much of the implementation literature. We believe that the framework proposed for measuring implementation impact is theoretically sound and operationally usable, and that it overcomes many of the shortcoming of the “success” measure of implementation.

The development of this framework was built upon the rich conceptual base that exists in the management control and management information systems literature. The framework represents an attempt to operationalize some of the generally accepted models by experimenting with ways of measuring outcome, using a consensus approach to determine specific values for our outcome measurements. The authors believe that this is a useful first step toward pushing implementation research beyond the exploratory phase since it helps to objectify measurement of the dependent variable and can be used with either a quantitative or case study methodology.

Ultimately, both researchers and practitioners are interested in a workable prescriptive model. As knowledge of the implementation process is gained, it should be possible to identify management actions that can help push an organization to higher, more effective levels of technology use. Such a prescriptive model, however, is impossible to construct without consistent descriptive models joining both streams of implementation research methodology.
