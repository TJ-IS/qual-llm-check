# Examining the Industry Comparative Traits of Hardware and Software Manufacturers

Robert C. Newman

J.D. Edwards & Co., Denver, Colorado, USA

William A. Newman

Dept. of Accounting and CIS, Univ. of Nevada / Reno, Reno, NV 98557, USA

Gerald E. Evans

Dept. of Accounting and CIS, Univ. of Nevada / Reno, Reno, NV 89557, USA

A survey of senior managers from 18 firms comprising 20 different operational divisions was conducted to determine the perceived difference between hardware and software companies. The survey questions addressed the structural, managerial, financial, product, and personnel distinctions between hardware oriented and software oriented companies. The results indicated that there are sizable factor and cultural differences between computer hardware and software firms. Software firms are smaller, more intellectually diverse, and more vulnerable than hardware firms. Hardware companies have considerable economic inertia and continuing revenue streams. These differences require unique management approaches and styles.

Keywords: Hardware firms, Software firms, Computer industry management style, Hardware/software firm differences.

## 1. Introduction

“Hardware is not difficult to understand. It is nuts and bolts and microchips. But what of software? Perhaps the easiest way to think of it is in terms of a simple analogy; hardware is to software as a television set is to the shows that appear on it. Computer programs, rather like those aired on TV, are a man-made effort to turn lifeless hardware into software one might want to spend some time with.” [1]

Hardware and software are two very different sides of the data processing coin, an important issue is whether or not the people involved need to be managed and supervised differently. There is some evidence that there are important differences between programmers and analysts $[3,5]$ as well as the characteristics of the organization in which they work $[4]$ . There is also evidence that data processing professionals differ markedly from information systems professionals in how they respond to deadlines and work loads $[2]$ . If there are differences between these categories of individuals, it is entirely possible that important differences exist between those working with hardware and software. Furthermore, since it is the people who make up an organization, possible differences in such specialists could produce important cultural differences between hardware and software firms. A knowledge of the answers to these problems would be of use to all DP professionals, particularly when:

1. Hardware companies acquire software companies and attempt to assimilate them into the company style.

2. Non-software companies attempt to develop software products.

3. Software companies expand into hardware manufacturing or sales.

4. Investors analyze the offerings of both hardware and software companies.

To begin exploring these differences, a telephone survey was conducted using nine questions. A convenience sample of twenty experienced managers from computer hardware and software firms primarily in “Silicone Valley” was used. The questions dealt with general and specific aspects of software and hardware firms. The companies and the interviewees are shown in Table 1.

## 2. The Survey Results

Hardware companies were characterized as being production oriented. This reflects the fact that hardware manufacturing is a more mature industry. Software firms were characterized as being more developmental. This reflects the fact that software development is often considered more of an art than a science. As McCellen has said:

"The programmer's muse is an untamed one. Great software, like great writing, adheres to no schedule of production." [7]

Being production/manufacturing oriented, the hardware industry is much more capital intensive than the software industry. On one hand, hardware firms are generally larger and have much greater semi-fixed and direct expenses; this results in a much smaller gross margin. Software firms on the other hand have relatively few fixed and direct expenses and much lower capital investment. This produces a potential for much larger margins and the short-term manipulation of operations, profitability, and strategic expenditures.

Question Two: "What are the important differences between the personnel in hardware and software firms?"

Hardware firms employ many semi-skilled workers like draftsmen and assemblers as well as many technically trained individuals, such as engineers. Consequently, hardware personnel are more easily managed, particularly with traditional management techniques. Software firms rely on highly task-oriented individuals who are often more like artisans than technicians. Table 2 presents the results of a recent survey on employee distribution in computer companies located in the U.S. mountain states.

Successful software products are seldom conceptualized by competent but possibly plodding engineers; they may be refined by such people, but they are conceived and first implemented by “genius programmers”. Mitch Kapur, the former Chairman of Lotus Development Corporation, when asked what he would do with another fifty good programmers replied that: “…there are not 50 good programmers in the world…” and he was lucky to have one. In code production alone, there is at least a ten to one difference between these super-programmers and the average practitioner; some say up to 100 to one. Hardware people seldom exhibit these vast differences in productivity.

Hardware companies tend to be organized like hierarchical pyramids with many levels of management using formal reporting channels. Managers in hardware firms control assets (space, equipment and inventories) by being caretakers. Software firms are usually smaller and have shallow, more participative management structures. Managers in software firms are often owners. It is not uncommon for the owner/manager of a software firm to be the genius behind and the principal champion of new products.

Design-to-market time in the hardware industry can be significantly longer than that of the software industry. Hardware companies try to avoid playing you-bet-your-company in today's fast-paced technology. They cannot afford serious mistakes, yet must hope to maintain their reputation and market share.

In a hardware firm, performance goals are often the result of market or competitive analysis – rarely the result of inspiration. For established products, customer demand, as voiced through the sales channel, can produce performance goals. Thus, the hardware industry has two important factors that differentiate it from the software industry:

1. Performance goals can be objectively specified;

2. Buyers understand the intended results well enough to appreciate and influence specifications.

In a software firm, performance is generally stated in terms of function and is somewhat subjective. The industry can still create new marketplaces by developing a unique paradigm and exposing potential users. Some well known examples of this include: spreadsheet programs, graphics systems, and data base retrieval services. Commonly, products are conceived by building a functional “wish list” and then selecting an appropriate subset (sometimes by sampling the target market). To summarize, the hardware designer worries about the quantitative factors, such as instructions per second, while the software designer worries about qualitative factors, such as the inclusion or exclusion of a function.

Question Four: "How does the concept of 'product liability' differ between hardware and software firms?"

Reliability is a key component of performance in both. Customer satisfaction is maintained only when useful work is performed for a very high proportion of the time. IBM uses the terms “Reliability, Availability, and Serviceability” to express this set of reliability tradeoffs. Devices will, of course, fail, but some components have a higher reliability (and often higher cost) than others. Redundancy in construction can allow continued operation, even when some components have failed. Quick and easy service or service concurrent with useful operation can help maintain high levels of availability. Such tradeoff opportunities to the hardware designer are legion.

There is virtually no parallel to this for software. It can have flaws (or bugs), but once corrected and within a fixed environment, it will not wear-out or deteriorate; serviceability is a consideration but only in terms of fixing bugs. Most hardware considerations, such as Mean Time Before Failure (MTBF) and Mean Time To Repair (MTTR) have no software counterpart.

Question Five: "How does cost estimation of hardware and software products differ?"

A hardware designer usually starts with a target cost and a good prior knowledge of make or buy alternatives. Invention, such as the design of custom chips, is a high risk proposition and is avoided if possible. Outside suppliers provide most, or even all, of the components to be used. Engineers can often synthesize and accurately predict manufacturing and assembly costs, required levels and costs of spare parts, and even “learning curves” (where costs decrease over time due to experience or reengineering, known as “value engineering”).

The software cost estimator has a more difficult time estimating development costs and an easier time estimating production costs than the hardware estimator. The various structured/modularizing techniques allow the software estimator to break up projects into smaller parts, allowing for better costing. Such techniques, however, are often cost-effective only for large and/or custom projects. When the designer's objective is to replicate another firm's product, "reverse engineering" can be performed and costs can be predicted with reasonable accuracy. Production costs are generally low, easy to predict, and subject to little variability.

Successful hardware products usually must interface with or allow easy transition from prior products. This is true because users (buyers) often have a large investment in software and personnel training. So, there is only moderate revision in architecture from one product cycle to another. At first glance, it would seem that designers in startup firms are not affected by the above constraint; but they often are, because even new products have to appeal to an existing base of users.

The impact of prior products on the software side largely depends on the type of product. Operating systems, for example, have a very long life (up to 20 years or more). New versions (releases) may be produced regularly (once or twice per year) with compatibility to prior versions of nearly 100%. Products like compilers and utilities need little interim change, but are often replaced in four to seven year cycles. Compatibility is not as crucial, but the new products are often a functional superset of the prior product. Compatibility in application products is even less important. It is common, however, for the vendor to provide conversion tools from one such product to its next generation.

The life cycles of software are nowhere near as formalized as those in the hardware industry. The appeal of software products is a complex mixture of marketing, utility, and cost and appears to be bi-modal. Some low-end, consumer-type products, like games, have short product (version) lives – often one to two years. Complex application and system software products, as we have seen, are much longer.

In summary, software products are knowledge based systems whereas hardware products are technology based. The long learning time for both users and servicers of software products does not justify more rapid product cycles. Therefore, the software designer has incentive to think further into the future to anticipate coming market requirements.

Question Six: "What are the differences between hardware and software firms in the production of their products?"

There are a number of differences in the production of deliverables (products). For hardware firms, deliverables are the actual computing devices. For software firms, the immediate parallel is the duplication and packaging of disketes containing the program. A more important software deliverable is the intangible knowledge around which the software is based. This knowledge is exemplified through manuals, training programs, client support organizations, newsletters, etc.

On the hardware side, great effort has been expended in reducing the labor content. A computer such as the IBM System 36, which sells for \$30,000 to \$60,000, has a direct labor (assembly) component of only 6 hours. Unfortunately, factory indirect labor (inspectors, clerks, etc.) still take a substantial portion of the firms employees, often up to 50%. Many such workers are semi-skilled or even unskilled.

On the software side, the production process is similar to duplicating tapes; it requires specialized equipment if high volumes are to be achieved. Also, although some software is transportable between several types of computers, the diskette formats differ. Thus, all but the largest software vendors use outside services to copy and package software. In effect, the software firm itself usually has no people engaged in direct manufacturing of deliverables.

Question Seven: "How do software and hardware firms differ in how they perform the marketing/sales function?"

Whereas hardware companies have the highest proportion of their employees in manufacturing, software companies usually have large marketing and sales departments: it is not uncommon to devote up to 40% of software sales to marketing; this is fully four times the ratio of the average hardware company.

Both hardware and software companies have large marketing budgets. A recent analysis of the advertising space in Computerworld revealed 115 of the 160 page-equivalents devoted to advertising. The split was approximately 45% hardware, 35% software and 20% other (such as employment ads). Specifications are featured in hardware ads, especially those which compare selected specifications with other vendor's products. Software ads almost never feature specifications (number of programs, transactions per unit of time, etc.). Instead, they tend to be “high-level”, stressing their benefits to management. Both types of companies recognize the relationship between good marketing literature and product success. Most firms completely rework their literature at least once a year; ad copy which runs more than three months is unusual.

In terms of customers, it has always been easier for a user to upgrade or change equipment within a given hardware manufacturer's product line, and therefore hardware companies aggressively court new customers. Sales incentives and product support are the rule of thumb. Software customers have shown much less loyalty to their vendors; sales incentives are correspondingly less.

The sales staff of both hardware and software companies include all types: aggressive, hard-closing, high-visibility to consultive, soft-sell, technical sales engineers. Each vendor has a favored look so that within each sector companies display different sales personalities. In general, there seem to be fewer salespeople with heavy product knowledge in the software industry.

A customer often buys many different software products but relies on a single hardware solution. The average software sale is thus much less than the average hardware sale; however, the number of calls per close, the length of the selling cycle, and the proportion of prospects closed are roughly equal. Therefore, for sales compensation to be competitive, the commission percent for software sales is higher and the sales quota much lower than for hardware sales. Commissions of 6–10% and quotas of \$1,000,000 per year are common in software while hardware sales are usually in the range of 1–2% and \$5,000,000 respectively.

Question Eight: "How do hardware and software firms differ in how they distribute and service their products?"

Suppliers of large computers and software for large computers almost always sell and distribute directly to end users. Low-end microcomputers and software are almost always sold through retail outlets or product centers. In between these two, there are multiple channels of distribution, particularly for hardware and the most popular utility programs. Channels include direct vendor distribution, Value Aded Resellers (VAR's), Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEM's), and dealers/brokers. Higher ticket software items often include a component of personalized service in the form of installation consulting assistance.

Physical distribution and the management of complete unit and spare parts inventories are major problems of hardware companies. Options, parts shortages, cabling requirements, and other factors (including late software) complicate inventory scheduling and frustrate planners.

The necessity of having inventory in the distribution channel exists for all sellers, however, production lead times are much shorter, and dependence on suppliers is much less for software firms. They do, however, suffer from sales that are much less controllable and therefore much more difficult to forecast. Stories abound of software companies being “blind-sided” by overnight turns in the marketplace. If the turn is down, high volume sellers can be stuck with inventories of inherently worthless products. Even worse, since most software vendors do business like book publishers, they allow retailers to return a certain percentage of unsold shipments for credit. Thus, prior software sales may carry contingent liability.

Service is an area where hardware and software companies differ considerably. Hardware service is basically: "if it breaks, we'll replace the board". There is little customer expectation that the hardware service people will offer any advice or help in the equipment's intended use. Hardware service people are usually technical and often lack the knowledge of rudimentary business data processing. Software servicers have a much broader and demanding job performing the following tasks:

1. Consult with users prior to the installation, suggesting the most effective product use.

2. Install and configure the products.

3. Train users.

4. Train maintenance programmers.

5. Diagnose system and user problems as they occur.

6. Coordinate both temporary and permanent system fixes.

7. Provide post-installation telephone or on-site help.

Question Nine: "How do hardware and software firms differ in their accounting philosophies?"

The largest independent software firms are small by hardware standards and they tend to be much younger. Software company management is much closer to the day-to-day work, often participating directly in new product development or marketing. There are few paper entrepreneurs and asset managers in the software industry. Close involvement by management often means that products are driven from top to bottom in the organization and thus software companies react rapidly and responsively to change in the marketplace.

Hardware companies are most often patterned after IBM, and even though it performs and is managed well, it is just too big to respond quickly to change. Senior managers are most often from a marketing and financial background. Products arise in company development labs, often taking years to see fruition (e.g. the 6800 chip).

These size, age, and management factors, coupled with vastly different investment needs, help explain the differences in company capital structure. With few exceptions, software companies are privately held and their owners work in the company. In fact, most software companies do not appeal to investors, because they lack tangible assets (inventories, plants, etc.) and often few intangible assets (patents, stable markets, etc.) other than perhaps goodwill.

The IRS, SEC, and the FASB all require capitalization of all R&D expenditures by hardware firms, under the theory that a tangible asset is being created. Until recently, software companies had no one standard to follow, but the SEC required most public companies to expense R&D costs, implying that no asset was being created. Financial Accounting Statement (FAS) No. 86, made mandatory in the USA for 1987, now requires the capitalization of some types of R&D. A preliminary survey by ADAPSO, the US software industry trade association, shows that most companies will be able to capitalize 15% or less of expenses. But even this relatively small asset allocation continues to be viewed suspiciously by the investing public and the banking community.

## 3. Conclusion

There are sizable factor and cultural differences between computer hardware and software companies. Software companies are smaller, more intellectually diverse, and more vulnerable than hardware companies. They feed on genius, but few control it. In many ways, they are more contemporary organizations: models for a rapidly-adapting, high-tech economy.

Their dark side is their size and competition:

too many small companies sell too similar products. Hardware companies have considerable economic inertia; continuing revenue streams (maintenance, parts, etc.) provide a buffer. Software companies, particularly those that rely on one product, die quickly if products fall from favor.

As the software industry consolidation continues, surviving organizations may have much different corporate personalities. Some believe they will be more structured, like hardware firms, while others believe they will more closely resemble publishing houses. A few believe the issue is moot, because new hardware (5th and 6th generation) will obsolete all programming.

Currently, the two types of firms require different management styles to compete effectively. When a hardware firm expands into software production, management cannot expect to supervise software developers in the same way as hardware engineers: little new software will be developed! Alternately, if hardware engineers were given the same freedom and unstructured management style as software heavyweights, perhaps hardware development schedules would become less predictable.

Our conclusion is that hardware and software people will be managed differently at least for another hardware generation. Attempts to adapt one management style to fit both have been largely unsuccessful; even structured, mature firms like IBM show differences in the ways in which they direct the two types. At present, organizations which understand the diversity have largely given up and there is little remaining push toward convergence.

Large independent software companies such as Computer Associates have publicly claimed that hardware companies cannot compete, in software, against software-only companies because of the cultural gap. Except in the area of system software (operating systems, compilers etc.), where manufacturers sometimes have a built-in advantages, the authors agree; history shows that software operations within hardware companies have not been profitable and have often been excised at the earliest opportunity. Of the successes, the strongest are those where software is run both physically and culturally independent of the hardware organization. Thus, our advice to hardware companies seeking a software affiliation is to:

1. Evaluate the make or buy decision carefully;

there are many factors other than the obvious, so err in the buy direction.

2. Avoid applications; application products are hard to support and require specialized industry knowledge which is difficult to retain.

3. Manage lightly; avoid the temptation of putting production-oriented people in charge.

4. Go as slowly as possible; provide only software to sell the hardware.

5. Change the culture of software developers slowly; some change will come, especially if the company has a strong cultural base, but large-scale change will be unsuccessful (as an example, the Big-8 us accounting firms, although they employ many skilled and well educated people, have been unsuccessful in developing and keeping professional-level programmers – primarily because of cultural gaps between accounting mind-set people and the software mind-set).

6. Understand that motivational factors, working habits, and the pace of achievement will differ between software groups and hardware groups.

7. Plan and budget for the long-term, since short-term success is not predictable.

Finally, if your staying power is questionable, don't bother.
