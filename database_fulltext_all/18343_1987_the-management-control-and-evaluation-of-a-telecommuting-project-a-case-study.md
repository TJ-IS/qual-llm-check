# The Management, Control, and Evaluation of a Telecommuting Project: A Case Study

Adolph I. Katz

Fairfield University, School of Business, Fairfield, CT 06430-7524, USA

There is considerable information available about remote site work and telecommuting. However, little information is available about how such a program can be implemented. This paper contains a design outline for a telecommuting program and the guidelines needed for the selection of related projects, personnel, and managers. It also proposes systems for monitoring the pilot project and evaluating the results.

This study is based on research of other workers and on the results obtained from a carefully controlled longitudinal study of telecommuting at the Textron Lycoming Division of Textron Corporation at Stratford, CT hereinafter referred to as Textron. Results are tabulated for three major areas of the study: the workstation hardware and software; communications between the telecommuter subject of the study and the project management at the plant; and finally, the morale of the telecommuter.

Keywords: Telecommuting, Remote work, Project management.

## 1. Background

## 1.1 Trends and Status

Telecommuting was defined by Jack Nilles as “the substitution of communications technology for travel to a central work location” [18]. Remote work began in the early 1970s when several companies set up telecommuting trials. In 1984, about 450 companies in the U.S.A. involved about 100,000 workers in some level of telecommuting [14,22]. Gordon, however, places the current numbers of telecommuters at about 10,000 because he classifies telecommuters as people who work regularly outside the office, excluding self-employed individuals and sales representatives [9]. This growth can be attributed, in part, to the proliferation of low-cost personal computers, electronic messaging networks, and office automation. Nilles [19] projects that telecommuting will increase to 10 million people by the 1990s but Olson [20], Salomon [21], and Gordon question to the magnitude and timing of the projections because these programs need to be institutionalized.

Ordinarily, telecommuting programs are found in “high tech” companies like IBM, DEC, and Control Data Corporation; but there is also a strong interest in telecommuting in companies like J.C.

## 1.2 Benefits and Costs

Telecommuting can reduce costs, increase productivity, and attract and retain valuable employees. Managers claim that telecommuting produces better work quality and worker satisfaction $[15]$ . According to Gordon $[8]$ , productivity increased from 15–80% with the average improvement about 25–35%. Control Data Corporation, citing an estimated 15–25% improvement in on-time delivery, goals met, and goals improved upon $[12]$ , concluded that “...at home workers generally perform better than in-house workers”. Increased productivity has been attributed to:

\* Fewer distractions and disruptions

\* The opportunity to work at one's peak period

\* Conversion of commuting hours to working hours

\* Fewer absences for medical appointments or minor illnesses.

McClintock [17] and Antonoff [1] caution that increased productivity could be attributed to the replacement of leisure time with work time by professionals who, using a personal computer or terminal, work at home on a regular basis in addition to regular work hours. The problems inherent in a remote work environment are the absence of direct supervision, reduced security, and indirect rather than one-site communication with employees. Also labor unions are increasing their opposition to “electronic cottages turning into electronic sweatshops” [2,3].

A survey [17] of 140 professional part-time and full-time employees (primarily self-employed (50%), males (70%) with an average age of 40 years, and at least 2 years of college) in the U.S. and Canada who work at home with computer based technology revealed “spillover” of work related issues including:

\* The isolation of the house, because the telephone is tied up with the computer

\* The location of the terminals and work materials cause conflict

\* Household members interrupt work.

Survey respondents also reported:

\* An increase in quality and quantity of their work

\* A feeling of control over work activity

\* Greater work satisfaction

\* A sense of increased responsibility

\* Reduced work stress

\* Increased satisfaction with home life.

## 1.4 Purpose of this Study

Most journal articles about telecommuting are overviews of the potential and the problems together with anecdotal comments by managers and participants. However, the research rarely captures the daily dynamics of telecommuting activity and management. Therefore this research addresses morale, interaction with peers and supervisors, and related subjects in a longitudinal study of telecommuter. The actors' impressions were secured as the events unfolded. As Franz points out, reconstructive interviews and static surveys of large numbers of participants "often provide interesting ideas, (but) they are no substitute for collecting data over time, as the events occur..." [6].

## 2. Textron Pilot Study

The Information Systems Department (ISD) of Textron initiated a pilot study to evaluate telecommuting. The goal was twofold: to explore the potential of new workplace options; and to provide a formal evaluation process for future telecommuting projects. In order to make the best use of funds and personnel in this project, it was decided to focus on a comprehensive study of a single test subject. By selecting pertinent parameters, the findings could then be applied more generally, as long as the extrapolation was reasonably and carefully defined. The volunteer test subject was a mechanical engineer with a B.A. degree and a strong interest in becoming a telecommuter. Her personal attributes matched the characteristics specified for a good candidate. She had spent half of her three years at Textron as a thermal analysis engineer in the Heat Transfer Group and half as a design programmer analyst in ISD, where she developed functional specifications, determined feasibility of user requests for new or enhanced software, and evaluated computer hardware and software for support of planned or on-going projects. ISD management, at all levels, participated in the project and supported it enthusiastically because they hoped to provide new work options and wanted to have a role in testing new technologies.

The test subject and her supervisor were a self selected sample. While their experimental results may not necessarily be generalized to all others, we believe that their reactions to the experience can provide valuable information about a remote work environment.

## 2.1 Data Collection

The telecommuter kept a computer-based Daily Log of all her activities. The log included actual versus projected time spent on tasks, telephone communications, and the nature and resolution of problems (Table 4). Project activities reported in the log were classified into one of the following categories:

1. Design/code computer programs

2. Test computer programs

3. Prepare documentation

4. Administrate (mail, memos etc.)

5. Participate in meetings

6. Participate in training

7. Provide support services.

These categories provided the basis for future Textron projects and workstation requirements and are consistent with the telecommuting tasks reported by McClintock.

Because it was believed that the isolation of the remote work site would significantly affect the morale of the telecommuter $[2,5,7]$ , she was instructed to keep also a daily morale log. Using a scale ranging from Low (1) to High (5), she recorded her mood, enthusiasm, tension, health, and outlook towards the job using nine variables originally considered relevant in determining the importance of morale on the success of failure of telecommuting (see Table 5).

The morale log was initiated the second month of telecommuting so we have no control data for in-plant work by the same telecommuter. However, assuming the telecommuter is her own best baseline, it is reasonable to believe that a report of high Enthusiasm (5) at the workstation would be equivalent of one in-plant. Any rating greater than the central value (3) is considered “better than average”.

In addition to the daily Project Activity and Morale logs, the telecommuter kept an audio-tape recorded diary. Only a single external consultant had access to the tape and it was used to give management objective feedback for addressing human relations problems. Further insight into personal issues came from interviews with users, supervisors, and peers.

For 75% of the days, the Daily Morale Report Log was completed before 0915 hours and before 0855 for 50% of the days. The “work” of setting up the workstation began before any report was filed, but the time of filing suggests that telecommuters may shift to a “natural” or self-determined workday that does not necessarily coincide with the in-plant workday.

## 2.2 Telecommuting Projects

The three software projects selected for the telecommuting study met the following specifications:

\* Deliverables were well defined and milestones could be identified and measured.

\* The telecommuter controlled the work-pace.

\* Work was done on a standalone workstation that could be linked to the mainframe computer.

## 3. Results

Productivity was measured by subjective observation only. The literature contains no quantitative measures that apply to this case and consensus could not be reached on new measures that would apply. “Lines of debugged source codes per day” were not considered a fair assessment of programmer productivity, because they are application, machine, and language dependent. Telecommuting productivity was evident in the reduction of “lost time” and the significantly higher rate of completion of planned tasks. These findings are illustrated in Table 8. An immediate supervisor noted an increase in the quality of the subject’s programs. A supervisor of the technical staff which used the CAEDS $^{™}$ software, rated as superior both the quality of the programs with regard to completeness, detail, and readability with comparable documentation done before the study.

It is not likely that the dramatic changes in performance were due to special attention and recognition of the test subject (Hawthorne Effect) since the telecommuter was the center of attention at many management and technical meetings during the pre-telecommuting phase and even so the subject's planned task completion ratio and the proportion of unplanned to planned tasks during this period were rated unsatisfactory. However, project task productivity improved during the time spent in the remote work environment therefore suggesting a direct relation to the remote workstation operation.

The time history of “received” and “originated” calls is summarized in Fig. 2. In September, the number of “received phone calls” was low, a possible indication of a breakdown in communications. On her own initiative, the telecommuter discussed the situation with her supervisor who subsequently told the CAEDS $^{™}$ staff that the telecommuter was ‘off-site’ and not ‘at home’ and was available for consulting and other work-related activities. This action produced a dramatic rise in the number of “received” and “originated” calls in October.

Telephone time consumed 14.9% of all reported project time during telecommuting. Originated calls averaged 30% longer than received calls (18.2 vs. 13.8 minutes). About 90% of all telephone calls had a brief social content which provided informal contact for the telecommuter with her in-plant colleagues.

A correlation analysis of the daily Morale reports and the daily Log shows [23]:

1. The mean value was 3.7 out of 5 for all factors for the 64 working days.

2. The telecommuter's feelings "about the job" depended on organizational events on the job (e.g. change of consulting responsibility), not telecommuting issues (e.g. lack of social contact with peers).

3. The telecommuter's feelings about tasks were directly related to the task and not to telecommuting issues.

4. The telecommuter's Mood, Outlook Towards Job, and Self-Confidence correlated with Enthusiasm and were not found to be dependent upon the telecommuting environment.

5. The telecommuter reported: (averaged figures)

a. A high rating for personal environment (4.65).

b. A high rating (positive feeling) for Tension (4.33)

c. A low rating for Outlook Towards Job (2.98) and Outlook on Tasks (3.14)

d. Medium ratings for Mood (3.90), Enthusiasm (3.44), Health (4.14), and Self-Confidence (3.56).

6. Twenty-three negative comments were recorded on 20 days: ten job-related, seven personal or sickness issues, six pertaining to organizational issues.

The following conclusions can be drawn:

1. Morale was not a function of telecommuting and not a serious issue.

2. Most negative comments were about job issues. The remainder were evenly divided between organizational and personal/sickness issues.

3. Morale is a function of events on job, not on the telecommuting environment.

Identity Conflict, was evident in such daily log comments as “...am I expected to take care of household chores because I’m home all day?” These comments reflect uncertainty and would exist for men as well as women [21]. These concerns appeared resolved after 3–4 weeks and replaced instead with a sense of “comfort”. “...I can relax in jeans... feel clean and neat”.

In Transition I, a sense of isolation occurs “...I’m unimportant at work…” with the loss of the workplace as a location for social interaction [24]. During this period, Morale fluctuated from high to average to high to low. When these findings were presented to management, new communication paths were provided to replace traditional face-to-face interaction:

\* Supervisor visits to the remote work site

\* Teleconferencing

\* An informal IcsD “electronic mail” system. Steady State, was a very productive period during telecommuting. Software development, including high quality documentation, was completed on schedule. Morale was high during the 13 week Steady State period for 70% of the time.

During the Transition II (remote to plant), the telecommuter again exhibited a sense of social isolation. “...no plans for me when I return...what will I be doing...”. This sense of isolation was reflected in a low Morale rating for the three week period.

During the project, the telecommuter was involved in continual self-analysis. This contributed to a reassessment of her career plans. She decided that, she would aim for managerial level responsibilities and advance her professional career rather than raise a family at this stage. This is an interesting and unexpected benefit. The more reflective atmosphere helped her realize that she was not ready for full-time telecommuting. Employees can place themselves on an unrealistic career track simply because they do not have the time to “recall in tranquillity” their original career plans. While the telecommuter’s assessment of her personal and career goals was a serendipitous advantage of telecommuting, the fact remains that telecommuting does provide an excellent opportunity to review what they are doing and how they feel about it.

A financial analysis of the pilot project would be seriously limited because there are several intangible benefits and costs in telecommuting. However, we provide the following analysis of the Textron pilot study to point out some of the factors we considered regarding the financial implications of this telecommuting project.

1. Cost avoidance: A technical person who was planning to leave the job to start a family, decided to remain, \$20,000–\$60,000; a function of relocation costs, agency fees, and training costs.

2. Cost savings: Available work time increased because of a decrease in absences (5.7 hours/month) and non-productive interruptions (1.25 hr./day): \$10,800 per year.

3. Overhead operating costs of in-plant employees vs. a telecommuting employee. For in-plant employees, \$150-\$200 per month. Cost includes office space, utilities, insurance and telephone. For a telecommuter (residing within the local calling area): cost would be \$50-\$100 per month; a savings of \$100 per month. For a telecommuter outside the local calling area, costs would be higher because of increased cost of voice and data communications.

4. The costs for hardware, software, and support for a telecommuter are similar to those for an implant employee.

## 4.2 Projects

A general cost/benefit telcommuting model is not feasible because:

1. The cost of hardware and software is changing rapidly.

2. Expectations for hardware and software are changing.

3. Not all benefits or costs will apply in any given situation. The ideal projects for telecommuting are those with intermediate checkpoints and clearly deliverables. Both the manager and the employee will benefit because the manager can judge the level of success easily and unequivocally and react more effectively to the project's progress.

## 4.3 The Telecommuter

Organizational communications problems are exacerbated when one of the parties is off-site and only visits the workplace at intervals. When the telecommuter has project responsibilities to more people than just an immediate supervisor, as in a matrix organization, then all communication lines must be clearly defined. The lines of communication must be carefully drawn and understood before a telecommuting job is begun.

If telecommuting is to be temporary, then it is essential to have a plan for the return to in-plant work. Suitable projects for the telecommuter must be identified and the manager should be aware that the worker will have grown personally and professionally during the off-site period. A project review meeting, held three to four weeks before the telecommuter returns to the in-plant environment may be one way to help the transition.

The person with the better chance of succeeding at telecommuting will:

\* Have a clear understanding of career direction and personal goals

\* See telecommuting as a stepping stone to these goals or as a desirable end in itself

\* Have an informed commitment to telecommuting, i.e. one which recognizes the nature of the telecommuting environment and the effect on personal work content and style.

A good telecommuting candidate, then, will believe that telecommuting is an effective way to meet his/her specific career goals. And since maturity usually brings changes in an individual's goals, values and motivation, the telecommuter candidate will benefit from guidance in career planning.

## 4.4 Telecommuter's Peers

The telecommuter's peers should know what telecommuting is, why the organization is using it, and what their role is. The in-plant staff must also understand the needs of the telecommuter and the best way to communicate with him/her. When communication is written or phoned, certain components of communication like gesture, body language, and facial expression are lost. In order to insure that this kind of communication is clear, some modification can help. For example, in a telephone communication one party can ask the other to rephrase and then repeat what has been said. It makes the telecommuter's job easier if his in-plant contacts are good communicators. If this is not possible, then the telecommuter must learn to be assertive enough to initiate communications and spur corrective action. It is generally unwise to assign new employees to remote work until they have well-established communications linkages.

## 4.5 Supervision

A good supervisor of telecommuters must have cognitive (work knowledge and skills) and affective (interpersonal) skills. In the cognitive area, the supervisor should be more interested in results than in process, style or method. While all these qualities are expected in the traditional on-site work environment, they assume even greater significance in a telecommuting situation.

On the affective side, the supervisor needs:

\* High general interpersonal skills as well as a sensitivity to special concerns and problems

\* A belief in the telecommuter's desire and ability to perform the job at a high skill level, even without close visual supervision.

It benefits everyone if the employee and supervisor establish a mutually beneficial relationship and clearly understand their roles. A relationship based on a mutual trust and sensitivity allows those involved to understand and respond to work-related or personal problems. A phased implementation of telecommuting gives the supervisor and telecommuter time to adjust to the idea of remote work and resolve technical as well as personal problems.

## 5. Conclusions

This study indicates that technical personnel, trained in software, can be as productive as regular employees. Even greater benefits will be realized from telecommuting when a motivated individual has motivated supervision and well defined tasks.

The telecommuting environment is not effective for in-depth technical evaluations that require interaction and advice on an ad hoc basis.

The reliance of the telecommuter on telecommunication rather than on direct personal contact is a change from the typical office culture. Therefore all participants in a telecommuting project must learn to communicate effectively by telephone, teleconference, and electronic mail. When the telephone is used for communication, it can serve as an affective filter against interruptions; but it can also become a barrier to essential business communication and interaction unless properly used.

Supervisors in a busy office may be so engrossed with on-site demands that they overlook the special circumstances of the telecommuter. Ad hoc communications, such as an informal meeting around a coffee pot are taken for granted in the office, but do not exist for the telecommuter. This isolating factor makes it especially important that supervisors maintain regular contact with telecommuters. This keeps the supervisor informed of progress and problems and the concomitant improvement in the telecommuter's morale means a more productive employee.
