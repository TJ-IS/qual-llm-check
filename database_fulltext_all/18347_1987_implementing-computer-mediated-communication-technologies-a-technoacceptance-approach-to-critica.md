# Implementing Computer-mediated Communication Technologies: A Technoacceptance Approach to Critical Mass Utilization

Kathy Brittain White

University of North Carolina, Greensboro, School of Business and Economics, Greensboro, North Carolina 27412, USA

John Massello

Ciba-Geigy Corporation, Greensboro, North Carolina 27424, USA

Voice messaging systems offer alternative communication tools to alleviate common communication problems such as telephone tag. Many organizations, however, report low utilization of voice messaging systems once they are implemented. This paper describes a utilization strategy used within Ciba-Geigy Corporation to launch and monitor a voice messaging system.

Keywords: Voice messagin systems, Behavioral approach, Technoacceptance, Utilization strategy.

Kathy Brittain White is associate professor of MIS at The University of North Carolina at Greensboro. She has published in Information and Management, MIS Quarterly and Interfaces. She is currently conducting research in the area of organizational issues in technology integration.

John Massello is manager of telecommunications at Ciba-Geigy Corporation. He holds a bachelors degree in management and has extensive experience in dealing with marketing strategies for implementing technology. He speaks frequently at professional conferences and has held the positions of director and vice president of A.I.S.P.

## 1. Introduction

Communication is of paramount importance to organizational functionality and productivity. It takes up as much as 75% of employees' time and often constitutes their primary responsibility. Yet, for many individuals who are frequently out of their offices and attend countless meetings, communication tools commonly available in organizations are inadequate.

Computer-mediated communication technologies have recently been introduced to offer alternative communication tools to alleviate common communication problems. These technologies are often aimed at individuals such as field-based personnel and high level executives who have not traditionally used technology. Recent evidence supports the contention that many organizations implementing communication technology (including teleconferencing and voice message systems) have not achieved widespread utilization. In a survey of 66 Fortune 500 companies, White [3] found firms reporting a low level of usage for both voice messaging and teleconferencing.

Utilization strategies required in computer-mediated communication technologies seem to require very different approaches than are necessary in other technology implementations. For instance, in technologies such as personal computing and wordprocessing, an individual worker can utilize the technology regardless of the utilization by other workers. By contrast, successful utilization of the communication technologies requires participation by a “critical mass” almost from the start of the implementation. If a critical mass is not reached, the most eager members of the group will discontinue use because of the inability to communicate with individuals not using the system. The key success factor then becomes convincing the “critical mass” to use the technology.

The authors contend that implementation and utilization success are highly dependent upon the utilization of a behavior strategy to influence and persuade group members to utilize the communication technology at project inception. This paper describes the technoacceptance approach adopted within Ciba-Geigy Corporation to launch and monitor one of the computer-mediated communication technologies – voice messaging systems. Background information is presented about the Ciba-Geigy Corporation and the decision to pilot voice messaging. The technoacceptance approach used in implementing voice messaging is presented along with a highlight of specific behavioral activities used. Results of the pilots are discussed as well as the formalization of the approach for use in other communication technology implementation efforts.

## 1.1 Background

Ciba-Geigy Corporation is a diversified chemical company engaged principally in the discovery, development, manufacture and marketing of a wide variety of special-purpose chemicals and chemical products. With affiliated companies in 52 countries and sales outlets in approximately 100 countries, Ciba-Geigy group companies employ about 80,000 people worldwide and in 1982 accounted for sales of 6.8 billion dollars.

Employee surveys and interviews are annually done to provide feedback to management concerning employee attitudes and to identify business practices and problems. Increasing numbers of reported problems centered around communication problems. Delays (i.e. telephone tag) were reported in trying to reach corporate personnel causing wasted time for field-based personnel and slowed customer response time. Internally, many people reported problems when trying to call other employees; leaving messages only to find that the class was returned while the called was away.

These communication problems pointed to a technological answer: voice messaging systems. Voice messaging technology has been specifically designed to offer an alternative form of communication media freeing both called and recipient from simultaneous communication. Messages can be sent at the convenience of the caller and are stored when they reach their destination to be read later at the convenience of the recipient. Some commonly cited benefits hit at the very essence of the communication problems reported by personnel. These included:

a. reduction of "telephone" tag

b. faster and more convenient communication

c. increased information communication among peers

d. elimination of time zone problems, and

e. fewer work interruptions [1].

Such benefits from voice messaging systems, however, can only be realized if the technology is utilized once it is implemented. A task force was appointed to investigate the technical and behavioral feasibility of implementing a voice messaging system at Ciba-Geigy.

## 1.2 Investigation

The task force conducted their investigation as a two-phased approach. One phase was evaluating features of the various voice messaging system available and discussing with vendors the technical requirements of Ciba-Geigy. Five vendors were contacted and their products were evaluated. The system marketed by vmx Corporation of Richardson, Texas was selected based on its network capabilities, ease of use, advanced features and its ability to be upgraded.

The other phase was on-site visits with other organizations that had implemented the voice messaging technology to assess the approach used to market the implementation and utilization of the technology. The organizations contacted reported varying levels of success. The technical functionality of the systems was reported as excellent; however, in many of the organizations the systems were vastly under-utilized. These organizations provided several reasons which could all be categorized as employee resistance. No guidelines could be located for preparing the organization to accept and utilize this communication technology. It became clear after this initial investigation that Ciba-Geigy was faced with a situation where voice messaging systems were clearly the technical answer for an identified business problem; yet, it seemed that full utilization by employees would require developing a strategy to change communication habits and behaviors in a very short period of time. Management decided to implement a three month pilot and determine employee reactions.

## 2. Pilot Project

Previous work on voice mail utilization had indicated successes most often occur where it had been implemented among a group of people:

\- who have a frequent need to communicate;

\- when all communications are on the system; and

\- who travel often or are otherwise frequently away from their offices [1].

The task force believe that by identifying groups meeting the above criteria, the motivation of the group to use the system would encourage the positive outcome of the three month project.

Two groups were selected within Ciba-Geigy Corporation meeting the above criteria to participate in the three month voice messaging pilot. One pilot group contained field based sales personnel with a requirement to communicate frequently with the home office. This group was the sales group of the Ciba-Geigy Dyestuffs and Chemicals Division located in Greensboro, North Carolina (hereafter referred to as Greensboro). The second group consisted of corporate managers with frequent travel requirements located in Ardsley, New York (hereafter referred to as Ardsley).

A project coordinator was selected for each site. The project coordinator of the Greensboro pilot is the co-author of this paper and at this point, the implementation strategies are from the perspective of the Greensboro project coordinator. Although contact with the other coordinator was maintained, many of the behavioral concepts and strategies are distinct to the Greensboro site. These distinctions will be further identified in the results section.

## 2.1 Theoretical Background

The Greensboro pilot project coordinator had a background in marketing/organizational behavior and turned to the behavioral sciences for assistance in developing an implementation plan for use with the Greensboro participants. A behavioral schema called the technoacceptance cycle was used as a way to categorize the behavioral processes involved in adopting and utilizing technology [2]. The basic premise of the technoacceptance cycle is that the most important part of the automation process is not technical but an “inner process” of automation that people and organizations go through. McDonald states there is a predictable process with a sequence of reactions that is fairly stable. Basically, the concept is that the technical phases required for systems development and operation have parallel behavioral phases that have rarely been recognized and if so, little has been done to prepare organizations for smooth progression through these stages.

The initial period occurs when workers first hear that a technological change may occur. It can be a vague sense of uneasiness that they will be unable to adapt or learn the new way or initial interest in the technology. Regardless of the emotion during this period, it is usually based on vague assumptions without real understanding of the new technology. McDonald labels this phase “feeling”. When the new technology is actually implemented, employees enter what is called a “thinking” phase characterized by a search for support in using the new technology. This stage ends with an emotion that might range from frustration to that of extreme interest depending upon the actual success or failure in utilizing the technology. The third state is the “behavior” phase and is marked by the actions of the individuals in using the technology. Three distinct patterns of behavior emerge during this time: rejection or non-usage of the system, resignation and little usage of the system, to acceptance and full usage of the technology.

The recognition of these distinct technoacceptance phases laid the groundwork for the behavioral utilization strategies developed in piloting voice messaging in Greensboro. The specific action plans that were identified and utilized during the voice messaging implementation are described in the next section. Their relationship to the technoacceptance stages are also discussed.

## 2.2 Technoacceptance Approach

The Greensboro coordinator developed a behavioral implementation plan using a s a base the phases identified by McDonald. As an initial effort to coincide with the feeling phase, a publicity campaign was launched. The main objective of this campaign was to develop an organizational awareness of voice messaging technology as well as to focus attention on the upcoming pilot project. During the campaign several carefully selected voice messaging articles were distributed to pilot participants, management, and other key individuals within the organization. An informational news letter was distributed within the division in an effort to publicize the effort and the participants in the pilot project.

Orientation sessions were held with individuals selected to participate at the inception of the project. The primary focus of these sessions were the business needs that were driving the voice messaging pilot project. Discussing the frustrations with current communication habits helped to build a dissatisfaction with the status quo and prepare for the change in the communication tools of the participants.

These orientation sessions consisted of carefully selected small groups. The coordinator tried to include participants that were very excited about the pilot project in every group. Verbal and non-verbal responses and comments made during the sessions were carefully noted. Individuals expressing concern about the pilot were visited to determine if they could be assured that the project would assist them in carrying out their job responsibilities.

Structured training sessions were held once the vmx system was activated at both sites to provide support in using the technology. Small group training sessions were offered to these participants that included demonstrations and hands-on training. The written material provided by the vendor was sufficiently easy to understand so no additional training materials were developed. This is one of the activities used to address the participants' reactions during the thinking phase of the technoacceptance cycle.

After one month, the participants were brought together for feedback sessions. During these sessions, individual success stories were related. Certain sales personnel who had used the system to deliver information to the customer in a more timely fashion than had previously been possible were asked to relate the experience to the group. Using the redirect message capabilities of VMX, success stories were forwarded to other pilot parparticipants as well as managers that would decide whether to continue the project once the three month pilot was completed. These activities were to determine the behavior of the participants and to minimize frustration that may emerge in using new technology.

Assessment of VMX utilization was also monitored. Monthly reports provided by the vendor were used to determine non-usage by pilot participants. These individuals were personally visited to determine if there were problems in using the system. Individual sessions were held if there were problems. These monitoring activities assisted in minimizing participants' rejection or non-usage of the system characterized in the behavior stage of the technoacceptance cycle.

Other potential users were identified by visiting work groups within the division. Invitations were issued to these individuals to attend the feedback sessions and they were provided with information on the vmx pilot.

## 3. Results

At the end of the three month pilot, users were surveyed to determine their responses to the voice messaging system. Fig. 1 graphically summarizes the results of the questionnaire for both pilot groups. Five benefits were reported from both groups. Responses from the participants at the two pilot locations will be discussed separately.

In examining the responses from the Greensboro division, 90% of the participants stated that the system was easy to use. The communication problems that had been identified and led to the installation of the voice messaging system were clearly seen as quite improved. Responses indicated that voice messaging had indeed improved communication, reduced telephone tag, reduced the number of notes that were taken and also reduced telephone conversation time. On each of these communication issues, over 70% of the Greensboro group perceived a benefit from having the voice messaging system in place.

In the Ardsley project, the same five issues were reported as benefits although the percentages were much lower. The communication issues that had originally suggested the installation of the system were not seen as substantially improved by the use of the system. As noted in Fig. 1, improved communication was seen as a benefit by 40% of the participants. Reduced telephone tag was seen as a benefit by only 30% of the participants. The other two reported benefits were noted only by a very small percentage of the participants.

Pilot participation was also a key factor that separated the two pilot groups. During the three month pilot, in the Greensboro division, the number of participants grew from 40 to 105 while participation in Ardsley remained at 50 participants. At the end of the six months, Ardsley participation declined while another 100 participants were added in Greensboro.

The authors are tempted to completely credit the training approach used in Greensboro as a way to account for such wide variations in the voice messaging implementation at the two pilot sites. In an attempt to be unbiased in the assessment, there were other differences that could have impacted the behavior of the two pilot groups.

While the individuals participating in the pilot study were in the same type of work (field based sales), the culture and environment of the two divisions were quite different. The parent company, where voice messaging was unsuccessful, had provided much of the mainframe computing for the other divisions. The Greensboro division had relied heavily on personal computers to develop systems. When the Greensboro division acquired a mainframe computer in 1982, they became the front runner in integration efforts because of the proliferation of personal computers already on site. In comparing the two environments, the parent company was heavily steeped in traditional mainframe computing and had done little experimentation extensively with personal computers. As a result, the Greensboro participants may have been more accustomed to using technology and had less resistance to the voice messaging concept than their Ardsley counterparts.

The physical location of the two facilities may have influenced the outcome of the project. The participants at the Ardsley site reside in the same building in close proximity to other individuals within the same work group. They rely heavily on informal communication, frequently walking down the hall rather than using memos or the telephone. Many of the Greensboro participants reside in different facilities and were already using the telephone or memos frequently for internal communications.

## 4. Formalization of Voice Messaging Implementation

In evaluating the activities that were conducted during the pilot project, seven distinct phases were identified that related to the technoacceptance activities used during the pilot. These seven phases used as a basis the technoacceptance cycle identified by McDonald but incorporated the administrative activities (such as selection of project manager) that were a part of the voice mail implementation. These authors believe these were critical for progressing through the technoacceptance phases and establishing high utilization of voice messaging at the Greensboro site. Although specific activities were initially planned, the formalization of the phases into the technoacceptance model occurred in evaluating the success of the Greensboro pilot and at the request of top management. ## 5. Conclusions

In retrospect, all participants wish a more comprehensive data gathering had been conducted to be able to provide more insight into the failed implementation. But as is often true in many real-world situations, this was simply not done because the disparity between the two pilot implementations was unexpected. In addition, the project manager in charge of the failed VMX implementation left the company so further information is unavailable. However, top management was sufficiently convinced by the results of the behavioral approach that they requested the development of the formal model that is shown in Exhibit A. It is currently being utilized by a teleconferencing technology team to rethink teleconferencing utilization. The technoacceptance model has been incorporated into the technology strategic plan that has been recently developed to guide the corporation in technology investments.

Future technology developments such as voice/data integration will further impact workers that have resisted technology. A challenge to organizations is to insure utilization of the technology that is deemed critical for strategic opportunities. The technoacceptance model is part of a behavioral portfolio that Ciba-Geigy Corporation believes must be developed to prepare the entire work force to fully utilize technology.
