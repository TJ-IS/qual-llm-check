# The Evaluation of DSS Groups \*

Hugh J. Watson

College of Business Administration, Department of Management, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

Tim Boyd-Wilson

Case-Western Reserve University, 2040 Adelbert, Cleveland, OH 44106, USA

and

Simha R. Magal

Bowling Green State University, Bowling Green, OH 43403, USA

DSS research has largely ignored evaluation procedures for organizational units which support DSS work. This article reports on a study to explore evaluation variables and procedures for DSS groups. It suggests the importance of multiple perspectives in evaluation and identifies evaluation variables relevant to these perspectives. Recommendations are made for evaluating DSS groups by matching evaluation variables with appropriate evaluation techniques.

Keywords: Decision support systems, DSS groups, DSS group evaluation

## 1. Introduction

A growing number of managers, professionals, and other organizational personnel are using decision support systems (DSS) to provide information to support semistructured and unstructured decision making. A number of factors have contributed to this development: computer hardware advances (PCs, data communications capabilities), computer software products (DSS generators, graphics packages), a desire for better information, competitive pressures, and an improved understanding of how computer technology can be used to support decision making. The result has been an increase in decision-making effectiveness in many organizations.

DSS activity in an organization can begin in a variety of ways. A manager may use Lotus 1-2-3 on a PC to explore a pricing decision. A financial group may use IFPS on a timesharing network to develop a corporate planning model or a marketing research group may use EXPRESS to analyze proposed marketing plans.

At some point, most organizations recognize the need to plan, support, and control DSS activities. This may include evaluating software products, supplying consulting services for users who are developing DSS applications, or providing access to data which reside on mainframe computers. The responsibility for activities such as these may be centralized or distributed. A single organizational unit may be responsible for planning, supporting, and controlling all DSS activities or responsibility for it may be spread over a number of organizational units such as Financial Planning, OR/MS, and Information Services. Whatever the organizational arrangements, these activities need to be carefully thought out, organization units responsible for their execution identified, and appropriate evaluation procedures put in place.

Evaluation procedures for DSS have focused almost exclusively on the various levels of DSS technology (i.e., specific DSS, DSS generators, and DSS tools). There is a growing body of knowledge of how specific decision support systems should be evaluated $[14,15,27]$ . The same is also true of DSS generators and tools $[20]$ . There is a much smaller body of knowledge, however, on other aspects of DSS evaluation.

A study was performed to explore evaluation variables and procedures for DSS groups. A DSS group is an organizational unit which plans, supports, and controls DSS activities as an important part of its overall organizational responsibilities. The study provides recommendations for evaluating DSS groups. It also provides a basis for a self assessment of DSS group performance.

## 2. The DSS group

A DSS group can have a variety of organizational origins. Sprague and Carlson [27] identify several possibilities:

\- A special purpose team of applications systems analysts

• A re-oriented tools group

• A management science or operations research group

• The planning department

\- A staff analysis group from one of the functional areas

\- A newly created organizational unit to support DSS activities

Some organizations are using yet another alternative:

• The information center

It is also likely that other possibilities exist currently or will exist in the future.

The organizational unit which begins DSS work may or may not remain as the focal point for DSS activities. In one company, DSS activity began in a Planning department. A short time later, the company created a five person Decision Support Systems department. DSS applications continued to be developed by the planning group for its own needs, but primary DSS responsibilities were assumed by Decision Support Systems.

With the exception of the situation where an organizational unit is formally charged with the responsibility for being the DSS group, most people in these units do not view themselves as a DSS group. They think of themselves as corporate planners, management scientists, production planners, etc. In fact, they may be the organization's DSS group only in that they assume more DSS responsibilities than anyone else.

There is no fundamental problem with not having a formally chartered DSS group, but it does increase the likelihood that important DSS activities fail to receive appropriate attention. As in many other areas, a variety of organizational structures can be used as long as appropriate authority and responsibility relationships are established.

Whether DSS responsibilities are centralized or distributed, they none-the-less exist and require attention. The responsibilities cover a variety of areas. ## 3. Literature Review

Research on the effectiveness of organizations and organizational units is fragmented and there is no widely accepted framework or model for evaluating organizational effectiveness. Many models have been proposed $[5,6,21,25]$ . The earliest were univariate, measuring effectiveness in terms of some “ultimate” criterion, such as productivity or satisfaction $[29]$ . In the more recent multivariate models, there is a lack of consensus as to what constitutes a useful and valid set of effectiveness measures $[29]$ .

One of the major approaches to evaluating organizational effectiveness is to include the perspectives of multiple, strategic constituents; that is, those individuals who have a stake in the organizational unit's performance [5,6]. Several researchers have suggested the appropriateness of multiple perspectives in information systems research. Hamilton and Chervany [13] identify the perspectives of users, builders, and management as being particularly relevant. Most research has centered on the user group [2,11,17], but the management group also has been included [14].

DSS evaluation research has focused primarily on specific DSS. A number of methods have been suggested or used, including value analysis $[15]$ , event logging $[27]$ , attitude surveys $[3]$ , cognitive testing $[28]$ , rating and weighting $[27]$ , systems measurement $[10]$ , systems analysis $[24]$ , and cost/benefit analysis $[30]$ . These approaches are not mutually exclusive and each is potentially useful.

A variety of DSS effectiveness measures have been used, the most common one being user satisfaction $[9,23]$ . Sprague and Carlson $[27]$ suggest productivity, process, perception, and product measures with a number of submeasures within each category. Hamilton and Chervany $[13]$ suggest three levels of effectiveness: the information and support provided, the use process and user performance, and organizational performance, with specific performance measures for each level. Other researchers have suggested or used the impact on organizational goals and performance [7], the usage of information [16], communication between users and builders [26], meeting user needs [9], and management's perception of the value of information provided [1,26]. In addition, a number of organizational factors such as the decision context, the level of task interdependence, and the level of task constraints have been investigated in regard to their role in DSS success [23].

## 4. The Study Method

A series of in-depth interviews was conducted to gather data on how to evaluate DSS groups. Four companies in the Atlanta, Georgia area participated in the study: Bell South, Gold Kist, Coca-Cola, and Lockheed-Georgia. This phase revealed that three perspectives need to be considered in evaluating DSS groups: management, users, and DSS group members. The people interviewed were asked to indicate the variables that were important from the perspective that they represented. Through an iterative process, variables important in the evaluation of DSS groups were added, and consolidated until an agreed upon list was developed for each perspective. Also identified was a list of organizational considerations (e.g., centralized or decentralized DSS support) which influence the importance of various variables. Insights were also gained about evaluation methods and procedures which could be used.

In the study's second phase, the list of variables, organizational considerations, and evaluation methods and procedures were presented to managers and DSS groups members in three additional companies: Planning Resources, Smith Kline & French Laboratories, and Northwest Industries. They provided minor suggestions for change which were incorporated in the evaluation approach for DSS groups which is presented here.

The study findings are believed to be applicable to a variety of organizations. There was consistency in the data collected in the various organizations. The organizations studied represent a variety of industries: communications, agri-business, consumer products, defense, consulting, pharmaceutics, and manufacturing. The focal point for DSS activity was located in different areas: financial planning, economic research, a separate DSS group, a specialized information support group, and outside consulting.

## 5. Findings

In none of the firms studied was there a formal procedure for the evaluation of the DSS group. However, some evaluation must occur in these and other organizations. DSS groups appear and disappear. They have budgets which are enlarged and cut back. Their responsibilities narrow or widen over time. Their members are hired, fired, promoted, and transferred. It appears that evaluation processes are mostly intuitive.

## 5.1. Evaluation Variables

Three perspectives need to be considered when evaluating the performance of DSS groups: management, users, and the DSS group itself. Each has unique as well as common concerns. It may be that management are users of a DSS or that users are members of a group which develops a specific DSS. This does not invalidate distinctions between the perspectives, although it may affect the nature and process of evaluation. The list of variables developed for the three perspectives appears in Table 2. Most are self-explanatory and unexceptional. As a group, they relate to all of the DSS group responsibilities identified in Table 1. The variables are concerned with aspects of group efficiency and effectiveness, with group dynamics, with return on investment, with the quality of group communication with users and management, and with other factors which affect user satisfaction [4]. User satisfaction is perhaps the most common measure of DSS effectiveness [9,22] and so it is not surprising that it appears to play a large role in the evaluation of DSS groups.

Each organization must decide which variables are most appropriate for its situation and associate the variables with one or more perspectives. It is important to recognize that many variables (e.g., the ability to anticipate future user needs (\#4)) are relevant for more than one or even for all of the perspectives. The association of variables with perspectives is likely to vary among organizations because of organizational considerations.

## 5.2. Fuzzy Set Representation

The concepts and notation of fuzzy set theory are useful in representing the relationships between the three perspectives and the list of variables. A fuzzy set is a set whose membership is unclear. The concept of a fuzzy set [8] can be explained simply by comparison with that of an ordinary set. If X is a set of objects (the universe) whose generic elements are denoted by x, then membership in an ordinary subset A of X may be viewed as a characteristic function $mu_{A}$ from X to [0,1] (the valuation set) such that

$$
\mathrm{mu} _ {A} (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if } x \text { is   an   element   of } A \\ 0 & \text { if } x \text { is   not   an   element   of } A \end{array} \right.
$$

Thus an element $x$ is either a member of $A$ or it is not.

By contrast, a fuzzy set has grades of membership; its valuation set is the real interval [0,1]. The closer the value of $\mathrm{mu}_{\mathrm{A}}(x)$ to 1, the more strongly x “belongs” to A.

The classic example of a fuzzy set is the “set of tall men.” Some men are definitely tall, some men are definitely not. All can be assigned 1s and 0s as grades of membership. But many men are neither. They are quite tall, or nearly tall, and so on. Their grades of membership reflect an ordering of the objects in the universe.

It appears that fuzziness is characteristic of human perception and cognition. Zadeh, the founder of the field of fuzzy systems theory, suggested that the human ability to summarize information “into labels of fuzzy sets which bear an approximate relation to the primary data” is an important facet of thought [31]. Almost all linguistic descriptions are fuzzy.

In evaluating DSS groups, each set of variables important to one of the three perspectives may be viewed as a fuzzy subset of the universe listed in Table 2. Each variable will be associated with a grade of membership in each of the three fuzzy subsets. A variable with a grade of membership greater than 0 in more than one set is thus regarded as having some importance from more than one perspective.

At this stage in the research, no attempt has been made to assess grades of membership more finely than “0” or “greater than 0.” The fuzzy sets and systems literature does suggest several approaches to this problem, however [8].

The appropriate classifications or grades of membership of variables for a particular DSS group appear to be affected by many factors. Some will affect each of the three perspectives, while others may be specific to a particular perspective.

## 5.3. Organizational Considerations

The study identified a number of organizational considerations which influence the significance and relevance of the individual variables listed in Table 2.

The placement and responsibilities of the DSS group are important organizational considerations. A group which is directly responsible to the technically sophisticated head of Information Systems may be expected to be evaluated differently from one which is part of a corporate planning staff, or one which is part of a user department. Groups which are formed for single projects are quite different from those formed with long-term goals.

The composition of the group will affect its evaluation. Groups with a number of members from users or management may be expected to be evaluated less formally than those with specialized staff.

The organizational history of the group may affect its evaluation. Some groups spring from OR/MS, some from planning groups, some from users, and some from Information Systems. The evaluation practices applied to the parent may carry over to the evaluation of the child.

Another organizational consideration is the level of senior management support for and commitment to the DSS concept. This may affect the stringency and formality of the evaluation applied to the DSS group, and the ease with which management can be convinced of the group's value. A group formed at the behest of an organizational champion to whom it remains responsible may be expected to be evaluated in a different manner from one which evolves into a corporate resource as it assumes more DSS responsibilities.

A further organizational consideration, arguably the most important, may be characterized as the phase of evolution of DSS efforts in the organization. McFarlan, McKenney, and Pyburn [18] have identified four phases of technology assimilation in the evolution of information systems technology: identification and initial investment, experimentation and learning, control, and widespread technology transfer. The appropriateness of this framework for DSS has not been established, but the phases have intuitive appeal. As the use of DSS within an organization matures, any change in emphasis from innovation through technology transfer will be reflected in the responsibilities of the DSS group and then in its evaluation.

Since the development of specific DSS is often the group's major responsibility, the evaluation of any specific DSS created by the group will naturally affect the evaluation of the group itself. Consequently, the nature of the specific DSS developed by a group is thus another factor affecting its evaluation. For example, it may be that an institutional DSS will be evaluated differently from an ad hoc DSS. DSS designed for use directly by management may be evaluated differently from those used through intermediaries or users at lower organizational levels.

One specific factor is the extent to which members of the DSS group have a professional rather than an organizational commitment. Groups with a strong organizational commitment may be expected to evaluate themselves in ways more closely related to those of management and users than those whose members' primary commitment is to their profession.

## 6. Recommendations for Evaluating DSS Groups

Based on our study, we have developed a recommended approach for the evaluation of DSS groups. Its efficacy, however, will be shown only after it is employed successfully in a variety of organizations.

The evaluation process typically will be initiated at intervals by the executive to whom the DSS group is responsible, in accord with the practices of the parent organization. Our study suggests that the perspectives of management, users, and the DSS group itself need to be considered in a comprehensive evaluation. Given the overlap between perspectives, a single evaluation seems feasible and it may be performed best by a team representing the three perspectives. The team may be composed of the initiating executive, one or more user representatives, and the DSS group leader. A senior representative from the information systems function and a general management representative from the information systems executive steering committee may be useful additions. The precise composition of the evaluation team is contingent on organizational structure, the scope and evolutionary phase of the organization's DSS efforts, and the group's responsibilities. Sprague and Carlson [27] note that any evaluation can be described in terms of an initiating system whose impact is to be evaluated and a target system upon which the impact is to be measured. For our purpose, the DSS group and its products and services form the initiating system. However, the target system varies with the chosen perspective. For managers, the target may be organizational performance as influenced by decisions made using the DSS. For users, the target may be the decision-making process and its outcomes. The DSS group's target is more difficult to pinpoint, since it will depend on the composition, history, and orientation of group membership. It may include, to a greater or lesser extent: the group itself, its products and services, and the target systems of management and users.

Even though evaluation variables may overlap the different perspectives, they will have distinct target systems; this must be retained in the process and used in the interpretation of the evaluation. The precise composition of the list of variables (Table 2) and those important to each perspective can be determined only in a particular case. Each evaluation team will have to decide which variables to include in which perspectives.

There remains the task of evaluating the group's performance with respect to the selected variables. We have made an initial attempt to match variables with appropriate evaluation techniques. The results appear in Table 3. It must be noted that this matching is not prescriptive. An evaluation group must consider its parent organization's evaluation practices, the nature of the DSS group and its responsibilities, and its own strengths in developing a comprehensive evaluation approach.

Some of the variables can be observed more or less directly. The ability to provide service within budgetary constraints (\#7) is an example. Event logging is a useful evaluation technique when quantitative measures are unavailable and when a time series of effects is of interest [27]. Here, event logging involves recording events relevant to aspects of DSS group activity. It may, for example, be suitable for evaluating opportunities for group members to advance professionally (\#21). An attitude survey, though complex to develop, is useful for measures of perceptions. It is therefore appropriate for a large number of the variables, for example those concerned with relationships (\#16, \#17, \#18). Value analysis is perhaps the simplest and most appropriate technique for evaluating the DSS group's products [15].

## 7. Summary

DSS activity is becoming increasingly common in organizations. Over time, it becomes apparent that one or more organizational units must assume responsibility for planning, supporting, and controlling DSS work. The specific identity of this group(s) varies from organization to organization.

Our study shows that groups with significant DSS responsibilities tend to be evaluated informally in regard to how well they carry out their responsibilities. Whether this evaluation continues to be performed informally or not, there are three evaluation perspectives involved: those of management, users, and the DSS group itself. Twenty-two evaluation variables have been identified as relevant to one or more of these perspectives. They relate to aspects of group efficiency and effectiveness, to group dynamics, to return on investment, to the quality of group communication with users and management, and to other factors which affect user satisfaction. Organizational considerations have also been identified which influence the importance of the evaluation variables to each perspective. Fuzzy set theory can be used to conceptualize the importance of each variable to each perspective.

A formal DSS group evaluation procedure has been proposed. It calls for a team approach with representatives from management, users, and the DSS group. The precise composition of the evaluation team will vary from organization to organization. The evaluation variables would be investigated by the team for the perspectives which apply for each variable. Observation event logging, attitude surveys, and value analysis are appropriate for measuring one or more of the variables.
