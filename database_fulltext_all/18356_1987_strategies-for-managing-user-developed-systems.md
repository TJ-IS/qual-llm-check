# Strategies for Managing User Developed Systems

Mehdi Beheshtian

Loyola University of Chicago 820 N Michigan Ave Chicago IL 60611 USA

and

Paul D Van Wert

Anheuser-Busch Corp One Busch Place St Louis MO 63118 USA

The arrival of microcomputers user-friendly languages and improved telecommunications have made information more directly accessible to end-users. This increased accessibility has caused an evolution from traditional systems development done solely by MIS professionals to a growing amount of development by end-users. User system development presents new management challenges for both MIS and user departments. These challenges are not different in kind from problems encountered by MIS in the past, but they are different in magnitude. Unless companies educate users and manage user development they will endure the same slow evolution. Only this time the problems will be multiplied manyfold by the sheer number of users and will be further exaggerated by users lack of technical expertise. This paper examines the conflicts created by the emergence of user development. It focuses on the symptoms of mismanagement and suggests key areas to be addressed by an effective management strategy. The evolution of user computing is described in terms of Nolan's stage theory. These issues are illustrated through a case study of policies, practices and attitudes toward user system development at a steel company.

Keywords End-User Computing Fourth Generation Languages Information Utility Management of Information Systems MIS Nolan's Stage Theory System Development User Developed Systems

## 1 Introduction

In the last decade, there has been an explosion in the demand for information services. Some authors attribute this boom to the maturation of MIS departments and their users. Others cite technological advances like microcomputers, fourth generation languages, and improved telecommunications, that make information more directly accessible to users

MIS departments are not satisfying this demand MIS managers speak of growing backlogs, including a “hidden backlog” of systems that may never be scheduled The sheer magnitude of the information shortage will virtually assure a larger contribution by users in future

Several years ago, the term “user computing” entered the vernacular of information systems literature. Recently, authors have begun to use terms like “user programming” and “user development.” This progression of terms indicates an awareness of the growing sophistication of end-users

The role of users in data processing presents a new set of management challenges today Top management will no longer be able to delegate information management solely to the MIS department This paper examines the effects of this transition on user departments, the MIS department, and the company in general

## 2 Background

A number of articles discussing the management of user system development have appeared recently. As early as 1979, McLean [18] discussed “the notion that end users can play an important part in the development of many types of applications”

In 1980 Zachman [24] suggested that the boom in user development activity might be connected with companies' entry into Nolan's stages three and four [20] He pointed out that in these stages, management begins to want to manipulate decision support information

Edelman [11] predicted a split between the “information utility” (the technicians), and an application group, which could easily be transferred to user departments Articles [5] and [6] further explore the idea of system development in user departments

It was not until 1983 that managing end-user development became widely discussed Gulden and Arkush [14] identified five elements needed to develop management strategies [4] and [7] examined several aspects of managing user development, they warned, “end user computing in the not-distant future probably will overwhelm the information systems department – unless the department is prepared for it”

Other articles have since appeared discussing issues related to the topic Olsen & Ives on chargeback systems [21], Shomenta et al on selecting development methods [23], and Rivard and

Huff on MIS departments [22]

Martin wrote the first textbook [17] devoted solely to the topic Other MIS texts, like [8] and [16], have begun to include chapters on it. Recently end-user development has even become a frequent topic in popular publications, e.g., [1] and [25]

In 1984, EDP Analyzer, discussed the long range management of user system development [3], the editors conclude, “cope (with end user computing) by way of aiming at a higher level goal, not just ‘making it work’” “tying end user computing more closely to the needs of the business” by perceiving it “as a tool to help develop innovative products and services”

However, little has been written about managing the movement from traditional development to an environment in which users develop their own systems

## 3 User Developed Systems vs Traditional Systems Development

For years, systems have been developed by specialized technicians with guidance from the eventual user. With the arrival of microcomputers and fourth-generation programming languages, end-users are able to develop some of their own systems

This new capability has the potential of reducing the tension between users and MIS departments. Yet attitudes are exacerbating the conflict MIS departments are reluctant to relax their control over system development, have improved their ability to plan and manage system development through increased control and integration. But many users are overconfident about their ability to satisfy their own needs, they have enjoyed the euphoria of having a system successfully solve a problem that was languishing in the MIS department's backlog and believe they no longer need the MIS department. They feel they have already succeeded at managing systems development in their department, but they have not had to consider backup, recovery, security, maintenance, staffing, capacity, communications, or a myriad of other issues. Most have little familiarity with the broader-scope and longer-range issues involved in effective systems management

This has produced undesirable effects in many companies MIS departments have tried to discourage user development by refusing to support microcomputers and user-friendly languages. Yet many users have sidestepped these restrictions by disguising expenditures for “bootleg” microcomputers, managers with enough power have simply forced a showdown with the MIS department, some even use their home computers for data they cannot process at work. Although intended to increase control, these actions have diminished it

In addition, this adversarial relationship severely limits companies' ability to clarify future directions Coordination between MIS department and users, and even among various user departments is difficult. The result may be duplication of effort and neglect of needs

These conflicts also cloud the role of information centers and user groups. Information centers are particularly vulnerable if (as is frequently the case) they report to MIS department management

In such conditions, the quality of user developed systems is likely to improve very slowly Nolan described a maturation process through stages of initiation growth, control, integration, and data orientation. Most MIS departments have by now progressed into the later stages. Yet users are still in the early stages. Without recognition of this difference and cooperation between MIS and users the experience of the MIS department will not be passed on

Companies are at a crucial stage in their management of information systems. Ignoring or resisting the growth of user development is a losing battle. If the change is not managed, possible excesses and failures in the organization could devastate its management of the information resources

## 4 User Development at a Steel Company a Case Study

## 41 Description of the Company

Perhaps the best evidence that user development is accepted today is provided by conservative companies, such as steel producers confronting these issues Top managers user department managers, MIS managers and system users at a Midwestern steel company recently participated in a study of their user-development practices and attitudes The company has several thousand employees, about 20% salaried. Recently, the company has become more cost-conscious and market-driven due to increased competition

The company uses IBM mainframe computers in a centralized, off-site computer center. All data processing costs are included in the MIS department's budget, user departments are not charged back for theirs. Application systems have been developed in most major functional areas. Through the 1970's, most MIS effort was spent upgrading existing operational systems. Early growth had given way to a somewhat complacent strategy aimed at maintaining existing capabilities

About three years ago, however, the corporation committed itself to modernizing its computer technology. It began instituting more standardization and control measures, undertook a BSP (Business Systems Planning) analysis of its long-range information needs, and selected the IMS database management system as its preferred tool for new system development

These actions represent movement into Nolan's third and fourth stages (Control and Integration) Competitive pressure has forced the company to move rapidly forward on several fronts simultaneously. While these efforts have generally been successful, the rapid pace of change has greatly complicated management of the information systems

## 42 User Developed Systems

Seven years ago the company purchased a simple monitor system to allow end-users to submit batch jobs and write programs in interactive BASIC and FORTRAN. Although there was some activity, very little development took place because the MIS department did not provide much training or support. Two years ago, the MIS management decided to increase support for end-user computing. They installed TSO/ISPF and a fourth-generation language and established an information center within the MIS department. At the time of this study, the information center had formally trained about 25% of the salaried workforce in simple database queries. About 20% of these also received training in basic database design and updating

The study participants responded to a series of questions about their attitudes toward the management and practice of system development Respondents represented a cross-section of departments, organizational levels, and computer experience. The following characterizations are drawn from responses, observation, and policy statements

Although there is no formal policy, company management considers the MIS department responsible for managing and satisfying all information processing needs. MIS managers concentrate heavily on the planning, development and maintenance of large IMS/COBOL systems. System development by end-users is considered peripheral to the information management. MIS managers have encouraged end users computing primarily to relieve pressure on their department

This is illustrated in several ways. First MIS management feels it is important that user-developed and MIS systems remain at arm's length Company policy segregates “production” from “user developed” files. End-users can only execute queries against production data by having it extracted into an independent file

In addition, MIS management considers fourth-generation languages to be primarily for end-users. Working programs developed using a fourth-generation language have been rewritten in COBOL, because MIS management feels that MIS staff cannot maintain the original program a reluctance perhaps natural among programmers and managers devoted to procedural programming. Nevertheless, the MIS department risks falling behind their users in the use of state-of-the-art system development technology. This could deny MIS developers a promising source of increased productivity

This causes confusion about the proper role of the information center. Its charter (from an unpublished company document) lists the information center's missions as

“ to encourage our user community to satisfy their own information needs by providing education, consultation, problem resolution and cost-effective environmental support ”

Yet MIS management frequently expects the information center staff to develop systems for users under urgent request. In fact, any request which is appropriate for development of a simple system with a fourth-generation language is routinely assigned to the information center. These activities dilute the user support of the information center

In addition to assigning such tasks, the MIS director delegated the management of user computing to the information center. Thus there is no high-level source of coordination, planning, or control for user development. Information center analysts make recommendations, but the advice is only occasionally taken by users under pressure to develop programs quickly. When users develop systems and then move on, maintenance of the systems is haphazard. Users plead with the MIS department for rescue, and the information center is asked to intervene

A final implication is that MIS managers' feel that all complex and essential systems should be developed by MIS analysts and programmers. The director feels that systems involving more than one department should not be developed using fourth-generation languages, because of their limited backup, recovery, integrity, and security. In a survey of attitudes MIS management chose "number of departments involved" as the most important issue in deciding whether a system was appropriate for user development

MIS managers expressed skepticism that their responsibility for information systems would be reduced by user development. Yet they believed that it reduces their control without a narrowing of responsibility. This has created apprehension about the consequences of “turning users loose on the computer” MIS management expressed particular concern about the effect of user computing on increase in computer resource consumption. Without a charge-back scheme or a cost/benefit justification standard they see nothing restraining users from misusing resources

Another concern is the users' lack of knowledge and experience MIS managers believe that users are more likely to have accuracy, integrity and maintenance problems and that MIS will be expected to solve them. This concern is not limited to technical aspects of development. Departments often assign the employee with the most available time rather than the most experience or knowledge. Hence, a poorly designed, inflexible system that breaks down easily, fails to handle unusual circumstances, or gives misleading information

In contrast to MIS concerns, users stressed urgency as the most important issue in deciding whether a user should develop a system When surveyed, many users expressed impatience with the sluggishness of MIS's responses. This frustration elicited different responses among users. Employees in operating departments generally felt they would be able to use simply query languages but not to develop their own systems. But staff employees felt they were more capable of developing high quality, responsive systems than the MIS department. Nearly all users felt they would develop simple systems that remain a low priority in the MIS backlog

Nearly all user employees viewed getting systems into operation as the most important part of the development process. All of the training from MIS has been in the technical aspects of how to obtain the desired results. Most users felt this was appropriate since “getting programs to work is the hardest part of the whole process.” Few users considered planning, controls, standards or documentation as important parts of system development. Consequently, most user system development has begun with database description and ended with programming

Although the idea in promoting user computing was to make most users self-sufficient, few fourth-generation tools actually help to do this, but “Local heros” have evolved Information needs especially requiring difficult programming, are typically met by these few rather than the user needing the information. Thus instead of being at the mercy of the MIS department, users are now virtually unprotected against turnover of this local hero

Such practice is worsened by the perception that MIS is always available to rescue users when a system is too big or does not work. User supervisors and managers have not become involved in system development, except in approving training allowing time to work on the system, and watching a demonstration when it is completed

Without a crisis, user management has been content to let employees pursue systems development. No user department in the firm has yet established development policies, standards, or controls. Nor has the MIS department pressured user management to participate in managing the development

Despite these stumbling blocks, user querying and even systems development is expanding rapidly. A number of influential people perceive advantages in user systems development and are helping it become accepted Most users surveyed expected continued growth

## 5 Strategies for Managing User Developed Systems

Many companies have arrived at a pivotal point in their management of user computing. As people have begun to recognize the power of the computer, they have embraced automation as a ubiquitous solution. Many seem not to have learned the hard lesson that failure is possible not all applications can be automated, not all will be cost effective and not all will be best developed by end-users

For user development to be effectively managed, several issues need to be addressed. Both top management and the MIS department need to recognize that user development has arrived and will grow. This requires management of the change. There is no consummate strategy for doing so, the history, philosophical outlook, talents, and resources of the company will all affect the success of any approach. For example, the strategic management of system development could be moved from the MIS department to a higher level group, or MIS could determine when user development is appropriate, acquire equipment, control projects, train and support users, enforce standards compliance, and even take over maintenance of the developed system

All three basic areas of management must be considered planning, policy-setting, and control. Effective planning focuses effort on the activities most beneficial to the company and makes sure that appropriate facilities, people and skills will be available. Examples include planning of

\- processing and storage capacity

\- facilities

\- manpower

\- project prioritization

\- employee training and development
- coordination of efforts between departments
Policy-setting identifies appropriate practices and the acceptable results This includes

\- determining types of systems that are appropriate

\- establishing design, development implementation, and documentation standards

\- selecting an effective methodology

\- setting performance standards

\- apportioning development and operating costs
Control ensures that planned activities are performed efficiently and in compliance with policies. It involves

\- estimating and scheduling projects

\- monitoring project progress
- verifying standard compliance

\- assigning development and maintenance personnel to projects

In establishing a strategy, companies must first establish responsibility for each aspect of user system development. Management in user departments should take the lead in project prioritization, development scheduling, and supervision. The MIS department will then handle capacity planning, system coordination, and mainframe performance standards. Many of the decisions will require input from both MIS and user management

In most companies, the MIS department is the only organization with enough experience to make informed recommendations. However, it is essential that responsibility accompany control. For example, if the MIS department provides ongoing maintenance, its management must have control over compliance with programming documentation standards. But if the MIS department is to control the use of development tools, its managers must be prepared to be responsible for the frustration of managers with unsatisfied needs

While MIS should help to recommend standards, controls and supervision techniques, they cannot be expected to enforce use of their suggestions. First, they could not do this for the large number of user-developers who will eventually be active. Second, such an activity would only intensify animosity between users and MIS. And finally, MIS does not have authority outside their department so attempts would probably be unsuccessful

Many companies already have an information center to support and train users. However there is a temptation to use it as a “quick-and-dirty development center,” developing urgent systems rather than simply supporting them. This weakens the most powerful tool for passing on experience to users. The training program should include both technical information and details on proper planning, designing, organizing, testing documenting, and maintaining of a system

In recommending policies and practices to users,

MIS employees must bear in mind users' different stage of computing maturity. While MIS departments are coping with the problems of integration and data orientation, the users are still struggling with growth and control. For example, though charge back for services and multi-application databases may be appropriate for the MIS department, such techniques are likely to be premature for user developed systems.

With guidance from the MIS department, user departments must accept responsibility for managing their own development. This implies the entire spectrum of responsibilities. Users must realize that MIS will no longer be responsible for user data accuracy and integrity. Unless this issue is resolved, MIS will be reluctant to give their support to user development, and user departments will continue to procrastinate instead of actively planning their systems. To paraphrase an old adage, companies who cannot learn from the experience of their MIS departments are doomed to repeat them

## 6 Conclusions

Other authors have examined the underlying organizational tensions that give rise to user development problems. We have tried to identify the relevant aspects of information systems management as they affect the management of user development and focused on the symptoms of mismanagement of user development we suggested key areas to be addressed by an effective management strategy

Future studies might include broader surveys of the pervasiveness of these phenomena, longitudinal studies of management practices and assessments of the effectiveness of various strategies in various organizational and technological environments

Because the conflicts discussed here are similar to past MIS issues, it would be easy to assume that we already understand their causes, effects and solutions. Yet their seeming familiarity is perhaps their most dangerous aspect. We should recognize their similarity to past problems, but we must also take care not to ignore the fundamental differences
