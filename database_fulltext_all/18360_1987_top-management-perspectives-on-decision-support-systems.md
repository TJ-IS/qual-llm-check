# Top Management Perspectives on Decision Support Systems

P K HOUGH and N M DUFFY

Graduate School of Business Administration University of the Witwatersrand 2 St David's Place Parktown Johannesburg South Africa 2193

In an increasingly turbulent business environment, Decision Support Systems (DSS) are receiving growing attention in the literature particularly at top management level. Since the decision making milieu of senior management is extraordinarily difficult to define clearly, the question arises as to how far DSS are addressing the real problems of assisting with such decisions. Two hundred and eighty-five senior managers from a stratified sample completed a questionnaire designed to test their perceptions on this issue. In general, the respondents were reasonably aware of DSS, about a third of them have information systems that seem to fulfil the functions of a DSS to a degree and the majority believe that decision aids such as DSS can at least to some extent be useful in addressing what they perceive to be their major difficulties in decision making. Of the ninety-eight managers who already have computer-based assistance, ninety-one consider them to be DSS

Keywords Decision Support Systems, DSS Top Management Decision Making

“human judgment and decision is indeed an art a process of such complexity that it cannot be completely described by science. Nevertheless, science can illuminate and improve art, and art can illuminate and improve science, at least by making it aware of both its virtues and its limitations and cultivating the art of decent humility” Boulding [35]

## 1 Introduction

The 1980s can be characterised as a decade of turbulence [9] A recent study [14] of 758 senior executives from ten Western European countries disclosed that $46.2\%$ of them expect to have the same, and $36.7\%$ , less control over the factors on which their performance is rated during the 1980s Some writers [11,34] expect the business environment to become increasingly hostile during the current decade The impact of all this is likely to be felt most acutely at top management level

where many chief executives are spending more time on environmental issues [33]

Against this background, Decision Support Systems (DSS) are receiving increasing attention in the literature. The definition of a concept such as DSS is made difficult both by the fact that it is a comparatively new concept about which complete consensus has yet to emerge and by the fact that definitions, in general, can refer to the purpose the components or the process of the thing to be defined. In essence a decision support system is an interactive, computer-based system providing easy access to decision models and information to support decision making

DSS is a label for a class of information systems which it is hoped will provide better management support than that traditionally given by Management Science (MS) or Management Information Systems (MIS) which are perceived as having been disappointing [17 32,36,37] at top management level. On the other hand MIS succeeded in providing management support at lower management levels where decision making tends to be relatively structured. Of late, however, the technological potential for management support has improved dramatically with the advent of microcomputers, communication networks and vastly enhanced software for computers of all sizes, together with a parallel advance in the basic understanding of human problem solving and its application in business [30]

There is little doubt that

“Added value results from investigating more alternatives, doing more sophisticated analyses of alternatives, using better methods of comparing alternatives making quicker decisions, etc” [6]

There is also very little doubt that the pressures on management for greater decision level support are rising

Some attention has been given in the literature to DSS performance to date in achieving its stated objectives and benefits $[23,32]$ . If however, DSS is not to go the way of MIS, these systems will have to be effective in terms of their claimed objectives and also relevant to the real needs of management. This question of relevance has been addressed by Alavi $[1]$ and is the focus of the research described in this paper

The broad objectives of the research were therefore to

1 try to understand the top management decision making environment

2 specify the benefits that DSS has to offer top management, and

3 see if these benefits are of any use to top management

In order to accomplish these objectives we will need to look at the nature of managerial work, decision making, the role of information and DSS

## 2 The nature of managerial work

## 21 Management

Recent research [19,22] tends to see the job of a manager in the following light

● A great deal of work at an unrelenting pace

● Work activities characterised by brevity, variety and fragmentation

● A preference for action

● A preference for verbal media

● A high level of interpersonal contacts

CEOs are spending increasing amounts of time dealing with environmental issues Steiner [33] found that the 50 CEOs in his study were largely through other people, devoting between 25% and 90% of their time to such issues, with the most worrying and time-consuming being government action and the effects of public opinion on public policy The necessary preoccupation of CEOs with such external issues are confirmed by other studies such as that of Donaldson and Lorsch [8]

## 22 Decision Making

In capitalist societies considerable store is set on “decision making” Vickers [35] explains that

“the ability to make ‘right’ decisions distinguishes the expert whatever his profession, craft or occupation, and a role which leaves no scope at all for this ubiquitous activity is rated as sub-human”

The tendency is to focus on the decision maker at the moment of choice $[30]$ whereas the process is much more complex. Not only are there preceding and subsequent processes but some decision makers bear a responsibility for the decisions taken by their subordinates and, of course, decisions are also taken by groups

In essence, a decision is a choice between options based partly on information [20] How it really happens is still imperfectly understood The overall perspective of decision making at a strategic level is captured by Donaldson and Lorsch [8]

"In short, from top management's day-to-day perspective, strategic turning points are infrequent and indistinct, except perhaps in retrospect. Rather top managers are engaged in a long series of choices and decisions that over time may add up to a significant change in strategic direction. In this sense even major change is an incremental process. True, on occasion there are dramatic management crisis points or significant board of directors' meetings. But often these events ratify the results of many informal discussions and decisions that have preceded them. They are the milestones that mark the achievement of countless forgotten steps."

Clearly decision making is often not a neat six step procedure with the relevant information at hand. Many decisions can be dealt with in a pre-programmed procedure since they are repetitive and routine Such decisions have been called programmed decisions [30] Nonprogrammed decisions are typically taken in a unique situation never previously encountered, in which the information requirements to support the decision are neither known nor understood The terms unstructured decisions and nonprogrammed decisions are often used as near synonyms

A number of decision models exist in the literature that help one to think about decision making [3,38] Simon [30] identified a three step decision process

Intelligence Searching the environment for conditions calling for decision

Design Inventing, developing and analysing possible courses of action

Choice Selecting a particular course of action

The value of such classifications is that they provide a starting point for analysing decisions – their level, their nature and who should be responsible for them. This then provides a basis for identifying the nature of the information requirements for each of these processes and a tool for evaluating existing information systems and structuring new ones [10]

There is a growing literature on decision making styles $[25,31]$ , cognitive styles and human information processing $[26]$ . Whatever their styles, top managers have to deal with networks of co-existent and inter-related issues, using intuition as well as rationality $[15]$ within the constraints of the financial goals system, constituency demands and their own beliefs $[8]$ . These beliefs are the result of learning from their elders and, together with the other constraints, play an important part in reducing the number of options that will be seriously considered and, hence, the complexity of the decision

The belief system in an organisation can be a stabilising, simplifying influence but, in times of necessary change, can also be an inhibitor and may require to be challenged and restructured [8]

Now that decision making has been examined in its role as a key management activity, what is the link between decision making and information, for they both appear to be vital to DSS support of management?

## 23 Information and Decision Making

One of the important inputs to the complex system called decision making is information, which is also the output of a decision support system. Good information is therefore, necessary but not sufficient for a good decision

Information can be received by a decision maker in various forms printed output, graphics, verbal, and visual observation While a computer can extend the memory of a human being substantially, humans are not very good information processors [7] and, for example, information overload can cause a rapid and severe degradation of performance

A recent study [21] gives an interesting account of how a small group of senior executives perceive their information sources. The five executives were asked to rank ten information sources and their combined ranking reads as follows.

1 Memos, letters

2 Scheduled meetings

3 Telephone

4 Unscheduled meetings

5 Non-computer reports

6 Computer reports

7 Office visits

8 Periodicals

9 Conventions

10 Social/civic

It is interesting to note that three out of the top four sources are largely verbal thus confirming Mintzberg's observation that managers prefer the verbal media Computer reports rate a fairly low ranking – which raises a few question marks over the subject of decision support systems

The main findings of this McLeod et al [21] study are

1 The executives rely more heavily on informal information sources than formal

2 Approximately three times as much information comes from non-computer sources as from the computer

3 Approximately twice as much information comes from internal sources as from external

4 Verbal media are the most preferred sources

We have considered managerial work, decision making and the information/decision link and it is time to examine DSS, the tool that has been developed to assist in these areas

## 24 Decision Support Systems

Since Scott Morton [28] first referred to 'management decision systems' in the early seventies, a proliferation of terms has since occurred. In a recent paper [29], he introduces the following additional labels

Management support systems the use of information technology to support management This is a recognition of the fact that decision making is only one of the management activities that require support Four types of MSS are identified

(1) Data support systems the information potential in traditional data processing data bases

(2) Decision support systems as defined above but seen to be losing its meaning for the reasons mentioned

(3) Intelligent support systems a form of DSS using artificial intelligence and expert system approaches

(4) Executive support systems broader than DSS and data retrieval-oriented rather than model-oriented like DSS

Compounding this proliferation of labels, Keen and Wagner [18] refer to 'executive mind-support systems' and others [4,27] to 'executive information systems' or EIS

To complete this foray into the DSS-related terminology Sprague and Carlson [32] summarise the situation well

"One view is that the natural evolutionary advancement of information technology and its use in the organizational context has led from electronic data processing (EDP) to management information systems (MIS) to the current DSS thrust In this view, DSS pick up where MIS leave off An alternative view portrays DSS as an important subset of what MIS have been and will continue to be Still another view recognizes a type of system that has been developing for several years and “now we have a name for it” Meanwhile the skeptics suspect that DSS is just another buzz word to justify the next round of visits from the vendors"

For the purposes of this paper we see information systems (IS) as depicted in Figure 1 Implicit in this is the definition of DSS from the Introduction a decision support system is an interactive, computer-based system providing easy access to decision models and information to support decision making

Keen and Scott Morton [17] suggest that the purpose of a DSS is to

Not everyone agrees, however, that support is limited to semi-structured decisions. Some writers [32,37], for example, consider that support of unstructured decisions falls within the scope of DSS as well. This is not a trivial distinction and it is one of the motivations for our research. It is perhaps useful to summarise the essence of the literature on the nature of DSS by listing their main characteristics

They are computer-based

They support rather than automate the decision process

They support semi-structured and, perhaps, unstructured decisions

They support all levels of management that take these types of decisions

They combine the use of models, analytic techniques and conventional data retrieval

They are user-friendly to noncomputer people

They are flexible and easily changed

They are a personal rather than an organisational tool

They provide easy access to data

They provide a choice of presentation forms

It would probably be more accurate to describe these as the desired characteristics since there are not likely to be many DSS that exhibit all of them

What then does a successful DSS do? Most authorities would respond that it should be used by the appropriate decision maker, be flexible and easy to change, be accessible, have a fast response and have the required functionality Clearly many DSS meet a sufficient number of these success criteria to be used since the reported usage is widespread and varied [5,16] DSS are also being used in group decision situations [13]

With all the above as background, a number of issues arise

1 Some writers such as Keen and Scott Morton [17] deliberately limited DSS to semistructured decisions. This, presumably, was because they did not think DSS could assist to any great extent in unstructured decisions that occur largely at top management level

2 In view of the unstructured, fragmented, informal, external and personal nature of much top management work and their preference for verbal media, how far can DSS really assist at this level of the organisation?

3 The literature contains many views of what top managers require in the way of support How realistic are these views and how far does DSS go towards meeting these requirements?

As a result of these concerns the specific objectives of the research were to discover

1 To what extent top managers are actually aware of DSS

23 Top managers who have computer-based systems to supply decision aids

2 How many top managers use DSS

3 Top management's perceptions of the difficulties they typically encounter in making decisions

4 Their perceptions of the usefulness of the support DSS may be able to offer in these decision making situations

5 How many top managers have computer-based aids to provide DSS

6 Whether top managers believe that DSS could provide useful assistance with their decision making

## 3 The research

A questionnaire (as shown in Appendix 1) was sent to 1985 top managers. As the prospective respondents were expected to be under considerable time pressure the questionnaire was kept as brief as possible and mailed to the chief executive by name. The covering letter asked the CEO to complete the questionnaire himself if at all possible. A stratified sample was randomly drawn from four industry sectors manufacturing, construction, wholesale and retail, and service

Of the 295 questionnaires returned a 15% response, 285 were processed. The vast majority were completed by managing directors. Since there were so few responses from people who were not managing directors, these are not considered to introduce a significant bias. Because company size varied from under 50 to over 1000 employees, it is recognised that the term “top manager” is somewhat elastic

## 31 Tests of Hypotheses

Based on the literature outlined above and the specific objectives, a number of hypotheses were formulated

H1 Top management have not heard of DSS

H2 Those top managers who have heard of DSS do not necessarily use it

H3 Some of the proposed theoretical difficulties in making decisions are not typically encountered by top management

H4 Top management do not perceive a need for some of the aids to decision making that DSS have to offer

H5 Top management do not have computerbased systems to supply the possible decision aids listed in the questionnaire

H6 Top management do not believe that computer-based systems could usefully provide assistance in their decision making

The tests of the hypotheses are all carried out at the 5% significance level and are one-sided tests, unless otherwise stated. For the sign test with a sample size of 285 and a one-sided significance level of 0.05, the critical frequency, d is 129 [24]. This means that if either of the two sample frequencies is less than or equal to 129 then it must be rejected that it may have happened by chance

## H1 Top management have not heard of DSS

On the basis that the benefits would be unrealised if management were unaware of DSS, it was decided to test their awareness. In response to the question, “Have you heard of the concept of 'decision support systems'?" 130 replied YES and 155 replied NO (See Table 2) so it can be concluded that there is a $50\%$ probability of top management having heard of DSS H1 is therefore rejected. Even allowing for some bias due to a possible reluctance to admit ignorance it seems as if DSS has begun to infiltrate the ken of top managers

## H3 Some of the proposed theoretical difficulties in making decisions are not typically encountered by top management

The benefits of DSS are aimed at certain decision making difficulties experienced by managers. We therefore decided to see if top managers believed that they experience these difficulties Table 3 shows the response frequencies and rankings of the difficulties typically encountered in making decisions The “occasional” category has significantly more responses than the other two So overall, these difficulties are sometimes encountered, but not particularly often This finding would agree with an intuitive view that, since there is such a wide variety of decision problem types encountered by top management, it is unlikely that they all to pose the same difficulties

The sums of the ranks for each category in Table 3 are 32, 14 and 32 respectively. The test statistic is

$$
Q = \frac {1 2 \left(R _ {1} ^ {2} + R _ {2} ^ {2} + R _ {3} ^ {2}\right)}{n k (k + 1)} - 3 n (k + 1),
$$

where n = 13, the number of questions, k = 3, the number of categories, and R is the sum of ranks for category i

Q is approximately chi-squared distributed with k - 1 = 2 degrees of freedom. The sample statistic is Q = 16.62. The critical value of a one-sided test at the 5% significance level is 5.99. It cannot therefore be accepted that the decision difficulties are equally encountered. The sum of ranks for the occasional category is significantly lower than those of the other two, so H3 is rejected as stated. It is found that the top management perceive that they do occasionally encounter all the theoretical difficulties in making decisions

Mintzberg [22] proposed that executive time could be better managed with the assistance of programmed time scheduling. This approach does not appear to be needed, although time pressure is a problem. It also seems that top management do not need assistance in interpreting information or identifying important decision criteria (critical factors). This is consistent with the previous finding that they appear to be sure what information is needed, what the problem is, and what should be done

H5 Top management do not have computer-based systems to supply the possible decision aids listed in the questionnaire

Using the sign test for $n = 285$ , the critical frequency is d = 129 There were 98 YES and 187 NO responses The number of YES responses is less than d, so it must be rejected that there is no difference between the number of top managers who have and those who do not have computer-based systems supplying these aids It appears that in general, the respondent top managers do not have decision making aids from computer-based systems so H5 must be accepted Of the 98 who have computer-based assistance 91 consider them to be DSS

H6 Top management do not believe that computer-based systems could usefully provide assistance in their decision making

Of the 285 respondents, 133 believed that most of the decision aids listed could be provided usefully by computer-based systems 140 believed that only some could be provided or that they could be provided only in a partially useful way. Only 12 respondents did not believe that they could be usefully provided at all. So there is a strong indication of a belief in computer-based systems providing at least some decision support. It appears that any resistance to the introduction of DSS at top management level is not due to their feeling that it would have nothing to offer. The respondent managers appear to have faith in information technology

The conclusions drawn from the results of this questionnaire may not necessarily apply to the whole population of top managers. They are also based on the perceptions of those managers and therefore subject to the uncertainties of such responses

## 4. Conclusion

The top management sampled appear to be fairly knowledgeable about DSS and about a third of them actually have information systems which seem to fulfil the functions of DSS to a degree. It is very likely that the insurgence of microcomputers, together with the publicity given by the hardware and software vendors, has something to do with this. It is also apparent that the vast majority believe that computer-based systems have at least something to offer in support of decision making

The link between what top management perceive to be their major difficulties in decision making and the decision aids they believe would be most useful seems quite clear. They generally seem to think that they understand what their problems are and what needs to be done. They feel they need more information and will know whether it is sufficient or relevant. This does not imply that they should be deluged with data but that more of the “right” information would be useful

The little time that is available to make decisions cannot be increased. However, quicker analysis, including more options might permit managers to make better decisions in the time available

The modelling capability of a DSS should allow exploration of the problem area (by suggestion and evaluation of alternatives and sensitivity or contingency analysis), this would not be to understand the real problems, but to deal better with the inherent complexity of top management problems and resolve the conflicts in the issues

This study and some of the literature, seem to indicate that we should not raise top management hopes too high with DSS. Although DSS can address only a portion of managerial work, they are being used by top management, are perceived to be useful, and could probably be more useful in the future. On the other hand, some of the aspirations expressed for DSS seem unrealistic. Seen against the background of the expectations raised for MIS and their subsequent non-delivery it would be prudent to dispense with the fanfare this time
