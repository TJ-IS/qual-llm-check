# Computer-Family Selection Methodology for Organizational Information Systems

Israel Borovits and Moshe Zviran

Computers and Information Systems Program, Faculty of Management, University Campus, Tel-Aviv University, P.O. Box 39010, 69978, Tel-Aviv, Israel

Many organizations with computerized information systems have to contend with the problem of selecting the computer system that best fits their needs. This paper addresses the subject, and focuses upon a specific aspect of the problem: the selection of a computer-family for the entire organization. The practice of selection is briefly described and the term “computer-family” defined. A structured methodology and the process associated with it are then developed and discussed. The outlined methodology is then validated through a detailed description of its implementation in a large organization.

Keywords: Computer-family, hardware selection, selection techniques

## 1. Introduction

The increase in size, sophistication and geographic dispersion of organizations and commercial firms has forced management to rely increasingly on the availability of information in making vital decisions. To meet this need, computer-based information systems are being introduced throughout the organization, with a concomitant increase in the rate and scope of investment in computers and allied data-processing equipment.

Decisions on the acquisition of computer resources are strategic for three main reasons:

a. The acquisition of computer systems usually has a large financial impact on the organization.

b. Computer configurations are fixed for the short term and may be difficult and expensive to alter. Decisions are, therefore, generally fixed for about five years and remain in effect for much longer.

c. The appropriate hardware configuration is a precondition for successful implementation of information systems.

As computers continue to drop in cost, increase in power, and become more widely used, it is

## 2. Traditional Selection Procedures

Selection and evaluation have been intensively discussed in the literature. A wide variety of models and methods have been proposed, but have generally been concentrated on individual stages in the selection process and on specific methods and techniques for each stage.

The process consists of the following phases: analysis and determination of needs, request for proposals, validation and evaluation of proposals, and system selection [3]. The first two steps are preliminary to the evaluation of the proposals, while the latter two are often blurred into a single step: evaluate the candidates and pick the best.

Traditional selection processes aim to find a specific system which best fits the user requirements. Preliminary analysis of user requirements and of the proposed new system must therefore precede all other steps [6].

The techniques for the evaluation of computer systems generally are either very simplistic or sophisticated, incorporating many criteria. The simplistic methodologies are basically intuitive, unsupported by any theory, or lacking a systemized approach. The sophisticated methods employ an analysis of the vendors' proposals, with a ranking of the proposed computer systems. Following this ranking, a hardware performance and capability analysis is performed on the most desirable. If it fails to perform adequately, it is rejected and a performance analysis is carried out on the next one.

The most commonly used computer system evaluation methods are:

1. Weighted scoring. This commonly used approach attempts to overcome evaluation problems by combining objectivity with consideration of apparently non-quantifiable factors $[12]$ . Relative weights are pre-assigned to all system items considered important, each competent system is subjectively rated with respect to each item and then the overall score is computed for each vendor. The rating is subjective: from “no good” to “entirely adequate”. The system with the highest total score is considered most desirable.

2. Cost-effectiveness ratio. This is simply a sub-category of the weighted scoring technique, where the cost of the entire computer system is divided by the sum of its points [11]. The system having the lowest ratio is considered the most desirable.

3. Cost-value. This approach uses cost as the sole measure. Cost and benefits are assigned to the desirable features of each proposed system. The sum of these values is then subtracted from the total cost of the system. The result represents the cost of the system to meet the mandatory requirements. The system with the lowest cost is the most desirable.

4. Requirement-costing technique. Here, the estimated cost-savings benefits are assigned to the preselected set of desirable features. Those not part of the proposed system are then added to the total cost of the system. In addition, if a desirable feature is offered at an additional cost that is less than the estimated benefit, the incremental cost is added to the system cost. The system with the lowest resulting cost is considered the most desirable.

5. Dynamic approach. This employs the projected work load growth trend and the cost-effectiveness ratios of each vendor's proposed product line [8]. The underlying assumption is that it would be feasible to install a system for as little as one year, but only if it will be replaced by a compatible system from the same manufacturer's product line and replacement is in accordance with the dictates of work load. Using the projected work load trend and measurement of system capacity, a schedule of replacement is worked out. The cost of each system (as determined by one of the methods described above) over the period of its employment is then discounted with respect to an estimate of cost of capital and its expected future life. The product line with the lowest discounted present cost is the most desirable.

6. Present value analysis. The objective here is to determine which system will cost the least and benefit the organization most. The net present value of the proposed system should be determined by discounting all cash flows associated with it, using the organization's cost of capital as the discount rate. The benefits projected by the proposed new system are the net cash inflows. The system with the highest net present value is the most desirable.

## 3. The Need for a New Methodology

The need to develop a new selection methodology arises from the trend towards decentralization and DDP. Organizations with decentralized systems, or in the process of carrying out decentralization of computing hardware, should be in a position to evaluate and select a computer-family.

The basic definition of a computer-family is:

A family of computers of the same type, consisting of several models from the same manufacturer's product line, ranging from microcomputer to mainframe, with full compatibility in the operating system and the system's software, to enable transfer of application software from one family member to another without change.

Selecting a computer-family will enable the organization to better implement its DDP policy, and provide it with three major capabilities:

1. The ability to perform a one-time evaluation and selection procedure, determine which computer-family best suits its needs, and ensure uniform hardware for the entire organization. Once this has been achieved, whenever new hardware is required, the appropriate configuration is constructed from the chosen computer-family, according to specific needs.

2. Full compatibility in both hardware and system software. This ensures minimization of the cost of intergrating systems with each other.

3. The ability to transfer application software from one family member to another without change, and thereby avoid duplication in development of software. This is the foundation for coordinated development of consistent and corporate-wide information systems.

All the methods discussed previously are addressed to the problem of seeking a specific computer or computers to meet specific and known requirements. However, none of them provides tools or techniques to evaluate and select a computer-family as defined above.

A generic evaluation and selection methodology has been formulated to meet the specific demands of selecting a computer-family (See Figure 1). The selection process is subdivided into 10 steps, each of which is described later in detail.

Before entering into a discussion of the proposed selection process, a number of comments on certain basic considerations are in place.

1. The objective of the methodology is to enable evaluation and selection of an entire computer-family, rather than to select a specific computer to meet known and well-defined needs.

2. Particularly because of the long-term repercussions of the decisions made, special attention is paid to vendor's characteristics.

3. The computer-family selection process does not dwell upon specific technical aspects, such as memory size and disk access time, though it may take them into consideration. Instead, it concentrates on evaluating the overall performance of each computer within the family.

4. Account is taken of the fact that organizations may already have a computer-based information system and the necessity to preserve investments in existing application software, or at least to minimize the cost and trouble of converting to the new computer-family selected.

## 5. The Selection Stages

The selection process follows a detailed analysis of the requirements (for a detailed discussion, see [14]). Following is a step-by-step description of the proposed methodology.

a) Identification of possible vendors and manufacturers. The first step in the computer-family selection process is identification of possible vendors whose product lines might suit the organization's needs, in accordance with the computer-family definition stated above. The end-product of this stage is an initial list of vendors and suppliers whose product lines may be suited to the organization's needs.

b) Preliminary elimination of irrelevant candidates. If the initial list of possible vendors and suppliers is too broad, any clearly unsuitable candidates are winnowed out. At the end of the second step, a list of potential vendors is provided.

c) Determination of mandatory requirements. Mandatory requirements are derived from both the information systems policy of the organization and the basic definition of a computer-family. The purpose of this step is to define basic features required of the computer-family, and to set certain criteria which may be considered as prerequisites for a vendor's candidacy. Such requirements may be:

\- Ability to upgrade each model to a higher one, without change.

\- Ability to connect members of the computer-family directly, or through LAN.

\- Full compatibility of the system's software.

d) Examination of vendor's compliance with mandatory requirements. On the basis of the mandatory requirements, a questionnaire is formulated and addressed to all relevant vendors. Responses to this questionnaire provide the selection committee with basic information about the proposed computer-families and each product line. The responses are screened to determine if they meet mandatory requirements. The vendors remaining after this elimination procedure constitute the mailing list for the Request For Proposals (RFP).

e) Setting quantitative and qualitative criteria and respective weighting-scales. The variability of the capabilities and performance characteristics of each element (hardware, software, service) of a computer system is immense, and the number of permutations is infinite. A properly constituted set of quantitative and qualitative criteria is critical, therefore, for the acquisition of a satisfactory computer-family [Ghandforoush, 1982]. Furthermore, in accordance with our definition of a computer-family, vendors are expected to propose not a single computer system but rather a wide variety of elements. This raises the problem of evaluating and comparing proposals. It is suggested that the quantitative selection criteria be applied to at least the following five issues, each of which has its own partial list of guideliness (see Table 1).

Certainly not less important, though nonquantifiable, are qualitative criteria such as:

\* how widespread is the use of the proposed computer-family

\* vendor support, such as training, maintenance philosophy and practice

\* software houses specializing in the proposed computer-family

\* current users' opinions and vendor's reputation.

f) Writing the RFP to be addressed to selected vendors. The primary advertising medium is the RFP, which is issued by the procuring organization and contains the system requirements. Typically, the RFP will include a summary list of specific requirements according to which the vendors will be asked to write their proposals, describing how they will meet each requirement. The RFP should consist of two major parts, the first relating to each model within the computer-family, and the second to the computer-family as a uniform entity. The second part is more general in nature and concentrates upon such issues as system software, conversion of present application software to the new computer-family, environment, etc. The RFP should be addressed to those vendors satisfying mandatory requirements.

g) Receiving, comparing and analyzing bids. The problem of analyzing and comparing bids is particularly acute, since it is more than possible that none of the computer-families under consideration exhibits a clear dominance over its competitors. This stage is, therefore, fundamental to the interim evaluation and selection process and should be accorded the appropriate measure of time and effort. It is required that bids be submitted in writing, and it is expected that they will all conform to the style indicated in the RFP, so that the selection process will not be affected by style of expression and use of selling techniques. The first step in the process of analyzing bids is subjectively rating each competing system with respect to each item. A score being assigned to each item for each vendor, as already noted. The weighted score is then computed for each proposed system. The next and crucial stage is the most problematic of the entire process, since there are no methods or guideliness for comparing computer-families. Our proposed method consists of the following steps: 1. Five categories of computers are established:

\* microcomputers (single station)

\* super-microcomputers

\* minicomputers

\* super-minicomputers

\* mainframes

These categories represent differences in computing power and other major hardware characteristics.

2. Relative weights are assigned to each category according to its importance for the organization.

3. Each relevant model within each proposed computer-family is assigned to the respective category on the basis of criteria such as memory size, maximum number of terminals, maximum disk capacity, and relative performance.

4. For each criterion, a computer model, or models, is selected within each category, which has dominance over its competitors, and the score of '100' is assigned to it. All other models of the remaining computer-families are then respectively and subjectively scored on the scale or 0-100.

5. The total score is calculated. The score attained for each criterion by each computer-family is the average weight calculated for all five categories. The score for each criterion is computed as:

$$
S _ {i} = \sum_ {j} R _ {j} ^ {*} S _ {i j} / 1 0 0
$$

where:

$R_{j} =$ relative weight of criterion $j,\sum_{j}R_{j} = 1$

$S_{ij}$ = score assigned to criterion i, for category j, on scale of 0–100.

$S_{i}$ = overall relative weight attained for criterion i.

6. A comparative table is drawn up, as illustrated in Figure 2, giving the scores attained by each family for each criterion.

7. The final score achieved by each family is calculated as:

$$
S = \sum_ {i} S _ {i} ^ {*} W _ {i}
$$

where:

$W_{i} =$ relative weight of criterion $i$ , $\sum_{i}W_{i} = 1$ .

$S_{i} =$ score assigned to criterion $i$ .

$S =$ overall score of the system.

h) Drawing up final list of vendors. On the basis of the final scores attained by each computer-family the selection committee is able to disqualify irrelevant computer-families, and select up to three or four vendors most likely to succeed. These computer-families are then further tested in order to ensure they have the proper capabilities and characteristics.

i) Benchmarks for performance of hardware and software. Study of the written proposals is sufficient for evaluating fulfillment of certain requirements. In such cases, benchmarking must be performed. A benchmark, in the context of this paper, is a set of live tests designed to examine the computer systems (hardware and software) proposed in response to an RFP, to determine if they meet certain requirements [5]. Selection of criteria to be tested may be performed according to their importance for the organization, using the relative weights already assigned. The purpose of these benchmarks is to verify the system's characteristics prior to drawing final conclusions.

## 6. Case Study

The following case study illustrates a detailed application of the computer-family selection methodology in a large organization. The organization under consideration had been using a variety of computers from different vendors and was unable to achieve compatibility or carry out effective decentralization of hardware and software. The effort was therefore directed at evaluating and selecting a computer-family best suited to the needs of the organization. To this end the organization appointed a professional committee of four members: the Head of the organization's Information Services Department (chairman of the committee), an outside consultant and two systems analysts from its Information Services Department. The committee was given four months to submit its final recommendations. The initial list of potential vendors (step 1 in Figure 1) was drawn up using several sources of information, including “The Datamation 100” [1], DATAPRO 80 and Auerbach Reports. The list contained 18 possible vendors and was reduced to 7 vendors after the preliminary elimination (step 2 in Figure 1). The third step was constructing mandatory requirements, derived mainly from the organization’s information systems policy. The final list of mandatory requirements is given in Figure 3.

These requirements formed the basis for a preliminary questionnaire, which was addressed to relevant vendors. The responses to this questionnaire enabled the committee to examine the vendors' compliance with mandatory requirements. All computer-families offered by the vendors met mandatory requirements and none was therefore eliminated during this step.

Regarding the fifth step, the committee had set quantitative and qualitative criteria and the respective weighting scale. Quantitative criteria were divided into two major sections. The first was addressed to hardware issues and therefore was applied separately to each model within each computer-family. The second section was devoted to other issues (such as software and communication) which, since they are common to all family members, need be set only once. For non-quantifiable factors to be considered during the selection process, five qualitative criteria were set: incidence of use of the specific computer-family (21%), service facilities (30%), user training (13%), current user satisfaction (18%) and general impression formed by committee members (18%). The final rating was calculated according to the ratio of 80% to quantitative criteria and 20% to qualitative criteria. The committee decided, however, to take into account the qualitative aspect only for those 3-4 vendors with highest quantitative scores. An RFP was then addressed to all competing vendors, with a time limit of about a month for submitting proposals. Next came the stage of analyzing and evaluating the bids received. As stated in the description of the methodology, this stage is the crux of the entire evaluation process. Seven prospective vendors submitted proposals, each of which had a different number of computer models within its family.

## 7. Summary and Conclusions

A methodology for evaluating and selecting a computer family has been presented. The methodology was developed in response to the necessity for systematizing the process of selecting a computer-family, consisting of a number of compatible models of the same manufacturer's product line.

The methodology outlined in this paper was found to be valid, flexible and easy to apply and to satisfy two major objectives. First, it provides the organization with a comprehensive methodology, a single procedure and a single set of tools for evaluating and selecting a computer-family. Though customization is required, since organizations differ in needs, and must therefore establish their own criteria and weighting scale, the basic methodology facilitates and clarifies the entire selection procedure. Second, though the systems offered are presumed to have the proper characteristics, there are differences, some of them substantial, among computer-families, and even greater differences among vendors. This methodology leads to the best match of what the vendor is offering and what the organization really needs.
