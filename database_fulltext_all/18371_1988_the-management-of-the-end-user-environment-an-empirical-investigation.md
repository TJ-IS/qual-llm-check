# The Management of the End-User Environment: An Empirical Investigation \*

François Bergeron and Carole Bérubé
Département des systèmes d'information organisationnels,
Faculté des sciences de l'administration, Université Laval,
Québec, Québec, Canada G1K 7P4

An empirical study of 212 end-users in 31 organizations was conducted to investigate the adequacy of management and support practices of end-user computing. It was found that end-users were more satisfied with their microcomputing activities when (1) the organizational microcomputing plan was incorporated in the information systems master plan, (2) there was an information center to support end-user activity and, (3) users had access to a hot-line to solve their microcomputing problems. On the other hand, end-users were less satisfied with their microcomputing activities when there were a large number of microcomputing policies. Finally, it was observed that end-users making the most use of the support services were those who were the least satisfied with their microcomputing activities.

Keywords: End-user computing, Policies, Information center, User satisfaction

## 1. Introduction

The advent of microcomputers has brought about an upheaval in the management of the computer environment within organizations. At the start, the area was reserved for specialists; but with the arrival of microcomputers, everyone jumped on the bandwagon. Today, we are witnessing a proliferation of microcomputers in the business world. The managerial structure required to support this type of environment must receive attention from the information systems department.

More specifically, users should be trained [2,14], they should have support from the information systems group [1,5,12,13], and various implementation strategies and policies should be set [3,6,9]. As past research has already shown [18], microcomputers represent a way to break with the information systems department and to give users a sense of control and independence. The purpose of this study was to shed some light on user satisfaction with various forms of support and management of their environment.

Training is often an integral part of the support provided by the information systems group [17,19]. In fact, it represents a fundamental element for the efficient use of microcomputers. The establishment of a range of policies for a better microcomputer environment has been the object of numerous recommendations. It is generally agreed that these policies should include material on the purchase of microcomputer material, security, development of applications, the integrity of data, the laws regarding software, etc. An equilibrium between local autonomy and central control of microcomputer management has also been suggested.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research model

This research attempted to answer three basic questions:

(1) Do specific organizational structures help increase end-user satisfaction?

(2) What is the importance of a microcomputer plan with regard to end-user satisfaction?

(3) How important are organizational microcomputing policies in that respect?

## 3.1.1. Research Hypotheses

A cross sectional study of a survey type was conducted to answer these questions. More specifically, the following research hypotheses were tested:

Hypothesis 1. The presence of a user support structure increases user satisfaction.

Much has been said about the need to set up a user support group to ensure harmonious development of information systems. The three main structures are: the steering committee, the information center, and the support group. Although each operates at a different level, all play an important role; the first adopts a planning and orientation role while the other two interact directly with users on a more technical level. The overall services offered allow users to approach microcomputers with less anxiety and greater ease because they feel that someone qualified is ready to answer their questions when problems occur.

Hypothesis 2. The more users consult with support structures, the higher their level of satisfaction.

It is important that users have someone to turn to when they encounter problems; if not, they experience frustration and exhibit resistance $[4,16]$ . We believe that the more recourse users have to information systems support groups, the greater their satisfaction as they become more familiar with the technology. In the same way, the more often they ask for aid, the less they will experience problems of poor structuring or poor quality in development of their applications. Good user support should raise end-user satisfaction.

Hypothesis 3. The establishment of a microcomputer plan increases user satisfaction.

McLean and Soden [12] underline the necessity of planning when systems become complex, a data base is shared, several functions or departments are involved, or costs are rising. They concentrate on large systems, but their ideas can also be applied to microcomputers. Other authors stress the need for an action plan based on the belief that good management improves systems efficiency. A microcomputer plan should have a positive impact on user satisfaction.

Hypothesis 4. The establishment of policies increases user satisfaction.

The compatibility of equipment and software is vital. Indeed, incompatible or low quality systems may give rise to user problems if they cannot operate the technology as expected (due to outdated software, difficulties in transferring data or programs, problems in connecting to a network, etc.). Legal aspects also must be solved in order to avoid expensive problems. Measures must be taken to assure that the portfolio of applications will not impede the evolution of new information systems, particularly the integrity of the data bases. It is important to avoid wasting resources due to poor usage of data bases or the development of less pertinent applications that are difficult to integrate into the overall system or impossible to support, etc. Organizational policies should increase end-user satisfaction in the long run.

## 3.1.2. End-user Satisfaction

Bailey and Pearson [2] demonstrated that satisfaction is a major consideration in increasing productivity, which can only be attained if the user is provided with adequate tools and information. They defined satisfaction as the sum of the user's sentiments and attitudes with respect to a variety of factors such as the quality of services with EDP staff, the characteristics of information product and users' involvement. End-user satisfaction is a perceptual measure of system success which serves as a substitute for objective determinants of information systems effectiveness.

End-user satisfaction was measured by using the Bailey and Pearson [2] scale of satisfaction as improved by Ives et al. [7] and adapted to the microcomputer environment by the authors. Based on a reliability test, 34 semantic differential scales were retained for a Cronbach alpha of 0.93. For each organization, a measurement of overall satisfaction was determined by calculating an average of satisfaction scores for the group of members of the organization. An analysis of variance showed a significant difference in the overall satisfaction between organizations (F = 2.25, p = 0.0006).

## 3.2. Sample

The procedure was divided into two parts. First, a number of organizations likely to qualify for the study were selected from various lists provided by suppliers, government, and others. The first condition to qualify was past experience with microcomputers. Of the 60 information systems department directors contacted by telephone, 46 agreed to participate. A questionnaire was mailed and 37 were returned. Following close examination of the questionnaires, 31 were identified as valid; this is a response rate of 67%.

In the second step, each director was sent a set of user-questionnaires, based on the number of questionnaires he/she agreed to distribute to a selected sample of end-users of his/her choice in the organization. This questionnaire was to be completed by microcomputer users (i.e., those developing applications, but excluding members of the information systems department and personnel using only word processing): 608 questionnaires were distributed. Of the 258 returned, 212 were found valid, for a response rate of 35%. This averaged to 6.8 respondents per organization, varying between 1 and 17. The data was gathered between July and November 1986. Following this, five department directors were interviewed to confirm our interpretation of the results within the context of their organization.

## 3.2.1. Description of the Organizations

The selected organizations operated in different sectors: federal and provincial governments (16), banking (3), municipal administration (2), insurance (2), transportation (2), consulting (1), education (1), food (1), manufacturing (1), oil (1) and utilities (1). The large proportion of public organizations is representative of the industry in the Quebec area but is not necessarily representative of other areas. Results should therefore be interpreted with this limitation in mind. The number of employees in these organizations varied between 11 and 5,000; the information systems departments employed up to 300 persons, and the number of microcomputer users varied between 3 and 300.

## 3.2.2. Description of Users

The majority of respondents worked in a functional environment related to administration and management, see table 1. User experience with microcomputers ranged from six months to 15 years (table 2). Of all respondents, 77 (33%) had a microcomputer at their desk. The remainder were required to work at the computer center or another work station. In 60% of the cases, microcomputers were IBM or DOS compatible; the remaining 40% represented a wide range of suppliers. Respondents mostly used electronic worksheets, data bases, and word processors, in that order.

## 4.1.1. Test of Hypothesis 1

An analysis of individual satisfaction of users shows that they are more satisfied when there is an information center in the organization; hypothesis 1 is therefore accepted at least for the information center (F = 4.423, Sig = 0.037, N = 31; average satisfaction with an information center = 5.3058, SD = 0.7435, N = 21; average satisfaction without an information center = 5.0473, SD = 0.9559, n = 10). No significant difference was noted in the satisfaction with the presence or absence of a steering committee (F = 0.366, sig = 0.55) or a support group (F = 1.93, sig. = 0.18).

Other services had also been established by organizations in response to user needs (table 5). Statistical analysis showed that presence of a hot line had a positive influence on end-user's satisfaction (F = 6.914, sig = 0.009, N = 208; average satisfaction with the hot line = 5.3305, SD = 0.7391, n = 140; average satisfaction without the hot line = 5.0154, SD = 0.9416, n = 68). No significant difference was obtained for other computer services.

## 5. Plan and Policies

## 5.1. Microcomputer Plan

Of the 31 organizations, 21 (68%) had developed a master plan for the management of their computer environment: this had been established for an average period of 3.5 years. Specific planning was conducted by 18 (58%) department directors over an average period of 2.8 years. Of all the organizations who claimed to have established a master plan, 16 (76%) claimed to have included the microcomputer plan in the master plan.

## 5.1.1. Test of Hypothesis 3

In order to determine whether planning had an effect on users, an organizational analysis was conducted. No significant difference was found in the satisfaction level from one organization to another, thus hypothesis 3 is not accepted (F = 2.663, sig. = 0.11, N = 29: average satisfaction in organizations with a microcomputer plan = 5.393, SD = 0.36, n = 18; average satisfaction in organizations without a microcomputer plan = 5.10, SD = 0.62, n = 11).

However, organizations having incorporated their microcomputer plan into the master plan generally had a greater number of satisfied users (F = 2.827, sig. = 0.10, n = 29; average satisfaction in organizations where plans are integrated = 5.41, SD = 0.359, n = 16; average satisfaction in organizations where plans are not integrated = 5.12; SD = 0.577, n = 13).

## 5.2. Microcomputer Management Policies

In order to analyze the existing policies in organizations and to determine which are known to users, a list of possible policies was prepared. This was shown to both directors and users. First, the director indicated the policies in effect in the organization. Second, users indicated the policies they felt were in effect and the extent to which they were respected, on a 7 point Likert scale.

Even if the organizations had not establish written policies, they still seemed to have them: 28 (93%) organizations had established (on paper or implicit) policies to regulate the development of applications, 30 (97%) to control the purchase of equipment and software, and 21 (68%) for microcomputer security. On average, organizations had prepared 15.9 policies (SD = 5.75) ranging from 0 to 27. Only one organization had established no policy.

## 6. Discussion

As to the preparation of a microcomputer plan (H3), it appears that a plan is not sufficient. Rather, the incorporation of a plan into a master plan shows a serious intent on the part of the information systems department. In effect, when microcomputers are approached as an entire system, better means are adopted, larger budgets are allocated, and user satisfaction is improved.

The establishment of policies is also a good way to assure efficient operations and to reach high productivity; it is wise, however, to be careful of the number of policies formulated. If the users' freedom is too restricted, they tend to be dissatisfied. The information systems group may encounter problems when users feel hampered with new constraints. Therefore, users must be made familiar with the rules of the game. A microcomputer plan might contribute to better knowledge of policies throughout the organization and a better acceptance by users.

This study demonstrates the importance of establishing an information center (H1). It is also noted that the existence of a hot line improves user satisfaction. It stresses the importance for the information center to provide users with training beyond mere technical aspects, and to offer a complete consulting and hot line service (technical, structuring of problems, etc.).

The rejection of the hypothesis on consultation (H2) can be explained by the fact that users who are satisfied encounter fewer problems, and therefore tend to refer less to the information systems group. Moreover, as observed in another study [11], it is natural that users, in the development of applications, consult with colleagues working in the same functional environment.

## 7. Conclusion

The management and support of end-user computing is attracting more and more attention. The development and integration of microcomputing plans into the organizational information systems plan as well as the implementation of information centers to assist users are ways to improve the end-user activity.

However, even though organizational policies are needed to ensure a minimum of effectiveness in the use of microcomputing, it is important to keep in mind that these policies restrain the user's freedom. Indeed, too many policies, organizational or departmental, tend to lower end-user satisfaction. In some organizations, microcomputing policies are so limiting in the kind of work a user can do, that outlaws may become a new breed of end-user.
