# An Empirical Evaluation of Process Alternatives for Developing Strategic Information

Edward J. Szewczak

Department of Management, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

and

William R. King

Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Strategic data bases (SDB) were prescribed by King and Cleland as the most practically useful way in which salient information could be incorporated into strategic planning processes. Critical success factors are one of several kinds of such strategic data bases, as are carefully-developed concise strength and weakness assessments. While the notion has great face validity, it has not been subjected to stringent empirical assessment. One such assessment is reported on here with the result that the SDB approach gives evidence of being useful and effective.

Keywords: strategic planning, strategic information, strategic data bases, planning process

## 1. Introduction

The notion of “strategic information” has become prominent in recent years. Much attention has been devoted to practical processes for the identification of the information that is most critical to supporting the strategic choices of the business firm. Such strategic information processes as those involving “critical success factors” (CSF) are now widely used and discussed in the fields of strategic management and management information systems (e.g., [22]).

However well known and accepted is the CSF methodology, it does not adequately portray the potential range of critical strategic information that is necessary to support sound strategic decision making. Moreover, there has been little empirical assessment of the efficacy of processes and methodologies for the development of relevant strategic information.

This paper reports on an empirical assessment of two strategic information development processes that are widely applicable to a variety of areas of strategic information such as critical success factions, strength – weaknesses analysis, competitive analysis and the analysis of other relevant strategic environments.

## 2. Strategic Data Bases

King and Cleland [15] introduced the idea of “strategic data bases” (SDBs) as concise carefully-chosen delineations of “the most significant strategic items related to various clientele and/or environments which affect the organization’s strategic choices” (p. 59). Assuming a definition of ‘information’ as “data that have been evaluated for use in a specific context” [14], SDBs can be viewed as strategic information – specifically, as information that has been evaluated to be that which is most relevant to the making of organizational strategic choices.

Central to the notion of an SDB is the idea that it will represent a concise, and therefore practically useful, statement of the most significant items of information of a particular category on which the future strategy of the business should be based. In other words, the Strength-Weakness SDB should not be an exhaustive listing of all of the business' strengths and weaknesses, but rather, a concise enumeration of those strengths which have been chosen to be the foundations of the future business strategy and those weaknesses which are to serve as warnings against the possibility that a contemplated strategy might require strength in an area where it does not exist.

Similarly, other SDBs are to represent the small number of most critical elements that have been chosen as the basis for formulating and evaluating future strategies. The Critical Success Factor SDB for instance, should be made up of the few areas in which it is most important to be strong if success is to be gained and the Competitor SDB should list those few areas in which competitors are so strong that recognition must be given to them in adopting a future strategy.

The process of designing an individual SDB that deals with competitors, CSFs, of strengths and weaknesses on a specific strategic context such as the regulatory environment is equivalent to performing information requirements analysis at the level of strategic management. The question of which items of information are the most significant to strategic managers, is fundamental to determining what information is required to make the most effective strategic decisions. The stages of SDB design and use can be mapped on to that stages of the strategic management process as shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1 shows that the design of a SDB can be viewed as forming part of the intelligence phase of the strategic management process [26]. Once designed and implemented, the SDB can be instrumental in the generation of organizational strategic alternatives as well as in the evaluation of proposed strategies that are the desired outputs of a strategic management process [14]. During the design phase of the process, this information can be used to develop alternative strategic options which can then be evaluated. During the choice phases of the process, the organization can make strategic choices relevant to organizational mission, objectives, goals and strategies.

Whether or not formal strategic data bases are developed, Figure 1 shows that the overall strategic management process is based on the identification and selective use of information. The SDB development process merely formalizes the alternating stages of creation and evaluation that are inherent in any worthwhile planning process [12]. Such a process involves alternating stages of creativity (generating information on alternatives) and choice (evaluation of the generated items to produce a smaller and more useful subset).

The SDB development process involves both creativity and evaluation in that data that are potentially relevant to each SDB must be identified and subsequently evaluated as to their relative importance. Each SDB will be unique since the organization will make the choices that are judged to be most relevant to its particular situation. The SDB developers must identify the myriad data that relate to the organization's key environmental elements and clientele and select those data that are so significant that they are to be incorporated into concise SDBs that will be the information on which future strategy will be based.

King and Cleland's [14] original proposal was for a team-based SDB design process, wherein managers representing diverse interest within an organization participate in a group process to make a more objective (i.e., a less functionally fixated) evaluation of the available information than would otherwise be possible in an individual-based SDB design process. King and Cleland ([14], p. 60) state that the SDB design process focuses on charging task forces, which are made up of managers representing various of the parochial interests within the organization, with gathering and evaluating the data in each of a number of areas and choosing – through the consensual process that guides most task force decision making – those that are the most important to the development of the organization's strategy.

Thus the ‘strategic data bases’ represent ‘information’ rather than ‘data’ in the sense that large quantities of data have been evaluated and condensed to a form which can feasibly be used in the strategic planning process. Thus, with the SDB approach, there is a greater likelihood that some of the information which is so universally regarded as being important to strategic planning will actually become an integral part of the strategic planning process.

Like many proposed management techniques, the SDB design process and its output (the SDBs)

have substantial face validity. SDBs have been used in a number of real-world corporate settings, but have never empirically validated on other than judgemental bases.

## 3. Objective of the Study

The objective of this study was to determine empirically whether, and in what respects, the SDB design process technique may be shown to be of value in developing strategic information to support strategy development.

## 4. Research Methodology

In the real world, the alternative to the SDB process is either a totally informal process of allowing each planning participant to develop a “personal SDB” (since cognitive limitations place severe limitations on the quantity of data that an individual can effectively utilize, or a formal process in which a voluminous staff study is performed [14].

Since it is difficult to replicate either of these two alternatives to a group SDB development process in a controlled environment, it was determined that a team SDB process should be evaluated in terms of comparisons with an individual SDB process. If the group process were judged to be significantly more effective, it would be reasonable to assume that it would also be more effective than these other real-world processes. The basic definition of a ‘group’ that was used is “two or more persons who are interacting with one another in such a manner that each person influences and is influenced by each other person” [25].

## 4.1. Evaluation Measures

In order to evaluate whether the SDB team design process is in fact superior to the SDB individual design process, six evaluation measures were identified to be relevant:

1) Number of ideas generated during the SDB design process.

2) Quality of the resultant SDB.

3) Time for the SDB design process to be completed.

4) Confidence of participants in the designed SDB.

5) Satisfaction of participants with the SDB design process.

6) Perceived implementability of the resultant SDB.

The first two measures are primarily concerned with “effectiveness”.

The number of ideas generated during the SDB design process is important since one of the goals of SDB design is to identify and evaluate relevant items of information for use in the strategic management process. The greater the number of ideas generated for evaluation, the better the chances of achieving the most complete set of information items from which to choose in making decisions as to which items are most significant, i.e., to be included in the SDB.

The quality of the designed SDB is important since different designers will make different evaluations of the available information items and some evaluations will be superior to others.

The time for the SDB design process to be completed is an “efficiency” measure rather than an “effectiveness” measure. If time is a scarce resource for an organization which must make strategic decisions, then a measure of the time for the process completion is relevant since the cost of strategic level management involvement will be significant.

The last three measures are primarily behavioral in focus.

Confidence in the designed SDB is a relevant measure since the SDB design process is a participative process in which the designers of the SDB will be the users of the SDB during the later phases of the strategic management process.

Satisfaction with the SDB design process is important since a disappointing process may well result in an inferior SDB. Also, a willingness to continue to use the process in the future would be greatly diminished if satisfaction with the process were low.

Finally, a perception of the degree of implementation of the SDB in the organization is an indication of its potential impact on the strategic decisions of the organization [23].

## 4.1.1. Task Setting

The task setting of the experimental study was the design of an organizational Strengths and Weaknesses SDB [28]. This particular SDB is a concise statement of the most significant strengths and the most significant weaknesses of the organization. These identified strengths and weaknesses can be used to guide strategic choice. For instance, the organizational strengths that have been chosen to be included in this SDB provide criteria that can be used to judge the merits of proposed strategies. The same SDB can be referred to in later phases of the planning process to suggest alternate strategies, to aid in strategy selection and to guide the development of plans for implementing chosen strategies [13].

The ideation phase of the SDB design process may be characterized as akin to “brainstorming” [10]. The evaluation phase of the process is a process of choosing a relatively small set of the most important information items from the larger set that is so generated.

## 4.1.2. Subjects

Ninety-five MBA-level students taking an upper-level Management Policy course served as subjects in the study. The subjects were enrolled in three sections of the course which were being taught by two different instructors. The Management Policy course was considered a “capstone” course in which students were expected to integrate and apply concepts from prior coursework in the analysis and solution of strategic management problems. All students had completed a Strategic Planning and Control course prior to entering the Management Policy course.

The experimental study complemented the Management Policy course objectives in that it provided the subjects with an opportunity to actually engage in one stage of a strategic management process using a case study of an organization faced with the strategic choice of whether or not to enter a new potentially rewarding market for its innovative product.

## 4.1.3. Case Study

The case study used as the basis for the organizational strengths and weaknesses SDB design process was chosen for the experiment in light of four criteria, on each of which it was judged by the experimenters to rate highly:

1) Balance of qualitative and quantitative data.

2) Importance of the identification of crucial organizational strengths and weaknesses as a basis for decision making.

3) Currency of data in terms of its contemporary subject matter.

4) Moderate length of case.

## 4.1.4. Experimental Design

The experimental design is shown in Figure 2. Two treatment groups were involved: Teams (T) of individuals who worked together to produce a consensus on an SDB; and individuals (I) who worked independently (i.e., without interacting). Because of the problems associated with communication, teams may tend to suffer a “process loss” compared to individuals in terms of actual productivity.

Team size was chosen to be five based on the finding of Bales [1] which showed that this size is small enough for meaningful interaction and large enough to generate an adequate number of ideas for problem solving.

The study design was constrained by the necessity of drawing the subjects from a university course setting. Hence the design must be considered “quasi-experimental” [28]). Three sections of the course, each of a different size, were involved. Within each section subjects were randomly assigned to groups using a section roster of student names and a table of uniformly distributed random digits to nullify such effects as may be attributable to personality variables (e.g. “cognitive style”; cf. [11], subject sociability, attitudes toward risk, and the subject’s preference to work alone or in a group [10]. Any bias attributable to the effect that different instructors might have on the subjects was judged to be negligible since the Management Policy course was a capstone course which all students were required to take after completing most other MBA program course requirements, and since at the time of the experimental exercise only three weeks of the course had elapsed out of a total fifteen weeks over which the course was conducted.

In order to conduct the experiment in an orderly fashion, each section was assigned to one of the two treatments. Care was taken not to reveal this fact to the subjects so that all subjects from all sections believed they were doing the same things in the same manner during the exercise. Thus, participants in the I-treatment were formally assigned to groups but did not operate as a “team” and did not engage in any form of group activity. The actual student teams in the T-treatment may be characterized as newly created (i.e., low cohesive), heterogeneous, and problem solving/decision making groups [33].

## 4.1.5. Experimental Procedure

The experimental procedure followed the phases shown in Figure 2. Each course section met separately. The conduct of a section meeting was determined by the treatment to which it had been assigned. The experimenter went over the exercise instructions, emphasizing the nature of the exercise question (“What are the case company’s strengths and weaknesses?”), and explaining what was expected of the subjects and the steps in which the task would be accomplished. The subjects were asked to think of themselves as case company managers during the course of the exercise. The experimenter asked one subject in each team to synchronize his watch with the experimenter’s so that a later time measurement could be taken. In the I-treatment each subject was asked to do the same.

During Phase 1 the subjects were directed to silently and independently develop their own ideas about the case company's strengths and weaknesses and to record these ideas on tablet paper provided. The subjects had been earlier assigned the task of reading the case material prior to the exercise. After 10 minutes had elapsed, the subjects were directed to stop work.

During Phase 2 the T-treatment participants were directed to interact together with their team members to record a comprehensive list of strengths and a comprehensive list of weaknesses. I-treatment participants were directed to develop their personal comprehensive lists of strengths and weaknesses, i.e., to continue working as in Phase 1 but to begin explicitly separating their ideas about strengths and their ideas about weaknesses into separate lists. After 12 minutes had elapsed, the experimenter interrupted work and directed the teams/individuals to make a decision about what in their opinion were the five most important strengths and the five most important weaknesses of the company relevant to the strategic decision at hand. The subjects were informed that the results of their decision-making would constitute their team's/own SDB. The subjects were also directed to record the time of day at the bottom of their lists when they were satisfied with their results. After 12 additional minutes had elapsed, the experimenter halted work.

During Phase 3 all subjects individually completed questionnaires dealing with the confidence, satisfaction and implementability measures prepared for the exercise. Each subject completed each questionnaire.

The experimental sequence is structured in a way to allow for individual and team distinctions in the collection of data. The sequence is similar to the “estimate – discuss – estimate” sequence presented in [7] to add structure to an interacting group.

Data on each of the evaluation measures were collected in the following manners:

(1) Data on the number of ideas generated during the SDB design process (VAR1) were compiled by a count of the total number of ideas on the composite lists of strengths and weaknesses for each team/individual. No interpretation of what constituted an idea was made by the experimenter, but rather an item listed by the team/individual was counted as one idea.

(2) The quality of the designed SDB (VAR2) was determined by an impartial panel of three experts (all professors of strategic management who were familiar with the case study) who used an agreed-upon scaling system to assign a point value to each of the designed SDBs. The higher the point value assigned to a SDB, the higher its quality was considered to be. The panel of experts was considered to be necessary because care must be exercised when considering the results of group evaluation when what is being evaluated is the result of own-group process. Ferguson and Kelley [5] and Worchel, Lind and Kaufman [34] found that groups tend to overevaluate the worth of their own-group product. This caution appears appropriate in the case of the SDB design process because designers may be left to draw their own conclusions about their own-group SDBs.

(3) The time for the SDB design process to be completed (VAR3) was determined by noting the time Phase 2 was begun and comparing it to the time recorded by the team/individual when Phase 2 was completed. Total time elapsed for the process was measured in minutes.

(4) Data on confidence in the designed SDB, satisfaction with the SDB design process, and perceived implementability of the designed SDB were collected using 7-point Likert scale items (1 = low, 7 = high) that were responded to during the third phase of the exercise. The items concerning confidence in the designed SDB and satisfaction with the design process were adapted from [18]. Data were collected on the subject's confidence that he/she would use the SDB in developing a strategic plan (VAR4); the subject's confidence that other managers would use the SDB in developing a strategic plan (VAR5); and the subject's satisfaction with the SDB design process (VAR6). The items concerning the perceived implementability of the designed SDB were adapted from [24]. Data were collected on the subject's perception of the impact of the SDB on his/her or other managers' job performance as corporate planners (VAR7); of the amount of communication between SDB users and SDB designers (VAR8); of the clarity and achievability of personal and organizational goals as a result of implementing the SDB (VAR9); of the amount or degree of change in formal organizational structure and communications that will result from implementing the SDB (VAR10); of the extent to which the SDB can be easily understood and used (VAR11); of the extent to which the SDB will help solve a user's strategic decision making problems (VAR12); of the amount of user education and persuasion needed for implementing the SDB (VAR13); of the relationship of expected benefits from implementing the SDB to the expected costs of SDB implementation (VAR14); and of the extent to which environmental events may be expected to have an impact on the implementation of the SDB.

The data indicate that the SDB team design process is superior to the SDB individual design process in terms of the number of ideas generated about organizational strengths and weaknesses as well as in terms of the quality of the designed SDBs. However, the time required to achieve these favorable results is significantly longer for the SDB team design process than for the SDB individual design process. In terms of the implementability of the designed SDBs, the impact of the designed SDBs on the manager's job performance was perceived as significantly lower by the participants in the SDB team design process than by the participants in the SDB individual design process. Also, the need for user education and persuasion during the implementation of the designed SDBs was perceived as greater by the participants in the SDB team design process than by participants in the SDB individual design process.

## 5.1. Discussion

The results indicate that the SDB team design process accomplishes the major objectives suggested by King and Cleland [14]. It produces significantly more ideas relating to organizational strengths and weaknesses than does the SDB individual design process. Also, the quality of the team-designed SDBs is higher than that of the individual-designed SDBs. There are two basic explanations of this superiority: (1) groups can bring a greater sum total of knowledge and information to bear on a problem; and, (2) groups can provide a greater number of approaches to a problem. As Feldman and Arnold ([4], p. 494) observe:

By bringing these points of view together, not only is more information brought to bear on the decision but also more different types of information. Each person's assumptions can be tested by others.

This “group advantage” appears to be the basis for the claim by King and Cleland [14] that the SDB design process produces a “more objective” collection of ideas which might not otherwise be achieved.

With respect to the quality issue it should also be noted that the results support Osborn's [21] contention that quantity of ideas begets quality and that brainstorming groups produce a larger number of ideas than do brainstorming individuals. Caution must be exercised, however, in drawing conclusions from these results. Some research succeeding Osborn [21] has found that individuals generate more creative, imaginative ideas than do groups [2,31]. Yet, while individuals are better than groups in generating new ideas, groups outperform individuals in evaluating them [9,32]. It appears that the process of evaluating the generated ideas is critical to the production of quality. The results indicate that the SDB team design process is effective in this respect, especially when team members are permitted to generate ideas on their own prior to entering into team interaction.

However, these accomplishments come at a cost measured in terms of time required for completion of the process. Hall, Mouton and Blake [8] argue that a decision from a context of group interaction is emergent since it represents more than a simple combination of member contributions or a reflection of the best member's effort. Part of the "emergence" however is a loss of productivity attributable to the group process [27]. The significantly longer time for process completion for the SDB team design process is probably attributable to the need for communication and coordination which is not present in the SDB individual design process.

This attribution would also help to explain the significant difference with respect to VAR13 – the perceived need for user education and persuasion during implementation. Those who participated in the SDB team design process presumably had a more definite perception that implementations of the designed SDB in an organizational context would require greater amounts of user education and persuasion than did participants in the SDB individual design process. Since much important organizational decision-making occurs in a group context, the perception seems justified and may have been a product of the team experience.

The team experience may also explain why participants in the team SDB design process perceived their designed SDBs as having a lesser impact on a manager's job performance than did participants in the individual SDB design process. Since the team experience involved all team members and the output of the SDB team design process was a single SDB, the participants may have perceived that the impact of the SDB would be diffused through all team members. On the other hand, participants in the SDB individual design process may have viewed their SDB as all their own and not something to be shared with others in an organizational context.

This “individual conceit” may also be responsible for the nonsignificant results for VAR4 and VAR5 which concern the confidence issue. Given the results concerning the number of ideas generated and the quality of the SDB, we would expect that the SDB team design process participants would exhibit a higher confidence than the SDB individual design process participants. Actually there are two ways of viewing these results: Did the SDB individual design process participants overrate the value of their SDBs, or did the SDB team design process participants individually underrate theirs? The collected data does not allow for a resolution of this question, although it is clearly important from a behavioral perspective.

## 6. Conclusions

No one study's results can conclusively give sanction to any proposed management technique nor can they conclusively condemn it. The authors believe that this study's results indicate that the SDB team design process proposed by King and Cleland [14] is a viable and useful management approach to determining information relevance to an organization's strategic management process in a number of important respects.

From a technical perspective, the SDB team design process results in a greater quantity of ideas relating to organizational strengths and weaknesses than does the SDB individual design process. The quality of the ideas is also higher for the SDB team design process. From a behavioral perspective – and this must be construed as a judgment call – the SDB design process generally produces good confidence in the designed SDBs, good satisfaction with the process among process participants, and good perceived implementability of the designed SDBs. However this judgment must be tempered by the concerns about team process loss and the possible effects of attributions concerning user education and persuasion during SDB implementation, the impact of the SDB on managerial performance and confidence in the SDB on the implementation and use of the SDB. Of course, since the instruments used to measure subject responses have not been validated using conventional psychometric techniques (e.g. [20]), these results must be considered subject to qualification in light of possible subject response bias. Also, it should be noted that the results may only apply to the context of designing on organizational Strengths and Weaknesses SDB and may not be directly applicable to other types of SDB. Lastly since the study was conducted in a laboratory context, there is no guarantee that the same results would be obtained in a real world setting.

In closing, since we adopted a minimal definition of a group as two or more persons who interact in a manner in which each person influences and is influenced by each other person [25], it is worth noting that the popular critical success factor (CSF) version of the more general

SDB process is a group-based technique that often involves only a manager and an analyst. “The CSF method focuses on individual managers and on each manager’s current information needs” ([22], p. 85). Further study evaluating the CSF method under the circumstances where a trained analyst is involved in an iterative refining of ideas is needed to determine more conclusively which of the two approaches is the more valuable in the strategic management context.

However, since the CSF method as discussed by Rockart, 1979 is primarily individual-oriented, it seems reasonable to expect that the objectivity which is characteristic of the SDB team design process is not easily achievable with the CSF method. This is so because where the SDB team design process involves the interaction of team members in the identification of, and exchange of, ideas, the CSF method is based on an interview process whose success appears dependent on the ability of the analyst to elicit information ideas from the manager and to “raise” the manager above the level of the individual. Our results suggest that in the area of information requirements analysis for strategic managers, the SDB team design process succeeds in identifying a set of ideas which represent the information needed at this organizational level which is superior to that identified by individual managers in terms of both the quantity and quality of the ideas. Further, if a trained analyst were to join the SDB team design process, it is quite possible that the results of the design process may show even greater improvement, since groups led by trained discussion leaders produce better results than those without trained discussion leaders [16–19,35]. This seems a good issue for further research on information requirements analysis for strategic managers and the process used to determine them.
