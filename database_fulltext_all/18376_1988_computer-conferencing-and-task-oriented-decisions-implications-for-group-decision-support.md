# Computer Conferencing and Task-Oriented Decisions: Implications for Group Decision Support

Jill Y. Smith

Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business Administration, University of Denver, Denver, CO 80208, USA

Michael T. Vanecek

Department of Business Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, University of North Texas, Denton, TX 76203, USA

An experiment was used to compare the impact of two very different communication modes during task-oriented group decision making activities. Subjects completed a task-oriented decision making activity using either face-to-face (simultaneous) or computer conferencing (nonsimultaneous) communication modes. Each subject had insufficient task information. Hence, subjects had to share task information for the group to reach a decision. The study used a temporal theoretical framework. The independent variable was communications mode. The temporal dependent and intervening variables consisted of the amount of information shared; the significance and comprehensiveness of the shared information; and, perception of progress made towards goal and freedom to participate. Communication quality was the outcome dependent variable. Data collection consisted of tape recorded sessions, computer generated transcripts, task questionnaires, and interviews.

Keywords: Group decision support, computer conferencing, task oriented decision making, interpersonal communication.

## 1. Introduction

Rathwell and Burns [17] stated, “A useful framework for understanding decision making processes in organizations is based on communication”. Our research develops a research framework and examines the communications process during task-oriented group decision making. In addition, it investigates the effectiveness of using nonsimultaneous computer conferencing as a GDSS tool. Nonsimultaneous computer conferencing means parties participate at the times they chose. ## 2. Group Decision Support Systems

The focus of decision support systems (DSS) has evolved from individual decision making to the broader concept of group decision making [1,11,13,17]. Group decision support systems (GDSS) support people joined together in a decision making activity.

The term GDSS includes all systems designed to assist group decision making. This includes, as Rathwell and Burns discuss, computer-based decision systems used by geographically separated decision makers. Rathwell and Burn emphasize the need for coordination and cooperation in a distributed decision making (DDM) system, where groups, not necessarily linked in a hierarchical manner, cooperate. A DDM system may integrate several independent DSSs, computer conferencing facilities, and project management software. For example, Bui and Jarke [2] describe a preliminary effort to combine DSS, GDSS, and computer conferencing.

Group make-up and tasks for GDSS vary. Tasks might include coordinating projects, determining goal priorities, co-authoring a text, responding to crises, and resolving conflicts $[7,10,20]$ . Group members may be tightly integrated and perform similar work such as in a university department. Conversely, group members may be relatively diverse.

Several characteristics are common to both groups. Individual decision making is often coordinated with, and constrained by, other people.

For example, professors share information, examine alternatives, and resolve conflicts to assign scarce resources among requests for travel, research, and academic support. Similarly, in business marketing decisions must coordinate with production schedules.

Another characteristic is the relationship between individuals of the group. Often, the groups are decentralized and peer oriented. Also, decision making may be via task groups (in a matrix organization) or through a committee. This may make coordination, control, and conflict resolution difficult to achieve [e.g., 22].

## 3. GDSS And Task-Oriented Decisions

Successful task-oriented decision making requires that participants share information, generate alternatives, and reach a consensus. Potential communication problems may arise in this process due to a lack of time to consider all the facts. Time pressures may hamper reflective thinking. External circumstances may also impede communication. For example, human differences, such as personality and demographics, inequality in power position, and differences in vocabulary – professional jargon – may impede communication.

Nonsimultaneous computer conferencing may alleviate some of these problems, because group members are able to choose when to participate. They may gather materials and think in a reflective and comprehensive manner; personality and power variables may be less influential. On the other hand, it may be more difficult to concentrate, and misinterpretation of others' comments may be more likely. Lack of instantaneous response may stifle conversation that helps to discover new insights. In summary, the influence of nonsimultaneous computer conferencing on task-oriented group decision making activities is unknown. Moreover, there may be a strong linkage.

## 4.1. Antecedent-communication Variables

This category includes environmental variables which are present before and during the communication interaction. Classifying variables as antecedent means that they must exist in order to start communications.

Human attributes variables include personality traits, cognitive, and demographic characteristics. Personality traits encompass cognitive problem-solving style, cognitive complexity, need for achievement, internal-external control, assertiveness-passiveness, anxiety, dogmatism, and leadership style. Interpretation of information depends, in part, on an individual's background. Therefore, demographic factors such as economic level, race, sex, age, intellectual ability, educational level, and so on can affect information sharing communications.

A person's power position may also impact communication effectiveness. This may occur when one party in a communication interchange wields more power. Imbalance in power may be due to official position, de facto political power, or technical superiority.

A semantic gap can result from the special vocabularies developed by persons in different professions. These semantic gap differences may impede communications resulting in reduced group decision making productivity.

The intervention by a third-party change agent skilled in organizational development methods can influence the interaction and positively enhance effective communication. This approach has been valuable when diverse decision making groups must make task-oriented decisions during development projects. Loftin and Moosebrucker [15] report success in using change-agent methods for IS implementation. DeSanctis and Courtney [6] recommend a team approach which includes end users, system analysts, and a change agent as a communications facilitator.

Communication occurs with both task and socio-emotional activities. Task activities, such as exchanging ideas, gathering, organizing, and sharing information are more technical in nature. Socio-emotional activities describe interpersonal relationships, positive or negative. Examples of positive ones include giving aid, relieving tension, understanding, and asking for another's opinion. In contrast, the negative ones include withdrawal, displaying antagonism, and critically evaluating another's contribution.

Individuals engaged in task-oriented group decision making have information available to them which they presumably will exchange during interaction. However, individuals will vary substantially in the information they have and are willing to share. Sometimes group members may overlook pertinent information or consider it not important.

## 4.2. Communication-mode variables

Communication mode refers to the channel used for the communication. The channel may be one or more of the following: face-to-face, hard-copy written, telephone, or teleconferencing. Teleconferencing includes computer conferencing, as well as audio and video teleconferencing.

## 4.3. Post-communication Variables

The post-communication variables are an intermediate assessment of the interaction. It includes the amount of shared information which is a measure of the amount of shared data meaningful to the recipient. Also included are the productivity dimensions. Leather $^{3}$ [14] defined communications productivity as the “ideational quality” of the decision or solution to a problem. His productivity rating instrument defined it as a measure of the degree to which the solution is based on ideas that are effective, feasible, creative, significant, and comprehensive. His definitions of these are:

(1) Effectiveness: Degree to which the ideas help the group achieve their objective of a realistic solution.

(2) Feasibility: Degree to which the decision or solution reflects social reality consistent with public attitudes.

(3) Creativity: Degree to which the decision or solution reflects original ideas not previously used to solve the problem.

(4) Significance: Degree to which the decision or solution is based on significant information as opposed to irrelevant or insignificant information.

(5) Comprehensiveness: Degree to which the decision or solution reflects a response to all dimensions of the problem.

The last post-communication variable, the satisfaction dimension, is the result of a literature search conducted by Heslin and Dunphy [8] of 450 studies. They pinpointed three factors as ubiquitous dimensions to group member satisfaction. The source of the satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) results from the communication interaction as opposed to the decision. The factors are:

(1) Status agreement refers to the agreement or disagreement among group members as to who, within the group, has the most status. In general, member satisfaction varies directly with consensus.

(2) Perception of progress toward goal is the group members' perception of the value of the problem-solving session in the progress made toward stated goals and objectives. Satisfaction depends more on perceived than actual progress.

(3) Perceived freedom to participate is the salient relationship between participation and satisfaction. Specifically, it is each group member's perception of freedom to contribute and not the degree of participation. Heslin and Dunphy define it as, "The opportunity to speak when a relevant idea comes to one's mind."

## 4.4. Outcome-result Variables

These variables are the final temporal product. Once the group reaches a decision, its communication quality is one measure of group productivity. It has two dimensions. One is how closely the decision is to an optimal. The other is the degree of convergence experienced by group members. Convergence refers to the level of mutual agreement and understanding among group members after making a decision.

## 5. Research Design

An asterisk, in table 1, marks each of the variables selected for measurement using an experimental design research strategy. Communication mode was operationalized using face-to-face and computer conferencing. The selection criteria for the temporal variables consisted of their feasibility and practicality. Feasibility limited the variables to those that could be measured using the available subject population. Practicality focused on the possible experimental results - whether they might be useful for GDSS planning.

## 5.1. Hypotheses

The study had 6 sets of hypotheses. Hypotheses H1 through H5 stated there would be no significant difference between the face-to-face and computer conferencing groups as measured by the post-communication variables. These included the amount of shared information (ASI), the significance (SI) and comprehensiveness (CO) of the shared information, and in the perception of both progress toward goal (PG), and freedom to participate (FP). Each of these variables represents a facet of interpersonal communication.

Hypotheses H6a and H6b examined the communication quality (CQ) of the communication interaction. It consists of two components. CQ1 is the deviation from the correct answer and CQ2 is degree of convergence. We wished to determine if the communication mode (CM) or any of the communication process variables (ASI, SI, CO, PG, FP) might effect, independently or in some combination, communication quality. With hypotheses H6, the post-communication or communication process variables are independent (or intervening) variables and communications quality is the dependent variable. Hypotheses H6a and H6b posited no difference in the combined effects of the six independent variables (CM, ASI, SI, CO, PG, and FP) on each dimension of the dependent variable, communication quality.

## 5.2. Methodology

The research design consisted of a laboratory experiment. The design paired subjects into two-member groups (dyads). Each dyad shared information and cooperatively solved a case problem. The control group met in a face-to-face mode. Their communications were tape recorded. After completion of the research task, dyad members completed questionnaires, both individually and as a group.

The experimental group used a computer conferencing GDSS. This was simulated by using a computer lab equipped with thirty-one MIME 740 Micro-Term terminals. A local area network provided a connection to the University of North Texas NAS 8040 mainframe. Subjects used a 3270 full screen text editor on the Multi-User System for Interactive Computing (MUSIC) system. Each dyad partner had separate files. Only the owner had read and write privileges; the partner had read only access. Each dyad partner could read new additions to the other's file only after the owner saved the file. Only nonsimultaneous computer conferencing occurred; no real-time simultaneous communication occurred during the two hour experimental session.

## 5.3. The Subjects

The subjects were recruited from about 850 students in an introductory communication course. The course was open to all majors. The experiment occurred early in the fall semester. Eligible students met two conditions. They answered yes to the question, "Is English your first language?". They also had a 2 or greater self-reported response, on a 9-point, typing-skill scale. The first forestalled any language problems. These may otherwise have confounded the results. The second assured the subjects had a basic familiarity with the typewriter keyboard. This lessened training and made the task easier for the experimental subjects in the computer conferencing mode. From the eligible students, subjects were randomly selected and assigned to a communication mode, an experiment time, and a dyad partner. Each communication mode had 33 dyads.

## 6. Case Description

The group task-oriented decision making activity consisted of reading a case study, culling it for relevant information, differentiating important from unimportant data, and sharing it with a dyad partner. Each had to consider competing alternatives and select the correct one.

The objective in selecting the task was to reject cases which favored a particular discipline (e.g., finance). However, we wanted to retain the information-search, alternative generation, and decision making intellectual processes found in task-oriented decision making. The task had to be simple enough for all subjects to complete. In a recent series of experiments, Jarvenpaa, Dickson, and DeSanctis [12] found that their original IS-related tasks were too complex and harmed internal validity. We decided a general knowledge, task-oriented case would control for an instrumentation interaction artifact. Specific selection requirements for the cooperative, decision making case were:

(1) The problem had to be task-oriented and objective. This allowed subjects to concentrate on information exchange (as opposed to the subjective, socioemotional communication interaction).

(2) Student subjects from various disciplines had to be able to accomplish the task in the allotted time frame. Task difficulty was not to be a confounding variable.

(3) All information to solve the problem had to be available in the case.

(4) The case data had to be divisible among the dyad members in essentially equal parts.

(5) The problem had to have one correct answer. A definite reason (Lased on the information available) to reject alternative answers had to exist.

The case chosen was a murder mystery. There were two case versions, each identical, except for five unique clues. There were seven murder suspects and the dyads selected one suspect as the murderer based on the information they shared. The information was the embedded clues. To solve the case accurately, dyad members had to share all their clues. The case, entitled “Murder One: Information Sharing", was adapted from a published case in Pfeiffer and Jones [16].

## 7. Measurements

Table 2 summarizes the data sources and measurement techniques used for each variable studied. The information available (IA) consisted of ten clues, equally divided between the two partners. Given the ten clues, the number of relevant clues exchanged was the amount of shared information (ASI). ASI was measured by reading printed computer transcripts of the computer conferences and listening to tape recordings for the face-to-face group. Grading bias was minimized, because the specific clues were predetermined (e.g., age of suspect) and, therefore, not particularly vulnerable to interpretation.

Dyads had to identify the correct suspect and provide a list of specific reasons why each of the remaining six suspects was not guilty. This was to determine the significance (SI) score. Six reasons (representing optimal SI) existed for clearing the six suspects. Correctly identifying the guilty person resulted in a point. Hence, the maximum SI score was 7.

Comprehensiveness (CO) measured how many problem-related dimensions, or case attributes, the dyads considered important in solving the case. The six predetermined case attributes were: height, weight, age, blood type, location at 7 p.m., and location at 10 p.m. Dyads recorded their decisions using a semantic differential scale. Assessment of CQ2 (degree of coherence) began by computing an absolute difference score between dyad members for each of the seven guilt-perception scales. The total of the absolute difference scores represented CQ2. The smaller the score, the greater the degree of apparent coherence generated by the communication interaction.

## 8. Data Analysis

The collected data was initially screened for usability and reliability, and then scored. Cronbach's alpha [3] was used to test the reliability coefficient for the PG, FF, and guilt-perception semantic differential scales. Several scale items were dropped from further analysis as a result of the test. As a result, the reliability coefficient for the seven guilt-perception scales ranged from 0.85 to 0.93. The PG and FP scales each had a reliability coefficient of 0.75.

Following these procedures, the hypotheses were tested. H1 through H5 were tested using the SPSS-X Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) programs. Stepwise Multiple Regression was used to test H6a and H6b. An alpha of 0.05 was used.

## 9. Discussion of Results

The MANOVA results for hypotheses H1 through H5 run counter to Rice and Williams' [18] synopsis of previous studies assessing the value of nonsimultaneous computer conferencing for sharing information. They concluded that users generally have accepted computer conferencing for information exchange. However, it is not clear whether these studies were limited to a nonsimultaneous mode. Nor is it clear whether these studies focused on problem-solving as well as information exchange.

Casual information sharing, or responses to information requests, constitute a different user context than the mandate to solve a task-oriented problem by sharing information at a distance. Results of the present study suggest that non-simultaneous computer conferencing has a detrimental effect on (1) thoroughness of information exchange (ASI); (2) relevance of the information shared in terms of making the correct decision (SI); and (3) comprehensiveness of the consideration given to problem dimensions (CO).

The finding that progress toward goal (PG) also suffered in the CC mode supports conclusions made by Hiltz et al. [9] and Williams [21] who found that dissension was more evident when communicators were geographically separated. Perception of progress in task accomplishment may deteriorate when communicators must wait for a response or receive a reply that was not quite what they expected. The latter situation requires another inquiry and a new wait for a reply. Perusal of the CC transcripts suggests that some shared clues were not noticed immediately by the dyad partner. This may have been due to the lack of immediate feedback which, in turn, may have impeded one's perception of PG.

Freedom to participate (FP) has not previously been assessed. Hiltz et al. focused on participation and concluded that dominant individuals are more apparent in the F-t-F mode. They interpreted this finding to mean that less dominant individuals may feel freer to express themselves in a computer conference mode without nonverbal pressures. In our study, the participation was not measured.

Instead, FP was a measure of one's perception of freedom to express a thought, if desired. In that context, individuals who contributed less may have perceived their FP high, they just did not desire to contribute more than they did.

As table 3 illustrates, FP was also significantly lower in the CC condition. This result, however, is difficult to interpret. Maybe FP was hampered due to nonsimultaneous communication and the result had little to do with the lack of nonverbal pressures. For example, since communication was indirect, verbal partners may have felt frustrated and this may have lowered the FP score in the CC mode.

Table 4 reveals the Stepwise Multiple Regression (SMR) results for hypothesis H6a. The deviation score from the correct answer, CQ1, was very significantly (p < 0.001) related to the significance (SI) of the discussion and to the communication mode (CM). Further, it appears F-t-F conferencing may enhance CQ1. The amount of shared information (ASI) also was related to CQ1 in the simple correlation matrix (-0.515). However, the SMR procedure bypassed ASI because the inclusion of this variable did not explain sufficient additional variation than already explained by SI.

Comprehensive information exchange may enhance communicators' ability to create pertinent and supportive arguments for task-oriented decisions. However, having this information at hand does not mean that it will be perceived as useful. It is the perception and use of information that is related to communication quality (lack of deviation from correct answer).

Recognizing the moderate correlation (0.507) between SI, CM and CQ1, only tentative conclusions are warranted. One is that decisions based on germane reasons may be a primary objective in task-oriented group decision making. Furthermore, nonsimultaneous computer conferencing may not be a desirable alternative to the traditional F-t-F meeting for task-oriented, decision making sessions.

Table 5 displays the results of SMR for hypothesis 6b. As displayed, the amount of shared information (ASI) and the progress toward goal (PG) are highly related (p < 0.001) to convergence (CQ2). However, the actual correlation is small (0.239). This is of small practical value. Yet it is reasonable that these two variables relate to CQ2 (mutual agreement and understanding). That ASI is related to CQ2 is consistent with the theoretical model presented by De Brabander and Thiers. They cite mutual agreement and exchange of all available information as twin criteria for a successful decision to proceed or halt a IS development.

## 10. Experimental Limitations

All the MANOVA results were significant (p < 0.001). However, two possible confounding variables interacting positively with CM may have affected the results. First, nonsimultaneous computer conferencing GDSS was simulated using MUSIC and the IBM 3270's editor. Subjects asked few system-related questions during the experimental sessions. However, the system was not geared for novice users and may have impacted the results. Second, the nonsimultaneous conference occurred in an artificial laboratory setting. An often cited advantage of nonsimultaneous conferencing is that participants are able to log on and contribute when they wish. That was not the case with this study. The subjects could communicate only during the fixed two hour experimental session. This prevented subjects from discussing the case outside the laboratory. However, the absence of a natural environment and a formal conferencing GDSS might be an experimental artifact.

Many researchers suggest GDSS applications are suited for unstructured decisions; however, our research used a structured decision. Of course, there is some routine information exchange in unstructured decision making environments. Our reason for using a structured decision context was to place more control over the variables. Semi-structured or unstructured cases would not allow direct measurement of the amount of shared information nor of the significance (correct reasons) for decisions made. Yet, the suitability of computer conferencing for semistructured and non-structured decision making needs further investigation.

## 11. Research Contributions

This research makes several contributions which will be expanded in the future. One contribution is the use of a general knowledge case and the selection of naive subjects (naive about business problems) to study the communication components of GDSS's. This represents an alternate approach to that used in some IS research. Typically, IS researchers have sought a test case which represents a recognizable business problem (albeit in a simplified form). The result is that one criteria for subject selection is their knowledge of a functional area.

The general case approach is valid to the extent that the variables are not tarnished by not having a business domain environment. Since the essential processes of task-oriented decision making (i.e., information gathering, information filtering, alternative generation, and alternative selection) were preserved, our use of a general knowledge case is justified.

In addition, the use of naive subjects has merit if their use has minimal effects on the interpersonal communication variables. Specifically, their use results in research design simplifications. Using naive subjects circumvents research problems associated with determining which end-user business domain to study and which persons will qualify as knowledgeable (usable) subjects.

Another contribution made is the structure of the experimental case to facilitate measurement of several elusive variables. For example, the amount of information available was controlled and the amount of shared information was recorded and counted. The number of relevant case attributes (comprehensiveness) and the specific reasons for rejecting competing alternatives (significance) were predetermined.

Another contribution is the novel approach used to measure the research variables. Rather than score a diametrically right or wrong decision, the measure was a deviation from the correct answer (CQ1). This allowed us to observe how close the dyad came to the right decision. Also, although admittedly crude, convergence (CQ2) was assessed indirectly – without asking subjects directly whether they agreed with and understood their partner's position.

A final contribution, and perhaps the ultimate value of this research, is the temporal theoretical framework. There is a plethora of interpersonal communication models available. However, to the authors' knowledge, this framework is an unique extension. Further, it is amenable to studying interpersonal communication variables as they are influenced by alternative modes of communication.

## 12. Conclusion

The research reported here describes an exploratory empirical experiment in the use of non-simultaneous computer conferencing communication for task-oriented group decision making. Nonsimultaneous communication has been proposed as a communication-mode component of a GDSS, particularly when users require task coordination and task problem solving between geographically distributed decision makers.

To form a research base, a temporal framework was developed from previous research in interpersonal communication, sociology, and IS. Several variables were tested in a laboratory experiment. The design compared decision making and communication processes between groups communicating face-to-face and using nonsimultaneous computer conferencing.

The experimental results cast doubt on the efficacy of nonsimultaneous computer conferencing communication as an equivalent communication mode to face-to-face for task-oriented group decision making. Typically, research raises more questions than it answers. The present study is no exception and the authors plan to extend this study to a field experiment using larger group sizes and a more sophisticated computer conferencing system.
