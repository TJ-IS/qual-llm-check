# Recognizing the Politics of MIS

Varun Grover, Albert L. Lederer

and Rajiv Sabherwal

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

MIS development and implementation is a highly political process where users and developers may be more concerned about furthering their self-interests than about contributing to their organization. At the same time, MIS professionals have often been accused of being oblivious to this political process and being unaware of the ulterior motives and hidden agendas that their users actually follow. This political process can be viewed as a system of games. A typology, adopted from Bardach and from Keen, describes the games. In-depth interviews with eighteen MIS practitioners produced numerous examples of these games and thus confirmed that practitioners can recognize them during MIS development and implementation. Furthermore, the interviews provided some practical implications for MIS professionals who know that they must face the political machinations within their organizations but do not necessarily know how to recognize them. The interviews also laid the groundwork for future investigations.

Keywords: Management of computing and information systems. System development, System implementation, Politics, Resistance, System success.

User resistance to management information systems is common. It is often due to a design based on faulty conceptualization or a deficient human interface. However, even the finest design does not assure the success of an information system. In numerous cases, expensive and elaborately-designed computer-based applications have failed miserably [17]. As Davis and Olson concisely described this seemingly capricious state of affairs, “In one organization, a well-designed system fails; a similar but poorly designed system in another organization succeeds” [7, p. 593].

## 2. Success and Failure

Intuitively, MIS researchers and practitioners might recognize a successful MIS as an easy-to-use system that makes a contribution to their job.

Likewise, an unsuccessful one would be difficult to use and would provide worthless information. However, despite this intuitive evaluation, MIS researchers and practitioners commonly use three means to formally identify such success and failure.

First, a positive financial return can indicate a successful project while a negative return can indicate a failure; this measure reflects such systems objectives as the reduction of costs $[8]$ or the increase in revenue $[4]$ . Secondly, heavy system utilization can show success while lack of utilization can show failure $[13]$ ; this is especially appropriate to systems where utilization is voluntary. Finally, high user satisfaction might demonstrate success while substantial user dissatisfaction might demonstrate failure $[10]$ ; this is more appropriate where utilization is mandatory.

These three means have been extensively used as actual, concrete measures of success. However, they convey meaningful information only after the completion of system implementation. They offer little assistance to managers during the development and implementation process [9].

This lack of assistance during the development and implementation process suggests the need for an additional, complementary approach. Keen [11] suggested such an approach. Without operationalizing (i.e., quantifying) it, he observed that excessive delays during the process may portend implementation failure. He focussed on the potential for the territorial interests of managers and others to cause various adverse effects (described in section 4) and resulting delays.

Due to excessive delays, resources could be wasted and a project might exceed its budget. The associated, exorbitant costs could lead to its failure.

Moreover, the delays might result in project cancellation. Resources would have been expended with no visible return. A potentially effective project would have failed. In that case, financial return, user satisfaction, and utilization would not have recognized the failure.

In short, excessive delays, wasted resources, and project cancellation can signify failure, and therefore, the completion of implementation can, to some extent, indicate success.

## 3. Game Playing as a Description of Resistance

Effectiveness in MIS implementation is a critical determinant of information system success.

However, to be effective, implementation requires significant organizational commitment. Failure to obtain this commitment leads to project resistance via political maneuvering. Because such maneuvering can influence the fate of MIS implementation, politics is an important factor to understand. One way to understand it is to consider it as comprising a system of games.

The foregoing argument is now briefly elucidated.

## 3.1. Commitment

Ginzberg [9] identified three issues which are critical for successful MIS implementation. Two of them relate to commitment. First, both users and management must develop a commitment to a project. Second, there must be an organizational commitment to change. Commitment reflects the willingness to change both behavior and procedures. Such changes are necessary for a new system to work. The lack of commitment and lack of willingness to change are the precursors of indifference and deliberate resistance to implementation. $^{1}$

## 3.2. Resistance

According to Markus and Pfeffer [16], deliberate resistance to information system implementation occurs when the power distribution implied by a proposed system is incongruous with that determined by other sources of power. In other words, those who expect to gain power support implementation while those who expect to lose power resist it. Markus [15] further argued that people sometimes resist system implementation not simply because of design features (technical quality, ergonomics, etc.) nor solely due to personal factors (cognitive style, personality traits, human nature, etc.). She observed, “To design systems that will not be resisted or to devise ways to modify resisted systems, the technical system analysis must be augmented with a social or political analysis…” [15, p. 441].

## 3.3. Politics

The importance of a political perspective for analyzing IS development and implementation is now increasingly recognized by scholars in the field [11,21]. One way to view the politics is to consider the development and implementation process as a system of games. Several authors [1,2,11,18] subscribe to this view. Mintzberg [18] further asserted that the best way to characterize a system of politics is as a collection of “goings on” or as a set of games [18, p. 187].

A game can be defined as an “ongoing series of complementary ulterior transactions, progressing to a well-defined, predictable outcome … often repetitious, superficially plausible, with a concealed motivation” [5, p. 48]. With regard to implementation, Bardach [2] observed that “the implementation process is (1) a process of assembling the elements required to produce a particular programmatic outcome, and (2) the playing out of a number of loosely interrelated games whereby these elements are withheld from or delivered to the program assembly process on particular terms” [2, pp. 57–58]. He also maintained the importance of understanding these games in order to better respond to the politics of implementation.

Conventionally, a game is thought of as having players, rules, a scoring method, and winners and losers. In the MIS context, users, managers, analysts, and programmers can be seen as the players. The prescriptive approach to systems management suggested by the system development life cycle [14] can be viewed as the rules. In the MIS context, the scoring process is one-time: Winners would be the players who support the completed implementation of a successful project or who resist a project which fails. Losers would be the players who support a project which fails or who resist a project which is successfully implemented.

## 4. The Classification of Games

Bardach [2] classified his implementation games into four categories depending on the games' major adverse effects. $^{2}$ Table 1 concisely defines 12 games. The categories are now briefly discussed.

Table 1
The Games.

Up for Grabs
Various parties try to take charge of a project if the decision that started the project was ambiguous or half-hearted.
Pilling On
Initial success of a system implementation leads onlookers to throw their own goals and objectives onto the heap. As a result the scope of the project itself might change and it might eventually fail to meet its original objectives.
Massive Resistance
Parties which see the MIS project as a threat to their power and influence might obstruct its implementation by withholding certain critical elements.
Easy Life
A party which is in advantageous position in comparison to the other parties, resists changes in order to maintain its comfortable position.
Tokenism
The parties attempt to appear to be contributing to the project publicly while actually conceding only a small (“token”) contribution.
Territory
When an MIS project has significant overlaps with the jurisdictions of a number of different parties, these parties might attempt to extend their control and power by taking it over.
Reputation
The actors seek out means of persuading an audience that they are doing more or doing better than they really are, or seek a reputation for boldness, toughness, or innovativeness.
Tenacity
The parties delay the progress of the project until their own particular terms are satisfied.
Not Our Problem / Their Fault
If a party sees the project as imposing a heavy workload or raising controversies and thus considers it unattractive, it may try to shift certain operating responsibilities to other parties. This “Not Our Problem” game might be followed by a more aggressive version - “Their Fault”, in which actors seek to deflect any prospective blame for project failure.
Odd Man Out
In this game, the player creates an option to withdraw if the project gets into trouble and then the chance to say “I told you do”.
Easy Money
Taking advantage of the unclear goals or ambiguous specifications of an MIS project, some parties support the project because it can be used to finance some needed activity within their own sphere of interest.
The Budget Game
The players “maximize” their budget, the stated manpower requirement, or hardware/software demands etc., to protect or extend control.

## 4.1. Deflection of Goals

The stated goals of an MIS project might be ambiguous. This might permit or necessitate their further definition. However, because the parties opposed to the project may have chosen to remain quiet when a consensus was reached to initiate it, this consensus may be fragile and temporary. The opposing parties might, during the implementation process, attempt to redefine these goals. This redefinition could cause a trimming down of the goals, changing them, or even adding to them in a way that could result in implementation failure due to the overburdening of the project. Deflection of Goals includes two games, "Up for Grabs" and "Piling On".

## 4.2. Dilemmas of Administration

The MIS implementation process requires the assembly of a number of diverse resources from various parties. These resources may be tangibles such as hardware, software, funds, personnel, etc. They might also be intangibles such as skills, willingness to participate, commitment, etc. The parties might take advantage of this dependence by threatening to withhold their support and thus forcing the managers to either meet their demands or to proceed without their resources. This category, Dilemmas of Administration, includes "Massive Resistance", "Easy Life", and "Tokenism".

## 4.3. Dissipation of Energies

Here individuals and groups waste considerable energy either by avoiding responsibility or by attempting to gain power and control. They try to set up situations that facilitate their own game playing strategies and that protect themselves from similar attempts by other parties. These games lead to project delays and poor performance. Dissipation of Energies embraces “Territory”, “Reputation”, “Tenacity”, “Not Our Problem/Their Fault”, and “Odd Man Out”.

## 4.4. Diversion of Resources

MIS projects involve many organizational resources as described above. The games in this category result in deflection of these resources away from useful causes and towards the achievement of the parochial objectives of the individual parties involved. Diversion of Resources comprises “Easy Money” and “Budget Game”.

## 5. The Study

The objective of the study, as mentioned earlier, was to determine whether practicing MIS professionals can confirm the existence of the games within MIS development and implementation. Therefore, it required qualitative information about the content and context of the previously described games. In addition, research and theory are in their early formative stages in this “sticky practice-based” area [6]. In such situations, Benbasat, Goldstein, and Mead [3] have suggested that qualitative research methods are appropriate. Hence, interviews provided a fitting method of data collection.

## 5.1. The Interviews

The interviewees received a two page description of the 12 games two or three days before the interview. Table 1 comprised a major part of the description. Because the games represent an unusual way to think about MIS development and implementation, the interviewees were given the advance material. Then they could think about their personal encounters with these games before the interviews without wasting time during them.

The same two interviewers conducted all of the sessions. They began the sessions with a brief explanation of the purpose of the research. They asked the interviewees to point out all games that they had definitely encountered during their work experience. Then, for each game, the interviewers attempted to elicit information about the events leading up to the game, the parties involved, their maneuvers, and the impact of the game on the project.

The interviewees had the full opportunity to repudiate the games framework. They could have denied that such games were played, and could have stated that users and implementers act frankly and candidly with the concerns of their organizations as foremost and their personal concerns as secondary. The interviewees had no present or possible future obligation to any of the investigators. The investigators had little more control over the interviewees than written questionnaire researchers have over their subjects.

Each interview lasted about an hour. As implied above, the subjects, themselves, classified their experiences into the 12 games. During the course of the interview, no attempt was made to debate the subjects' original classifications. The interviewees' active role in the categorization is essential to the conclusions discussed later. Had the interviewees been unable to identify the games, the primary purpose of the study would not have been met.

After each interview, the interviewers reconciled their notes into a systematic format. When all the interviews had been completed, the instances of each game were collated to serve as the basis of the following discussion.

## 6. The Games

This section applies Bardach's theory of games to the interview data and describes one representative example of each game. Each subsection begins with a sample motivation. $^{3}$ A brief expansion of the definitions from Table 1 follows. An example selected from the interviews comes next. Each subsection concludes with a discussion of the adverse effects of the game suggesting how it might have contributed to a failure. Each game is presented within its category.

## 6.1. Deflection of Goals

6.1.1. Up for Grabs: "If they don't know what they want, we'll take over".

In some cases, the decision that initiates the development of an information system is ambiguous or half-hearted. As a result, a number of different parties fight to gain control of the few ambiguously assigned program elements with the intention of later converting them into useful political resources.

In a large multidivisional organization, ar outside consulting group recommended an Office Automation System (OAS). However, the objectives of the system were not stated clearly. The Human Resources Development (HRD) group was responsible for the funds that had been allocated for this system. They realized that a human resources system could be developed utilizing microcomputers and the OAS funds. Therefore, they wanted control of the OAS project. On the other hand, the MIS group had the expertise to develop the OAS system and felt threatened by the HRD group. Thus a conflict of interests between the MIS and HRD groups arose because each wanted to gain total control of the project. A considerable delay ensued.

The OAS, instead of meeting its original objectives, was eventually absorbed by a corporate level human resources system project.

Both parties involved in this example, the MIS group and the HRD group, had different interests and were competing for total project control at the start of the project. The result was a considerable project delay and a modification of the scope of the project. Therefore, this example illustrates a project, “Up for Grabs” for the two parties involved. The game ultimately led a “Deflection of Goals”.

6.1.2. Piling On: "Let's do it right! - We have to make sure our interests are included in the project".

When an MIS implementation project meets with initial success, some parties see it as a new political resource, an opportunity to throw their own goals and objectives onto the heap. The game causes the “Piling On” of a number of additional objectives, thereby changing the nature of the project and eventually causing its failure to meet its original objectives.

A Fortune 500 manufacturing company had an IBM mainframe computer at its headquarters and Honeywell minicomputers at its plants. The plants also used the mainframe, however, and were charged for their use. A new MIS project provided an inventory control system on the minicomputers at the plants. After the project succeeded, a plant manager saw the possibility of using the minicomputer to lower his costs and, more importantly, to reduce his need to use the mainframe. This would increase his power and control by diminishing his dependence of headquarters. With this objective, he moved his more sophisticated applications (materials requirements planning, job scheduling, shop floor planning, etc.) onto the minicomputer. The additional demands overloaded the minicomputer and the performance of the inventory control system deteriorated.

Here, the plant manager used the inventory control system as a political resource to reduce his dependence on headquarters. The transfer of local plant applications onto the minicomputer resulted in a considerable modification of the original scope of the inventory control project. The example therefore illustrates how “Piling On” produced a “Deflection of Goals”.

## 6.2. Dilemmas of Administration

6.2.1. Massive Resistance: "We don't believe in it and we're not going to let it work".

If an MIS project appears to some parties as a threat to their power and influence, they might obstruct its implementation by withholding critical elements. Massive Resistance may be the product of conscious coordination or of numerous uncoordinated and independent efforts. The resistance could be caused by inertia, by a disinclination to learn, by stories or rumors about problems associated with the new systems, or by the fear of new technology.

In a Detroit school district, 15% to 20% of the teachers reported sick everyday. The district superintendent initiated a computer-based project with a program for substitute teachers. This project required that secretaries come in one half hour earlier everyday and therefore, they were dissatisfied. Also, the principal of an important school was not initially involved in the project and was resentful. He used his own powers along with those of the secretaries' union, to demand a central processing facility for the system. In contrast, the district superintendent and his data processing team were advocating a distributed processing system. The strong and collective opposition from the principal and the union eventually caused the project to be deemed infeasible.

In this example, the principal consciously coordinated his efforts with those of the secretaries' union to oppose the project. This "Massive Resistance" cause "Dilemmas of Administration" for the district superintendent, as the project either had to be done in accordance with the views of the opposition or not done at all.

6.2.2. Easy Life: "Make sure we're in charge and don't let outsiders cause trouble; take it slowly".

This game is usually played by a party holding an advantageous position. It resists change to maintain its comfortable position. The reasons usually given against the project are that it demands too much effort, it leads to numerous unnecessary changes in operations, or even that it does not benefit the organization.

In a software development company, the programmers never prepared adequate documentation. They were also hesitant to reveal information about their software. As a result, they alone knew the application system well. Therefore, when demands were made for better documentation and for the use of more sophisticated and easy-to-learn software development tools, the programmers felt threatened. They strongly resisted the demand for better documentation on the grounds that it required excessive effort. They also opposed a second demand for the use of more sophisticated software development tools by arguing that these would require numerous changes. Both demands were eventually dropped due to the programmers' strong opposition.

In this example, the programmers resisted change to maintain their comfortable, advantageous position. Their “Easy Life” stance led to “Dilemmas of Administration” for their management who could not implement the improved documentation without the support of the programmers.

6.2.3. Tokenism: "Let's do the most small items, rather than doing the big ones".

Some parties might fail to endorse a proposed MIS development strategy. However, they might at the same time realize that the project has top management support. In such a case, these parties might attempt to appear outwardly to be contributing to the project while actually conceding only a small (“token”) contribution. Other variations of Tokenism are the procrastination in making any contribution or the substitution of a contribution of inferior quality.

A bank had subcontracted with a software development house to provide computer programs for processing deposits. The bank adamantly pressed the software house to deliver their work rapidly. The software house responded by concentrating on items which required little effort, rather than on the more important ones. It then claimed major progress and it pressed the users to expedite further specifications. The resulting system did not work and the bank eventually sued the software house.

Software development contractors played this game because they were unhappy with the alacrity demanded by their client. By providing an inferior contribution to the project while publicly claiming progress (i.e., by playing “Tokenism”), they were creating “Dilemmas of Administration” for the bank because it could not evaluate their progress. It had to accept their work based on its outward appearance or cancel the project.

## 6.3. Dissipation of Energies

6.3.1. Territory: "This is our job". "We think we should run the project since ..."

Certain MIS projects have significant overlap with the jurisdiction of different parties. When such a project appears to be successful, the parties might fight to control it in an attempt to extend their control and power. The game differs from "Up for Grabs". "Territory" is usually played in the later stages of a project when the project appears to be successful, whereas "Up for Grabs" is played in the early stages when success is uncertain.

In a large manufacturing company, the engineering group used a manual system to acquire design data. Top management decided to replace it with one using electronic links and microcomputers for data acquisition and display. However, the responsibilities and control of the new system were not made adequately clear. When the project proved to be a big success, a power struggle ensued between the engineering and MIS groups. The engineering group felt that it should control the system since it had been acquiring and using the data before automation. On the other hand, the MIS people wanted control on-the grounds that the system utilized their tools and expertise.

In this game, two organizational units attempted to gain control of a successful project and to extend their power instead of directing their attention towards organizational goals. The effort that went into the power struggle for “Territory” led to a considerable “Dissipation of Energy”.

6.3.2. Reputation: "I want an integrated-on-line-real-time-data-base-management-distributed-processing-planning system. My system will ..."

In this game, the actors seek out means of persuading their audience that they are doing much more than they really are. More specifically, they may seek a reputation for boldness, toughness, or innovativeness. Players of “Reputation” make moves which are very closely aligned to their projected reputation.

The MIS director of a manufacturing company portrayed a tough exterior and dealt very firmly with user requests. This image was considered helpful in forcing the users to realize that they could not take the MIS group for granted, should give the specifications properly, and should not ask for too many changes. The users eventually responded by sending their requests to some other executives who pooled them together and then discussed them with the MIS director. The reputation of the MIS department suffered as a result of this “ganging up” by the users. The MIS director was eventually fired.

The major actor in this game, the MIS director, had designed a tough image for himself. The relationship he maintained with the users was consistent with this image. To counteract this “Reputation”, the users had to devise a clever means of gaining approval for their requests; normal channels would not work. The users’ efforts to organize and unite led to considerable “Dissipation of (organizational) Energy”.

6.3.3. Tenacity: "No." "One more time." "We're not happy about..."

In this game there is usually no disagreement that the MIS project should be implemented. However, there is a disagreement on the distribution of responsibilities for project implementation. The parties rigorously adhere to their position and delay the progress of the project until their own particular terms are satisfied.

A commercial bank began a major software development project. Normal procedures required periodic meetings of the managers of a batch programming group and an on-line programming group for decisions on project specifications. While the meetings did convene on a regular basis, not much was accomplished, due to the batch group manager's consistent objections to any proposal made by the manager of the on-line group. The primary basis of the objections was the distribution of responsibilities between these two groups. Repeated meetings took place before any compromises could be negotiated. This resulted in considerable delay and feelings of hostility between the two parties.

In this example, the two parties indulged in arguments and negotiations about project responsibilities, thereby wasting valuable organizational energy on causes useless to the organization. Therefore, the playing of “Tenacity” led to “Dissipation of Energies” and further delays.

6.3.4. Not Our Problem / Their Fault: "Marketing really ought to handle this".

During the implementation of an MIS project, the parties responsible for certain elements might realize that their responsibility will impose a heavy workload on them or raise controversies. If the project thus appears unattractive, the concerned party might try to shift some of the responsibilities to other parties. This “Not Our Problem” game might be followed by a more aggressive version, “Their Fault”, in which the actors seek to deflect any prospective blame for project failure.

The corporate MIS group of a large manufacturer was to install a new purchase order system in one of its regional plants. It had requested that the plant manager make certain preparations for the installation, before the DP team arrived. When the DP team did arrive, they found that the preparations had not been made. Even though the team could have put in a few extra hours and installed the system, they refused to do so. Instead, the team returned to the corporate office and blamed their failure to carry out the project on the plant manager.

Here, the DP team shirked its responsibility and indulged in mudslinging. By refusing to install the system (“Not Our Problem”), and then blaming the project failure on the plant manager (“Their Fault”), they wasted considerably energy (“Dissipation of Energies”).

6.3.5. Odd Man Out: "We're certainly interested and we'll be happy to provide some inputs, but..."

There may be considerable uncertainty about the success of an MIS project. As the project progresses, this uncertainty may increase or decrease. The players of "Odd Man Out" attempt to create or maintain their option to withdraw and cut their losses if the project appears likely to fail. At the same time, they continue to be involved with it in order to benefit from its possible success.

A reputed software development firm recommended one of its experimental office automation packages to a large university. The software firm wanted the university to beta test the package. The Computing Resources (CR) department of the university initially agreed. However, during implementation, uncertainty increased about the appropriateness of the software package. The CR department became reluctant to commit its time and resources to the beta test. It therefore started finding faults with the software. It also started subtly requesting that the software firm install the package on the condition that the university could return it without completing the beta test if the package did not meet expectations. The university eventually rejected the package and returned it to the software firm.

In this example, the CR department started playing "Odd Man Out" when they saw that project success was uncertain. They subtly manipulated the software firm by creating their option to withdraw in the event of failure while still maintaining a degree of commitment to the project. This extensive, unnecessary monitoring of the project and playing of "Odd Man Out" led to considerable "Dissipation of Energies".

## 6.4. Diversion of Resources

6.4.1. Easy Money: "Get a little more than we give back".

Skilled players of "Easy Money" capitalize on the unclear goals or ambiguous specifications of some MIS projects. They support a project because it can be used to finance some other necessary activity within their own area of interest. These parties have a different set of priorities and support the project only to “get a little more than we give back”.

In a large technical college, the responsibilities for library facilities and computing resources were under two separate groups. The chief librarian learned that a significant portion of the college's computing budget had been allocated to the computing resources group rather than to her library facilities group which she wanted to enhance. Thus, when the Dean proposed the unification of the library and computing groups, she ardently supported him in order to get the resources for her own sphere of interest.

The chief librarian's only reason for supporting the proposal was to facilitate the movement of funds to improve her own facilities. Thus, the game of “Easy Money” permitted the use of resources for parochial interests and therefore illustrates the “Diversion of Resources”.

6.4.2. The Budget Game: "We never turn down money".

The traditional Budget Game involving moving money quietly and quickly from one account to another even at the cost of project objectives. The players “maximize” their budget to protect or extend their control. Players might also maximize their stated manpower requirements and hardware or software demands.

In a large manufacturing company it was traditional practice for the MIS department to inflate its annually proposed budget by 15% to 20%. This was done by overstating the requirements in the area in which MIS management wanted flexibility (e.g., personnel, microcomputers, canned software, etc.). Due to its perceived strength, the MIS department usually received its requested allocation of funds.

Because the players of the "Budget Game" pad their budgets, they can employ useful organizational resources for their special interests. The game therefore illustrates the “Diversion of Resources”.

## 7. Conclusion: Implications for Practitioners and Researchers

This study has made several contributions to MIS practice and research. Through the reported instances of the games, the study confirms that MIS professionals can perceive their existence. The study also shows that the games typology serves as a means of identifying situations in which political maneuvering is taking place. Finally, the study serves as a stimulus to investigators to examine political maneuvering and to attempt to understand its implications in MIS development and implementation.

## 7.i. For Practitioners: The Usefulness of the Typology

The interviewees responded without great difficulty to the request to identify games. When questioned, they spoke effusively about the players' hidden motives and devious strategies, sometimes well beyond the allotted one hour time period. Hence, the typology served as an excellent means both of recognizing the politics of MIS development and implementation and of eliciting the interviewee's accounts.

Nevertheless, careful readers might challenge this observation. They might assert that the maneuvers in many of the aforementioned cases may have been a justified response to poor quality (or to expected poor quality) rather than a reaction to threats to the political interests of the players. In such cases of poor quality, users are obligated to work constructively with implementers to correct the inadequate system [19].

Keen [11] dealt with the question – was the resistance due to poor quality or to threats to political interests – by suggesting that implementers initially anticipate and predict some specific amount of delay. Later, if the implementers respond to the resisters' stated objections with improved quality, and the delays continue beyond the predicted amount, then the implementers would reasonably suspect that political interests, rather than poor quality, motivated the delays.

Thus the definitive answer to the question (was the resistant due to poor quality or political interests?) is rarely known with absolute certainty. Although the investigators accept the interviewees' interpretations and categorizations, different participants in this article causes might have perceived the situations quite differently. However, by diligently following Keen's prescription about anticipated delay, practitioners would be more confident that they had correctly separated genuine quality concerns from self-interest motivation. (The source of the delay suggests that appropriate action: Either correct the inadequacy of the information system or respond to the political interests.)

On a related matter, the subjects' facile responses strikingly contrasted one expectation. That is, it had been suggested that MIS professionals lack political awareness [11]. However, not only did the interviewees recognize game playing but in addition, MIS professionals were the protagonists in several games! By implication, MIS professionals are not completely politically naive. Still, the question lingers: Are MIS professionals as politically savvy as their peers within their organization's functional departments?

If they are not (or if they feel that they are not), then the typology might serve as a means of raising their political awareness. In fact, several interviewees spontaneously acknowledged that the typology and the subsequent interview had enabled them to consider the politics of implementation in a new light. Hence, MIS professionals might study the typology and its illustrations and attempt to recognize their own similar experiences. Recognition of hidden motives and devious strategies can be a first step before applying techniques to prevent future games or favorably resolve current games. Two such techniques, scenario writing and fixing, have been prescribed by Keen [11].

## 7.2. For Researchers: The Need for a More Robust Typology

In this study, the interviewees classified their experiences into any one of the twelve games on the basis of brief narratives describing the games. While the use of interviews to facilitate the classification was particularly appropriate in this study, future research should attempt to use more rigorous and conventional methods. The ability of MIS practitioners to identify the games in this study suggests that these methods might be feasible although they might not provide such rich examples as the interviews did.

Another issue related to the classification quickly became evident to the investigators during the examination of the interview results. This was the subjectivity of the interviewees' classification. Interviewees might have designated many of their experiences as representative of one or more different games. By implication, the categories are not exclusive. Hence, the typology is clearly not parsimonious.

These concerns suggest the need for a new typology. Its theoretical basis would be of paramount importance. (Although the theoretical basis of game playing is clear, the basis of the existing typology is not.) A new typology would need to be derived from new or existing theory and need to be verifiable through empirical data. Potential causes, rather than adverse effects, might serve as its basis.

The typology would need to accommodate the variety and richness of interpersonal behavior. It would have to reconcile the fact that games can be played by all parties, simultaneously and sequentially. Furthermore, it could provide great utility. It might help researchers better understand the politics of development and implementation. Most important, it might suggest the means of prevention and resolution of games.
