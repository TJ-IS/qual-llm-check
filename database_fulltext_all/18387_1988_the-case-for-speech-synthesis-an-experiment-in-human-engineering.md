# The Case for Speech Synthesis: An Experiment in Human Engineering

Randalii G. Sleeth, A. James Wynne

and Gerald S. Saunders \*

Virginia Commonwealth University, School of Business, Richmond, VA 23284-4000, USA

Despite expected widespread use of computer speech synthesis in manufacturing and production applications, no one has yet determined the effects of synthesized speech on task performance. This laboratory study introduced speech synthesis in replicating parts of two previous experiments that had used human-voice feedback. Both instructions and productivity feedback were examined for their effect on arithmetic manipulation tasks. Findings show that there are interaction effects; it appears necessary to develop profiles of persons and conditions which predict how computer speech synthesis may be used most productively. We must attempt to determine the way that speech synthesizers may replace and enhance the communication process, while maintaining high motivation of people who interact with these machines.

Keywords: Speech synthesis, Feedback, Communication, Computer-assisted manufacturing, Human factors, Productivity, Motivation, Work ethic, Human engineering, Psychological warmth, Leadership style.

In one case, the management of a grocery store chain installed a speech synthesis system in their supermarket cash registers to complement the laser scanners reading universal product codes (UPC) from markings on the product. Information was used in the cash register and data processing functions, e.g., inventory control and accounting. In some other cases, implementations seem successful, despite lack of proper planning or study.

Thus, organizations are often implementing systems without knowing their impact upon the human beings they are serving [14]. Speech synthesis applications will benefit from examination of the same organizational variables that confront a worker attempting to perform the functions; but this assumes a conceptually simple substitution of the computer for a human voice and does not address fundamental differences in the nature of the process. Productivity gains due to widespread implementations of speech synthesis will depend, in large part, upon research into the appropriate manner for employing it. Objectives should include not only replacement but enhancement of the communication, while maintaining levels of task motivation among the users. Prior to taking risks with their customer bases, organizations would benefit from a thorough understanding and accommodation of speech synthesis by their own employees. Thus, two related questions warrant immediate study: (1) What are the effects of synthesized speech feedback upon human task performance? and (2) What are the effects of synthesized speech instructions upon performance of subsequent tasks?

Despite the acknowledged importance of various forms of feedback on performance, motivation, and satisfaction with work, literature reviews are generally critical of the lack of understanding about how the process of feedback actually operates. In a thorough review, Ilgen, Fisher, and Taylor [5] stressed the importance of three aspects: (1) source of feedback, (2) feedback frequency and sign, and (3) individual differences among recipients.

A questionnaire survey found that the supervisor was viewed as an important source of feedback in determining job requirements, while self and task were important sources of feedback about meeting job requirements. The authors' interpretation was that sources which are "... intrinsic, or psychologically closer to the individual, are seen as providing more feedback information than those sources identified as external or psychologically distant" [2]. Ilgen et al. noted that credibility of an external source may be important in differentiating the impact of external and internal feedback. An external source gains credibility when a recipient assesses that the feedback is appropriate to his or her own needs.

There are two somewhat overused rules: (i) Frequent feedback is uniformly better, and (ii) Negative feedback is both hard to deliver and hard to accept. However, too-frequent feedback may be perceived as over-controlling, an effect which is likely to cause opposition to acceptance. Further, emotionally oriented feedback has produced differences in the relative acceptance of positive and negative feedback [6]. Overall, increased feedback which does not threaten recipient self-control is generally seen as desirable in the workplace. But there are limitations on the proper administration of negative feedback. Stopping or changing behavior logically requires negative feedback. Yet, it tends to be accepted only from “high-status” sources [3], and “… supervisors’ reluctance to give subordinates negative performance feedback seems likely to affect both the timing of that feedback as well as its accuracy” [11].

Recipient characteristics appear to interact with source, frequency, and sign of performance feedback in determining recipient reactions. For example, in contrast to low-self-esteem subordinates, high-self-esteem subordinates were found to rely more on their own self-perception than on their job environment for behavioral guidance [16]. Although not directly addressing feedback, this finding tends to support a personality effect for the notion of the “psychological closeness” of the feedback source. There is evidence, however, for acceptance of and even positive effects for negative feedback. Underachievers were not inhibited by negative feedback in the form of blame [8]. High endorsers of the Protestant work ethic (PE) responded quite well to negative feedback [1]. Scores on the Protestant Ethic Scale (a) were positively associated with authoritarianism and expectancy for internal control (personal control over outcomes in one’s environment), (b) when high were associated with choices of occupations demanding a concrete, pragmatic approach to work, and (c) when low were associated with choices of occupations typically requiring emotional sensitivity, theoretical interests, and humanistic values [12].

On a simple, repetitive task, high Protestant ethic endorsement and positive feedback were generally associated with high performance. However, the high PE/negative feedback subjects showed considerably greater performance improvement than subjects in the five other conditions. These were: high PE and (1) neutral feedback, (2) positive feedback; low PE and (3) negative feedback, (4) neutral feedback, (5) positive feedback. Some evidence of this interaction effect was found in a survey of holders of loans listed with the U.S. Small Business Administration. In this study, owner-managers facing conditions of negative feedback (i.e., financial problems with their business) showed greater potential for business recovery and performance increase when they were high PE endorsers. $^{1}$

Instructions prior to a task may benefit from such considerations. However, it is better that the instructions be viewed in a leadership context. The effectiveness of leadership style depends on the presentation mode. With current synthesis technology, it is possible to investigate subtle leadership effects through the impact of instruction content and voice on selected performance characteristics. Specifically, the dimensions of voice warmth vs. coldress, and imperative style vs. nondirectiveness can be integrated into the form and content of the synthesized speech. Using voice alone to convey leader attitude would generally yield a much weaker treatment than voice plus eye contact, gestures, and body language in humans. Research by Tjosvold [15] found an interaction between warmth and directiveness of human leaders in explaining subordinate performance on a subsequent task. Warmth was associated with highest performance when coupled with directive instructions and associated with lowest performance when coupled with non-directive instructions. The two other conditions (cold/directive and cold/non-directive) were of intermediate effectiveness.

In the directive approach, leaders provide detailed direction and assistance to get the job completed; they give instructions and specific guidance to subordinates. In the non-directive approach, leaders give little direction but allow subordinates the autonomy to work on the task independently. ...Warmth conveys interest, openness, and friendliness, whereas coldness conveys distance and disinterest [15, pp. 422–423].

Directiveness may result in lowered subsequent motivation and performance if subordinates resent the closeness of the supervision [10] or see it as unnecessary [4]. This is particularly true on simple, repetitive tasks in which directions from a leader may become overbearing and redundant. The leader faces a problem of making task performance appear important to subordinates. In this case, leader warmth may induce acceptance of the leader's values [7]. Leader coldness has a less-clear effect: the acceptance associated with leader warmth may account for both high and low performance, depending upon the level of production requested (high for directive and low for nondirective).

In summary, existing findings point to feedback source, feedback type, and recipient characteristics as interacting factors in determining performance response to feedback. Computer speech synthesis may provide a means of capitalizing upon beneficial aspects of these interactions.

In principle, computer-synthesized feedback provides the potential for feedback that is timely (immediate), recipient-controlled, objective, credible, accurate, and possibly “psychologically closer” as an integral part of the task environment.

This study examined the combined effect of source of feedback (synthesizer versus oral), type of feedback (positive versus negative), recipient characteristics (PE endorsement), type of voice (warm versus cold), and leadership style (directive versus nondirective) on the performance of simple tasks. Such tasks were chosen to reduce the effect of ability and therefore to make it possible to assume that performance was primarily a function of effort (motivation).

## 2. Method

A laboratory experiment was designed to examine the effects of synthesized speech feedback and instructions on task performance in a controlled environment. Seventy-four undergraduate business majors enrolled in a course in organizational behavior volunteered to participate. All students had previously completed a test battery which included the Mirels and Garret [12] Protestant Ethic scale. The final sample size consisted of sixty-nine sets of complete data, five subjects being dropped due to incomplete data and procedural errors.

As urban students in a university school of business, these students generally have some computer literacy, at least part-time employment, and are similar to the typical employees of today. They may be considered entry-level people likely to encounter computer speech synthesis as a substitute for supervisor instruction and feedback. As shown in Table 1, subjects represented a work force of persons possessing some work experience, including supervisory responsibilities. In sum, these subjects were considered a representative sample of persons soon likely to encounter synthesized speech in their work environment.

All subjects worked at separate times on the same series of four timed, five-minute tasks. Tasks were performed at a personal computer work station that included the speech synthesizer (Digital Equipment Corporation's DECtalk). The first task simulated quality control observations. Predetermined strings of digits were displayed 36 at a time in a six-by-six matrix. Subjects scanned the 5-digit strings of random digits and entered "Y" of "N" following each string to indicate whether the string contained a pair. Each subject received the same edited sequence of strings containing equal ratios of pairs and non-pairs. Random responses would be correct 50 percent of the time, allowing no "dominant strategy" for playing the task as a game. Such gaming might occur if a subject became severely bored – a specific concern, because this task was designed to be boring.

There were two reasons for employing a simple, boring (repetitive) task. First, this task level removed ability as a factor in performance. Performance was therefore seen as a direct result of effort. Since effort is itself considered the result of motivation, performance on this task was directly related to motivation. Second, such a task allowed observation of the impact of instructions and feedback in prolonging the duration of a subject's willing performance and attention (i.e., the motivational impact).

The second task was a continuation, performed after the subject had received feedback on the first task. Voice feedback on task performance came from either a laboratory assistant in the role of the subject's supervisor or from the synthesizer.

The third task was a modified simple computer game (“Moonlander”) in which a subject used the keyboard function keys to activate “rockets” that controlled the landing of a space ship on the surface of the moon. A large number of possible landing outcomes involved the amount of fuel remaining, speed at touchdown, and geographical location relative to the target.

The fourth task was a set of addition problems using two, two-digit or three-digit numbers, presented one problem at a time. This task was considered the equivalent of the first and second tasks in terms of being boring, repetitive, and not substantially involving ability as a factor in performance.

## 2.1. Validation of Warm and Cold Voices

The subjective nature of the concept of interpersonal warmth allows no objective measure of a voice as warm or cold. Typical experimental manipulations of warmth involve (1) telling subjects a person they will encounter is either warm or cold or (2) engaging in (or avoiding) behavior assumed to be representative of warmth. For example, Tjosvold used spacial closeness and eye contact with subject in addition to body posture (leaning forward, sitting on edge of chair). None of these devices was attempted in this experiment, for the following reasons:

First, the act of telling customers, clients, or subordinates that a synthesized voice they are about to hear is warm seems ludicrous. Second, visual indicants of warmth are simply not available to voice alone. This leaves no clear means of producing the impression of interpersonal warmth beyond the sounds (tone, pitch, inflection, rate, etc.) in the voice itself.

Our designed voices were determined to be warm and cold in two ways. In the first validation, an independent group was presented the synthesized speech instructions to the moonlander task. Three voices were involved: the warm voice, called "Walter," the neutral voice "Paul," and the cold voice "Kevin." Two-hundred-forty students were involved in a total of twelve conditions, one for each possible ordering of the three voices used. Because the concept of warmth is so subjective and personal, there was no attempt to predefine the term for the subjects. After hearing the three voices, each subject simply circled a number on a nine point scale labeled cold (10) through warm (90). For this measure, on average, the warm voice scored 52, the neutral voice scored 49, and the cold voice scored 32. As expected, there were some overlaps and reversals in perceptions. Several subjects scored the intended cold voice as the warmest and the intended warm voice as the coldest. All pairs of average scores were, however, significantly different (P < 0.05) in the desired directions.

For the second validation of the warm and cold voices, 54 additional independent subjects responded to the moonlander instructions [13]. Warmth was measured using a sixteen-item semantic differential scale. Cronbach alpha scale reliabilities were 0.87 for the warm voice and 0.77 for the cold voice. Mean total scale scores and mean items scores indicated consistent and substantial differences in perceived warmth of the two voices (T = 7.04, P < 0.00001).

## 2.2. Design

The experimental design included the five dichotomized independent variables with their levels combined to produce the eight conditions shown in Table 2. Subjects were first classified as high or low PE (median split) and then assigned at random to one of four available treatment conditions (1 through 4 for high PE and 5 through 8 for low PE). Only eight experimental combinations were attempted, due to limited sample size. As a result, all subjects who received negative feedback (after the first task) also received non-directive instructions (for the third task). A full test of all 32 possible combinations was not attempted.

## 2.3. Dependent Variables

Performance measures included the number of right and wrong responses for tasks one, two, and four, and difference between scores for tasks one and two. This difference became the intertrial increase in performance on the quality control task. Task three was a treatment to operationalize directive and nondirective instructions, and no scores were recorded for moonlander landings.

## 2.4. Procedure

A predesigned synthesized neutral voice, called Paul (designed by DEC), introduced the experiment in the same manner to each subject. Use of this voice and message provided consistency and content uniformity to the introduction. In addition, this voice became a neutral referent for the warm and cold voices used later in the procedure.

The two feedback sources (oral and synthesized) and feedback sign (positive and negative) were manipulated during a three-minute break between tasks one and two. Oral feedback was delivered by one of the authors or a graduate student assistant. Both oral and synthesized conditions used identical statements for feedback (after Greenberg): (1) Positive – “I see you are really doing quite well. This is well above average. In fact, you are doing about as well on this task as anyone we have seen;” (2) Negative – “I see you are doing very poorly. This is well below average. In fact, you are doing about as badly on this task as anyone we have seen.” Positive synthesized feedback was delivered by “Walter” and negative was delivered by “Kevin” (both designed by the authors).

Warm or cold voice and directive or non-directive styles were applied to the instructions for the third task ("Moonlander"). All instructions for the third task used the synthesized voice of either "Warm Walter" or Cold Kevin" (represented to subjects only as Walter and Kevin). Directive instructions were detailed and took approximately four minutes to explain the functions of the rockets and targeting of the spaceship landings. Directive instructions included statements such as: "It is important that you do well on this task" and "Now get going and do well." Subjects continued to receive spoken advice and comments after each of the eight landing attempts. For example: "It helps to slow downward movement first"; "The crash completely blew up your ship. You can do better if you concentrate more." Some directive-mode comments were both displayed on the screen and spoken, e.g., "Excellent soft landing. They can't get much softer."

Non-directive instructions were very simple, lacked detail, and took only about thirty seconds. They did not cover details of activating rockets and targeting landings. Subjects in the nondirective mode received only a screen display (no voice) of the brief comments, for example: "Nice job. But you landed far off target." The longer times required to deliver directive instructions and feedback resulted in subjects spending about twenty-five minutes with the directive style and about thirteen minutes with the non-directive style.

For each subject, the same voice used in task 3 introduced task 4, announcing, "This is the last task. For each question, type your answer and press enter."

On this task a subject read from the screen two numbers of length 2 or 3 digits, displayed vertically to be summed, with a question mark in place of the answer. The subject then entered at the keyboard the number chosen as the answer. At this point a new problem appeared, and the process repeated for five minutes.

At the finish of the fourth task, the “Paul” voice concluded the procedure and signaled the beginning of the debriefing. A debriefing questionnaire assessed several participant perceptions ranging from effort expended to interest level and feelings about the exercise. A series of analyses of variance examined interactions and additional hypotheses generated a posteriori.

## 3. Results

Duncan's range test on debriefing questions verified ( $p < 0.05$ ) the following groupings of conditions on main effects: (1) perceived negative feedback versus positive feedback, (2) greater task satisfaction under positive feedback, and (3) higher credibility of the positive feedback. Response scores also indicated that the number tasks were uniformly “boring,” “easy,” and not ones subjects would “want to do again right now.” Performance data on average percent correct confirmed the uniform lack of task difficulty, averaging over 98 percent on each task. Minimum individual percent correct was 92.2 percent (118/128) for task one and 84.8 percent (151/178) for task two. Minimum number of responses included 71/72 correct on task one and 64/67 correct on task two. Maximum number of responses were 218/229 on task one and 274/286 on task two. This paper highlights findings regarding differences between task one and task two (the “quality control” task) and number wrong on task four (the addition task), selected as best reflecting the general results.

Inter-trial improvement in number of correct responses showed F > 1 for two sources of variation. These were FEEDBACK (F = 2.22, ns) and PE × SOURCE (F = 3.40, p = 0.07). The remaining five sources (including interaction effects) were nonsignificant. On the number-adding task, notable sources of variation in number wrong were PE (F = 4.29, P = 0.04), PE × VOICE (F = 4.27, p = 0.04), and PE × VOICE × DIRECTIVENESS (F = 1.17, ns). ## 4. Discussion

Inspection of means suggests several interesting relationships needing more work. Inter-trial task improvement appears unrelated to source of feedback or PE as main effects, while there appears to be the expected slight improvement favoring positive feedback. Of greater note, however, is the apparent relationship involving negative feedback. Low PE subjects seemed to show greater improvement with oral/negative feedback. High PE subjects improved more after synthesized negative feedback. Neither oral nor synthesized source of feedback appears favored for positive feedback. Consistent with these suggestions were the comments from some subjects that they “... could handle the negative comments from a machine but might hit a person who talked to me like that.” If this type of reaction is systematically true, then a synthesizer may aid both reluctant supervisors in delivering negative feedback and reluctant subordinates in receiving and responding appropriately to negative feedback.

On the number-adding task high PE subjects made significantly fewer errors than low PE subjects. With smaller main-effect impacts, non-directive instructions were associated with fewer errors than directive instructions, and the cold voice was associated with fewer errors than the warm voice.

The interaction effect for PE and VOICE occurred for both directive and non-directive instructions. Regardless of directiveness, high PE subjects performed better after a warm voice than after a cold voice, and low PE subjects performed better after a cold voice than after a warm voice.

The previous outcome, finding warmth associated with best performance (directive) and worst performance (non-directive), occurred with the introduction of PE. That is, the most errors among the eight conditions occurred in the condition including warm voice, non-directive instructions, and low PE. Fewest errors occurred for the condition including warm voice, directive instruction, and high PE. All conditions involving the cold voice were associated with an intermediate number of errors. The cold voice produced better averaged performance than the warm voice. Yet, the warm voice was associated with both the two best and the two worst performance conditions of the eight. This is a manifestation of the PE × VOICE interaction. Low PE accounts for the two lowest performance conditions, while warmth plus high PE accounts for the two best. It appears that both voice and personality are involved in the “acceptance” of directive instructions from the synthesizer.

## 5. Conclusions and Recommendations

Several implications are apparent for designing MIS applications using speech synthesis. The noted relationships provide focal points for additional study prior to implementation.

First, for persons who have some minimal amount of exposure to computers, during short work periods the synthesizer appears to create no systematically unacceptable intrusion in the work environment. This implies that when there is already publicly available spoken comment about a task, substitution of a computer for a human voice would be acceptable. This does not imply that emotionally or personally sensitive information will be accepted. Separate study seems necessary in cases where the synthesized voice will make comments which are private or currently not spoken. A clear technical alternative is to receive the speech through a headset.

Second, interaction effects on performance suggest looking beyond simple main effects when considering use of a synthesizer. Performance gains may depend upon differentially targeting it to uses in which it shows improvement over human communication. It might be particularly promising to use synthesized-speech reprimands or other negative feedback for correcting or improving performance. However, the full personality, not just the work ethic, of the person receiving the feedback may be crucial in determining acceptance and the gain in performance. Given the rule of thumb to "criticize in private," details of the setting seem important in realizing this potential.

Third, particular attention should be directed at the general question of relative effectiveness of different speech-synthesized reinforcement schedules. A computer synthesized voice can be made to deliver continuous feedback, and this is clearly not realistic for humans. The apparent novelty of intermittent and shortterm use of the synthesizer may wear off under intensive use. On the other hand, the ability to vary and control voices may indeed prolong such novelty.

Fourth, comprehensive study seems warranted in profiling all elements of the synthesized communication process in order to create productive matches and to avoid unproductive mismatch among individuals, voices, and tasks. This involves going far beyond the technical development of industrial systems. It requires awareness of the workplace behavioral issues of personality, job enrichment, and organizational climate. While these elements may complicate the total picture, such is the nature of human performance. Comprehensive treatment of these issues is not only possible but probably necessary for full realization of the productivity gains available with speech synthesis when applied in systems design and implementation. Workplace applications require both technology and behavioral science.
