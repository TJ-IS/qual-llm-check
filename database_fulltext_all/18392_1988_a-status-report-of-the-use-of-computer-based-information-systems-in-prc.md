# A Status Report of the Use of Computer-Based Information Systems in PRC

Ming-te Lu
College of Business, St. Cloud State University, St. Cloud, MN
56301, USA

Youzin Qiu

School of Management, Fudan University, Shanghai, PRC

Tor Guimaraes

College of Business, St. Cloud State University, St. Cloud, MN 56301, USA

This report presents the results of a survey on the use of computer-based information systems (IS) in the Shanghai area of the People's Republic of China (PRC). It represents an assessment of the current computing practices in the PRC. A total of seventy two organizations participated in the survey. The information reported here include: when computer systems were first introduced, the current applications, makes of systems, system development languages used, the size of the IS department, and its locations in the organization. The report concludes with a discussion about the future outlook of computing in PRC.

Keywords: People's Republic of China, Information systems, Application portfolio, Computing problems.

## 1. Introduction

After the cultural revolution, the People's Republic of China (PRC) adopted a so-called "open policy" to improve its relationship with the outside world. Since the open policy and economic reforms were implemented in 1978, there has been a growing exchange of goods, services, and technologies between the PRC and the rest of the world. Today, the PRC has become an important player in the world economy. In a similar manner to other developing countries, the PRC regards the use of computer technology as a major factor in the effort to improve socio-economic conditions.

One area in which computer technology has proven effective is the use of computer-based information systems (IS) in business organizations. In recent years, the PRC has channeled sizeable resources to acquire more sophisticated methods,

The success of this technology transfer will have an important bearing on China's ability to attain the goal of becoming a developed country in the twenty-first century. Despite the obvious importance of this for PRC and the world, very little has been done to shed light on how it is progressing. The survey on which this report is based was conducted in the Fall of 1987, with the objective of assessing the status of computer-based IS development and operations in firms in the Shanghai area. ## 2. Study Methodology

Organizations included in the survey represent a sample of businesses and governmental agencies in the greater Shanghai area of the PRC. Questionnaires were mailed out to executives from 100 organizations that had sent their upper management personnel to Fudan University, Shanghai for management training. The City of Shanghai has 12 million plus population and is known as one of the most concentrated industrial zones in China. Since Shanghai is not a typical region in the PRC, the survey results are not generalizable over the whole nation, but they should provide insights into the use of IS in more progressive Chinese organizations.

The person who filled out the questionnaire was, in most cases, a high ranking official in the organization. In ninety percent of the cases, the plant manager, the vice plant manager, the chief engineer, or one of the department heads filled out the form. These persons either are knowledgeable about the use of computers in their organizations or have ready access to that information through their peers or subordinates.

As Table 1 indicates, the 72 firms responding to the survey represent a cross-section of industries. In addition to the type of firm, information about its size was collected. Firm size was measured in three different ways: the number of employees, the assets in Chinese dollars (RMB), and the gross production in RMB. While the latter two attributes are straightforward to Chinese managers, western readers should use them with caution. Since a historical cost-based accounting system is used in China, the assets held by an organization are generally undervalued. Instead of sales, gross production amount was often used by Chinese firms because of the nature of their planned economy.

## 3. History of System Acquisition

Viewing the introduction of computer technology as an organizational change, the authors intended to find out whether the role of the change agent would affect the effectiveness of the introduction. Therefore, data were collected on the organizational level at which the decision was made to bring in the first computer system: people higher up, people outside the organization, from top management, by the unit's operations personnel, or by its technical staff. As the survey results indicate, in over ninety percent of the cases the decision was made by the top management of the organization, therefore the intended analysis is not possible.

As Table 2a indicates, the earliest systems were introduced in 1980, two years after open policy was adopted by the Chinese government. Therefore, it is safe to say that business computer use in China was negligible before 1980. The largest number of systems were acquired in 1984 and 1985; they declined in 1986 and 1987 due to the more conservative foreign exchange policy adopted by the PRC at that time.

There is a relationship between the system starting date and the size of the firm (as represented by either the number of employees, the size of the assets, or gross production), at the 0.05 level of significance. The data clearly show that larger organizations were able to computerize at an earlier date.

Systems starting dates generally lag behind the system arrival dates, considerably more than is usually experienced in firms in the fully developed countries. Six systems had a total elapsed time between arrival and operation of one year or longer. In most cases, the exceptionally long set-up time is due to a lack of packaged software, which leads to a widespread requirement for custom programming in application development. Another major reason is the general lack of previous experience with computer technology in business.

## 4. Characteristics of the IS Organization

As expected, there is a significant relationship between the size of the firm (as expressed by the number of employees and total assets) and the IS department size. There is also a significant relationship between the number of computer systems of the firm and the size of its IS department.

The location of the IS department in the organization varied. Sixteen of fifty seven (approximately 28 percent) had their IS department reporting directly to the head of the organization, the plant manager (equivalent to a CEO), in most cases. Twenty six out of fifty seven (approximately 46 percent) have their IS departments reporting to the plant vice-manager, the second ranking official. The remaining fifteen responses (26 percent) were at a lower level.

## 5. Hardware and Software Configurations

IBM PCs or compatibles are the predominant group of computer systems used. Fifty five of the seventy two organizations (76%) have only microcomputer systems. Nine organizations (12.5%) have minis or larger systems. The computers in these nine are: three PDP/VAXs, two IBM 370s, two NECs, and one IBM 34, IBM 4381, and HP3000. Four organizations used supermicros, three use Altos and one Wang.

Since most of the organizations reiy on IBM PCs or compatibles, it is not surprising to find out that dBase and Basic are the most popular system development software used. dBase is used by almost 50% of the organizations.

Due to the predominance of microcomputers, only seven organizations use COBOL. As Table 4b indicates, many organizations used more than one development language.

## 7. User Participation and Satisfaction

Some of the items in the questionnaire dealt with the user role in the system development process. Forty three firms responded to these questions. Twenty nine organizations (64%) reported that users were instrumental in initiating the applications, twenty six firms (58%) reported that users also directly participated in systems development. Thirty six firms (80%) report that users directly participate in system operations. This is to be expected, since PCs are the predominant type of computer systems.

The respondents' opinion on user satisfaction with existing ISs was measured with a Likert scale ranging from -3 (extremely unsatisfied) to +3 (extremely satisfied). The reader is cautioned to beware of bias due to the considerable cultural pressure on the respondents to be positive. Thus response bias is assumed for this case. The average rating is 2.1. No negative rating was reported, and there were only two zeros. Respondents reporting direct user involvement in system operations tend to report greater satisfaction with existing ISs (significant at 0.05 level). There is no significant relationship between respondents having user participation in system requirements definition and user satisfaction. The same can be said about user participation in systems development and user satisfaction.

## 8. Conclusions

Based on the survey results and personal interviews with IS professionals in the PRC, the state of IS use in that country may be characterized as:

Microcomputer-based Systems. Except for a limited number of cutting edge computer applications, such as the People's Daily and Shoudu Iron & Steel Works [2] [3], most of the ISs in the PRC are microcomputer-based. Microcomputers possess the following advantages over minis and mainframes:

Low cost - To many PRC firms, microcomputer systems are the only hardware they can afford. Since microcomputers are being manufactured and assembled domestically, they have the major advantage of not requiring scarce foreign currency.

Low level training required - Microcomputer have much simpler architecture and operating systems. The system and application software have been designed for users with little training and no technical background.

For the above reasons, microcomputers have become a low cost and low risk alternative for Chinese firms willing to experiment with computer technology. Besides these advantages, many PRC firms have difficulty acquiring state-of-the-art mini or mainframes due to the American export licensing restrictions [6] [7].

Lack of connectivity. Most of the computer systems in the PRC are standalone systems. Except for a limited number of large installations, networking is seldom used. This lack of connectivity may be attributed to lack of:

A national telecommunication infrastructure – The present national telecommunication network is incomplete and unreliable.

Local area networks - Acquisition of LANs would add to the already relatively high hardware and software costs. LAN implementation and support would also require state-of-art technical knowledge.

Types of Implemented Applications. Most of the installed applications are for payroll or operational level processing, such as production and financial reporting, inventory control, etc. Most of the applications are in supporting functions, and not in life-stream functions such as order entry or billing. This means that system failure, which happens frequently due to unreliable hardware or power outage, does not create such a business operation problem as it would in large corporations in developed nations. Computer outputs are seldom used for planning and control purposes at the management or strategic control levels.

Apparently, no computer-based decision support systems (DSS) are presently in use. This lack can be explained by the following factors:

Lack of development personnel – Many IS personnel are computer scientists and technicians who generally have difficulty communicating with end-users. DSS are well known for their relatively difficult process of user requirements definition.

Lack of DSS generators - Lotus, IFPS, and other popular DSS generators have been available only in the last few years. Chinese character input/output make their use slow and cumbersome.

Low level of user sophistication - Many end-users prefer to follow the traditional approach and make decisions subjectively. They are either unaware of IS capabilities or are afraid to lose their decision making power.

Few decisions to be made - The hierarchical structure of PRC organizations reduces the requirements for planning and decision making at the lower ranks. Directives are dictated from the top down.

Computer resources management. As has been suggested [5], as an organization becomes more experienced in computing, it shifts focus from computer management to data resource management. While the basic assumptions of the stage theory and its use as a prediction model have been challenged [1] [4], the stage theory generally describes the path to IS maturity.

It is apparent that most of the firms in the PRC are still in the early stages of computing where, the emphasis is on the technical/operational aspects of computing. This is reflected in the names for most IS departments in the PRC, often called the “computer room” or “computer center”. This is also related to the technical background of most IS managers. In the organizations surveyed, there is no attempt at organization-wide planning and management of computing, such as the use of high level steering committees, data administration, etc. Possibly this is due to the relatively small scope of computing in these organizations.

## 9. Future Outlook

Continuation of the open policy in the PRC will result in an influx of state-of-art technology into the country. The move of its government toward a market economy will create a more competitive environment for firms, thus creating a demand for improved decision making and better information. The availability of the technology will push the PRC toward greater sophistication in the use of computer technology. This progress, however, will not come easily. A number of serious obstacles must be overcome. These include:

1. The lack of a reliable national telecommunication network.

2. The establishment of standards to facilitate Chinese I/O, communications, information flow, and computing resources sharing among different organizations.

3. The relatively low computer literacy level of the average business manager.

4. The lack of trained experts on data analysis and systems design methodologies.

5. The lack of IS management training necessary to reduce learning by trial and error.

6. The establishment of software sharing centers to reduce "re-inventing the wheel" among PRC organizations.
