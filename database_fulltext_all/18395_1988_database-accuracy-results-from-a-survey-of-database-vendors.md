# Database Accuracy: Results from a Survey of Database Vendors

Essam Mahmoud

The University of North Texas, PO Box 13677, Denton, TX 76203, USA

Gillian Rice

Rice Mahmoud Associates, 2207 Canterbury Ct., Denton, TX 76205. USA

The accuracy of databases is the subject of our survey of vendors of databases. This study examines external databases. Among the issues covered by the survey are: selection criteria for data sources, formats of data, and the approaches followed in maintaining database accuracy. One specific focus of the study is external databases for forecasting. Forecasting, as a tool of managerial planning, is sensitive to the availability and accuracy of databases. The survey results suggest that there are some practices followed by database suppliers that could produce unreliable information.

Keywords: External databases, Database accuracy, Database evaluation, Business planning, Business forecasting.

## 1. Introduction

Advances in computer technology have meant a great increase in the capacity to communicate electronically and this has been accompanied by a growth in the availability of electronic data sources for use by businesses. By the early 1980's there was a growing maturity and acceptance of database systems. Most organizations were directing their major systems development toward on-line data base systems [22]. These developments have contributed to improved efficiency in managerial planning.

This study addresses the accuracy of databases that are used in planning. A particular focus is the use of external data for forecasting. Decision-makers, of course, use both internal and external databases; they have little or no control over the accuracy of the latter. As an element of planning, forecasting is particularly sensitive to database quality. The more widespread use of external databases means that it would be useful for managers to have some understanding of the accuracy and reliability of databases and how they are maintained. Here we report some results of a survey of database vendors. Among the issues covered by the survey are: the data collection methods of the suppliers; the types of data collected; the formats in which data are available; and the approaches followed in maintaining data accuracy.

## 2. Data Accuracy and Forecasting

Forecasts are vital in helping to answer the questions posed by planners. These concern the future direction of competition, the needs of the customer, the likely behavior of competitors, and how to gain a competitive advantage [17]. Thus, examples include sales, market share, industry, economic, and social and technological forecasts.

The evaluation of databases is a significant part of the development of a forecasting decision support system (DSS). Indeed, Lawrence [8] stressed that one of the objectives of a quantitative forecasting system is assistance to the forecaster in “processing” past historical data.

Data are evaluated for forecasting purposes for two reasons. One is that the forecaster must determine any patterns in the data in order to be able to select the most appropriate model for a data set. The second is that the accuracy of the forecast may depend on the quality of the data. There is some evidence that the accuracy of quantitative forecasting models is not much affected by data errors and data revisions [for example, 1]. Fildes and Wood [5] however, point out that often “it is not how you forecast but what you forecast” that is important. Fildes and Howell [4] stress that although revisions to a “true” data series are unimportant, the choice of data can be quite critical. From a survey of Australian firms, Lawrence [12] found that among the reasons for firms abandoning quantitative forecasting were: (1) the forecaster had jeopardized the success of the model by failure to maintain its database, and (2) forecast users complained of the large effort required to manage and remove extraordinary events from the forecast database. While certain forecasting models may be able to deal differently with data problems such as outliers, the general problem of data quality – accuracy – affects all forecasting models equally. Hence, the issue is what level of accuracy is required for a particular forecasting situation. This will vary according to the importance of the management decision and the role of the forecast in that decision [23].

## 3. Data Accuracy: The Evidence

One difficulty in assessing accuracy might be that not all databases are well documented and it may not be clear how data were collected or coded [19]. Generally, there is little information about the level of accuracy of published data sources. Few systematic research efforts have attempted to establish empirical levels of data quality [7]. Wheelwright and Makridakis [23] discuss an extensive study undertaken some years ago by Morgernstern [13]. This study examined the accuracy of governmental data sources and found that the National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA) consumption expenditures had a probable error range of +10 percent to +15 percent. If this error range is still applicable, with consumption expenditures at their current levels, the variable could be over- or under-stated by more than \$100 billion. In NIPA, consumption is reported to the nearest \$0.1 billion. Wheelwright and Makridakis comment that such reporting obviously can be misleading to the manager who does not examine the accuracy of the data source. Managers who are aware of such a situation may be able to overcome this problem by adjusting their estimation. The problem remains unknown, however, to many managers, though their forecasts would be adversely affected.

A Business Week article in 1985 [2] discusses how many government statistics are becoming more volatile and unreliable. One factor contributing to this is declining budgets [11]. Another factor is that the government's statistics are based on assumptions made years ago, that fail to capture adequately revolutionary shifts in the economy. Examples of these are the surging employment and output in the service sector, high-tech manufacturing growth, and the way imports are seizing a growing share of consumer and capital spending. While there is concern over the quality of government economic data, Parker [16] argues that there is no easy way to measure this quality statistically. The NIPA estimates, for example, are constructed from hundreds of different sources, and the integration of these sources into the methodology requires a high degree of judgment on the part of governmental staff.

Although the private sector is a large provider of economic data, most information companies take raw data from the government, then reassemble, interpret and sell it. Without the government's ability to gather quality data, the ability of private companies to provide good economic information is constrained. Trade associations and market research firms gather data at a narrower industry level; these data have also been found to be unreliable, however.

Rice and Mahmoud [18] reviewed the problem of reliability in international data sources. Data from the U.S. and the highly industrialized nations are likely to have a higher level of accuracy than those from developing countries, largely due to the methods of collection. The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) provides some of the most accurate data, but it has sometimes been criticized for adjusting forecasts to the official line of its member governments [3]. In the case of the International Monetary Fund's "International Financial Statistics," however, while the data are provided by national sources, they are screened and reviewed before being included. If the compilers have any reason to believe that the data provided by a national government are "doctored" they simply exclude them from their publication.

Despite the lack of evidence on the accuracy of data obtained from databases external to the firm, it should be noted that it is almost always less expensive to use these data than to conduct primary research. It is often possible to use data for ways in which they were not originally intended. Furthermore, when users are charged for information, there is an incentive to ensure quality and consistency on the part of the information providers. When using government statistics such as Gross National Product (GNP), as an input to a causal model, however, it might be preferable to base forecasts on a range of GNP assumptions, and likewise for other environmental variables [6]. Fildes and Howell further suggest that if a forecasting model is unduly sensitive to the data on which it is based, it must be used carefully since the magnitude and consistency of data errors are often unknown.

## 4. The Survey of Database Vendors

## Methodology

Rather than investigating vendors' perceptions of the overall accuracy of their databases, emphasis was placed on learning how the vendors maintained the accuracy of their databases. Several authors, for example, Stewart, have stressed that database evaluation should include issues such as: the purpose of data collection; the potential biases on the part of the organization collecting the data; definitions of data units and concepts; the method of data collection; and the degree of consistency between the data and other sources. Database integrity is related to the database supplier's responsibility for the accuracy of data and can be achieved through the use of validation checks, loggings, dumps, backup and recovery procedures, and auditing procedures [21]. A major problem with data errors is that data enters the computer already contaminated. Some causes of bad data include human error in initial recording or formatting of the data, faulty transcriptions of the data, falsified or tampered data, accidentally omitted data, etc. [16]. Optical disk technology, however, which allows documents to be entered in a visual form, requires fewer data entry operations and may therefore reduce errors [14].

In this study of how database vendors maintain the accuracy of their data, a number of variables related to database accuracy were examined. These were: whether the database includes calculated or raw data; the method of data input; the frequency of data input; the method of checking accuracy; the frequency of accuracy checks; the primary source of errors; the method of correction once an error is detected; the speed of correcting data errors; and the method of dealing with outliers,

A comparison of the 1984 Database Directory [20] and the 1984 Omni Online Database Directory [15] revealed a list of 185 suppliers of historic and interactive databases for business forecasting use. These vendors were located in the U.S. and ten other countries. They were selected using a careful judgmental evaluation of the directory listing and whether it implied that the vendor provided data useful for business forecasting. A questionnaire was mailed to all the 137 U.S. organizations and 48 overseas organizations that were identified. The questionnaire sought information on the characteristics of the vendors' databases and details on the collection of data and the maintenance of data accuracy. After one mailing and a follow-up letter reminding potential respondents to participate in the survey, the rate of response was 31 percent.

## Discussion of Results

Types of Databases Offered by Responding Vendors. The majority (94 percent) of the suppliers offer source databases which contain numeric, textual numeric, and full text information. These databases are therefore suitable for quantitative forecasting. Twenty-eight percent of the vendors have reference databases which may also be used for qualitative and technological forecasting. For example, in technological forecasting, the data used are specialized, often primary scientific reports, intelligence data and general information from publications. The data themselves are not of uniform importance in such situations. Rather, it is the way the data are processed to form future alternatives that is critical [10].

Most of the responding organizations supply more than two databases. The number of variables included in the databases ranges, for example, from eight for FISCAL (National Computer Network of Chicago) to 25,000 for CANSIM (on-line database of the Conference Board of Canada). Most organizations offer data on hundreds of variables.

The wide variety of databases available stresses the importance of selecting that which has the right amount and type of information for forecasting purposes. The types of business data collected by the organizations include: price indices, stock prices, asset holdings, sales and profit figures, demographic data, gross national product, imports, exports, composition of labor force, and patents and trademarks. Although some of the databases may provide information in the same subject area, the number of variables and the specific data may differ. The user, therefore, must examine a database and match his or her needs with the database offerings before subscribing to a particular database. An important issue from the point of view of the forecaster is the format in which data are provided. Almost three-quarters of the respondents supply raw numerical data. It is crucial for forecasting that data be available in disaggregated form [10]. Sometimes when data are aggregated, for example, to provide figures on the national level for a particular variable, forecasters may need to have information on regional or local levels. Hence, levels of aggregation may be inappropriate and classifications may not match the forecaster's particular needs [19]. Wheelwright and

Makridakis discuss data aggregation in financial statistics collected by various federal agencies. As each agency collects data relevant to a somewhat different population, it is generally impossible for a corporation using the statistics to sort out the differences in a meaningful way. Therefore, in the context of database evaluation, it is more efficient to access data available at the most detailed level possible and then to aggregate them rather than to purchase data at an aggregated level and later discover they should have been broken down. Of further importance is the fact that the process of aggregating data was classified as one of the common sources of data error by Morgenstern [13]. Another advantage of access to disaggregated data is that they allow the forecaster to explore more easily the characteristics of the data, in particular, their pattern. Graphics, provided by 30 percent of the database vendors surveyed, may also be useful here. Because different forecasting methods vary in their ability to identify different types of patterns, it is important to match the presumed pattern(s) in the data with the appropriate technique. This may have implications for the accuracy of a particular forecasting technique.

Sometimes it is also valuable for forecasters to access well-organized statistics or forecasts and these are provided by 58 percent and 10 percent of the organizations, respectively.

Data Sources and Selection Criteria. In order to understand how database vendors evaluate the data sources they use, they were asked to rate the importance of a number of selection criteria that might be relevant to the choosing of data sources. The results are contained in Table 1. The criterion of data availability is rated the most important in all cases. Only if data are available can they be used to compile a data base. Reliability is also seen as an important criterion for the selection of data sources by database vendors. This is encouraging for forecasters as the vendors are implying that they are concerned about data reliability. The criterion of reputation, which may be related to perceptions of availability and accuracy, also appears to be of some importance for most of the suppliers. Completeness of data is a criterion of more importance in the case of federal government or corporate sources than it is for the other sources. Indeed federal government data are used often because of their completeness. On the other hand, incompleteness of international data is often a problem for users of these data.

The least important selection criteria in general are organization of data and high quality documentation. The database vendor, as a compiler of raw data, probably provides its own documentation and, in compiling the data, organizes them according to user's needs. The actual processes of compiling and organizing data may have some effect on the accuracy of the database.

Maintaining Database Accuracy. With respect to the data input method, 28 percent of the database vendors input data instantaneously and 47 percent use a batch input method. Several organizations use more than one. The particular input method used has implications for the timeliness of data for forecasting. Lack of timely data can affect the appropriateness of a forecast for a certain time period, and this could have ramifications for the accuracy and success of market planning. It is therefore of some concern that only 28 percent of the vendors input data instantaneously. In the case of using databases provided by vendors implementing batch or batch update methods, the forecaster would need to know when the batch processing and updating took place. This would allow him or her to obtain the data after it was updated. Forecast model accuracy could then be checked as the actual data become available. Data collected and published on a timely basis are of greatest value to the forecaster [9]. This, of course, is also a function of the importance of the currency of the information needed, and of the volatility of the variable in question.

Almost 20 percent of the database suppliers responding to the survey indicated that they do not check the accuracy of the data they receive (see Table 3). Most vendors also do not apply sophisticated methods, such as using a special editor or analyst, or mathematical or statistical testing. If an error is found, only 48 percent of the database suppliers reported that an expanded sample would be taken to search for additional errors. Furthermore, 24 percent of the database suppliers do not recheck the accuracy of their database information. Forty-four percent of the suppliers did not provide details on how often they rechecked accuracy. Fourteen percent check accuracy only monthly or at even longer time intervals. This could be problematic for the forecaster trying to build a model using bi-weekly or daily data. With respect to error correction, about half of the suppliers correct errors immediately and most do so within 48 hours. Forty-eight percent of the vendors make corrections to errors with other updates through the batch mode (see Table 4). There is, therefore, a probability that a forecaster is accessing uncorrected data. It should be noted that database suppliers may check and/or correct data at different times for different variables. It would be valuable for a forecaster or a decision-maker to have access to such information pertaining to a database being used. The results of this survey suggest that there is an opportunity for many database vendors to improve the accuracy of their databases. Meanwhile, database users are advised to learn when and at what time the correction process takes place. The users would then be able to acquire their data when the correction or update occurs thereby insuring more accurate data before it is used in modeling.

How database suppliers deal with outliers found while checking can be an important issue for a forecaster. Outliers most often create the greatest difficulty for the forecaster since they are generally unexplainable. A thorough understanding of the source and accuracy of the data is required to recognize the true importance of irregular data [9]. The results pertaining to dealing with outliers are shown in Table 5. From a forecasting perspective, data should be processed into the database “as is”, (once verified), to enable the forecaster to make his or her own decisions about data adjustment. For example, Levenbach and Cleary [9] stress that if someone is attempting to build a model to explain past results, the past data probably should not be adjusted. They may be useful in understanding how extreme or unusual effects affect the process of generating data. If the forecasting task is extrapolation, on the other hand, it is important to negate outliers by replacing them with more “typical” values. An extremely disturbing finding, therefore, is that 21 percent of the database vendors report that outliers are deleted from the data. Only one vendor stated specifically that his organization confirmed the outlying data with the source for possible immediate correction. In this way the vendor can determine whether the outlier is caused by an input error or whether it represents an extraordinary event. The question thus can be raised whether most of the responding suppliers are fully aware of the impact of outliers for forecasting and modeling purposes.

## 5. Conclusions and Implications

Database selection is a significant decision for managers using external databases, especially when there are a number of different vendors from which to choose. The results of the survey of database vendors raise a number of issues relevant to both the vendors and their customers. While the focus of the study was external databases for forecasting, the results are applicable to other managers and decision-makers who use external databases as part of their DSS and for modeling. The investigation revealed how the vendors perceived the relative importance of criteria for selecting data sources, and information concerning the checking of accuracy and dealing with outliers.

The results indicated that vendors perceived the reliability of the source from which data are collected to be important. It is possible, however, that the results are subject to response bias, lead-i.g. respondents to overstate their concern for issues such as reliability and accuracy. Whether or not such bias exists, it is of considerable concern that there are inadequacies in how the vendors actually check accuracy and deal with outliers. These procedures were revealed during the survey. From the point of view of the customer, much can be done to improve the quality of databases if full cooperation and communication exists between the customer and vendor. For example, electronic mail can be used to inform users about data updates and special characteristics (such as outliers). It is most advantageous for users to access the database following revision and rechecking procedures on the part of the vendor. Also, if the vendor can identify not only outliers but possible causes of these outliers, (for example, an economic event), this service can be useful to the model-builder. This is, of course, more important in the case where a database vendor is supplying models and not merely the raw data.

Users should be particularly careful to enquire about the nature of the original data sources accessed by the database compiler and vendor. It may be possible to find out whether there is typically over- or under-estimation from a specific data source. The model-builder can then make appropriate adjustments. For forecasting, it is also important to have consistent data; for example, in the case of the Gross National Product, the forecaster should use all “early release” data or all revised data. The purchaser of the database or of access to it should also be aware of data updating times.

The study used the survey method to measure accuracy in a general and indirect fashion. Further studies of database accuracy could take more specific approaches, by investigating and objectively testing the accuracy of particular variables such as price indices and demographic data with regard to different database suppliers. Research in other disciplines may shed light on ways to proceed. For example, Laudon [7] has developed and applied a methodology for examining record quality in automated criminal record systems. Other issues in database accuracy may be studied. These could include: the relationship between the growing privatization of data and accuracy; and whether private providers will be more or less concerned with accuracy. Database accuracy is an important area of study because successful planning and business decisions depend on accurate forecasts, which, in turn, depend on accurate data.
