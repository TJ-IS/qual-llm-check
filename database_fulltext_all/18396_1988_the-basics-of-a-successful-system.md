# The Basics of a Successful System

Gerhard Plenert

California State University, School of Business, Chico, CA 95929-0011, USA

Personal Computer (PC) sales have taken a downward turn primarily because PC systems are still not sufficiently user oriented. Some basic systems guidelines should be followed that will improve the acceptance of PCs by users that are not familiar with the technical side of a computer. A set of “rules” and some “systems analysis basics” are supplied. These will help to make future PC systems that are user oriented.

Keywords: Personal Computers (PC), Systems analysis, Software systems, Documentation, Expert systems, Rules of systems analysis. Management reporting, Systems analysis phases, Systems analysis basics.

What is the Problem?

Sales in Personal Computers (PC) have been dropping, but not because of a saturated business marketplace. They are dropping because of a basic flaw in marketing policy. $^{1}$ The PC was the machine that was predicted to put computer power in the hands of every potential user. Everyone in the office could use the computer to make jobs more efficient and data more accurate. Unfortunately, one key point seems to have been forgotten. If we are put a PC in the hands of every potential user then we need to build systems (hardware and software) that are user oriented. $^{2}$ For example:

(1) User documentation for a system is too seldom written by a user. It is generally written by either the programmers or the systems analysts who designed the system. Unfortunately, these individuals make untrue assumptions about users and many statements that the “users” are not able to understand.

User documentation should be written by someone that knows nothing about the internals of the system and is being trained how to use it for the first time. $^{3}$ Such documentation then needs to be checked by another user who is being trained on the system for the first time. This technique will ensure user oriented documentation.

Another requirement is that user documentation should be written at two levels: for the first time user and for the individual who is fairly familiar with the system. In the first case, the documentation should be simple though detailed. Second, the documentation may be nothing more than a listing of codes and a one-line explanation of how each is used.

(2) Users are consulted on the outputs they desire, but they are rarely asked how they wish the system to operate. Interfaces are dictated by the system designers. Even when defining output, we often see its format dictated by the systems analysts, not the users. It is not surprising that users find such systems inconvenient to operate.

Even in the case of Expert Systems, analysts try to tap all of the expert's experience, but then they dictate how this experience will be utilized and incorporated into the system. Is this then a real expert system?

Users should become a significant part of the design team. They know what input they need, how they need to manipulate it, and how they will use the output.

(3) Hardware salesman and consultants have been known to sell computers that “will answer all the problems”, without fully explaining to the future PC owner that other startup costs will be two to three times the hardware cost. These other costs include training, facilities, and software development.

It probably takes an investment of about 10 times that required to purchase the system before a user is fully able to take advantage of the system. Unfortunately, most users are not ready to invest much time. Thus about one-third of the PCs sold to business are not being used; another one-third are being used for word processing or some other low level function, certainly not for the main reason for which they were purchased; the last one-third are being used effectively. This is not a high level of success.

PC users need to buy systems. They should be sold complete tools, with an explanation of the time commitment required for success. They need to understand the additional costs of installing a complete system.

These three points illustrate the frustrations that users have with the PC world, especially those incurred by the actions of individuals who overemphasize the benefits without explaining the amount of work and cost required to make a system effective. Of course, users blame the salesman.

## The Rules of Systems Analysis

The failures in the PC marketplace encourage another look at some of the basics of Systems Analysis. A good place to start is by reviewing a series of rules formulated after many years of systems analysis experience:

(1) Any system not designed by a user is only half as effective as it could have been.

(2) Do not entirely discard the manual system. If nothing else, it can serve as a backup if the computer system fails.

(3) Do not computerize something that does not work. Many systems are sold on the principle that they will force discipline on the company and the system will enforce structure that will enhance the company processing. This philosophy can be quickly dispelled by the argument that a manual system which produces useless information because of a lack of discipline, will simply be faster in producing it if computerized. A computer cannot improve the quality of the input: data must be validated and the method must be planned before the system is installed. $^{4}$ Otherwise the new system only offers an excuse for the problems previously blamed elsewhere. Computers cannot fight back, so they are a good scape-goat. Thus anything that is to be computerized should be checked out manually to assure its feasibility.

(4) Buy the software system, not the hardware. Define the user's needs first and select the software system based on those needs. Then the selection of the hardware is trivial.

(5) When looking at the three portions of a system (input, process, and output), analyze the output and define the user's needs first. Next, analyze the input to make sure these needs can be met. Then analyze the process phase, which at this point is probably easy.

(6) The point of view of the systems analyst is generally wrong. The user, who is closer to the operation of the system and must live with it on a day-to-day basis, has a better understanding of the system needs. It does not imply that the analyst, who can take an objective view will not have valuable inputs. But the final decision on how the system should operate is the user's.

However, there is another message: if the users have the final say on the system, it becomes theirs and not the analyst's, with a much larger chance for success.

## The Systems Analysis Basics

As the reversal of PC sales has highlighted, there is a need to relearn the following basic systems analysis principals.

(1) Structure and control are critical. An individual, such as a Systems Administrator, is needed to approve of the original system or any change to be implemented. This person is then responsible for all the data processed by the system and all the information it produces; the added control of the systems development process passes through this controller, who thus adds structure by the discipline enforced.

(2) There are three levels of management reporting, and most systems cross some or all these levels. The top level (strategic management) requires exception and summary reporting. The middle level (tactical management) also depends on summary reporting but in a shorter time frame. The bottom level (operational management) requires detailed reporting in order to run day-to-day operations. Each system must incorporate reports that satisfy the need of all three.

(3) A hierarchy of management reporting needs exists. They cover strategic (five year perspective) to operation (day-to-day). A production planning system is often implemented before a company has good control of the master production scheduling process or the master scheduling system is implemented before a company has good control of forecasts. But a production plan is useless if all the inputs into the production plan are not working correctly.

(4) Every systems analysis project should be viewed as a series of phases and each should be considered carefully before going on to the next. (a) The Survey Phase occurs when users, analysts, or managers look for problems that may potentially be solved by a system. These are identified and the ones with the highest payoff (financially or in employee or customer relations) go to the next phase.

(b) The Study Phase requires an intensive study of the current information system. This should utilize tools like HIPO, systems flow charts, and data or information flow diagrams, as appropriate. Once the old system is understood, its problems become obvious. With an understanding of the old system, suggestions for improvement can be made and the system cost justified.

(c) The Definition Phase is where the new system is designed; specifications should be laid out for the “ideal” system.

At this point, a decision must be made. Will the proposed system be purchased or developed in-house? If home development is desired, then the development is constrained by the development time and its cost. If the system is to be purchased, compromises must be made on features and capabilities. Rarely will a system be available that fits the “ideal”. Interaction with vendors becomes necessary. $^{5}$

(d) Development Phase - The newly defined system is programmed and tested or purchased possibly modified, and accepted.

(e) Delivery Phase – The new system is tested as a stand alone and in conjunction with the other existing systems. Training programs are implemented, the system is installed, and a feedback or review procedure is initiated to allow management to review its on-going performance.

(5) Documentation begins with the Survey Phase and follows the entire life of the system. Multiple sets of documentation are necessary; these should include documentation for the

(a) programmer,

(b) systems analyst,

(c) starting user,

(d) advanced user.

The documentation should be written by the persons involved at each level.

## Conclusion

By forgetting the systems analysis step, the sellers of PC systems have failed to satisfy their consumers. The need to return to an emphasis on effective systems analysis will help to make the usage of the PC more satisfying to its purchasers.

This article highlights those basic areas that have been found to destroy the best systems, including PC systems. This was not, however, intended to cover all the steps necessary to assure a successful systems implementation. But, by following a few basic principles, such as paying more attention to the user, the chance of success will improve dramatically.
