# An Information Processing Framework for Understanding Success and Failure of MIS Development Methodologies

Louise H. Jones

Center for Information Systems Management, Education, and Research, College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716, USA

Christine T. Kydd

Department of Business Administration, College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716, USA

Organizations seeking to improve systems development performance have implemented a variety of project methodologies. The success of these efforts has been mixed. Some organizations have found that implementation of a systems development methodology leads to on-time, within budget project completions with improved productivity; others have experienced reduced productivity. This paradox can be explained by viewing both systems development and the methodology development in terms of two key information processes - uncertainty reduction and equivocality resolution. Project methodologies that are implemented with the procedures, definitions, and policies necessary to reduce the uncertainty and resolve the equivocality associated with the systems development process can be successful only if the management of the process allows those mechanisms to be fully utilized. Implementation experiences with a particular methodology in different environments discussed here demonstrates that equivocality resolution during the implementation process is critical to realizing improved productivity. Suggestions for management action necessary to resolve equivocality during the implementation of a systems development methodology are discussed.

Keywords: Change management, Information processing, Management information systems development, Project methodology, Systems development process, Systems development productivity.

A systems development methodology can be viewed as a system which provides the information required to build information systems. This means that much of what is known about designing and implementing information systems in organizations can be applied to understanding successful and unsuccessful systems developments through use of project methodologies. While previous work ([7], [11], [12]) provides insight into many implementation issues associated with systems development methodologies, a recently published view of organizational information requirements [3] is especially useful. In addition, the implementation of a system or a systems methodology always involves people, and this means that the success of a system often depends on understanding and managing individual behavior during the change caused by the implementation [8], [9], [10], [13], [15].

In this paper, we present two case studies where the same IS methodology was implemented in two distinct organizations with very different results. The differing results are attributed to the way in which the management of each organization dealt with the uncertainty and equivocality that was caused by the introduction of the new methodology. Although the cases focus on a particular methodology, we feel that the lessons learned from these cases can be equally applied to the implementation of new information systems as well. Conclusions reflect on how to implement systems as well as systems development methodologies.

## 2. An Information Processing View of MIS Development

It has been suggested [3] that organizations process information to reduce uncertainty and to resolve equivocality. Uncertainty is defined as the absence of information; uncertainty can be reduced by asking questions which cause additional objective information to be gathered and analyzed. Equivocality is defined as absence of clarity, or ambiguity, due to multiple interpretations of the same information; equivocality can be resolved by relying on intuition and judgment to define the appropriate questions which can reduce the number of different interpretations.

Different organizational contingencies produce different levels of uncertainty and equivocality, and different organizational structures have varying capacities for reducing uncertainty and equivocality. For example, high interdependence between two departments of an organization is a source of high uncertainty because great quantities of objective information need to be passed back and forth to coordinate behavior. On the other hand, large differences between the departments is a source of high equivocality based on the fact that the departments would have very different interpretations of the same ambiguous situation. Rules, procedures, and MISs are effective for reducing uncertainty because they transmit large quantities of objective data and provide any necessary data analysis. However, these mechanisms are poor ones for resolving equivocality. Group meetings, use of integrators, and face-to-face contact are effective in resolving equivocality because they allow for personal contact between parties where divergent viewpoints can be discussed and synthesized into a common framework. Such mechanisms are not required simply to reduce uncertainty. Some of these factors have been identified previously [2] as key factors in implementation success.

Figure 1 shows a fourfold typology of the MIS environment as defined by the two dimensions, uncertainty and equivocality. Each cell describes a general situation representing various levels of these two factors. Although some systems development projects fall into cells, 1, 3 or 4, typically the environment in which systems professionals develop application software systems for business clients is a high uncertainty/high equivocality environment (cell 2). High uncertainty is caused by the variety of technology tools used in systems development, the interdependence of client and systems organizations, and the usual absence of reliable information on project structure and status. High equivocality is caused by the craft aspects of the development process, the large differences between mission, goals, and objectives of the client and MIS organizations, and ever changing client expectations. Client expectations for MIS usually change considerably as their understanding of their information needs changes during the course of a project and as their systems objectives change from improved efficiency to improved effectiveness.

The success of a systems organization depends on how well user expectations for the systems product and the systems project itself are managed during the development process. The product must meet expectations with respect to system functionality, user acceptance, and value added to the organization. The project must meet expectations with respect to on-time, within budget delivery of an agreed upon product. In the high uncertainty/high equivocality environment of application systems development, success is determined by how well uncertainty reduction and, especially, equiv-ocality resolution are managed.

Following Daft and Lengel [3], these factors can best be managed through use of the appropriate organizational mechanisms. In a situation of high uncertainty and high equivocality, several types of mechanisms are warranted, i.e., those that provide objective data to answer predefined questions (addressing the uncertainty), and those that allow divergent viewpoints to be discussed and organized into one common framework (addressing the equivocality). In an MIS environment, the first type of mechanism includes formal system documentation, standard departmental policies and procedures, information-providing meetings and special reports. The second type of mechanism includes group meetings between users and systems analysts, use of a systems champion (see [2]), or integrator, and various forms of face-to-face contact among the stakeholders of the new system or development methodology.

As implementation of any new information system or systems methodology generally represents significant change within an organization, it is important to consider how change management is related to the information processing framework described above. The effectiveness of tools can be characterized in terms of their impact on the speed of short term change and on the control of the direction of change [14]. Tools, such as structural changes, increase the speed of short term change by reducing uncertainty. Tools, such as management behavior, provide control over the direction of change by resolving equivocality. Tools, such as quality improvement programs or programs based around other measurable dominating values, provide control over both the direction and speed of change by both reducing uncertainty and resolving equivocality. The need to decrease the levels of both factors in the systems implementation process has been recognized by others [7], [11]. This need is also important for successful implementation of a development methodology.

## 3. Systems Development Methodologies

Systems development methodologies provide the methods, procedures, definitions, principles, and policies needed for successful development of information systems. All such methodologies contain a client-MIS communications subsystem, a project management subsystem, and a technical work subsystem. The communications subsystem provides policies to reduce uncertainty in key project areas and procedures to ensure that equivocality-resolving mechanisms are invoked at appropriate times in the systems life cycle. Typically, the procedures include risk assessment meetings, quality review meetings at various stages of the life cycle, and, possible, cooperative use of prototyping tools for definition of specifications.

The project management subsystem provides tools for resolving multiple opinions about project tasks and for determining current project status. In general, the project management subsystem consists of a standing task plan where tangible results for each task and quality standards for each tangible result are specified. Project progress is based on completed work where a task is complete only if the tangible result has been completed and has met the quality standard. Project plans developed by editing standing task plans are more likely to be complete than those created without reference to a standing plan. Within the standing plan are tasks that call for structured meetings of systems and client personnel to review completed work, to resolve a variety of project issues, and to deal effectively with changes in client understanding during the course of a project.

Although the task structure of most project methodologies assumes use of certain technical approaches to systems development, the specific choice of tools and techniques is not important. What is important is that the number of different tools and techniques be limited to a manageable number; that technical training in the use of the tools and techniques be provided for the systems development staff; and that the standing project plan make sense with respect to the technical approach selected by the organization.

Implementation of a systems development methodology is similar to the implementation of any information system in that it is a system for building a systems product and managing a systems project. It represents an organizational change which creates uncertainty and equivocality. Successful implementation of a project methodology leads to a work environment in which appropriate organizational structures are in place to manage this uncertainty and equivocality. This work environment provides well-defined goals, both short and longer term, and, through the project plan, a means for measuring and reporting progress against goal. Such implementations have been suggested as contributing to significant increases in systems development productivity [4].

Introduction of a new systems development methodology generates equivocality, in particular, because there are often multiple interpretations of the demand for “on-time, within budget systems projects that meet client business needs”. Some systems professionals focus on the uncertainty-reducing, or control, aspects of the methodology. These people resist implementation of a methodology that they perceive will routinize their work and detract from their professional status. Other professionals focus on the equivocality-resolving, or enhancing contribution, aspects of the methodology. These people embrace a methodology that they perceive will magnify their contribution and enhance their professional status.

Resolution of the equivocality associated with implementation of a systems development methodology is critical is obtaining both on-time, within budget project completions and increased product quality and organizational productivity. The direction of change must be controlled. This means that management must send clear signals regarding their support for use of the methodology and their intended meaning of “on-time, within budget projects”. It also means that lack of such clear signals, or ambiguous signals, may lead to undesired results.

## 4. Implementation Experiences

This section provides a retrospective view of the implementation of a single systems development methodology in two different organizational settings with drastically different results. Each case is discussed in terms of the uncertainty/equivocality framework presented earlier.

## 4.1. Organization A

## 4.1.1. Description of the Implementation

Organization A is the central systems development organization of about 200 systems professionals within a staff department responsible for providing computer, telecommunications, and other systems services to a large industrial organization. Over the years, Organization A had developed a reputation both for developing application systems of extremely high technical quality and for completing projects late and substantially over budget. Previous attempts to improve project performance had focussed on the use of improved tools and techniques, such as structured analysis, design, and programming.

Project performance was identified as an issue critical to the department after new department management found that two multi-million dollar projects would be delivered late and substantially over budget. A new section, the Systems Quality section, was formed within Organization A to define and implement a systems development methodology that would ensure on-time, within budget project completions. Management introduced the new section to the organization in a series of meetings which emphasized the need to change to improve project performance. However, management's message in these meetings was inconsistent with the quality of the Systems Quality section staff (considered below average for Organization A) and with the office arrangements for the new manager (who had neither a permanent office nor a secretary for several months).

The staff of the new section, working in cooperation with a number of staff members of Organization A, developed specifications for a systems development methodology, reviewed and evaluated commercial offerings, and recommended to a broad-based steering committee, composed of senior systems professionals and lower-level managers, that a particular methodology be purchased and tailored to Organization A's special needs. This recommendation was accepted. The methodology was named ASDM (A Systems Development Methodology).

ASDM was a modified version of the commercially available systems methodology SPECTRUM. This methodology provides a large project task structure which attempts to deal with both the systems environment (including technical, managerial and organizational issues), as well as the interaction between the client and programmer/analyst. SPECTRUM is centered around the notion of planned communication and what is called a “well-posed” plan. Such a plan consists of 20- to 40-hour individual tasks, where each task has an associated tangible result, and each result has an associated quality standard. A task is considered completed when that standard has been satisfactorily met. There are versions of SPECTRUM for both traditional large projects and for iterative prototyping projects.

Implementation planning for ASDM proceeded through workshops and seminars for both the systems professionals and client managers. Although the primary objective of the seminars was to establish a common base of understanding and buy-in on the key principles of ASDM, the division director also wanted the section managers to take a more active role in managing projects than in the past. This was a significant change in the expected role of the section manager; previously these managers had maintained an appropriate distance from large projects which they felt were likely not to meet schedule and budget commitments. Since it soon became clear to the section managers that they had little to gain and much to lose by implementing ASDM, they did not support the new methodology.

At this point, the Systems Quality manager received an unannounced promotion, and three key section managers accompanied the Systems Quality manager to the annual meeting of the ASDM User's Group in Palm Springs, California. Upon return, these managers were slightly more supportive of ASDM, an evidenced by their identification of projects in their sections which could be used as prototypes. Their behavior indicated a shift in attitude from a position of active resistance to one of neutral non-support.

As the prototype projects moved forward using ASDM, it became clear that use of the methodology was producing better project results and, more importantly, was also driving a significant cultural change. The supervisors were beginning to lose control of the junior project managers, who were now able to plan and execute their project work independently of the supervisors. When the supervisors realized this shift in control, they discouraged the junior project managers' use of ASDM. The Systems Quality staff combatted this problem by offering to support any project manager who wanted to use ASDM on an "early implementation" basis, and to provide an opportunity for the first dozen project managers to present their experiences, good or bad, to high levels of management. Several junior project managers responded positively to this offer.

Meanwhile, the Systems Quality steering committee met regularly to hear reports on the progress of the early implementation projects. Detailed minutes of these meetings, with concerns and objections attributed to individuals, were prepared and distributed to committee members, section managers and directors of the department. The early implementation project program showed that ASDM could be used successfully to improve both project performance and project productivity, and the reports to management were uniformly positive.

Eventually, a policy document covering key aspects of the systems/client organization relationship was developed by the steering committee and signed by the director of Organization A; the steering committee was dismissed with thanks; the Organization A managers were directed to implement ASDM; and the Systems Quality manager was reassigned as manager of the General Systems Group. Project performance, which had been a critical departmental issue for two years, was removed from the critical issues list.

Two years later, Organization A was routinely delivering technically sound systems on-time and within budget. However, there was a perception that many of these systems were unduly expensive and that Organization A was no longer a cost-effective systems development organization due to the overhead associated with use of ASDM. Over these two years, use of ASDM in Organization A generally declined.

Within one section of Organization A, the General Systems Group, however, use of ASDM as a tool to develop projects became well accepted. Decreasing project costs indicated increased productivity. Project managers found that they liked working from the detailed task plans because they could measure their progress and knew how to make the projects “come out right”. Those who completed projects at less than 90 percent of the budget were treated to lunch by the manager. Clients were happy with the early, underbudget project completions (which had become routine), and the high quality and low cost of work done in the section.

Within other sections, a few highly successful project managers also used ASDM regularly, but most project managers elected to address the issue of ontime, within budget completions by very conservative schedules and cost estimates. Currently, the primary management objective for Organization A is once again on-time, within budget completions of quality systems projects.

## 4.1.2. Discussion of the Implementation

Identification of project performance as a critical departmental issue and the information of the new Systems Quality section to identify and implement an appropriate systems methodology created a situation of high uncertainty and high equivocality in Organization A. Uncertainty was centered around the questions of how the new methodology would work, what analysis and design techniques would be part of the methodology, and whether use of a methodology would actually improve project performance. Considerable equivocality was based on multiple interpretations of what management meant by “improved project performance”, what management’s objectives really were in forming the new Systems Quality section, and how the new methodology would change the jobs of the systems development staff.

Uncertainty was fairly effectively reduced through the participative process that was used to define the requirements of ASDM, through the multiple review committees charged with comparing the candidate methodologies with the requirements that had been defined for ASDM, and through the experiences with the pilot and early implementation projects. Each of these mechanisms provided additional objective information which answered the questions of the systems staff regarding ASDM.

However, resolving the equivocality was much more difficult. From the beginning, when the formation of the new Systems Quality section was first announced, the perceptions of management's objectives and values were ambiguous. It was generally believed that management was primarily concerned with on-time, within budget completions of applications systems, and that management really did not care how that result was obtained. Furthermore, management's words about the importance of the new Systems Quality section and their deeds in staffing this section with people perceived as below average performers were inconsistent and raised questions about management's objectives. Additional equivocality appeared in the behavior of the Systems Quality

Steering committee which for some time publicly expressed its concern over the risk to the organization posed by introduction of a less than perfect methodology. Similarly, a number of the supervisors told their people that ASDM would soon disappear from the organization. In their lack of support for the methodology, they created further ambiguity.

According to the uncertainty/equivocality framework, this equivocality should have been addressed by providing individuals with the appropriate communications mechanisms in a timely manner, i.e., face-to-face meetings, personal telephone or written contact, or use of an integrator. The systems development staff did not have use of any of these mechanisms and so could not resolve the equivocality which they felt. Eventually, some of the equivocality associated with ASDM was resolved through the review meetings for management of experiences with the “early implementation” projects. In these face-to-face meetings, management clearly communicated their values, objectives, interest and satisfaction with the initial ASDM experiences. After the first project review meetings, members of the steering committee dropped their objections to implementation of ASDM, clear evidence that their ambiguity regarding management’s intentions had been resolved. However, these meetings came too late, as evidenced by the decisions of many section managers to not implement ASDM in their sections, and to sabotage implementation in general.

In the General Systems Group, things went differently. The manager (previous manager of the Systems Quality section) defined “improved project performance” as “on-time, within budget completions of cost-effective projects” and sent consistent signals indicating a belief that these objectives could be achieved by using ASDM. These behavioral signals included feedback on weekly project status reports, active participation in monthly large project problem solving sessions, and creation of an award for project leaders who completed large projects substantially below estimate. Typical results from using ASDM in the General Systems Group included significant reductions in estimated project budgets (e.g., by as much as 50%); below-budget project completions (e.g., at 75% of budget for a three man-year project); and significant productivity improvements in maintenance work (e.g., a 35% reduction in the staff needed to support a general system with 100 users at a specified service level). Here, equivocality was obviously dealt with correctly and efficiently.

## 4.2. Organization B

## 4.2.1. Description of the Implementation

Organization B is a medium-sized, not-for-profit museum originally established and endowed by a wealthy collector. The organization's primary value is high quality, and all projects and programs are evaluated on the basis of their perceived quality. Access to the collection is primarily by reserved tour.

Automation of labor-intensive information-handling functions was identified as a significant, strategic cost-reduction opportunity. However, the organization was concerned about avoiding the severe problems that similar organizations had encountered with their automation efforts. A computer study committee was formed and asked to develop and prioritize a comprehensive list of systems needs. The committee, with help from a consultant, recommended a purchased software strategy.

ASDM was chosen to be implemented in Organization B with the intent of establishing an environment in which the project manager and users with limited systems and computer experience could analyze their systems needs and deal effectively with vendors responding to requests for quotation. Training was provided in project planning and monitoring, systems analysis, preparation of system specifications, acceptance testing, and conducting structured group meetings. The management of Organization B provided full support for the implementation of ASDM, as evidenced by its selection of the manager of the computer study committee (perceived as astute and high-performing), and the devotion of human resources to the training effort.

Specifications for the first priority need, a computer-based reservations system, were developed and carefully reviewed by a quality review board representing a number of groups within Organization B. The board elected to deal with important issues by reviewing the detailed specs as a group following the format commonly used in “walk-throughs”. The review sessions of this group were collaborative group efforts; group members soon developed such an extensive knowledge of the reservations function and system that they could challenge the advice of the software consultant who often attended the meetings. The use of ASDM to plan, complete and monitor projects was soon accepted wholeheartedly by Organization B.

Although an unfortunate vendor choice was made originally for the reservations system, the quality review board, operating as a collaborative problem solving group, was able to resolve the problem and the reservations systems was successfully completed. The ASDM methodology was used in addressing systems needs associated with the business office, development office, and registrar's office with good results. The methodology was not used in addressing the systems support for the direct mail operation of the marketing office. Severe, unresolvable technical problems associated with the software package purchased by the marketing office eventually led to a management decision to move the direct mail operation to an outside organization.

Progress on Organization B's automation project was reviewed regularly with the board of trustees, most of whom were business executives, who were impressed with the functional value that had been delivered for the price. All major priority needs had been satisfied with the money provided by the original fund plus accrued interest.

In addition, within the museum community, Organization B has become recognized as a leader in successful implementation of computer systems. Some professional staff members have elected to broaden the expertise they developed with the automation project and are now recognized as individual leaders in the field. Other museums within the local area, as well as visitors from distant institutions, have approached Organization B to learn how the automation objectives were accomplished so effectively.

## 4.2.2. Discussion of the Implementation

In Organization B, the committee process which led to the priority list of functions to be automated and the purchased software strategy produced an environment with considerable uncertainty but relatively little equivocality. Uncertainty was centered around questions of how to prepare specifications for applications software and how to deal with software vendors. Implementation of ASDM and delivery of ASDM-related training provided information and direction about how to proceed. The ASDM framework reduced uncertainty about technical aspects by providing an environment which permitted use of the appropriate organizational mechanisms, namely extensive training sessions and documentation of system specifications. Most importantly, the staff of Organization B was able to use ASDM to deal effectively with their software vendors.

In this case, equivocality was originally created as a result of the introduction of an unknown methodology to be used to generate computer systems which were unfamiliar to naive workers. However, this equivocality was addressed from the beginning of the process through use of appropriate communications mechanisms. The computer study group met face-to-face intentionally for the purpose of resolving the differing viewpoints, multiple interpretations and general ambiguity caused by the extensive automation project facing the group. Further, an integrator was utilized (the chairman of the computer study group) to act as a buffer between upper levels of management and the systems personnel. In both cases, equivocality was resolved early and thus, did not create problems for the successful implementation of ASDM and development of the reservations system.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

In comparing the two cases described above, it is clear that implementation of a single systems development methodology produced very different outcomes. Organization A was not successful in gaining the support of its personnel for use of ASDM, while Organization B was. While the implementation of the methodology introduced uncertainty and equivocality into both organizations, Organization B recognized the problems and utilized appropriate organizational structures to resolve them; Organization A did not.

Implementation of a development methodology usually occurs in an environment characterized by both high uncertainty and high equivocality. This means such methodologies for successful MIS development must include information processing mechanisms both to reduce uncertainty and to resolve equivocality. Thus, all systems development methodologies must have communications, project management, and technical work subsystems designed to reduce uncertainty and resolve equivocality by managing expectations, planning and measuring progress of project work, and guiding technical work processes.

Implementation of a development methodology can be viewed as an organizational change process which also is usually characterized by high uncertainty and equivocality. The implementation experiences described in this paper indicate that resolution of the equivocality associated with the implementation process, in particular, is critical to the success of a development methodology. Thus, it is important that the objectives of any performance improvement program be specified carefully so that there can be only one interpretation of what “improvement” means.

The uncertainty/equivocality information processing framework is also useful in understanding other programs directed towards improved organizational performance. A recent analysis of quality improvement programs [5] has shown that programs which use appropriate organizational structures to resolve equivocality and to reduce uncertainty are far more likely to produce substantial improvements than those that deal with uncertainty alone. Another recent study of the organizational systems and structures used in turnaround situations [6] confirms the importance of managing both uncertainty and equivocality as part of any turnaround process. All managers responsible for managing organizational change need to be sure that appropriate organizational structures are used to deal not only with the uncertainty but also with the equivocality associated with the change.
