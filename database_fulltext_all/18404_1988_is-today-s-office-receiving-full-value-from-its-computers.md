# Is Today's Office Receiving Full Value from its Computers?

Carl Hammer

Research Consulting Services, Washington, DC 20007-2843, USA

The office of today makes intensive and extensive use of electronic computers and data processing systems which provide powerful support for a host of realtime and transaction oriented applications essential to the offices' smooth operations. These systems do the work of uncountable "virtual clerks" who are managed by the real, human office and knowledge workers. A cursory examination shows that, in general, offices are doing their job rather well, the work is getting done. Nevertheless, the office of today is still using ADP largely as a tool – ADP is not the office. Not enough attention has been paid to the office's real structure, a complex system of procedural processes. Upon closer examination, many office "horror stories" and "computer goofs" turn out to be flawed or incorrectly executed procedures. Tutorial case examples cited include salary and grade review, realtime program testing, computer security, and bank check clearing operations. A fifth case, an accounts payable system, illustrates the benefits that proper system design and implementation can provide. Office procedures, therefore, are an urgent target for providing computer assistance. By going this route, significant improvements in the office's functionality and performance can be attained, operational costs can be reduced, an improved office environment can be created, and confidence in virtually error-free procedural operations can be restored.

Keywords: ADP, Data processing, Integrated procedural systems, Office management, Procedural processing. Real work force, Virtual work force.

## 2. ADP's Pervasiveness

Now, forty years after ENIAC, computers are used everywhere in the world. Millions of small systems are found in offices, schools and homes; hundreds of thousands of medium sized systems are deployed in offices and factories. Thousands of supercomputers are installed in research laboratories and number-crunching organizations. The user community has obviously done something right. Witness the results. Data processing, computation, communications, and even networked systems are increasingly pervading all societal activities, delivering computer power where it is needed. An equivalent human force of clerical workers would number in the trillions in the U.S. alone. Consider a clerical worker's ability to sustain operational speeds of about one Baud (one bit per second) in both data processing and computation, for a day's output of 28,600 bits perhaps. But a single 48-bit machine with a 10-nanosecond instruction speed equals a virtual workforce of 4.8 million people requiring no sleep and coffeebreaks!

The ADP scenario is the office [17]. Not the office of the future but the office of today. That is where the millions of virtual clerks are busy, effortlessly making the most complex engineering calculations, maintaining colossal data bases with hundreds of millions of records, moving millions of transactions every hour over national and international data networks to access and update accounts or transfer funds electronically [7].

## 3. The Evidence for Full Value Received From Computers

Automatic Data Processing can be defined as:

The execution of a systematic sequence of operations (“program”), without external interruptions, performed upon data within an electronic computer/communication system, designed to accomplish a well-defined, structured task.

ADP programs are expected to run smoothly and without human intervention. Even interactive programs in the inquiry mode keep running; they are designed to process each transaction as soon as it is formatted and entered by the user. That is part of the well-defined, structured task. The more typical data processing operation may comprise a large number of programs which are executed in some predetermined sequence until the specific task at hand is completed.

Thanks to the power of ADP systems we have made spectacular progress in every type of activity carried out in today's office. By the following four examples we aim to show that the office is getting its money's worth from ADP.

## Case 1: Computational Power

The computation of PI, mentioned earlier, furnishes a good example. Until 1940 PI was known (correctly) to 660 decimals. In their “Mathematics and the Imagination” Kasner and Newman asserted that “it would take ten years of calculation to determine PI to 1000 places.” George Reitwiesner’s 1949 ENIAC calculation [12] began an unbroken chain of improvements [14]. The current record is 134,217,000 decimals [4].

There and similar innocuous events stimulated computer scientists to explore new fields of research, which include such topics as systems complexity, structure and certifiability of algorithms, and the security of cryptographic systems.

Case 2: Very Large Data Base Management Systems

No less spectacular are our accomplishments in data processing. Record keeping and data base management evolved as the stellar component of

ADP systems. The U.S. Social Security System is a good case in point. Each month it makes payments by check or electronic funds transfer to about forty million persons. Data base changes by the millions per year arise from status alterations and new registrants. But when the system was conceived in the 1930s by President Franklin D. Roosevelt and his advisor, Harry L. Hopkins, they could have neither conceived of the magnitude of such an effort nor the technology required to carry it out. Today, billions of virtual clerks at the headquarters of the U.S. Social Security Administration manage quite well with the help of a few thousand real office workers.

## Case 3: Management Information Systems

This third example focuses on transaction-driven, reactive systems which comprise large host computers and networking technology. Throughout the government and the private sector there exist countless such systems which process in real-time myriads of formatted transactions or queries. With their help the knowledge worker can access a variety of data bases over central or distributed systems to obtain specific data or assemble tabular reports. The status of a single U.S. Social Security recipient's record is displayed almost instantly on the case worker's terminal screen. Import or export statistics, trade deficit figures, and shipments of agricultural products are at the fingertip (literally) of the computer literate U.S. government analyst.

## Case 4: Electronic Funds Transfer Systems (EFTS)

One U.S. Government “Office” which gets its money literally over its own highly secure financial network is the Federal Reserve. It is part of a worldwide system which carries most financial transactions. But the magnitude of the collective network traffic is perhaps not too well known. Currently, the global EFTS traffic amounts to about one trillion Dollars per day! One quarter of that, \$250 billion, travels over the U.S. Federal Reserve’s Fedwire; about the same amount over the U.S. private sector financial networks. That’s about \$3 million per second for each of the two networks, speeding along the invisible channels of electronic systems.

The four cases are rather convincing:

First, ADP in the office is doing its job well. Often it is doing the kind of office work that was well known forty years ago. But more often it is carrying out realtime-related tasks which were not even conceivable then.

Second, ADP's versatility has grown with office requirements. Since its infancy in 1946, ADP has been introduced into a still growing variety of applications. The bottom line is, the jobs are getting done.

Third, despite persisting lamentations, the cost of ADP in the office has steadily declined while its capability has increased. The cost of executing an instruction or transmitting a bit, in the opinion of many, is still nowhere near its potential level.

Fourth, the generic power of computer systems has spurred research in the area of large, complex problems. Their solution has opened new frontiers and stimulated new technologies. Economic modeling and imaging graphics are but two striking examples.

Thus one might conclude, at this point of the inquiry, that all is well in the office's computerland. Indeed, it is. But it could be better!

## 4. What About Procedural Processing?

Is there anything the ADP applications crafters have overlooked? Let us examine the all-too frequently mentioned “computer goofs”. Upon closer examination they always turn out to be human errors which greatly upset the life and tranquility of the office. As it turns out, the source of most such problems has to do with the execution of office procedures. Every organization, from small to large, is governed by rules and procedures. They are written out in guidelines, rule books, and office procedure manuals. They may come in slim volumes; more likely they are sets of large three-ring binders, for ease of update, we are told. They are readily called to mind by every office worker, but they are evidently not as readily consulted.

Filling out an application blank for employment is a procedure. So is filling out an application for a credit card. Applying for Social Security benefits in the U.S. is another procedure which much earlier required that one obtained a U.S. Social Security Number by another procedure. Getting an unknown telephone number from Information is a procedure which produces no results if we deal with an unlisted number. Of course, that number is not really “unlisted” – it is listed in scores of computer data bases but the telephone company would like you to believe that it is not readily accessible. The yearly filing of tax returns by taxpayers in all countries is a Janus procedure: It has two heads. First the taxpayer’s view, then the Government Tax Authority’s view. Writing out a check to pay tax is a procedure – don’t forget to put your tax identification number on it. Life is full of procedures and any office can be a veritable gold mine for their study. How many are there? Are they all documented?

The most elusive property of procedure processing is that everybody seems to know all about it. Unlike data processing, procedures have been carried out in offices for centuries, perhaps millenia. Each procedure can be dissected into a number of well-defined, discrete steps. Some of them may require that we obtain data or information from an outside source before we can proceed. Others may necessitate arithmetical or alphabetical operations. Still others may require that we fill out a form or transfer data from one document to another. All such activities are called directives (sic) in computer-based procedure processors.

Many office workers think of procedures as being free-standing, operating in isolation. Nothing could be further from the truth. The office environment provides a linkage between all extant procedures, documented or simply “known”. There may be shared data bases or common procedural steps. Procedural mechanisms might include the calendar, clocks, and time of day, or triggering events from external sources.

## 5. Five Procedural Processing Tutorial Cases

Let us take a closer look at some typical procedures and observe how they can become sometimes the cause of problems, embarrassment, or financial loss, if not carried out correctly. Or, as in our last case, how a well-designed procedural process can successfully improve an operation's efficiency, reduce its cost, and enhance revenues.

## Case 1: Salary and Grade Review

How to get a raise and perhaps even a promotional change in one's professional rating is certainly a well-known procedure. It's been around since the Egyptians built the pyramids. The basic steps are quite simple:

(1) Allowing for sufficient lead time, the supervisor reviews the employee's performance records, past merit raises and promotions.

(2) The supervisor develops the necessary recommendations with supporting documentation.

(3) The supervisor submits the completed papers for approval or denial to his own superior. He makes any suggested changes in the documentation until concurrence is achieved on both levels. (4) Having obtained approval, the supervisor notifies the employee, enters the necessary changes in grade and payroll records with effective dates and returns all paperwork to the employees' records department.

Right? - Wrong! - Witness the following case study.

Case 1 Continued: The U.S. Navy's Illegal Promotions [2]

During the last three months of 1985, over 300 active and reserve U.S. Navy officers were promoted “illegally” to the rank of Commander. The error resulted from improper handling of a fiscal 1986 promotions list by a secretary. She was told to hold the list while final checks were run on certain officers. So she put the list into her desk...and subsequently retired. The Navy did not discover the “glitch” until after the deadline for Senate confirmation of these officers. Senator Sam Nunn (D-GA), a member of the Senate committee, merely criticized the Navy for “frocking” officers (awarding a new rank when the promotion is approved while withholding the pay raise until the officer formally moves up). Fortunately, no one suggested “defrocking” the hapless victims of this bungle until the pay raises were legally and retroactively approved. But neither has anyone addressed the procedural aspects of the problem – the uncoordinated handling of personnel papers without appropriate safeguards to protect both those who handle the papers and those whose papers are handled.

Hindsight suggests that the handling of papers and documents should be monitored by a computer-based program to ascertain whether all of the procedural steps are carried out correctly and timely. Who knows what damage this bungle did to the Navy's senior officer responsible for it, the retired secretary, and her successor?

## Case 2: Testing of EFTS Realtime Programs

Such tests involve computer networks and the most sophisticated type of software. They must be performed in a live environment but not on live data, at quiescent network hours, such as on weekends. The network users receive advanced notice of the duration of the test. At the indicated time the test program is logged in, data and parameters are entered, and the test procedure is carefully monitored. At the test's conclusion the program is logged out and the results are recorded and reported.

Could anything possibly go wrong? Well, there is always Murphy's Law...

## Case 2 Continued: The Virtual Float [1]

The U.S. Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco routinely transmits funds electronically for most banks in the Western United States. But on the 21st of January 1986, the Fed accidentally and erroneously transferred \$2 billion to each of nineteen financial institutions after bank employees failed to erase a computer program and dummy file which they had tested a few days before. A bank spokesperson called this a "snafu", admitting that the Fed was highly embarrassed. Seventeen of the nineteen institutions cancelled the erroneous funds transfers the same day, but two waited a day before notifying the Federal Reserve Bank. "None of the institutions had benefitted from the episode", said the Fed's spokesperson, who obviously failed to grasp the significance of two banks having a "virtual float" of \$2 billion in their tills for 24 hours. The mere presence of this money in the two banks' asset accounts clearly produced a windfall for them – about \$500,000 at the going interest rates. The program being tested obviously worked well but the procedure for its use left much to be desired. One hopes that other financial institutions have taken note and will develop procedural protocols to avoid similar "embarrassments".

## Case 3: Procedural Computer Security

The procedural steps one must take to protect vital computer data are rather well understood, especially in the financial community. Access to the computer-stored funds data is controlled by the use of passwords, issued only to authorized users. An important maxim is the segregation of authorizations; the password file is not accessible to password holders but only to a non-user with special clearance. What can happen if this vital doctrine is ignored? Well, the District of Columbia found out the hard way:

## Case 3 Continued: The Lost Password [10]

Alvin Frost was a model financial analyst for the District of Columbia. But he also had some disagreements with his superiors about how and where to invest the District's millions of Dollars in cash flow. Frost, a Harvard graduate, had unrestricted access to the computer system – the financial data base and the password file as well. In early February of 1986, when his superiors got on his nerves, he simply changed the computer's access code. Conveniently, he "forgot" the new code words at once, thereby successfully blocking DC's financial management from all access to their own funds. To add insult to injury, he also started a "Guess the Password Contest" in the local press by providing daily clues. District officials finally managed to "break into" their own computer system, at which time their "frozen" assets became liquid again. Since then, Mr. Frost has been fired and his superior, Deputy Mayor Alphonse G. Hill, has been indicted by a Federal Grand Jury in connection with the awarding of city accounting subcontrasts. However, nothing has been said about changing the password management procedure which permitted the whole ruckus in the first place. The final chapter of this incident has yet to be written...

## Case 4: MICR and the Clearing House [9]

Writing of checks is practiced by individuals and legal corporations with equal facility. Thanks to the Magnetic Ink Character Recording (MICR) standards, adopted by the American Bankers Association in the 1950s, the mechanical devices used to read and sort checks can operate at high electronic speeds. Numerous controls are built into the system which effectively debits the check writer's account and credits the recipient's account in less than three days. The physical check is returned by mail to the bank of issue where it is sorted for the last time and placed into the account holder's bin or folder.

As with most electronic systems, there are sources of potential errors. First, the initial data entry of the MICR coded check number and amount may not be correct causing a problem when the check writer tries to reconcile the account balance. Second, any part of the MICR at the bottom of the checkface may be smudged and cannot be read by the electronic devices. In that case the check is rejected by the machine and placed in a special hopper. It is also taken out of the mainstream of its siblings, entering into an entirely different, non-electronic, manual world. Here, the MICR data at the bottom of the check are painstakingly verified and copied into a narrow white strip, affixed to the very bottom edge of the check which folds up for convenience. But the marvel of electronic speed is greatly impeded by this diversion. While the credit action has taken place, debiting may take as much as nine days. During this period of time the writer's account enjoys a "virtual float".

No one understands this better than E.F. Hutton, a well-known, large investment firm in the U.S. "Hutton's New Float Flop" caused the Bank America in California to complain that half of the Hutton checks could not be processed on the bank's MICR equipment and that they required the manual treatment. "Improperly coded items", were the words they used, obviously an elegant euphemism for fraudulent data. Next, the Chemical Bank of New York reported "improperly coded items" amounting to \$48,668,437; that should have netted the E.F. Hutton branch office that issued these checks about \$80,000 in virtual interest.

It appears unbelievable that these operations went unreported for years; it is not even conceivable that they went unnoticed. But office procedural processing is not as highly disciplined as computer data processing and the clerical staff observing the flood of MICR rejects "going manual" on E.F. Hutton's checks may have never heard of virtual floats and thus did not understand what went on.

## Case 5: Procedural Accounts Payable System

General Motors, the world's largest corporation, buys about \$50 billion worth of supplies annually from some 20,000 vendors and pays their five million invoices by as many checks a year. Roger Smith, GM's Chairman some years ago developed a personal interest in the "paperless office" concept, after noticing how many letters had to be typed in addition to the checks, how many envelopes had to be addressed, sealed and stamped, realizing that they all would have to be opened again at the other end where the "check in the mail" would be recorded and deposited. If that were not enough, he must surely have thought about how many checks got lost in transit, or arrived too late, with predictable, unpleasant consequences.

Roger Smith decided to do a complete redesign of the corporation's accounts payable system. GM's new computer facility is located in Dearborn. It works smoothly, more efficiently and less costly, having recovered most, if not all, of the initial investment made in its design and implementation. It uses the most advanced networking technology to process millions of transactions per year. Invoice formats have been standardized; they are transmitted from the vendors' terminals to Dearborn where they are logged in and electronically acknowledged. The electronic routing number of the vendors' banks is part of the large data base which supports the system, as are the banks' working hours and special holidays. From the terms of payment, shown on the invoice, the system can determine when to initiate the electronic transfer of funds to pay for the invoice.

The system gives GM better control over its cash flow. Unfortunately, it also eliminates the three-day float of which GM routinely took advantage, an amount estimated by some analysts as \$20 million per year. A decade in planning, the system also eliminates much of the hard-copy correspondence between GM and its vendors – electronic mail has taken over. In summary, it seems as if Chairman Smith's vision of a paperless office has become reality, at least for one major component of GM's operations.

## 6. Procedural Processing in Retrospect

The common elements extracted from the foregoing examples can now be assembled into a definition for procedural processing, much like that given earlier for data processing. We cannot yet designate it as automated procedural processing ("APP") analogous to automatic data processing ("ADP"), simply, because it is not yet automated. Except for the GM accounts payable system, computer-based procedural processing systems are still few in numbers; many are special designs [3], [6], [8], [11]. The U.S. Federal Courts are working on procedural case tracking and docketing systems. Elements of procedural processing are found in a number of terminal-oriented customer service functions from the verification of utility and similar accounts to on-line displays of some financial records in several Automatic Teller Machine systems (ATMs). Computer Aided Instruction (CAI) and a plethora of computer-based technical training manuals are further examples of partially implemented procedures.

Procedural processing must be considered the most basic functional activity found in the office environment. We shall define it as:

The not-necessarily-uninterrupted execution of a systematic sequence of manual operations (“procedure”) often interacting with data processing (“programs”), performed upon hard copy data, forms, records, and the like, including perhaps computer-stored records as well, designed to accomplish a well-defined, structured task.

We emphasize that one of the most fundamental properties of a procedure is its interruptibility. At a certain point in the procedure we may have to look up some special data or information or we may have to make some simple computation; or consult a computer for more complicated calculations; or make a telephone call; or simply go to lunch. All that, before we can tackle the next step in the procedure.

The procedure may also have some built-in deadlines and other decision points. But we also understand that the generally accepted concept of "slack" in office work must also apply. Slack must be built into the time-dependent part of the procedure. In case of conflict between procedures, we must be able to delay work on some of them to meet the higher priority of others. There is an analogy between well designed procedures and digital data networks: Both will break down under saturation.

Office procedures are usually documented. Most offices have handbooks of systems and procedures. They are supposed to be consulted in cases of doubt. Procedures come in all sizes from small to medium to large and colossal. The U.S. Personal Income Tax Code is a well-known procedure. Its short form version has already been computerized by the U.S. Internal Revenue Service and tax consultants. The complete tax code, however, is a procedure which can not claim logical completeness and consistency; it contains loopholes and contradictions.

## 7. A Strategy for Success

For procedural processing to become an effective management tool, we need first to determine whether a procedure selected for computer-assisted implementation is logically complete and consistent; we might also research the source of its authorization and current usefulness. If a procedure no longer serves any identifiable purpose it should be abolished, of course. If its goal has shifted, it should be redefined and newly structured, not modified or retrofitted – both well-known sources of failures in the data processing environment.

There are many striking similarities, besides that one, between data and procedural processing. For example, the study of integrated data processing systems will cause us shortly to consider integrated procedural processing systems. Some procedures are executed in parallel, others are hierarchical in nature. Interlocking procedures must be unravelled by removing inconsistencies. Most procedures have data base access requirements often resulting in the creation of new data which must be stored. Linkages between procedures must be established to accommodate flow of data and logical control signals.

The manager who endeavors to "go procedural"

must follow some basic steps to assure the success of his program; they are much like those which obtain for data processing:

(1) A Procedures Inventory must be set up, listing every procedure within a given office environment, perhaps even some outside the office's jurisdiction because of their interrelationships. The inventory must include a narrative description of each procedure, its purpose and authorization, data and information requirements, timetables and trigger events (which affect the logical course the procedure might take), and linkages to other procedures.

(2) A Logical Analysis for completeness and consistency must be made of all inventoried procedures. Procedural errors must be emended with concurrence and approval of appropriate higher levels of management.

(3) Risk Analysis and Assessment for each procedure must be made the same as for data processing. The automated packages in wide use for ADP will likely also prove applicable here.

(4) First Experience should be obtained for some simple cases as long as the manager feels this is still a learning experience. In the case of smaller applications we have found two software packages worth looking at: FCMC's Staffware [5], an MS-DOS (PC) based system, and Unisys' OFIS Procedure Manager [18], a Unix bases system. Larger applications, such as GM's, must be designed and developed much like large data processing applications. Open-ended, modular designs are preferable to permit future modifications and enhancements.

(5) Testing of a computer-assisted procedural processor must be thorough to assure compliance with the program specifications and to discover logical discrepancies. Personnel training is readily derived from parallel operations of the manual and the computer version.

## 8. The Bottom Line

Clearly the introduction of procedural processing can improve many aspects of office work. We keep in mind that we intend to emplace the computer-assisted version of procedural processing, replacing the prevalent manual one. We have learned from four decades of computerized data processing that managers are more likely to go along with the transition to procedural processing if it is given the context of being a computer-assisted, rather than a computer-managed activity [13]. But it is clear, much as with ADP, that components of procedural processing which are plain rote will and must be taken on by the system, if for no other reason than to relieve the office tedium and boredom.

Computer-assisted procedural processing will prove to have many merits:

(1) Improved Performance will be evident as the office will function more smoothly. The crisis mode of management will be greatly attenuated, even if not totally eliminated.

(2) Reduced Cost of operations will result from the more streamlined execution of procedures and the concomitant reduction of counterproductive chaos so prevalent today.

(3) Fewer Errors will perhaps be the most noticeable and significant contribution. As office functions will be better understood, human error will be reduced to tolerable levels, if not exactly to zero.

(4) Improved Environment will prevail as the office recaptures an atmosphere of serenity and office workers at long last will find their job relaxing, not taxing. They may even learn to like their jobs!

(5) Management Confidence in the correctness of office procedures is bound to increase as procedural, human errors are gradually phased out. With fewer “unpleasant surprises” facing managers and knowledge workers, the growing collective confidence should also produce a greater cohesiveness within the office workforce.

## 9. Is Today's Office Receiving Full Value from its Computers?

We can at last answer the question with a confident "Yes!" Where ADP is concerned, the office is doing as well as can be expected. The system is functioning – not too many glitches. Organizations are carrying on with their assigned missions, meeting deadlines and responsibilities. Offices carry out their tasks with the help of millions of virtual clerical workers; they are managed by a few hundred, perhaps thousand, data processing professionals. It has been a continuing success story, ever since ADP came first into the office.

But the user community has been slow in recognizing the value of computer-assisted procedural processing. Office management has not been the innovative leader that it was during the beginning of the computer revolution. The cries of “There must be a better way of doing things” have swelled into a chorus that is heard in offices everywhere. But procedural errors continue to upset the office equilibrium as much as ever. The notion that ADP systems could also support procedural processing is certainly not new; it has its roots in command and control systems. While procedural processing has found its way into some offices, most recently in the PC environment, it simply has not been recognized as an equal partner with data processing.

Our industrial leaders are currently seeking ways to achieve significant improvements of output and productivity. An important ingredient to success in this endeavor is a significant improvement in the use of our information resources. What better way can there be if we learn to improve, concurrently, the efficiency of our offices. Procedural processing has the potential of triggering the transition to the office of tomorrow – the tools and the knowledge to accomplish this step exist today!

This is a great challenge. But it promises to yield rich rewards to the manager who accepts it, much as the transition to data processing came to his rescue during the past thirty years. Then it was the onerous burden of computation and records management, which we have successfully bequeathed to ADP. Let us continue the success story by relegating the structured components of office procedural processes to the power of the electronic system.

In conclusion, the office managers will receive full value from computers by looking beyond the data processing horizon to computer-assisted procedural processing as the way of the future.
