# Competition and Cooperation in Information Systems Innovation

Eric K. Clemons and Marc Knez
Department of Decision Sciences, The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA 19104-6366, USA

Information systems may be strategic and may have competitive impact on a firm and its industry. Properties of an innovation or an environment that may predict the competitive impact have already been studied; now the process of determining an information systems strategy must be addressed.

Recent work in economics addresses a game theoretic approach to selecting strategy for an innovation, where the decision maker's key choice is when to adopt the innovations. A strategy can be selected, based upon: beliefs concerning cost of development as a function of time of introduction; and estimates of profits, with and without the innovation, and with and without introduction by competitors.

This work can be modified to relate more closely to the selection of a strategy for an information systems innovation. Most specifically, since many information systems are expensive, offer scale advantages, and are available to all members of an industry, cooperation through consortium development may make more sense than competition. Thus the key aspects of an investment decision in strategic information systems are timing, as an early or late developer, and cooperation, as an independent innovator or as a member of a development consortium.

Keywords: Strategic and competitive information systems innovation.

## 2. Review of Industrial Organization

Space does not permit a complete discussion of the theory of industrial organization (IO) that would do it justice. However, a brief description will allow the reader unfamiliar with this area to appreciate the topics to be discussed. As described by Sherer [19] or Waterson [20], the standard approach in IO is the “structure-conduct-performance” paradigm. Structure refers to the specific market structure of the industry in question (i.e., competitive, monopolistic, oligopolistic, etc.). It is understood that the structure of the industry is directly dependent on the underlying demand conditions (i.e., the elasticity of demand); however, these considerations are typically taken as given in the modeling exercise. Given the industry structure, the specific industry conduct is analyzed. At its most basic level, industry conduct describes the behavior of the monopolist that is able to set prices by selecting a point on the demand curve, or the competitive firm that must take price as given. At a less basic level, it captures such things as advertising, R&D, etc. Given structure and then conduct, performance is treated at both the normative and descriptive level. Descriptive performance analysis seeks the identification of the outcomes expected in specific structure-conduct environments, given empirically verifiable experience, while normative performance analysis focuses on the expected outcomes, given the welfare implications $^{1}$ that arise from different market structures. $^{2}$

This area of economic research was initiated by Schumpeter [18]; in his famous treatise Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy, he described the innovation process through his theory of Creative Destruction. The idea is that the prospect of acquiring monopoly rents motivates firms to engage in innovative activity, and without such prospects such activity would not exist. The first firm to innovate will enjoy monopoly rents until those rents are deteriorated through imitation and further innovation by other firms. Given the existence of patents at the time of Schumpeter's treatise, this was not a completely new idea, but it did inspire new and better economic research in the area of technological innovation. The main theme of the research since Schumpeter has been the determination of the market structures that maximize innovative activity. This is in the spirit of studying normative outcomes in the S-C-P paradigm. As one can imagine this requires studying innovation at each step of the S-C-P process. The following is a very brief description of work in this area.

The first models were both static and deterministic. That is, the consideration of time was left out, and there was no uncertainty with respect to the outcome of the introduction of the innovation, much less the research and development process itself. One of the earliest works following this approach is by Arrow [1], who investigated the difference in innovative incentives between competitive and monopolistic environments. In this simple framework the competitive firm has a greater incentive to innovate than the monopolist, simply because there are then greater existing gains to be realized, while the monopolistic firm is already enjoying extra-normal profits. Arrow's results were heavily dependent on the initial conditions that he assumed existed before the introduction of the innovation: he assumed the monopolist starts out with a substantially lower output level than that which would exist in a competitive industry. However, if the monopolist's level of output is closer to the level in the competitive industry, then Arrow's results are reversed. The sensitivity of these results illustrates the importance of initial conditions when making claims about economic outcomes.

One of the next major steps was to introduce time. Mansfield [13] modeled the introduction of an innovation in an industry as a diffusion process. He conjectured that the spread of an innovation through an industry would follow a specific functional form, initially assumed to be S-shaped; that is, the innovation spreads slowly at first, then quite rapidly, and then slowly again until the whole industry has adopted the innovation. This was clearly an extreme abstraction, but it provided an attractive framework in which to consider the dynamics of innovation. This approach was expanded by Reinganum [17] and others. The problem with the diffusion approach was that it did not give much of a description of the strategic interaction between firms. Diffusion models essentially assume the innovation is exogenously introduced to the industry and then analyze the effect of market structure on the diffusion process.

Another approach to the problem does, in fact, focus on the strategic interaction of firms; i.e., on how the prospects of preemption and imitation affect a firm's decision to engage in innovative activity. This type of model will be the focus of the remainder of this paper.

First we give a brief description of the decision theoretic approach adopted by Kamien and Schwartz [10], which appears to have motivated the later game theoretic approach. This model of economic innovation is decision theoretic in that the decision making firm in the model does not take the strategies of the other firms in the market into consideration when making its own strategic choices. Instead, the behavior of the other firms is treated in an abstract probabilistic fashion. The decision variable of the firm contemplating innovation is the date of adoption. The firm implements at a selected adoption date by a specific cost expenditure associated with that date, where it is assumed that the shorter the time to adoption, the higher the expenditure. Firm rivalry is captured in the model through a hazard rate function that describes the probability that a rival firm will adopt the same innovation in the next instant of time. If the rival firm adopts the innovation first, then it has preempted, while if the rival firm adopts second, then it has imitated. With this type of model, Kamien and Schwartz were able to capture rivalry intensity as a key exogenous variable, their model also distinguished between offensive and defensive innovation. Their basic conclusion was that the shortest adoption time will occur either when no rivalry exists of when some intermediate level of rivalry is present. The reasons behind this result is straightforward, especially given the mathematics that underlies its derivation. If the innovation is quite valuable to the firm relative to the costs of adoption, and if no rivalry exists, then it is clear that the firm will want to adopt as soon as possible. Also, if some average level of rivalry is present then the firm will be motivated to adopt the innovation sooner, to obtain the extra profits that come from being first. However, as the threat of preemption increases, it will be optimal for the firm to give up trying to be first and postpone adoption, thus incurring lower adoption costs. Another interesting result is that under reasonable conditions it will be optimal for the firm to adopt the technology, even though its profits will fail. This is simply an example of a prisoner's dilemma outcome: the prospect of another firm's adopting the innovation forces all firms to adopt it. $^{3}$

The average level of rivalry or the threat of preemption is captured in specific mathematical forms, and the above description does not do them justice.

However, a problem with the approach is that it abstracts away the type of strategic interaction between firms that one would expect in: a competitive environment. Capturing this behavior can be accomplished by utilizing the tools of modern non-cooperative game theory. We now provide a full description of a recent model of economic innovation by Katz and Shapiro [11], and, in so doing, introduce the reader to some of the concepts of this modeling approach. We have chosen this model because it is simple, and because it provides insights into selection of a competitive strategy for the introduction of technological innovations.

## 3. Game Theoretic Models for Adoption of Innovation

Katz and Shapiro (KS) distinguish between two different incentives to innovate: (i) Stand-Alone Incentive: if firm i believes that its rival will not innovate, then it compares its post-innovation profits with its current baseline profits. (ii) Pre-emptive Incentive: if the firm believes that its rival will innovate if it does not, then firm i's incentive to innovate is driven by the difference between its baseline profits as the "winner" of the R&D race and its profits as "the "loser" to its rival.

KS work in a duopoly setting, where the two firms must decide when and if they should put an innovation into practice. $^{4}$ They are working with process innovations rather than product innovations. They denote by $PI_{i}^{0}$ (i = 1, 2) firm i's flow of profits, prior to the development of the innovation, under the existing technology. If neither firm ever develops the innovation, then each firm receives a total payoff equal to $PI_{i}^{0}/r$ , where r>0 is the interest rate. $^{5}$ They let T denote the time at which the first firm innovates, and the current cost incurred by the firm at time $I'$ is $K(T)e^{rT}$ , where $K(T)$ is the present value, at time t=0, of development expenses.

The following important assumptions are made about the cost structure that both firms face. Costs of development are extremely high at t=0. This captures the idea that it takes some positive length of time for basic research to make development economically feasible. Cost of development decreases with time, so $d(K(T)e^{rT})/dT<0$ . This captures the intuition that the basic research, which supports development of the innovation, will continually be able to reduce costs. And finally, they assume that development costs fall at a decreasing rate, so the second derivative of $K(T)e^{rT}$ is positive. This is primarily an assumption made for tractability reasons, but it also makes economic sense.

Firm i's flow of profits after it develops the innovation are $PI_{i}^{i}$ , and after firm i develops firm j's flow of profits become $PI_{j}^{i}$ . Note that they allow for both $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{0}$ and $PI_{j}^{i} < PI_{j}^{0}$ . If firm i is the first to develop, doing so at time T, then it will earn flow profits $PI_{i}^{0}$ for t < T, and $PI_{i}^{i}$ for $t \geq T$ , and will incur cost $K(T)$ . Firm i's payoff if it wins the development race at T is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} W _ {i} (T) = \frac {1 - e ^ {- r T}}{r} P I _ {i} ^ {0} + \frac {e ^ {- r t}}{r} P I _ {i} ^ {i} - K (T), \\ i = 1, 2. \end{array}\tag{*}
$$

while the payoff to firm j if it loses the race to firm i at T is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} L _ {j} (T) = \frac {1 - e ^ {- r T}}{r} P I _ {j} ^ {0} + \frac {e ^ {- r T}}{r} P I _ {j} ^ {i}, \\ i = 1, 2 j \neq i. \end{array}\tag{\((^{**})\}
$$

To determine what type of outcomes would occur in this environment KS employ the subgame perfect equilibrium concept. Each firm's strategy will be its selection of a date to develop the innovation. As KS point out "Firm i's incentive to develop the innovation depends upon the profits that the firm would earn if it did not develop. These profits, in turn, depend on whether its rival, firm j, would develop the innovation if firm i did not." The use of the subgame perfect equilibrium concept allows KS to build this logic into their predicted outcomes. Specifically, each firm chooses a strategy based on its beliefs about the strategy of the other firm. An equilibrium outcome occurs if firm i's strategy choice maximizes its profits given firm j's strategy, and simultaneously firm j maximizes its profits using the strategy that firm i believes it is going to use, while itself believing that firm i is going to use the above specified strategy. The assumption of subgame perfection merely insures that each firm is believed to be following strategies that are credible. This leads to several simple but very interesting results.

We also note that this approach is descriptive, to the extent that it is assumed that each firm maximizes profits. But it can clearly be seen as prescriptive, given this maximizing assumption. Also, it may seem that extreme assumptions have been made about each firm's level of information concerning future profits. Obviously the necessary Ps are not available with certainty or precision. And, just as obviously, firms do make assumptions about their future profits and the profits of competitors, under various sets of actions by both players. The value of KS's analysis is that it shows how these estimates of profit can be combined to determine a firm's strategy. Uncertainty in the $I^{1}$ s can be readily treated using expected values; if necessary, the model can be extended to accommodate the decision maker's risk preferences.

Firm i's stand-alone incentive is $PI_{i}^{i} - PI_{i}^{0}$ . KS show that if firm j is not going to develop, and if $PI_{i}^{i} - PI_{i}^{0}$ is sufficiently large relative to the development cost, then there will exist a time T > 0 such that firm i will develop the innovation. Note that the stand-alone incentive must be positive for development to occur in this case.

If instead, firm i believes that if it does not develop at time T then firm j will, then firm i must choose between developing or letting firm j develop first. When the profits from winning at time T are greater than the profits from losing, $(W_{i}(T) \geq L_{i}(T))$ , then firm i will choose to preempt firm j at time T. The two expressions above imply that if $PI_{i}^{i} - PI_{i}^{j} \leq rK$ , then firm i will never choose to preempt, where K is the limit of current development costs. Here $PI_{i}^{i} - PI_{i}^{j}$ is firm i's preemption incentive. If the preemption incentive is greater than rK then there will exist a T > 0 such that firm i will choose to preempt at any time after T.

Up to now, we have only discussed the firm's decision to preempt or not, and little has been said about the incentive to imitate. In fact, KS do not specifically distinguish between the follower firm's profits before and after the rival firm develops the innovation. This difference is embedded in $PI_{i}^{j}/r$ . However, they do extend their model to capture explicitly how different levels of imitation ability affect the equilibrium outcomes. The general result that emerges from their formulation is:

If one firm has a substantial technological advantage (a cost leader), then that firm will be unlikely to develop innovations that are easily imitated and tend to equalize costs across the industry. These innovations will come from firms that have high costs and low market shares prior to the innovation.

We see the KS framework as an effective modeling paradigm for analyzing the economic implications of IS innovations.

## 4. Competition and Cooperation in Information Services Innovation

## 4.1. Unique Characteristics of Information Systems

Information systems innovations, particularly those large enough and important enough to be considered strategic, often possess characteristics that distinguish them from other objects of research and development competition:

\- There is a large fixed cost, and low or even negligible variable cost, for all transactions processed; this implies the possibility of significant scale advantages.

\- The fixed cost is actually a sunk cost, which implies a significant penalty for failure.

\- It is extraordinarily difficult to protect innovative applications of information systems through patents, trade secrets, of the use of proprietary technology; this increases the viability of the follower strategy and increases the risk of being the innovator. There is considerable evidence that the follower can often match the innovation at significantly lower development costs.

We have evidence that information systems innovations can benefit all industry stakeholders, including the innovating firm, copying competitors, and customers. Information systems can accomplish this by reducing costs while increasing the quality of service; in the absence of barriers to competitors, the innovative systems are duplicated, and competitive pressures force the benefits to be shared with customers.

There are conflicting interpretations of these characteristics. Scale advantages argue for the existence of first mover effects, and thus would prompt early innovation. However, the cost of failure, and the difficulty of protecting the benefits of success in the development rate, would argue against innovation, early or late, except as a defensive move. And yet, as we have seen time and again, the benefits that IS innovation can convey, in reduced costs and improved quality, is sufficient to justify innovation in many industries.

## 4.2. Strategic Options in Competition and Cooperation

Existing game theoretic analysis considers two options – winning and losing the development race. A third option exists, and is increasingly important in strategic information systems development: pursuing the development as a member of a consortium. As the implications of the previous section become more widely accepted, and as firms accept both the benefits of innovation and the limited incremental benefits of winning the development race, we believe that the consortium approach to strategic infrastructure development will become increasingly common.

Benefits of consortium development include:

\- Reduced unit costs, due to increased transaction volumes.

\- Presence of significant network externalities - e.g., the more banks that join an ATM network, the more valuable the network is for all member banks.

-- Reduced development costs for each individual player in the consortium.

Moreover, the consortium solution appears to be stable in a game-theoretic sense: companies that defect and drop out of the consortium forfeit considerable sunk costs involved in the development effort.

Of course, acceptance of the consortium strategy does not mean that firms choose not to compete, and it does not even mean that they choose not to compete in the area of the information systems innovation. It does mean that they choose not to compete in the development of what will prove to be an unprotected, commodity-like service.

Again, ATMs provide an excellent example. Banks that have joined an ATM network like NYCE in New York or MAC in Philadelphia continue to compete in retail financial services; they do not compete in the routine provision of ATM service, since competition for customers in this way no longer appears possible.

Thus we see two dimensions to a firm's decision on entering into a development race in information services. If the firm believes that there are potential competitive benefits to winning the race, and it believes that it can win, it may choose to attempt to be a technology leader; it may alternatively choose to be a technology follower. Additionally, if it believes it has all the resources needed to compete and sees benefits to competing without assistance, it may choose an independent or solo development strategy; alternatively, it may choose to be a member of a consortium development effort.

These options can be summarized in the following $2 \times 2$ strategic grid:

<table><tr><td></td><td>Technology leader</td><td>Technology follower</td></tr><tr><td>Independent developer</td><td>Independent leader (aggressive, expects advantage)</td><td>Independent follower</td></tr><tr><td>Member of a consortium</td><td>Consortium member leader</td><td>Consortium member follower (defensive)</td></tr></table>

We see six possible strategic choices implied by this grid:

\- prior independent leader – the firm decides, in advance of competitor actions, to develop early and independently.

\- prior consortium leader - the firm decides, in advance of competitor actions, to develop early as a member of a consortium.

\- prior independent follower – the firm decides, in advance of competitor actions, to adopt the innovation independently but not to attempt to win the development race.

\- prior consortium follower – the firm decides, in advance of competitor actions, to adopt the innovation as part of a consortium and not to try to win the development race.

\- ex post independent follower – the firm decides, after seeing a competitor's adoption of the innovation, to attempt to duplicate, acting independently.

\- ex post the firm decides, after seeing a competitor's adoption of the innovation, to attempt to duplicate, acting as a member of a consortium.

## 4.3. Notation

Our treatment of competition and cooperation in information services innovation requires the following notation: $^{6}$

$PI_{i}^{0}$ status quo:
profits for firm i in the absence of this innovation.

$PI_{i}^{i}$ victory profits:
profits for firm i if it wins the innovation race; stand alone incentive = $PI_{i}^{i} - PI_{i}^{0}$ .

$PI_{j}^{i}$ loss penalty:
profits for firm j if it loses and firm i wins the innovation race; this does not preclude firm j from imitating the innovation at some later date.

$PI_{j}^{i,-j}$ capitulation penalty: profits for firm j if firm i wins the innovation race and firm j then chooses not to attempt to duplicate.

$PI_{i\ in C}^{C}$ consortium incentive:

profits for firm i if it joins consortium C and the consortium wins the innovation race.

$PI_{j\text{ not in }C}^{C}$ non-participation penalty: profits for firm j if the consortium wins the innovation race but firm j chooses not to be a member.

$PI_{j\text{in}C}^{i}$ consortium loss penalty: profits for firm j if it joins consortium but the innovation race is won by firm i.

$PI_{j\text{ in }C}^{i,-C}$ consortium capitulation penalty: profits for firm $j$ if $j$ joins consortium, innovation race is won by firm $i$ , and the consortium chooses not to attempt to duplicate.

We are now able to analyze each of the six strategies. Different firms may make different assumptions about the profits from their adopting or not adopting a specific information technology innovation; moreover, these firms may also differ in their assumptions concerning the benefits to be gained when competitors do or do not innovate. We show how each of the six strategies can be justified by different assumptions about these profits. These are normative analyses, justified by economic theory and limited numbers of specific investment decisions in information technology. Moreover, they appear plausible. We have not yet attempted to verify them empirically.

## 4.4. Prior Independent Leader

A firm that chooses to develop independently, and as a leader in the technology innovation, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{i}^{i} > PI_{i}^{0}$ (Innovator's profiles will increase due to innovation).

\- $PI_{i}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i}$ (Innovator will receive greater benefits than follower).

That is, the innovator should believe that the innovation can provide competitive advantage. Variants of the prior independent leader strategy are possible. For example, a firm that lacks the resources to develop independently, but which believes that the innovation may provide competitive advantage, can choose to develop through a joint venture with a partner outside the industry. This partner may provide financial resources, technical expertise, or knowledge of a specific market or industry. Unfortunately, firms providing scarce resources in joint ventures often appropriate for themselves some of the resulting benefits, and may even enter into competition with their partners. This can be captured in assessments of the PIs. Alternatively, in geographically fragmented markets, non-competing firms in the same industry may share development in a cartel; this may appear to be a form of consortium strategy, but actually is better modeled as parallel simultaneous examples of prior independent leader development.

Our experience is that competitive advantage through information systems, like competitive advantage anywhere, is quite difficult to achieve. It requires significant barriers to competitors copying, or early and successful preemption of the market. Conditions likely to result in competitive outcomes are analyzed in more detail in an earlier paper [2]; these results can be used to estimate PIs. Successful examples include Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Accounts [7], and United and American Airlines Travel Agent Reservation Systems.

## 4.5. Prior Consortium Leader

A firm that chooses to develop as a member of a consortium, and with the consortium as a leader in the development of the technology innovation, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{k}^{i} > PI_{k}^{0}$ , and $PI_{k}^{i} > PI_{k}^{i - k}$ , for all $k$ choosing to adopt the innovation (Unit profits will be better for all players in the industry that develop the innovation regardless of who actually develops it first)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} = PI_{i}^{i}$ for all $j$ (There is little or no beneficial impact on unit profits for individual firms developing early)

\- $COST(t_{1}) > COST(t_{2}), t_{1} < t_{2}$ (Costs decrease over time; this is a reason for waiting and it imposes a cost penalty for developing early, in advance of individual competitors)

\- $PJ_{\text{in } C}^{C} > PI_{\text{in } C}^{i}$ (And yet, there is an incentive for the consortium to develop early and to preempt development by an individual competitor)

There is only limited experience to date with the consortium leader strategy. Most costly investments in information technology have been intended to provide competitive advantage. Additionally, regulatory problems could have been anticipated from attempting to develop strategic IS in a non-competitive fashion. However, there is growing recognition, at least in the banking community, that many IS have effectively become commodities rather than competitive weapons, and that there are benefits to all players from cooperative development [12]. Regulators are starting to permit cooperative systems development, where reduced product competition is not expected to result; the newly announced European integrated Credit Card and EFTPOS network is an early example [8].

## 4.6. Prior Independent Follower

A firm that chooses to develop independently, and as a follower in the technology innovation, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i - j}$ (Given that firm $j$ chooses to lose the technology development race, $j$ still expects its profits to increase as a result of adopting the innovation, which is a reason for innovating eventually)

\- $COST(t_{1}) > COST(t_{2}), t_{1} < t_{2}$ (Costs decrease over time, which is a reason for waiting)

\- Reduction in development cost due to waiting, $(COST(t_2) - COST(t_1))$ , will exceed decrease in profits due to foregoing winning the technology race $(PI_j^i - PI_j^{i,j})^*(t_2 - t_1)$

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{i}^{i}$ (Decision maker believes that the initial innovator will not preempt the marketplace, and that it will still be possible to compete)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i, -k}$ (Or, alternatively, the decision maker believes that there is still potential to compete, and to gain advantage, through the innovation; even though he chooses not to be the earliest developer, he believes that it is possible to gain advantage over other non-innovators or late developers)

The final two points explain why the firm chooses not to participate in a consortium. It believes that it enjoys differential comparative advantages, which will enable it to compete using the innovation more successfully than other firms. Since it believes it will enjoy a competitive advantage from the introduction of the innovation, it is unlikely to want to transform the innovation into a commodity by shared development.

Much of the benefit from the independent follower strategy comes from price reductions over time; e.g., banks that obtained ATMs after prices declined. Also, there is some evidence that late competitors, operating solo with significant market share, gain stronger negotiating positions when joining a consortium. For example, Provident National Bank developed a strong independent ATM network. This gave it considerable bargaining strength when negotiating with Philadelphia National Bank to join PNB's shared ATM network MAC; Provident was the first member of MAC granted the right to do its own processing of transactions originating from its customers on its own machines.

Variants of the independent follower strategy exist. For example, for truly routine, commodity applications, it may make sense to wait until a third party provider makes a standard system available, and then to tailor it to specific needs of the company. This is unlikely to provide advantage, of course, but is a reasonable way of obtaining non-strategic applications inexpensively. We see the decision to enter late, but still to attempt to compete, as an unusual one; if it is possible to gain advantage, early innovators may have preempted the market, and if it is not possible to gain advantage, there is probably little reason to attempt to compete. We have found examples where it is appropriate, but it is a risky strategy, requiring careful justification.

## 4.7. Prior Consortium Follower

A firm that chooses to develop as a member of a consortium, and as a follower in the technology innovation, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i,j}$ (Even as a late entrant, the decision maker expects profits to increase as a result of the innovation, which is a reason for innovating eventually)

\- $COST(t_{1}) > COST(t_{2}), t_{1} < t_{2}$ (Costs decrease over time, which is a reason for waiting)

\- $PI_{k}^{i} > PI_{k}^{0}$ for all $k$ choosing to develop the innovation, or there is certainty that firm $i$ will develop the innovation (Either the innovation will benefit all firms in the industry, as a beneficial strategic necessity, $^{7}$ or there is certainty that it will be developed by another firm and a need to avoid being placed at a disadvantage by this development)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} = PI_{k}^{i}$ , for all $j$ and $k$ imitating $i$ 's innovation (There is little or no competitive impact on different players adopting after $i$ , implying that the development mandated by the previous points should probably be undertaken as a shared consortium)

\- $PI_{j\text{in }C}^{C} > PI_{k\text{not in }C}^{C}$ for all $j$ in consortium, $k$ not in consortium (There are benefits, like network externalities, that accrue to all players in the consortium)

\- $PI_{i}^{i} = PI_{i}^{j}$ (There is little or no beneficial impact on unit profits for developing early, and hence the consortium should choose to develop late)

We have extremely limited experience with decisions made, in advance, to be a consortium follower. It appears a very rational response, if it is believed that there will be limited opportunities to compete through the innovation and significant benefits to development when costs are reduced.

## 4.8. Ex Post Independent Follower

A firm that chooses to develop independently, and as a follower in the technology innovation, and that makes this decision after it is clear that it will lose or has already lost the development race, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i,j}$ (Given that $i$ 's innovation is already in place, firm $j$ believes that its profits will be greater after duplication than before)

\- $COST_{j\text{in} C}^{i} = COST_{j}^{i}$ (there is little benefit to be obtained by reducing costs through shared development as a member of a consortium)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{i}^{i}$ (The decision maker believes that the initial innovator has not yet preempted the marketplace, and that it is still possible to compete)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{k}^{i, -k}$ and $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{k}^{i}$ (Or, alternatively, the decision maker believes that there is still potential to compete and to gain advantage through the innovation; even though he knows he will not be the earliest developer, he believes that it is possible to gain advantage over other non-innovators or late developers)

In short, a firm adopting the independent follower strategy, and choosing it after the introduction of an innovation by a competitor, should believe that the innovation has potential competitive impact, and that it will enjoy some comparative advantage in this competition despite its late start.

United Airlines' experience in the late 1970s in matching American's Sabre with Apollo indicates that there may be room for a second individual competitor when the market has not yet been preempted; this move followed very rapidly after American's initial move. The more recent experience of TWA with PARS and Texas Air with System One indicate the difficulty of late solo responses to established competitors. In most externally focused applications aimed at controlling a distribution channel, and in most applications with large scale advantage, there appears to be little benefit to the late solo strategy:

\- Earlier players will have learning curve benefits.

\- If the market can be preempted, it will have been preempted; if it cannot be preempted then there is probably little benefit to the solo development strategy.

## 4.9. Ex Post Consortium Follower

A firm that chooses to develop as a member of a consortium, and as a follower in the technology innovation, and that makes this decision after it is clear that it will lose or has already lost the development race, should believe the following:

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i, -j}$ (There is clear benefit to duplicating the competitor's innovation)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} = PI_{k}^{i}$ , for all $j$ and $k$ other than initial innovator (There is little or no benefit in attempting to develop a competitive response solo)

\- $COST_{j\text{in} C}^{i} < COST_{j}^{i}$ , for all $j$ (cooperative development will cost each player in the consortium less than independent development

\- $PI_{j}^{i} > PI_{j}^{i,j}$ , for all $j$ choosing to innovate (Unit profits will be better for all players in the industry that develop the innovation)

\- $PI_{j}^{i} = PI_{k}^{i}$ , for all $j$ and $k$ developing the innovation after firm $i$ (There is little or no competitive impact to different players adopting after $i$ )

\- $PI_{j\text{ in }C}^{i} > PI_{k\text{ not in }C}^{i}$ , for all $j$ in the development consortium, and for all $k$ not in the consortium (There are benefits, like network externalities, that accrue to all players in the consortium)

The late consortium approach, when selected after the initial introduction of a technology innovation, appears to be a rational response to an attempt by a strong competitor to preempt and dominate the market. NYCE in New York may be seen as a response to CitiBanks' ATM network. Similarly, Shawmut and Bank of New England's consortium can be viewed as a response to BayBanks' success with its proprietary ATM network.

## 5. Conclusions and Directions for Future Research

The material presented here extends the game theoretic treatment of innovation investment decisions to incorporate the possibility of cooperation within an industry consortium. This, although needing further work, represents a contribution to the strategic planning process.

We believe that the paper has two principal contributions:

\- It presents a comprehensive taxonomy of the available options when facing a decision to invest in innovative information technology.

\- It summarizes what the decision maker should believe in order to select each of the options on a rational basis.

These incorporate both timing considerations and anticipater actions by competitors explicitly. While our supporting evidence is drawn largely from the MIS area, the work presented should facilitate the analysis of any decision to invest in a potentially strategic innovation.
