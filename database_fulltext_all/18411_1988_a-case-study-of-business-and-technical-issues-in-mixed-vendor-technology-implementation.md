# A Case Study of Business and Technical Issues in Mixed-Vendor Technology Implementation

Kathy Brittain White

Bryan School of Business & Economics, University of North Carolina at Greensboro, Greensboro, NC 27412-5001, USA

James Gabler

Management Systems, Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital, Greensboro, NC 27401-1020, USA

The Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital is challenging the rationale used in the traditional mainframe approach to computerization. Now that Local Area Networks (LANs) simplify interfacing and micros/minicomputers have reversed the economies of multiple computers, a new approach can be taken which connects multiple computer systems. Rather than consolidating all data in one data base, the systems approach allows relevant working data to be stored in each functional module while focusing on the small percentage of summary data that actually needs to flow between systems. Moses Cone's approach involves two complementary design processes. First, the foundation is a hospital-wide LAN called SIAI LAN which connects functional nodes in each department. Second, the open architecture of this foundation allows Moses Cone to build its information system using component systems from multiple vendors. This approach supports the integrative nature of their information flows while also supporting their decentralized management style.

Keywords: Systems approach, Economies of scale, Hospital information systems, Grosch's Law, Decentralized management, Microcomputers, Local Area Networks (LANs).

## 1. Introduction

Significant advances in technology have greatly increased the capabilities of communications networks. Costs are declining, and both deregulation and the AT&T divestiture have introduced a tremendous array of new options. In such an environment, the strategic opportunities for the application of technology have never been greater. Many companies now find that information networking can be exploited to create competitive advantage in the marketplace, to increase productivity, and to enable new forms of organizational design.

## 2. Background

Historically, automating hospitals has been difficult due to the complexity, heterogeneity, and interdependence which characterize a hospital information system (Simborg, 1983). Individual hospital departments are more complex than their counterparts in other industries. For example, the hospital accounts receivable department differs from that in other businesses because few of the hospital's "customers" pay their own bills. The resulting mix of insurance company payments, partial payments and reporting requirements makes for a uniquely complex environment. Also, a hospital scheduling system must allow for a number of unpredictable factors (e.g., when a bed will be vacated) that other schedulers, such as those for airline reservations, do not have to deal with. Heterogeneity results from each department having significantly different computer processing requirements. For example, the primary processing needs of laboratory, radiology, and pharmacy are process control, text processing, and dynamic data base updates, respectively. Such variety often results in conflicting optimization strategies. Interdependence recognizes that a patient will involve all or many hospital departments on a given stay or visit.

In 1982, Moses Cone Hospital, a 543 bed regional teaching center located in Greensboro, North Carolina, began a computerization search process based on the centralized mainframe approach typically found in other large hospitals. Given the three attributes (complexity, heterogeneity, and interdependence) previously described, no mainframe system optimized the needs of 'the various departments within the hospital. Two other factors also played a role in redirecting the process of computerizing the hospital toward decentralized departmental processing. First, the administration at Moses Cone Hospital believes in decentralized management and decision-making. Management' believes that if departments are accountable for delivering their product or service, and are allowed to control the resources directly, greater productivity naturally follows. Management wanted computer technology that would support the existing organizational structure. Second, technological developments such as LANs and microcomputers have created significantly new tools for addressing computerization which would support decentralized departmental processing.

Interface complexities and economies of scale (Grosch's Law) have discouraged multiple systems, but technological developments in these areas have made the Moses Cone approach both practical and economical. These were addressed briefly in the introduction but require further expansion to explain the computerization approach used at the hospital.

Interfaces have historically been discouraged, not only because they were difficult to develop, but also because they were difficult to maintain over time. Complexity increases exponentially as the number of components interfaced increase. For example, connecting two computers requires two interface programs – one in each machine to “talk” to the other. Adding a third computer requires six programs, four computers requires twelve programs, etc. This is known as the “ $N^2$ - $N$ problem” (Simborg, 1984) where $N$ is the number of computers. Although limiting the hardware and software can simplify the maintenance, one rarely saw more than two or three computers interfaced. Local Area Networks (LANs) have significantly simplified development and maintenance since only one program per computer is necessary to interface to the LAN. The LAN delivers addressed messages similarly to the Post Office delivering mail. It is critical to note that LANs simplify only the mechanics of interfacing; the content of messages (data exchange) is a separate issue and must be addressed by the overall design as in any system - computerized or not. Thus, it is practical to connect multiple modules.

The second technological change involves Grosch's Law. Economies of scale have led to volume combinations in many areas. Computer centralization is typically based on this assumption. If all computers are plotted on a single price performance curve, the price per operation (e.g., MIPS or whatever) would appear to decrease as computer power increased (Grosch's law). However, "when computers are grouped according to their size and power, Grosch's law seems to hold within each group, but not between different groups." These groups are "microcomputers, minicomputers, small mainframes, large mainframes, and super computers" (Ein-Dor, 1985). It is critical to note that a microcomputer is clearly not equivalent to a minicomputer for pure processing (just ask anyone who's processed a large data base on both). However, if the processing can be redefined to function well on a combination of computers in a lower price/performance group, it is quite likely the total cost will be less since multiple units in the lower groups can be purchased for the same cost as one unit in a higher group. Savings result when large central processor requirements decrease even though the cumulative processing power increases. Thus, it may be economical to decentralize processing into multiple modules.

## 3. Design

Moses Cone's approach involves two complementary design processes. First, the foundation is a hospital wide LAN called STAT LAN, which connects functional nodes in each department. STAT LAN is a commercially available product of Simborg Systems Corporation. Rather than the typical use of LANs to connect terminals to computers, STAT LAN focuses on computer to computer communication. Each node is a complete processing system (i.e., a small mainframe, mini-computer, microprocessor, or departmental microprocessor LAN), which includes its own terminals, printers, disks, etc; thus intra-system communications are isolated allowing system designers complete freedom within their niche. A natural concern in any system is the ability to handle the workload. With departmental systems handling direct user interactions, most processing requires minimal network activity. Since only necessary control data is broadcast, the Network Manager itself handles relatively few transactions (one for each registration, admit, discharge, or transfer). Other transactions which do not involve the Network Manager may also be used to request patient information, order entry, order status/cancellation, result display, and electronic mail. Although the total LAN activity is not insignificant (thousands per day), the departmental systems collectively handle internally the hundreds of thousands of transactions necessary to process the information needs of the hospital. Response time is more consistent since many centralized bottle necks have been eliminated and each departmental system focuses on its own capacity requirements.

## 4. Information Flows

To understand how the design relates to our business, the information flows must be understood. As a patient progresses through a hospital visit, there are many varied activities which may occur. Information is generated and used in five basic areas-registration, patient care, support services, billing and management. Rather than focus on the manipulation or storage of data, our focus was on the “movement of information” (Sullivan and Smart, 1987). For example, when a patient is registered, information clearly must be communicated to patient care, support services, and billing, but the exact data content is much more variable over time than the triggering event. The information flows precede and thus influence the definition of lower levels of detail. There information flows are also more easily understood by non-computer managers since they logically connect natural work activities. This skeleton provides a flow structure for multiple information (data) flows.

If standards are established for the flows, a modular component hospital information system is created (similar to a component stereo system). Now each function can be addressed as a “box” which either junctions satisfactorily or not. Technology issues become secondary to the primary business issues since managers need only ask: “What features are needed?" "How much do they cost?" "Are they worth it?", etc.

## 5. Standards and Procedures

The Network Integration Protocol, provided by STAT LAN, is a written document specifying what individual vendor systems must do to participate in the network. This document became the guiding management tool in vendor negotiations. The initial selection of vendors was a joint process involving departmental personnel and a Management Systems systems analyst. The systems analyst acted as a consultant to the department to help translate sales hype and to evaluate technical implications. The contract negotiations with the vendors again consisted of a coordination between the department and Management Systems.

The analysis and design of each departmental system is another joint venture between the department and Management Systems. This procedure has significantly redefined the information expert and user expert roles. The user expert within any given department focuses on the functional flows by which they operate regardless of how those functions are technically implemented. The information expert, an employee of Mangement Systems, must generally understand the functional flow whitin the departmental system, but concentrates on the global information flows between systems and in particular that department's role in those global flows. This specifically includes synchronization, data transfers, and reconciliation issues between systems.

The emphasis on modularity not only allows departmental ownership, it also provides almost complete isolation from total outage since each module performs virtually all departmental processing. All computer-related problems are initially handled by Management Systems through an SOS (Systems Operation Support) procedure which has been in use for over a year. This is a single phone number through which all problems are logged and the appropriate support personnel dispatched (hospital hardware support, vendor support, user expert, etc.). Thus, problem resolution is under the control of Management Systems but is simplified by the modular design. The number of trouble shooting problems is dramatically reduced by the modular approach since individual vendors are responsible for intra-system problems. Inter-system problems can be quickly defined by verifying whether the protocols are being met-a system is either responding as defined or not.

In addition, project management techniques have been modified to fit our approach. Prototypes developed on the first interfaces have been documented and used on subsequent interfaces. Many of the standards and procedures have evolved as Moses Cone has gained experience since there were no models to use.

## 6. Management Process

Although the installation of a modular departmental LAN system is now technically possible, the institution's management philosophy must allow and control the process. Since each department is dependent on others, the integration of departmental systems stresses the management structure because independent decisions made at the departmental level must be coordinated as the LAN becomes operational. The socialization process of integrating a decentralized management structure to a common goal is similar to the management of messages through the LAN. Control is necessary but freedom to pass information in an organized manner is essential.

In keeping with the decentralized management style, the department is ultimately responsible for the selection, implementation, operation, and if practical the possession of their system. The department head is expected to take a leadership role in the entire process, not only by positively promoting the system's value to the department but also by reinforcing conformance through the performance appraisal process. This requires visible ownership within the department throughout the project.

## 7. Limitations

One limitation is finding willing vendors, although this has been less of a problem than was originally expected. If vendors see themselves in a niche role, this approach is attractive since it provides another sales opportunity and their systems become a participant in a larger system. Vendors who see themselves as a single source provider are less inclined to participate since they already do multiple functions that the hospital has already implemented or desires to implement. Obviously, they would rather get the whole business than a portion of it.

Consistent data flows between departmental systems is necessary for control and naturally imposes some minimal limits on departmental systems selection. If departmental changes or additions are made simply for convenience without careful scrutiny, the logical flow structure could become less effective. For example, if a department obtains its own information from the first or easiest source available, dependencies could develop between systems which “lock” them together. Another example would be making an intra-departmental change which affects the data sent to other systems.

To make sure these limitations of the system approach are minimized, an active information systems individual is required to constantly monitor needs to insure the simplest, most reliable information flows. The authority of this individual to control these flows must be strongly supported by top management.

## 8. Benefits

Moses Cone's benefits can be categorized as operational, managerial, and economic. Operational benefits result from the department's ownership of their system. Participation is obvious necessary. Specialized systems – independent of hospitalwide compatibility compromises – are more consistent with the way the department views its responsibilities and accountabilities within the organization. This has resulted in improved quality since the users want their system to work for them. Difficulties are positively addressed, minimizing “finger pointing” at data processing. Their isolation from other hospital activities allows them to continue processing even when other components of the system may be down. In addition, the departments are in a better position to incorporate advances in their field since upgrades, additions, or replacements are easily accommodated and minimally impact the total system.

Managerial benefits result from improved control, greater flexibility, and strategic use of options. Managerial control is a direct result of being able to evaluate functional results without computer expertise. By defining systems at the functional working level, responsibility is kept within existing accountability structures. Managers more easily understand and accurately evaluate cost and benefits of their systems at this level, and their decision process is easier due to the freedom to make relatively independent choices. Thus, the manager can focus more on the non-technical issues since technical issues are now secondary. One example was the Pharmacy department working with their system vendor to achieve appropriate performance rather than complaining about the hospital's system.

Flexibility is the ability to change after plans have been initiated. The freedom to modify implementation sequences or to replace existing systems because of management prerogatives, political issues, or rapid technical change is inherent due to the relative independence of each system.

As new products are introduced that fit into the institutional plan, sequencing can be altered to take advantage of price, technology, or cooperative relationship. For example, the Radiology system was implemented several months early to take advantage of some unique economic opportunities. More than one system can be installed at one time, while Management Systems works to insure the integration of the modules on the LAN. Even before the departmental system is fully operational on the LAN, benefits are derived in the department, thus furthering the goals of the total institution. Also, new functions can use existing data sources rather than build an entire system. And since multiple-vendor systems are being connected within the hospital, existing systems in other healthcare providers can be just as easily connected without ownership or direct control. This clearly allows strategic, value-added linkages.

A third benefit is economic savings. The overall capital costs have been less than traditional approaches. The total cost for STAT LAN and the departmental systems will be just under \$5 million starting with the lab system in 1984 before STAT LAN existed. A significantly smaller hospital in the area spent \$6 million two years ago but did not include some of the departmental functions included in the Moses Cone system. When Moses Cone started, the traditional mainframe approach was estimated at \$6–8 million for a 500-bed hospital exclusive of some of the functions included in the system now. Also, these expenditures have been phased over time rather than paid "up front." In addition, staffing levels are less than hospitals of similar size. Management Systems has a total of 29 positions including managers, supervisors, systems analyst, operators, and secretarial support. Other initial benefits have included direct cost justification and quantifiable benefits at the departmental level and the ability to leverage competition for each departmental system.

On-going economic benefits result from continued competitive leverage when adding or replacing any modular system (not “locked” into one vendor). Staffing can remain at or below initial levels since development and maintenance are the responsibility of system vendors who spread these costs over a large customer base rather than a single customer. Hardware capacity is more stable since growth is primarily volume related. Volume growth is generally gradual and more predictable. Function growth is more rapid once it starts and is less predictable, particularly if it is absorbed into existing capacity. This starts another vicious cycle where capacity expands in large increments, generating excess capacity, encouraging the absorption of new functions, and then exceeding capacity sooner than expected. With our approach, functional growth is modularized and more easily evaluated as to its value to the institution (management “deciding” rather than data processing “absorbing”).

The bottom line benefit is that management is controlling technology instead of the normal approach where in most instances the technology, hidden away in the data processing area, constrains (controls) how efficiently an institution can take advantage of computerization.

## 9. Conclusion

Communication technology offers opportunities that can create competitive advantage in the marketplace, increase productivity, and enable new forms of organizational design or support existing organizational design rather than change it. As in any new program development, management must gather the data, examine the cost and benefits, and be willing to decide which course is best for the individual situation. Decentralized management has been the organizational design at Moses H. Cone Hospital and it was practical to choose an information systems design that supported and complemented the existing organizational style.

From a technological perspective, the systems approach allows for a redefinition or problems that may fit the organization better than traditional mainframe approaches. The focus on the movement of information adds a perspective that produces systems to support critical business information flows rather than emphasizing the storage and manipulation of data. Although most large organizations in the United States are characterized by mainframe computing, many continue to have some departments marked by standalone systems without any type of linkage among systems. This approach could be modified in such environments to accomplish total integration. Smaller organizations may desire to link microcomputers. The technical design issues may be relevant to those firms.

The authors' believe the theme of this paper goes beyond the technical issues that were addressed and point to the willingness of an organization to rethink traditional computerization approaches and address technological opportunities in the light of new technological tools. Such an approach is relevant to every organization and to every manager.
