# Competitive Information Systems in Australian Retail Banking \*

Michael T. Sager

School of Management, Deakin University, Victoria, 3217, Australia

Competitive information systems may provide sustainable competitive advantage to a firm through the use of information technology and systems. Current research suggests generalizations about their nature, based only on a limited range of apparently successful systems from different industries and periods. However, the approach in this study is to look at one industry, and so at a set of comparable businesses and competitive information systems. The Australian banking industry, an industry in transmission from oligopoly to competition, is used as the basis for a study of competitive information system, using an industry-wide framework. Competitive information systems appear as a more complex phenomenon than is generally proposed and their use in a competitive industry seems to be characterized more by a number of small interactive moves than one sustainable competitive breakthrough.

Keywords: Competitive information systems, Competitive advantage, Retail banking systems, Information systems planning.

## 1. Introduction

Competitive information systems are intended to provide sustainable competitive advantage to a firm by using information technology and information systems. This paper describes a continuing study of the following questions:

\- To what degree do firms in competitive industries actually use competitive IS (CIS) to take up and defend strategic positions?

\- Is focusing on IS a sensible approach to the integration of corporation strategic and IS planning?

\- Can CIS provide an advantage to a firm that is sustainable relative to others in the industry? Most attention has been paid to successful CIS

Most attention has been paid to successful CIS in North America. The current status of the field is that:

\- examples of CIS have been described and analyzed.

\- frameworks for practitioners to identify such opportunities for their own firms have been put forward.

However, closer examination suggests that theory in this field consists largely of assertions generalized from a few examples of systems successful in diverse industries at different times. These generalizations are often difficult to test, since they do not contain or share well-formulated definitions of the key concept of sustained advantage.

A more rigorous approach would be to look in detail at one industry. Such an approach has advantages, particularly:

\- the degree of competitive advantage achieved can be identified by comparing the financial performance of all firms in the industry;

\- the whole population of CIS can be studied; and

\- the interaction of CIS can be tracked.

The study reported here therefore concentrates on one industry, the Australian retail banking industry. It is an industry that has:

\- since 1980 moved (by world standards) from a high to a low level of regulation.

\- since 1985 changed its industry structure from a oligopoly of four major banks to a highly competitive one with 30 participants, and

\- wide geographical distribution and high transaction volumes.

It therefore offers a valuable testing ground for theories about the role of information systems and technology in a competitive environment.

## 2. Strategic Theory

## 2.1. Systems for Competitive Advantage

A number of articles have appeared in North America that describe the phenomenon of IS for competitive advantage. Frequently quoted are the histories of the Merril Lynch Cash Management Account, of McKesson Corp., and of the United and American Airlines reservations systems. These are stories where IS have apparently achieved a sustained advantage, usually in obtaining or defending large market shares. (see [15].)

The concern of most writers has been to describe frameworks that intend to help practitioners identify similar opportunities for their own firms. Many of these have origins in Porter's work on obtaining a competitive advantage within an industry [11], [12]. Porter writes little himself about the role of information technology. However, his framework has influenced a number of IS authors – [4], [7], [9], [10], [13]. This is presumably because it has both explanatory power and convincing anecdotal evidence. Not all authors use the Porter framework; e.g., Ives and Learmonth [6] proposed a life cycle approach of 13 stages for improving customer's business using information technology.

Such articles are changing the perceptions of many managers about the role of information systems in business. A survey of key information systems issues [3] showed that competitive advantage came from nowhere in 1983 to be ranked second in importance in 1986.

## 2.2. Study Framework

The descriptive framework used in this study also has origins in Porter's ideas on obtaining competitive advantage. However, the study focuses on one industry in order to understand the interplay of CIS. The study was therefore considerably refined during the early period.

Key concepts of the study are:

Competitive advantage: the establishment of profit levels higher than the average for the industry over the long-term (five years), measured as return on shareholders' equity.

Competitive strategy: long-term plans to define and create a firm's competitive advantage.

Industry: a group of firms whose products and services are reasonably interchangeable by customers.

Retail banking industry: the provision of loans, deposits, and financial transactions to individual consumers.

Strategic profile: the characteristic pattern of a firm's competitive information systems.

Competitive information system: an IS with the objective of providing a competitive advantage.

Competitive move: an instance of the application of a CIS.

## 3. Industry Practice

The study identified a number of CIS in the Australian retail banking industry; these were obtained through discussions with members of the industry and analysis of market-place activities. There were too many to include in one paper. In order to provide a feeling for industry practice, this section describes, in more detail, the three strategy types in action in the Australian banking industry. This is followed by a selection of participant and their strategic profiles, strategy types, and competitive moves, as adopted for the technology of retail electronic funds transfer (EFT).

## 3.1. Strategy Type - Improve Price and Performance

Currently Australian banks spend \$A10 billion yearly on operating expenses in an industry by \$A3 billion annual profits before tax. A halving of cost per transaction – a figure that some believe possible with information technology over ten years – has the potential to reduce costs by an amount equivalent to total profits in the industry. This saving, in a competitive industry, will not necessarily be evenly distributed. It may to to existing banks as increased profit. It may be taken by consumers as lower cost and/or improved services. New entrants may take a profitable marked share. Suppliers may make greater profits from the industry.

## Examples

1. Alliances can share the benefits of improved price/performance.

National Australia (NAB) established a close relationship with IBM that enabled the bank to be one of the first installations worldwide of the model 3090, IBM's most powerful computer at that time. (a1) (a2)

2. Standards have the potential to reduce costs by encourage supplier competition, which can lower the input costs of a financial institution.

Agreement of an Australian Standards Association standard for EFTPOS by network operators, banks with established networks, and some NBFIs that define interface areas, such as key management and network communications. (b1) (b2)

3. Backwards integration (taking over part of the input to the production process) can improve the relative balance of power of a competitor.

Chase-AMP, a new entrant, took over the cheque processing and clearing business of an NBFI. (c1) (c2)

## 3.2. Strategy Type - Influence Market Access

Influencing marked access has been an area of strategic use of computer systems that has received much study. It involves the use of systems to lock out competition and lock in customers – or, conversely, to break into the marketplace.

This often results in complex strategies that require careful targeting to avoid dilemmas, such as those that occur during establishment of a national EFTPOS network. This raises questions such as:

\- Should established banks combine to exclude new and potential entrants from access to the national network?

\- Can the established banks treat the network as a source of revenue by running systems on behalf of new and potential entrants?

\- If new entrants joint the network, how can they differentiate their product and obtain market recognition?

\- If new entrants develop their own network, how can they afford the start-up costs and jostle competitors' network terminals out of retail stores?

\- Should retailers save costs by entering an existing bank network, but incur higher costs if they change later?

## Examples

1. First movers can create a technology-based entry barrier that others need to circumvent.

Cashcard is a consortium of building societies that was well established in ATMs and EFTPOS prior to entry of Citibank, who preferred to join this network rather than set up their own. (d1)

2. Second movers typically wait until a technology is mature, enabling them to cut down first mover advantage cheaply.

Coles / Myer, a retail chain with 20% of national retail spending, waited until EFTPOS had penetrated the industry before announcing their guidelines for allowing the system into their 1500 stores. (e1)

3. Switching costs are those, such as investment in finance and training, that would be incurred in a move to an alternative computer-based service.

Chase-AMP, a new entrant, offerent to consolidate existing credit card loans from established banks on a new Chase-AMP Visa card. (f1) (f2)

4. Product control can be established by using technology to discourage competitors from offering new products.

The ANZ, an established bank, is widely believed to have a refined home banking system that could by readily introduced if a competitor were to offer such a service. (g1)

## 3.3. Strategy Type - Develop Products

The objective of product development is to use information technology to raise the value of the product to the consumer. This is a strategy that requires careful targeting. It is rarely compatible with the “improve price and performance” type of strategy, since product development is often relatively expensive.

Banks have a special interest in this type of strategy, because of what is termed the problem of "distintermediation" or "securitization". This is the trend for non-banks to bypass banks by packaging funds and directly connect borrowers with lenders. Global communications networks help connections to be made and packages to be assembled. The danger for banks is that they may be squeezed out of much of the banking business.

Thus banks need to consider how they can retain their unique position in the financial services market-place, through the creation of products that attract and retain customers business and support.

## Examples

1. New products can be created and supported through information technology.

Chase-AMP, a new entrant, offered a cash management system that, for Woolworths, was estimated to save \$1.5 million per year on \$5 billion of cash store takings. (h1) (h2)

2. Product improvement is possible by using technology in ways that lower charges, improve service, and allow product variation.

## 4.1. CIS are Independent cf Technology

There appears to be no clear relationship between the form of information technology and competitive information systems. Examples of using technology competitive have been identified from all major forms of advanced information technology – both soft and hard. The same enabling technology can be found to support each of the three basic strategy types. This is illustrated in Table 3.

## 4.2. CIS are Complex Phenomena

The next conclusion to be drawn from the experience (to date) of the Australian banking industry is that CIS are complex phenomena: they are more subtle than is often acknowledged. CIS, it seems, can have multiple objectives. They can be used to attack a participant's market or financial position and also to support alliances with other participants. A CIS may target one or more participants. The intention may be to help or undermine this target.

## Example

In 1974 the major international credit card organization Mastercard widthdraw from Australia when the local consortium product, Bankcard was introduced. Westpac and Mastercard (and later the Commonwealth Bank) took the technological opportunity to link the reentry of Mastercard into Australia with the pioneering introduction of an EFTPOS system. This lead to Westpac trying to downgrade Bankcard by not supporting electronic readability and then leaving the Bankcard consortium. The remaining members then reacted by setting up another organization to oversee the new electronic Bankcard, although generally moving account processing “inhouse” in order to become separated and more competitive. Meanwhile, Mastercard’s competitor, Visa, adopted a strategy of supporting the Visa card as a supplementary card to Bankcard for overseas use by Australians. The Visa card was intended to be issued by as many banks (especially new entrants, such as Chase-AMP) and used on as many electronic networks as possible.

## Comment

Theories of competition that are relatively simple, such as the five key questions of MacFarlan or focusing on the needs of customers, offer less value to practitioners than those, such as Wiseman's [14], with a framework that encompass the whole industry. Without a wider framework it is difficult for strategists to identify possible reactions of other participants.

## 4.3. Sustainable Advantage is Possible Through CIS

A third conclusion relates to the frequent claim that sustainable advantage can be achieved. In the

Australian retail banking system, no bank appears yet to have achieved such an advantage.

## Example

Westpac were the first to introduce both an ATM network (1977) and an EFTPOS network (1984) in retail stores. They discouraged sharing of the EFTPOS network by other financial institutions. They tried to bind retailers by opposing network structures other than their proprietary model. The only early network alliance they established was with the Commonwealth bank in mid-1985. It appears that this approach has subsequently needed modification.

Woolworths, a retailer involved in the EFTPOS pilot, in September 1986 was still refusing to pay EFTPOS fees. In February 1988 Westpac lost Woolworths as a clearing bank customer to Chase-AMP, who offered a superior cash management system for the retailer's cash receipts. In October 1985 Westpac sold access to their EFTPOS network to the Redicard network of credit unions. In late 1987 they agreed to share (EFTPOS only) with the NAB and other smaller banks. By the end of 1987 their share of installed merchants was behind the 27% of the ANZ (who established an open gateway network) and the 24% of the NAB (who participated in the largest number of network alliances).

## Comment

The Australian experience shows evidence of valuable advantages gained by consumers in areas of product quality and delivery, but none have changed the basis of industry competition. A careful reading of the literature on CIS would suggest that this is probably normal experience. The dramatic examples often quoted may, in fact, be exceptions.

## 4.4. CIS for Defending Positions

A fourth conclusion is that CIS seem to be better at defending positions that attacking them. By 1986, after one year in Australia, only 9 of the 19 new foreign banks still considered themselves to be players in retail banking [8]. This can, in part, be attributed to the use of EFTPOS networks by the established banks as a means to retain control of retail product delivery. Those remaining in retail banking generally intend to link into existing networks rather than differentiate themselves with proprietary networks.

## Example

The Bank of America entered Australian banking in alliance with Coles as Bank America Australia (BAA). Subsequently Bank of America was forced to concentrate on recovering from unwise developing country loans and Coles merged with Myers to form a giant Australian retailer. The new bank spent \$3 million, intending to open 30 outlets in three years, 20 of these in Coles stores. Since Coles-Myers had 1.5 million credit card holders and 1500 stores, this represented a strategic opportunity to obtain an immediate electronic branch representation.

Coles-Myer was, at the time of entry of foreign banks, a pilot for the EFTPOS system of an existing bank – the NAB. Coles-Myer and NAB then became embroiled in a dispute over the payment of fees. In the end BAA did not take up the Government's offer to apply for a retail banking license. The company chairman commented: "The consumer is able to get a very good deal today in Australia and the Bank no longer felt that it could operate competitively in this marked and still earn a satisfactory return on its investment." [2]

## Comment

Clemons [5] observed that a major application of externally focused systems is to rely on customer switching costs to defend positions. The Australian experience offers much support for this observation. To consider CIS as weapons of war or, as McFarlan's words, "electronic heroin", greatly overstates the potential for new entrants to change the basis of competition. Examination of practice suggests a rather more subtle interplay between new and existing participants.

## 4.5. CIS and Planning

A final conclusion concerns the linking of IS and business planning. In banks, the connections between IS and planning for electronic product delivery of retail banking have, on the whole, become more effective since the introduction of deregulation and competition.

## Example

In February 1986, following a massive corporate decentralization, the ANZ created a division of 850 people to develop EFTPOS as a product to be sold in its own right. The division, now called Network, negotiated a wholesale service rate from Telecom (the Australian common carrier) and built a switching system around Tandem equipment. This package is sold to retailers. It covers communication charges, training and hot-line services and terminal lease/rental packages. It also offers private label card services for medium-size retail chains, such as Sportsgirl and Mitre 10. All financial institutions were encouraged to join, including ANZ's banking and non-banking competitors. By early 1988 this network represented the largest of the Australian merchant terminal bases and was linked to 51 card issuing institutions.

## Comment

One can hypothesize that such strong development of ATM and EFTPOS networks occurs because it is relatively easy to see this technology as the electronic extrapolation of existing brick and mortar systems. The potential for CIS to create new or improved products does not, however, seem to have taken root so firmly for business planning in banks. Overseas innovations based on IS, such as mortgage securitization and home equity loans, do not yet seem to have to make inroads into Australian retail banking. Product development activity remains largely centered on variations in the terms of plastic cards and accounts.

## 5. Summary

Analyzing competitive information systems from the point of view of an industry is an approach that links the different perspectives of IS and business planners. A competitive industry seems to require a sophisticated framework to make sense of the development and use of CIS.

The experiences of the Australian retail banking industry, to date, suggest that CIS are better at defending existing positions. No dramatic examples of sustainable advantage have yet emerged. The evidence from studying this industry, suggests that it is uncommon to obtain, or even seek, competitive advantage with one breakthrough. Rather, competitive information systems typically involve a number of relatively small initiatives.
