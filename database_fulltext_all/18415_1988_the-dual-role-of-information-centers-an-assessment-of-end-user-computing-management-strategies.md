# The Dual Role of Information Centers: An Assessment of End User Computing Management Strategies

Markku Sääksjärvi, Jukka Heikkilä

and Timo Saarinen

Helsinki School of Economics, SF-0C100 Helsinki, Finland

This article evaluates the impact of different management strategies on the success of End User Computing (EUC). The study is based on the empirical survey of sixteen organizations with 151 end users. The analysis showed that accelerating management strategies resulted in significantly better success than the controlling strategies. However, there seemed to be no clear connection between the existence of a formal information center (IC) and the success of EUC. Instead, an IC seemed to play a dual role. If the organizations with an IC were managed by accelerating growth policies, success measures were significantly higher than if managed by controlling growth policies. In the organizations with controlling growth policies and an IC, success measures were even lower than in the organizations without an IC. On the basis of our findings we recommend that the IS managers should critically re-evaluate and adjust their EUC strategies: an IC alone does not guarantee any success.

Keywords: End user computing, Information center, Management, Success.

1. Management Strategies for End User Computing

Many organizations face a rapid growth of End User Computing (EUC) which offers promising opportunities to improve the effectiveness of office work. However, it involves new risks, for example, in the form of hidden costs and amateur programming. Therefore, management principles and strategies for EUC have become an important topic for both researchers and IS managers. Many authors have proposed their concepts for the management of EUC [1,5,7,8,9,10,14]. The most important distinction between different strategies seems to be the right balance between organizational slack and control. Munro and Huff offered some operational measures to identify the adoption of a growth policy. They proposed the following measures to estimate the intensity:

(a) the information flow between users and IS people,

(b) charge-back of equipment and services,

(c) ease of acquisition of technology,

(d) support and assistance for end users,

(e) freedom of choice available to the users.

These measures were tested in case studies by Saarinen et al. [15] and it turned out that such surrogate measures as the level of total expenditure on EUC, EUC expenditures per employee and per white-collar worker were good indicators of the acceleration at least in the starting phase of the EUC.

Another important component of the EUC management strategy is the formal organization. The basic main alternatives are the formal Information Center (IC) and other less formal alternatives such as extensions of the IS department and local support personnel. Considering only two major dimensions – the growth policy and the formality of the support – we can classify the most typical management strategies of EUC in the following four categories (classification is based on Heikkilä et al. [6]:

(1) Accelerating organizations with an IC, "Active information centers".

(2) Accelerating organizations without an IC, "Open acceleration".

(3) Controlling organizations with an IC, "Passive information centers".

(4) Controlling organizations without an IC, "IS monopoly".

Despite the fact that many authors have proposed management strategies for EUC, there are no studies reporting their real implications or effects. Are the accelerating strategies really better in terms of the number of end users and their success?

In this article, we report on the results of an empirical study in which our aim was to evaluate the impact of different management strategies by measuring the success of EUC directly in the end users' work environment.

## 2. Evaluation of EUC Success

Results from many studies have shown that the real benefits of EUC are the contributions to the end users' work, not to the IS department for example in the forms of decreased backlog or maintenance load [3,7,12]. The problem of measuring the real success of MIS/DSS has been the subject of active research and controversy. In their study Barki and Huff [2] showed that the traditional surrogate measures of the success – information satisfaction and system usage – were problematic in the case of entirely voluntary DSS usage. On the basis of their tests, they concluded that the decision makers/users perceive DSS that cause work environment changes to be more successful and that they tend to be used more extensively. Sääksjärvi [16] tested these promising new measures on a group of fifty senior executives. On the basis of these tests, we developed the following four variable instrument for measuring the EUC success:

(1) The frequency of use (USE), measured by an absolute scale (hours/week).

(2) The extent of work environment changes caused by EUC (CHANGES), from user estimates on a Likert scale 1 to 5.

(3) The impact of EUC on the information provided by computing (INFORMATION), on a Likert scale 1 to 5.

(4) The improvement of the user performance caused by EUC (PERFORMANCE), on a Likert scale 1 to 5.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data Collection

Sixteen IS managers in large and middle-sized Finnish organizations were contacted and they were asked to participate in our study. They represented organizations with and without a formal Information Center and with a variety of EUC management strategies. All IS managers were interviewed with a questionnaire focusing on EUC and its management. The IS managers were asked to choose end users from different functional areas with varying experience to participate in our study. A structured questionnaire consisting of background variables and success measures was mailed to these 175 end users. Altogether we received responses from 151 end users (86% response rate). Fourteen of them were interviewed in order to test and revise the questionnaire.

## 3.2. Statistical Analysis

The analysis of frequencies and descriptive statistics showed that the success measures were not normally distributed. This, in addition to the ordinal scales, forced us to use non-parametric tests. The differences in the users' success according to the growth policy and formality of support were analyzed with a Mann–Withney test. The differences between the four organization groups were analyzed on the basis of Kruskal–Wallis's nonparametric analysis of variance. The significance level was 0.05 in both tests. One-tailed probability was used with Kruskal–Wallis's test and two-tailed probability with the Mann–Whitney test. All statistical analyses were carried out with the SPSS/PC $^{+}$ software package.

## 4.3. Distributions of the Success Variables

Frequencies and descriptive statistics of the success variables are shown in fig. 5. The values of these variables are on average quite high. This may indicate that end users are successful, but it might show bias, because we cannot be sure that the IS managers have not only chosen successful end users for the survey. However, there is some variation in the success measures and it is important to test if the management strategy adopted, end users' experience, or their organizational position can explain the variation.

## 4.4. Growth Policy and an Information Center

Fig. 6 shows the descriptive statistics of the success variables in the four groups with different EUC management strategies. The Mann-Whitney test in fig. 7 shows that the end users in the organizations with accelerating strategies have higher values for the success variables than the users in the controlling organizations. However, success measures do not differ significantly in the organizations with or without an IC. The amount of information is the only exception. The establishment of an IC alone does not seem to guarantee the most likely success.

## 4.5. Joint Impact of the Growth Policy and Organized Support

Fig. 8 shows the results of a Kruskal–Wallis's oneway analysis of variance which was applied to test the differences of success variables in the four groups. The active information centers are clearly the most successful, and the differences between the groups are statistically significant.

The analysis indicates that a formal IC seems to bring a real synergy to the accelerating organization. On the other hand, an IC combined with a controlling growth policy seems to lead to even lower success values then a traditional IS monopoly alone.

## 4.6. End Users' Experience and Organizational Position

Fig. 9 shows the distributions of experience and organizational position of the end users. There are considerable differences between the groups and it is, therefore, important to be sure that it is indeed management strategy and not only the longer experience of the end users in the accelerating organizations that explains the higher values of the success variables.

Fig. 10 illustrates how the success variables are associated with the EUC experience. The line diagrams of the success measures show that there are no growing trends of the success measures as a function of experience.

The differences due to the management strategies are the largest in the first years of computing. This may indicate that most organizations are not yet prepared to support experienced end users effectively.

As fig. 11 shows, success measures do not seem to be sensitive to the organizational position. However, there are two exceptions. First, the weekly computing hours in the organizations with accelerating growth policies are higher than in the other organizations; this especially concerns clerical workers. Second, success measures of junior managers seem to be much lower in the organizations with a passive information center than in the other organizations.

## 5. Reliability of the Results

We have taken an initial step towards creating proper success measures tailored for EUC. The variables we used to estimate the EUC success in the end users' work environment are surrogate measures based on the earlier studies. The use of surrogate measures inevitably decreases the reliability of our analysis.

The following restrictions should be kept in mind when evaluating our results:

(1) The number of organizations was quite small and their grouping in the major grid we used was not in perfect balance. Large organizations without an IC and small organizations with an IC were missing.

(2) The end users were chosen by the IS managers; hence the sample may be biased though the IS managers were asked to select end users from several user departments and skill levels.

(3) The use of single-item (= question) measures may partially have caused the success measures to be skewed and kurtic as compared to normal distribution. This forced us to use non-parametric tests. However, we think that more complicated measures would make the filling out of the questionnaire too time consuming and provide potentially ambiguous results with less clarity.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

In this study, our aim was to take a step towards a better empirical evaluation of the effects of different EUC management strategies. It was necessary to develop and use concrete measures of the EUC success directly in the end user work environment. The success measures proposed by DSS researchers seem to operate quite well but we think that there is a need to further develop success measures tailored for the EUC, not only from the end user perspective but also from the IS manager and organizational perspectives.

The analysis of the success measures showed that end user computing was well accepted. The high averages of the success measures indicated that end users can benefit from computing. However, there seemed to be variations between the organizations with different EUC management strategies: the accelerating strategies increased both the number of active end users and the EUC success variables.

On the basis of our analysis, we concluded that the variation of the success measures could be explained by the management strategy which seems to be more dominating than the computing experience or organizational position of end users. Therefore effective management of EUC is valuable and needed, and accelerating strategies lead to better success than controlling strategies. This is especially clear in the start-up and learning stages of EUC.

Finally, the dual role of the information center was of importance. Combined with an accelerating growth policy, a formal IC seemed to improve significantly the effectiveness of the adopted strategy. On the other hand, combined with a controlling growth policy, an information center seemed to stagnate the evolution towards higher success measures. It is, therefore, recommended that IS managers should not rely on pure IC strategies but critically re-evaluate the use of information centers and combine it with an intensive acceleration strategy.
