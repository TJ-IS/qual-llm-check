# Setting the Scene for I.R.M.

A.W. Zijlker

Adviesbureau Zijlker BV, Villapark 9, 7491 BT, Delden (Ov), The Netherlands

Information Resource Management is projected against the background of the continuing developments in the relationship between people and information technology. It is argued that the technology is caught in the paradigm of 'information' as measurable matter, and thus still confined to concepts of management directed at the efficiency of utilisation of the technology. Those concepts were appropriate in the recent past but now stand in the way of IT's productive use. With the advent of the cheap, easy-to-use workstation and facilities for open communication the time: ripe for putting the 'user' central again and manage their ability to perform valuable 'acts of information'. I.R.M. is thus regarded as a step in the right direction yet to be followed by a real change of paradigm.

Keywords: Information, Information technology, Computer, Communication, Resource, Management.

## 1. Introduction

I.R.M., Information Resource Management: ??? Acronyms are intended to convey a message, but what - in the case of I.R.M. - is that message meant to be? Does 'Information Resource Management' stand for a sensible idea or is just another example of bad grammar, sufficiently interesting to attract people to a conference and sufficiently fuzzy to let people fill in their own variety of interpretations?

Several suggestions stem from the acronym and its composing words:

\- firstly: the community is becoming aware that 'information' is the stuff to pay attention to instead of to the computer or whatever else came out of the laboratories of information technology.
- secondly: there might be something to regarding information as a 'resource' instead of an at best unavoidable cost-item.

\- and thirdly: there may be something to 'managing information as a resource' on penalty of loss of hitherto unheard of benefits.

But that is about as far the three letters I, R and M can take us. For 'setting the scene', as this paper is intended to do, more is apparently needed.

## 2. Where to Start

As a point of departure, a short review of the evolution of Information Technology may well provide some clues. The story is one of many levels; stories within stories, as it were. The pioneering world of von Neuman, Wiener and Shockley was full of the excitement of the new phenomenon. Machines which could 'reason' seemed to be close at hand. Logic was combined with switching techniques and the perennially optimistic pioneers suggested once again they were leading the way towards solving, once and for all, the problems mankind was intellectually struggling with. This was alchemy revisited in the shape of such concepts as industrial dynamics (until two oilcrises abruptly plugged the plug on the predictive value of the whole idea).

Nobody actually managed this new 'resource'. This was a time of pure adventure. Notwithstanding the inherent presumptuousness of the pioneers, it has to be admitted, that they were the ones, who conceptualised many of the things currently claimed as 'breakthroughs' but which at that time could not reach beyond the laboratory due to their cost. Machines consisting of 18000 vacuum tubes like the ENIAC were not 'personal' toys for teenagers not to speak of their tiny capacity compared to even the simplest homecomputer of today. While the hard technology developed from vacuum tube to transistor to chip the essential result was that the technology become affordable and was introduced in the area of administrative dataprocessing: not the brilliant algorithm but disciplined procedure became the object of automation.

The scene was thereby set to start thinking in terms of ‘managing data’. Nolan told the story within a story [2]: learning curve following learning curve, from pioneering to maturity. Bad luck for the perennial optimists, that after maturity there is only decline and death; but never mind that minor hitch, new learning curves can be introduced to fill the gap. ‘Data is a company resource’ became one of the many slogans following the ‘invention’ of the database (was it not Babbage who conceptualised the idea of separating data and programs, to begin with?). And since resources have to be managed, the birth of the database manager was quick to follow. Old wine in new bags and also curious grammar ('data is...').

Accountants, computer professionals and 'business machine' manufacturers found each other in their obviously common interests and collectively promoted the idea that the newly invented resource had to be managed preferably at the vice-presidential level; notably also 'information' became the preferred object of attention. Information sounds better than plain 'data' (after all information is usually defined as 'useful data', isn't it?) The technology could hardly be regarded as cheap, nor was setting the machine to work really easy, so the cost involved with equipment and 'professionals' provided an effective argument for degrading all 'outsiders' to the status of 'user'.

Outside of the by then predominant domain of the business machines other developments sneaked in. The technology provided the means for transaction processing (distinguished from data processing) purposes: means for people (!) to send each other their basically individual messages in order to add mutual coherence to their activities. The ensuing 'minicomputer' was different. It was conceptualised for communication and easy interaction and was not just smaller and cheaper, as the established industry was quick to claim. The telecommunication industry – notwithstanding their established conservatism – was finding out that digitisation would lead the way to previously unimaginable developments and had to adopt the rate of innovation the young computer industry was, almost by nature, accustomed to.

Communicating messages is fundamentally different from automating dataprocessing; where the former is undisputably concerned with human interaction and cognitive behaviour, the latter is not and thereby totally submittable to the rules of the 'system' [3]; both, however, obviously have something to do with 'information'. And the fight is on: between 'datamanagers' and 'facilitators', between computer industry and the telecommunications industry (in spite of all claims of convergence), between mainframe machines and distributed computers, between controllers and the controlled.

Transaction processing provides another story within a story: on the one hand interpreted as 'data-entry' into the managed and controllable database and on the other as a means for 'open' communication between individuals via the computer, in the role of 'intelligent' – but neutral – 'telephone exchange'. System analysis had to become system design to accommodate the people involved; the latter according to some as 'part of the system' and according to others 'on top of' or 'manning' the system. Whatever the theoretical standpoint of the professional community, the human element itself was less and less inclined to be 'automated away' and claimed their right to participate instead. Systemintegration seemed one way of tipping the balance in favour of the 'controllers', thereby carefully guarding the 'integrity' of their databases and their own position.

No more could the telecommunication community avoid the issue: controlled, closed star-networks vie with open 'amorphous' (ARPA-like) networks, circuit-switched with package-switched, closed proprietary standards with open industry standards, control over the network with facilitating open communication.

What, in this perspective, is there to be managed, for what purpose, and what, in fact, is the resource?

On the fringe of the computer industry, individualisation struck. Little cheap computers for games, hobby- and personal use, fun-to-play-with, easy-to-learn and easy-to-use, accessible to anyone made their presence felt: a real watershed! Neither the established computer profession nor the telecommunication industry initially seemed to grab the meaning of where this 'small ugly duckling' might lead to: not to just very small conventional computers but to 'easy-to-access' workstations. The technology, all of a sudden, was approached from the point of view of natural individual behaviour instead of technical feasibility. This, in short, was the prototype of the expert system in practical use. Another story within a story: the bad dream of human-intelligence-replaced-by-'artificial intelligence' converted into the awakening of the concept of undisputably human intelligence assisted and amplified by technology with do-it-yourself capabilities.

The message of this transition is not lost on the younger generation. The established profession has been rocked on its foundations. The 'hobby computer' was not given professional status until 'big blue' provided their stamp of approval, under certain conditions and industry standards included. Conferences are organised on how the process of 'threatening proliferation' can be contained, and training is to provide to bend 'hobbyism' and 'amateurism' into 'professionalism' once again.

What is there to be managed after all: machines, algorithms, data, information, message transfer, knowledge, fun?

## 3. 'Information'

Apparently individual standpoints determine what the word ‘information’ stands for. It seems arguable that cultural differences have their influence on this basic point. May it be generalised, that Americans tend to think of data as the stuff 'information' is made of and that Europeans are not so sure about this static simplification and tend to include human elements, like intention and meaning, in it's definition?

It is highly debatable whether 'information' in the latter sense can or should be managed. Projected on Third World political debates the answer to the free world is obviously NO! Managed information in this sense is manipulated information. This is true in the domain of business information too.

Of course, corporate data and databases have to be managed, like any good old library or any other collection of potentially interesting documents. And some information has to be collected, processed and provided in a formal manner for all sorts of reasons. That's not new and certainly no argument for inventing the acronym I.R.M.

But does that make information a resource to be managed, 'tout court'? This is at least debatable. Algorithms, messages, knowledge...: are they managed as such or should they be recognised as part and parcel of the dynamics of the human environment managed for maximum added value.

As the technology went from expensive and clumsy to cheap and easy-to-use and as the future seems to have much more of the latter in store, the issue to be addressed is how to exploit this pervasive technology for maximum value instead for minimum cost. Value of information was, is, and will be unmeasurable (in the rational sense of the word). It does not only depend on its use by people it is the value of people. To be able to communicate, to have access to knowledge (whether in the form of algorithms or fuzzy expertise) or to have the means to 'doodle' on a colourgrafics screen: these are matters relating to human behaviour and human creativeness. The value apparently is in the capability rather than in the 'stuff'. Information in this sense does not exist, is not, but stands for a human act. It is the same difference in meaning that is apparent between the concepts 'creation' in the sense of the thing one can buy and in the sense of 'the act of creation' [4]. The resource therefore is the human capacity for adding value and obviously this is the thing to be managed.

Maybe the time is over that ‘information’ should only be associated with the measurable stuff of Shannon and Weaver [5]: bits and bytes, processable by means of digital switches, 'a resource to be managed'. Maybe the implicit message behind I.R.M. is the recognition that it is high time that the course is radically changed. The technology has now evolved so far, that at last attention can revert to managing human potential for adding value, as of old?

## 4. Information Technology

Technology is the art of manipulating basic material by means of artificial tools. Chemical technology, physical technology, bio-technology and other technologies are such 'arts'. Information Technology is no exception; it is the art of manipulating 'bits and bytes' by means of all the know how and electronic gadgetry the last century has generated and produced at such an accelerated pace. Information Technology is the means to an end and thus a true resource.

'Information Technology for your competitive edge', 'IT changes the way you compete' [6]: these are the slogans of the late eighties and many, if not most, managers are frantically trying to absorb the message.

Until recently, the technology was totally unaccessible to the average layman, and the unassailable domain of the professional and an industry bound together by their established interests. They had to give in, slightly, to outside pressure upon the arrival of interactive processing, but countered with the 'idea', that 'the human organisation had to change' to reap the benefits of systems they still conceived with their control-oriented minds and means; 'participative development' and prototyping arrived at the scene, but still system had to be and 'resistance to change' was there to be overcome.

Management remained focussed on cost-control for good reasons and financially measurable results. They were hardly to be blamed for doing so. With the state of the art as it is now, however, management is all-of-a-sudden being made aware (if not only by their teen-age sons and daughters) that Information Technology can provide hitherto unexpected added value, like motivation, creativity, marketing advantage, professional expertise, capability to communicate...and consequently begins to question the wisdom of one-sided cost control. Admittedly, management is still worried about loosing control to a phantom, but at the same time it does not want to miss ‘the golden opportunity’. They are wary of the underlying industry interest, and justifiably so, but are ready to experiment and find out for themselves.

Evolution from expensive industrial product to commodity implies the affordability of moving from general purpose machine to special purpose tool. It is not the maximum utilisation of scarce technical resources that is paramount anymore but their potential for increasing the added value of the individual. Accepting this notion of 'special tools for special purposes' logically implies the need to gain better understanding of the purposes. It is no longer acceptable to force an organisation into a system designed by the technician according to the dictation of the technology, but the challenge is now to provide real tools to be shaped and used by people throughout organisations themselves shaped by – or at least with – the people involved.

Behavioural science and – in their own way – the artificial intelligence laboratories are leading the way. Chips, glass fibers, databases, and computer languages will have to follow. Human behaviour – not the most prominent interest of the IT profession – is claiming its place as the dividing factor between automation for cost efficiency and providing the tools for adding value.

All systems can ultimately become 'expert systems': not systems between controller and controlled, but systems providing a means for true 'integration and selfcontrol' as befitting Theory Y of Douglas Mc Gregor [7]. The issue is to avoid systems between laymen and experts and to introduce systems adding expertise to the layman, not systems standing between the operator and the engineer but systems, that provide wider thinking horizons to the operator.

All this is easier said than done, and implies rethinking almost from scratch, high investments and loss of status for all those professionals who can not overcome their resistance to change (note how the roles have been mutually exchanged).

Glimpses of this ‘fifth generation’ future are already appearing: software in the hands of the ‘user’, open communication at the discretion of the individual, desk top functions at everybody’s fingertips, individually shapable screens in colour graphics re-establishing the recognisable forms of yesterday over the technology inspired formats of today for really user friendly interfaces. Who will still design 'order-entry' systems? Five years from now electronic mail and multiwindowing will allow for recreating operational processes as they were always meant to be: horizontal communication between human participants. The only real limitation now is the rate at which the performance of today's machines can be improved. All other objections should be at least screened on the bias of the ones who make them.

Technology once was in the driving seat; objectively speaking, it is no longer even the brake.

## 5. Challenge

The technology does not stand alone. Mankind as a whole, in west and east alike albeit from different starting points, seems to be moving toward demassification, deregulation and individualisation [8]. All of a sudden they find themselves caught in the webs of the mainframe computing centre, the corporate database, the controlled star network, and the profession that goes with it. Scores of manyears invested in complexity turn on their sponsors. The prime challenge is not in prolonging the life of this idea by means of program generators and automated design but in reconceptualising a world around people with individualised tools and the facilities to communicate openly. The prime challenge is not to increase or maintain control but to liberate the creativity of people so that they can produce their added value for the common good. The prime challenge is not to ‘prevent proliferation’ but to diffuse the appropriate tools as they come and become affordable.

Corporate life is back where it started many years ago: no scientific management, no automatic control, but the need to organise men and women to do their collective jobs in due coherence [9]. There is no need for professional staffs authorised to keep the tools away from the people, but for professional staffs prepared to help setting them to the best possible use: 'information centres' under a more appropriate name.

But: Is that what the established industry sells and what the established consultants say and is that what the established professionals were trained to provide?

Large organisations, for once, do not have the advantage. The 'better' they are organised, the worse may be their problem. The smaller the organisation and the younger the staff, the less 'the law of crippling advantage' holds. The larger and 'better' the organisation the more the laws of 'equivalent complexity' and of 'self-affirming complexity' may apply. Undisciplined behaviour on the verge of mutiny may be needed to break the flow. Newcomers to the industry will be there to grab the opportunity and provide the desired tools. The established industry will follow. The risk of a limited 'wild' experiment is far less than the risk of a 'well controlled' corporate-wide mis-investment (let alone of the scores of less well controlled ones). This is the time of 'good ideas' and 'turnaround' and 'proactive use of IT' [6]. It has all been said begore and some of it is already being done, but there still is a long road ahead.

## 6. I.R.M. Revisited

The suggestion that I.R.M. should convey is that there indeed is a resource to be managed and that new rules govern this new game. This game is to make information Technology add maximum value, by putting it in the hands of the people who were there to take care of the value adding process anyway. The game is not to automate those people away.

It is a change of paradigm that is urgently needed: away from the concept ‘information’ that still carries the smell of the recent past, a past in which illusions regarding the measurability of information were pursued far beyond the ideas generated by those who laid the foundations for the technology. Information Technology is rapidly evolving into the art of facilitating ‘acts of information’ supported by electronic means, the manipulation being the sole domain of the humans involved. It is that technology that needs to be managed.

This is not a simple matter. At the ever steeper rate of obsolescence, counted already in single years, options have to be kept open while larger investments can not be postponed. Choices from an increasing variety of tools have to be made, while a certain coherence has to be maintained. Arguments have to be screened for their value or bias, and all of this against the background of insufficient understanding available on the part of those who have to make the choices.

The issue thus returned to where it all started: It is about organising people to be productive with the help of technology. It is about embedding the technology in that specific environment. It is about reaching out to people and their cultural values to set the technology to work. It is about putting the technology back into the neutral place it could escape from for a while due to its being exotic, expensive, new and ill understood.

Concepts of only a few years age, have to be abandoned and replaced by new ones by a profession that was selected and trained for their ability to master the old ones. Human behaviour is rapidly becoming the touchstone of their value. In all these respects the development is not different from developments of other technologies, but the pace at which they occur is such, that no time is allowed for waiting for the next generation to react.

Decentralisation and line management in the driving seat seem obvious goals to pursue. Distribution of machine capacity, down to the desk-top, in such a way that special tools are made available for special purposes logically fits the trend. Open communication should be facilitated between people and people and machines and machines with optional controls available. Integration should be based on the ability to communicate (between individual functions) rather than on technical efficiency (like 'unique datacapture' etc.) and be kept at the lowest needed level to avoid crippling complexity. Databases shall be based on (almost) natural grammar (the relational database already comes close to this). Software should maximally be put into the hands of the users and man-machine interfaces should become really user-friendly not to say fun-to-work-with. The quality of the system will lie less and less in the efficiency of its inner working and increasingly in its external role.

For the time being, it is this transition that will have to be managed. There will be a time in the, hopefully, not so distant future, that the real resource – the value adding human being – will again receive all the attention of line management. We hope to see ‘information management’ once again limited to the home fishing pond of the ‘bookkeeper’ (accountant and librarian), ‘information technology management’ a function in the background keeping the infrastructure alive and kicking and ‘information resource management’ a step in the direction of a new paradigm based on the recognition that the real resource to be managed is people’s ability to perform valuable ‘acts of information’. Another milestone in the learning process, no more and hopefully no less.
