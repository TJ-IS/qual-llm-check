# Information Resource Management in the Public Sector

B.K. Brussaard

Ministry of Home Affairs/Delft University of Technology, 2500 EA's-Gravenhage, The Netherlands

In applied informatics, the term information resource management (IRM) is used in many different ways, while in the field of public administration, the term public sector covers many different activities and varies for different levels of government from country to country. Here a classification system which defines these different situations is presented. With its use, one may establish a picture of IRM in the public sector. This classification system is used in the Netherlands to formulate policy on information systems and services (IRM) for central, regional, and local government in a more of less coordinated way. It may also be of practical value for comparative studies of different countries. Our major conclusion is that the structure of a country's public administration has always implicitly been dependent, inter alia, on the information technology available. It follows that, all other things being equal, new information technologies will influence public sector organisation and its relations with society as a whole.

Keywords: Information resource management, Information systems, Public sector, Information management, System management, (De-)centralisation, (De-)concentration, Administrative organisation, Geographical distribution.

## 1. Introduction and Terminology

The idea that information should be regarded as one of the principal tools of public administration appears trivial, after all, the same applies to personnel (public servants) and money (the financial resources). Nevertheless the formulation of an explicit policy on IRM in the public sector is still far from common practice. The development of information resource management is a step in this direction, but it requires further elaboration and needs, above all, to be expressed in concrete terms. In the private sector, too, the concept still has no generally accepted meaning. Furthermore, the concept of the public sector as the complement of the private sector is interpreted in a completely different way in different countries. Not only the division of responsibilities between central, regional and local government but also demarcation of the private sector and the semi-public (more of less regulated) social sectors vary widely. Finally, even the terms information technology and information systems, which are older and better established than IRM, are not always used in the same manner. This applies within a single language such as English, and even more so when translating from one language to another. And although we shall adhere as far as possible to Karl Popper's adage, "Speaking clearly is speaking in such a way that words do not matter", we consider it necessary first to explain several basic concepts, beginning with the term information system.

By information system we mean, analytically speaking, every ensemble of hardware and associated software, with all people plus the procedures according to which they work, in conjunction with the databases. In our view, every information system consists of these five system components. Functionally speaking, information system involve everything required for understanding, and in most cases influencing (controlling), part of reality (a real system).

The distinction between analytical and functional views is necessary because each of the analytical components can be used for more than one functional system or subsystem in more than one organisational unit; e.g., a database on immovable property used for both taxation purposes and physical planning. Our view of an information system therefore differs from much of the literature of the English-speaking world, where it is generally understood to be either the database and the necessary software, such as data management systems, or the combined systems used by an organisational unit (e.g., a business enterprise or governmental body).

By information technology, we mean all the know-how and aids used for the transfer, processing and storage of information. By information methodology we mean all the know-how and procedures used for the creation, maintenance, and use of information systems (e.g., development and maintenance methods and techniques).

A third pair of concepts need to be distinguished before continuing further: system management and information management. By system management, we mean all the activities necessary to make and maintain available information systems (the system components) for use by one or more organisational units. By information management we mean the operation and use of information systems, including their management and supervision. The key problem in the management of information systems as a means of production (IRM) is the fact that information management and system management may not be in the same hands. This is becoming increasingly the case and the division of responsibility may even extend outside the boundaries of their administrative units.

These three pairs of concepts make it possible to formulate more accurately the problems involved in IRM in the public sector.

## 2. What is the Public Sector?

The public sector comprises more than the governing bodies (in the narrow sense of the word)

together with the representative bodies and the apparatus which directly supports them. The largest part of the public sector, about 90% in terms of staff, investments, and expenditure, consists of services, companies, and other organisations that provide products and services. What these are and which level of government provides them varies from country to country, and it is (in principle) subject to constant change.

Furthermore, in most countries there is a vast semi-public sector, consisting of private organisations which are financed to a greater or lesser extent by the government or according to government regulations. Examples of such regulated sectors are: parts of the health service, education, and the social security system. The manner in which and the extent to which public utility companies can be included in the public sector also vary widely; examples are: energy supply, telecommunications, and public transport, but equally the operation of ports and airports, and, in particular, the maintenance of the physical infrastructure (such as the road and waterway system). There are only a few areas which always form part of the public sector in a narrow sense of the word. They include defence, public order and safety, and the legal system; but even in these cases, the extent to which certain activities are left to the private sector or are carried out by private companies commissioned by the government varies widely (e.g., security services may be provided by either).

Before information policy in the public sector can be analysed and given shape, it is therefore necessary to indicate exactly which areas of activity are to be included and which are not. Compared to the manufacturing section of the private sector, the public sector is particularly information-intensive. Commercial services such as banking and insurance are however comparable to the public sector, in this respect. It should also be noted that a major part of the government budget consists of income transfers. Only the costs of the executive bodies (tax authorities, bodies which issue social security benefits, etc.) should therefore be included in IRM in the public sector. As a result of all this, its expenditure on IRM in highly developed countries ranges from 20% to 40% of the total IT market.

As already pointed out, the term IRM or simple “information management”, which is often used in the same sense, is not unambiguous. At

present there seems to be little reason to distinguish these terms from what may be called "information systems and services". The addition of the word "resource" appears to have no other function than to draw attention to the fact that information and everything that is needed to provide it is, like men, money, and materials, not only one of the chief means of production but also the chief product of the public sector. In large parts of the public sector the primary process consists of the processing and production of information, not only in organisations responsible for income transfers from and to private individuals and private companies, but also all public registers and bodies concerned with the provision of official statistics. In all these organisations, the application of information technology plays a role at two levels: in the execution of the primary information-processing activities, and in the management and policy-making regarding these primary processes, as is the case in every organisation, whether in the public or private sector.

To sum up, the public sector is taken to cover everything which is deemed to be connected, in a given concrete situation, with a view to information policy. This differs from country to country. Furthermore IRM is understood to embrace every policy that attempts to control the development and maintenance of information systems (system management) and the operation and use of information systems (information management).

## 3. Factors Affecting IRM in the Public Sector

Figure 1 presents an overview of the main factors affecting IRM in the public sector. The starting point is the available information technology and methodology used to produce politically desirable products and services as efficiently as possible. The available technology and methodology is influenced, in turn, by the products and services offered by the IT industry. This includes not only hardware and system software but also after-sales service and advice. On the other hand, research and training institutes, financed largely by the government provide the initial know-how and expertise of IT specialists (which is often followed by on-the-job learning until the person becomes an “expert”).

The application of information technology in the public sector is influenced by at least four groups:

\- the world of politics, in the form of representative bodies (Parliament, municipal councils), the governing bodies in the narrow sense (the Cabinet and municipal executive) and the political parties, etc. General social developments, such as prevailing views on deregulation, privatisation, and the role of the government in society as a whole, are reflected in this world and influence the way in which IPM is organised in central and local government;

\- the general public, whose opinion is considered to be expressed in the general press and other mass media, and in the activities of political action groups, etc. Campaigns calling for data protection measures, and improvement in the quality of public services are often initiated by this “public”;

\- specific interest groups such as employers' organisations, trade unions, the information technology industry, professional IT organisations, etc. Their influence is sometimes direct, such as in advisory and consultative bodies, and sometimes indirect, such as via the world of politics and public opinion;

\- public servants, who are the first to be directly affected by the use of information technology. This group includes the users of information systems, the specialists who develop and maintain them, and the senior staff responsible for directing these activities. The vested interests of these organisations and individuals constitute one of the principal factors behind the introduction of, or failure to, introduce innovations by the application of information technology.

What matters ultimately is the performance of the public sector; i.e., the efficiency and effectiveness of the products and services produced. These are ultimately destined for individuals and private organisations (both profit-making business and non-profit-making enterprises). For most individual information systems in the public sector, it is relatively simple to ascertain precisely which of the aforementioned groups have had an influence. Systematic studies aimed at finding a general explanatory model and then using it to develop and test information policy are, however, extremely scarce. Most studies have gone no further than to examine one particular aspect, such as data protection, and even then usually in a normative sense rather than an empirical sense; more frequently they have concentrated on the extent to which computers are used in government organisations, for instance in small or large municipalities. This provides no insight into the extent to which the efficiency and quality of public services have actually been enhanced by the use of computers, and the reasons for this.

## 4. The Structure of IRM in the Public Sector

To make the very extensive field of IRM in the public sector more readily understandable and easier to discuss, a classification system has been developed to distinguish between the different types of information systems and clarify the organisational and administrative structure of IRM (Brussaard, 1983, 1987).

Briefly, three types of functional information systems can be distinguished (see figure 2).

Type A comprises these information systems of a socially descriptive nature. These include, population registers, vehicle and shipping registers, commercial registers and other registers which provide information on legal persons and their place of establishment, records of immovable property such as buildings, plots of land, and the technical networks of public utility companies, and registers of cultural objects (in museums, libraries etc.). Government activities extend to the objects described in these public registers. The registers are generally public in a dual sense in that they are not only necessary for the government to discharge its duties, but usually have a general social function as well; e.g., to further orderly legal transactions. For this reason, they are generally accessible to everyone.

Type B comprises information systems which describe government activities proper, both beforehand (planning and budgeting) and afterwards (reporting and rendering account). These systems are specific to each area of government concern, but in some countries extend beyond the governmental sphere (e.g., in the fields of health and education). The most extensive systems of this type are those concerned with income transfers (taxes, benefits, and subsidies).

Type C comprises information systems for managing the resources through which the government discharges its duties, such as personnel information systems, financial information systems, and information systems for the management of materials, equipment, and governmental and other documents. Type C also includes those information systems used for the management of the information systems themselves. This entire category of management information systems is not functionally specific to one area of concern and is not specific to individual organisational units within an area of concern.

This classification is important because the administrative organisation is different for each category and is directly related to the structure of the public sector. As far as this structure is concerned, it is, by definition, essential to distinguish between the degree of centralisation and the degree of concentration, and this should be done separately for system management and information management.

The degree of centralisation (ranging from full centralisation to maximum possible decentralisation) is the extent to which decision-making powers in the field of both activities are allocated to a smaller or larger number of points. The degree of concentration (ranging from maximum concentration to maximum deconcentration) refers to the number of geographical locations where the operational activities take place (see figure 3).

In practice and in the literature, there is generally a great deal of confusion as to what is meant by terms such as decentralised, distributed or dispersed information systems. This can be illustrated with an example. In the Netherlands, system management, i.e., the decision-making power with respect to the organisation of the population register, is regulated by statute at central level down to the smallest detail. The execution of system management (the construction and maintenance of information systems), however, can be deconcentrated, and even entrusted to private companies (configuration C). On the other hand, information management, i.e., the use of the population information system, is significantly decentralised to municipalities and is also carried out exclusively by those municipalities on their own hardware, i.e. deconcentrated (configuration D). Similar striking differences, though often with opposite results, between system management and information management on the one hand and centralisation and concentration on the other, can be found in other fields, e.g., with regard to immovable property records (SM according to A, IM according to C), hospital information systems (SM according to B, IM according to D) and financial information systems (all configurations occur, and this therefore presents a problem with respect to the quality of public financial information in the Netherlands). ## 5. Changes in IRM in the Public Sector

A key question is: What degree of autonomy has or can have each government organisation in the field of IRM. It lies in the essence of information that it should be exchanged within and between organisational and administrative units. Autonomy can therefore never be absolute. The constraints are partly of a technical and economic nature and for that reasons are in a constant state of flux (improving price/performance ratios). Also of importance is the division of responsibilities, which may have been established over the course of time or been laid down by statute, and the resulting vested interests in terms of budget size, number of staff, personal career expectations, etc. There has recently been a great deal of discussion as to whether information technology can provide an incentive for, or even bring about (or necessitate), changes in the organisation of public administration. On the continent of Western Europe there appears to be a tendency to answer this question affirmatively (e.g., Reinermann, 1987) on the basis of both actual developments and theoretical analysis. This contrasts with North America, where few changes have taken place in recent decades and where there is no readiness to open up a discussion on the division of responsibilities between the various levels of government and autonomous self-governing public and semi-public organisations (e.g., Kraemer, 1987). Interesting explanations have been devised to account for this difference, but an important factor is undoubtedly the far higher population density and the very highly developed political and social infrastructure that is partly the result of this. In any case, the organisation of IRM in the public sector has been laid down to a far greater extent and in far greater detail in some Western European countries (e.g. Jansen, 1987). The administrative and organisational problems of information resource management in the public sector are increasingly the subject of discussion at the political level too. Some of the policy measures which have been devised and partially implemented, or are at least being considered, are outlined below.

(a) A gradual and universal shift is taking place in policy emphasis from hardware (and system software) and individual information systems to IRM for an organisational unit which extends beyond the boundaries of administrative units. This means, for instance, that industrial policy considerations in the application of information technology in the public sector are taking second place to administrative policy objectives.

(b) Hitherto large governmental agencies have generally been structured according to type of service and processes within them (e.g., according to type of public records, type of tax, or type of benefit). Information technology makes it possible to improve quality and efficiency, provided the organisational structure is switched from “type of product” to “client groups” (c.f., similar developments in the private banking and insurance sector and commercial services in general).

(c) This switch leads to a redivision of responsibilities, not only within public services but also between services which come under the same public body, and even between public bodies at different levels of government. Whether and in what manner this restructuring takes place depends (to a large extent) on the legal form of government (central or federal, degree of decentralisation at regional and local level). It is significant, for instance, that in the Scandinavian countries, the Netherlands, and West Germany there is a far greater degree of agreement and cooperation between municipalities in the field of IRM than in English-speaking countries.

(d) The main technical conditions for a restructuring of the public sector are the establishment of telecommunication networks between government bodies and the introduction of application systems built according to standard specifications (central system management). The main problem is financing, since the advantages to be gained are often enjoyed by a different body than those currently responsible for information management (largely decentralised and deconcentrated). The benefits for society as a whole, however, are evident, namely reduced government expenditure, less need for reporting and registration, and fewer surveys (c.f., the Paper Reduction Act of 1980 in the United States and Sprehe's Overview, 1987).

This is not intended to present a normative framework for the organisation of IRM in the public sector. The theoretical insight necessary for such a purpose is still lacking and no representative empirical test has been devised. It is however possible to give certain indications, partly by analogy with similar developments in some parts of the private sector.

Just as information technology in the private sector has thrown relations between sectors of industry, the various chains of production and the individual companies within both into a state of flux, so the spread of information technology in the public sector will generate forces which will lead to structural changes. In the public sector such changes will not come about through competition but through political pressure. In both cases, however, it is external forces which are the decisive factor. The administrative management of a public service or the political management of an autonomous public body is not going to advocate structural changes which reduce its own authority and responsibilities. The ultimate initiative will have to come from the appropriate level of government and, in contrast to the private sector, change may be enforced by pressure from independent research and public opinion.
