# An Empirical Study of the Use of Business Expert Systems

Mehdi Beheshtian-Ardekani

and Linda M. Salchenberger \*

Loyola University of Chicago, Chicago, IL 60611, USA

The evolution of computers from computational tools to "thinking machines" is causing businesses to evaluate their views of the computer's role. The inevitable availability of smart computers leads to questions of how and when fifth generation hardware and software will be integrated into corporate culture. Here, we present the results of a survey given to information systems managers to determine the extent of expert systems development by data processing departments and expert systems usage in organizations. The attitudes of management toward the future of expert systems are also discussed using the survey data. It was discovered that, while computer managers are receptive toward this new tool, most have no definite plans to develop expert systems in the near future. These results seem to be in conflict with other evidence about the growing numbers of expert systems in business applications. One explanation is that this new technology is part of the continuing "grass roots" movement of end-user computing.

Keywords: Business expert systems, Artificial intelligence, Knowledge-based systems, Fifth-generation.

## 1. Introduction

The role of expert systems in business organizations has recently been the subject of much debate. An expert system is a sophisticated computer program that is designed to replicate the expertise of human decision-makers in diverse fields. Using decision rules and artificial intelligence reasoning techniques, expert systems can have the ability to diagnose problems and/or recommend solutions [9], [13]. In many organizations, individuals with special expertise are scarce. One of the benefits of an expert system is that if a computer can be “taught” to think like an expert,

Some computer observers forecast that expert systems represent the next major development in the history of computer-based applications [5], [8]. Others believe this new tool will have limited application and use in most firms [1]. It is, however, impossible to ignore a few facts about future hardware and software capabilities. The Japanese established ICOT (Institute for New Generation Computing Technology) in 1981, a consortium of companies with a \$450 million dollar budget whose mission is to focus on the development of fifth generation computers. Likewise, the United States, Great Britain, France, and other countries have research groups who are working to expand computer processing capability and develop more sophisticated applications [see Table 1]. As a result of private and government-supported research projects, a variety of hardware is currently available, including dedicated AI machines, intelligent workstations, and microcomputers which can support expert system application development.

Researchers are also developing sophisticated software like expert systems shells and natural language interfaces that could make business expert systems more available and affordable [4]. Expert systems are usually viewed as high risk, expensive systems requiring sophisticated programming expertise. However, the proliferation of microcomputers in the workplace and the availability of microcomputer expert system shells (e.g., EXSYS, GURU, KEE, Expert-Ease, Goldworks) have made this technology more attractive to businesses of all sizes by allowing them to experiment on a smaller scale. Automated programming [6] and the electronic workstation are two more areas where expert systems have the potential to increase productivity and the quality of decision-making in the work environment. Programs currently available for office applications include decision-making, training, and intelligent retrieval systems.

## 2. Evolution of Expert Systems

The first use of computers in industry was to support routine data processing. For such applications, the computer's main contributions were to perform calculations with speed and accuracy and to store data. As the hardware, software, and users became more sophisticated, MIS and mathematical models based on MS/OR algorithms were designed, implemented, and adopted by companies. This led to the development of DSS which provide the human decision-maker with a flexible tool used for solving semi-structured problems. Many believe that expert systems represent a natural extension of DSS because of their ability to acquire knowledge, make diagnoses, solve problems, and recommend solutions [3], [10].

## 4. An Expert Systems Usage Survey

The main reason for conducting this survey was to gain information about data processing management's interest in and knowledge of expert systems. We selected this group for our study because it has been suggested that management involvement in a new technology can strongly influence the rate of its adoption [14]. Through budget control, training programs, and other means of support for an innovation, management can accelerate or retard the diffusion of new technology. We also wanted to determine the extend to which expert systems development was implemented or initiated by the information systems department.

Survey data was to be used to determine if companies in certain industries (e.g., financial and manufacturing) were more likely to be using expert systems. In order to determine management perception about the future of expert systems, we included questions which would elicit their opinions on this. From the results of the survey, we planned to develop a user-oriented criteria for the selection of expert systems, by asking management to suggest important purchase factors. The survey was also to be used to determine if organizations which have successfully implemented expert systems had any common characteristics, such as organization size and industry type.

We decided to use the extent and level of microcomputer usage in an organization as a measure of the organization's readiness to be innovators in this field [2]. This hypothesis was formulated based Nolan's stage model [11] of information system growth. We wanted to test whether an organization in the later stages of growth would be more likely to adopt a relatively sophisticated technology, such as expert systems.

## 4.1. Methodology

The survey instrument was a questionnaire mailed to data processing managers of 126 companies, which were selected at random. The target population was middle and upper management in corporate information systems departments or related areas. Only one questionnaire was sent to each organization, with a request to the recipient to respond or give it to the appropriate manager. The questionnaire was designed so that it could be completed in fifteen minutes or less. Respondents could remain anonymous if they so desired. A follow-up letter was sent six weeks later. Responses were processed using a mainframe statistical package.

Because of the ambiguity surrounding the term "expert system", we included a definition in our questionnaire, to help the organization determine whether it was using any expert systems.

## 4.2. The Validation of the Survey Instrument

The questionnaire was checked for reliability and content validity which means that the instrument is considering all aspects of the attribute. In general, the claim that an instrument has content validity in insured by the manner in which the instrument and its items are prepared [12]. A group of colleagues from local universities evaluated the characteristics of the questionnaire for completeness and relevance. To further establish instrument validity, we met with two individuals who have been very involved in the development of expert systems for over five years and who work in different application areas. We incorporated their constructive suggestions to develop the final version of the survey instrument. The reliability of the survey was tested by determining a reliability statistic from the survey results. The value of Cronbach's alpha for the survey instrument was 0.724, an acceptable level of reliability for this type of application.

Approximately 40% (47 out of 118 valid responses) of the managers responded, returning the questionnaires within ten weeks. The majority of the respondents were vice-presidents of data processing or managers in the area of finance and accounting. We performed independence tests to see if the type of industry, firm size (number of employees), microcomputer experience, or microcomputer application software usage were related to the belief that an expert system would be useful in a business. We surveyed the respondents to determine which factors would be most important in the decision to purchase or develop an expert system and included factors traditionally important in software purchase decisions and several which are considered to be relevant to expert system software. Cost, ease of use, documentation, hardware and software compatibility, user training, and the ability to run on a microcomputer were among those most frequently cited. Features of expert systems software that were important to managers were not unlike those which customers consider in any type of software, i.e., ease of use, good documentation, and vendor support.

## 5. Conclusions

The study revealed both lack of current usage of expert systems in our sample and definite commitment to future purchase or development of these systems by data processing departments.

Other studies and reports indicate that expert systems usage is growing throughout many different types of business organizations. This leads us to question whether or not expert systems are being developed by technical staff without the knowledge or support of the formal information systems department. In attempting to explain this conflicting evidence, we postulate that this type of software innovation percolates through an organization (i.e., in bottom-up fashion). Certainly the use of microcomputers has put more power into the hands of the users and allowed them to develop personal systems without the knowledge or approval of the formal information systems group.

From the data, we conclude that information systems managers feel that expert systems are not cost-effective now and they believe there are no compelling reasons for utilizing this technology. One explanation is that computer managers view expert system projects as large-scale projects with long development times and high development costs. However, Harmon and King have predicted the first “wave” of expert systems development will involve small-scale, relatively inexpensive personal expert systems. Our findings support this.

Another conclusion is that the type of industry is the only significant factor uncovered in predicting positive attitudes toward expert systems as a useful tool. This finding was not surprising, since business problems encountered in certain industries can be more easily solved using this technology. In addition, the type of expert systems applications has changed throughout the past few years and will continue to do so.

What similarities are there between the expert systems of today and the DSS of yesterday? Will organizations unwilling to use DSS embrace the new expert system technology to support business problem solving? The information provided by our survey is that most managers report practically no usage of DSS. We were not surprised at this result, since organizations which are not currently using them are less likely to be involved in expert systems.

## 6. Recommendations

Most of the companies who responded are using their microcomputers mainly for word-processing and spreadsheet applications. Therefore, there is need to educate users on ways to utilize the power of the next generation of hardware and software. So far, the computer has been a support tool capable of providing efficient computations, but not sophisticated enough to assist extensively in business problem-solving and decision-making. A new way of thinking about problems and how to solve them while using the computer to support decision-making is required before expert systems are widely adopted.

There are several ways that businesses can prepare themselves:

(a) Managers should become familiar with the potential benefits of expert systems through reading books and articles on the subject, attending seminars with a management perspective, and increasing communication with their technical staff.

(b) Technical staff should be encouraged to experiment with small-scale expert systems to serve as prototypes for future larger systems. Individuals interested in experimenting should be encouraged to join local special interest groups.

(c) Management should network programmers, analysts, and users across organizational boundaries to move more rapidly along the learning curve.

(d) Management should insure that experts are available to help in developing expert systems. The commitment and cooperation of the expert and the developer becomes critical to the success of the system.

(e) Management should coordinate the direction of expert systems development to support organizational goals.

(f) It will be important to integrate these systems with existing computing resources. This will require expert system development tools which provide such interfaces.

(g) Management should be cautious of vendors' claims about expert system shells on the market.

Based on the results of the survey, we believe that data processing managers should become more aware of the potential benefits of expert systems. The “wait and see” attitude could be costly if expertise is vital to a business and/or competitors are using expert systems to enhance services and improve productivity to their competitive advantage.
