# Factors Facilitating the Use of Information Technology for Competitive Advantage: An Exploratory Study

Boon Siong Neo \*

School of Accountancy, Nanyang Technological Institute, Singapore 2263, Republic of Singapore

A content analysis of fourteen published cases showed the importance of an organization's existing systems in the use of information technology for competitive advantage. Such uses were often precipitated by threatening external events. There were little support for the literature's assertions that competitive pressure is a key factor motivating strategic uses of IT. There were indications that understanding the potential strategic impact of IT and integration with business planning processes may lead to greater IT utilization. Finally, the study confirms the importance of customer needs and management support as factors facilitating the use of IT for competitive advantage

Keywords: Information technology, Management of information systems, Strategic uses of information systems, Competitive advantage.

## 1. Introduction

Computer applications in the 1950s and 1960s were mainly transaction processing and operational in nature. During this period, IT (Information Technology) essentially served an administrative support role. The DSS (Decision Support Systems) movement in the 1970s showed how IT can be used to support managerial decision-making. Although authors speak of DSS as improving effectiveness compared with the efficiency goals of transaction processing in the past, the potential strategic impact of IT was largely ignored. It has only been in the last few years, fueled by success stories reported in the news media [6] that the strategic role of IT in accomplishing business goals is beginning to be recognized. The traditional wisdom is that strategy begets structure, which in turn determines the kind of IS (Information System) support needed. Today, the converse also holds: IS begets strategy [14].

Brancheau and Wetherbe [5], in their Delphi study of general managers and top IS executives, identified IS strategic planning and use of IS for competitive advantage as the top two key issues facing the IS profession for the rest of the decade. In identifying research in the use of IS technology for competitive strategy, Mason [15] notes, “The 1980s is a period in which no organization can afford to ignore its commitment to information systems and services, nor in many cases can it afford to subordinate them to any other business process.”

The new strategic role of IT compels management to manage it in a manner that will maximize its potential; continuing developments must be monitored and impact on the organization assessed. Competence must be gained and protected and suitable applications that either facilitate or drive an organization's strategy must be identified. These strategic applications must be developed and implemented in a timely and cost-effective manner.

## 2. Literature Review

In one of the earliest efforts to explore the link between IS technology and corporate strategy, King [11] discussed a process whereby IS can be aligned to support corporate goals and strategy. This approach has been adopted by IBM in their Business Systems Planning (BSP) methodology and Enterprise-wide Information Management [4] concept. They assume that an IS will fulfill its appropriate role in a firm if organizational strategy is used as the basis for developing IS strategy. This was later extended by considering reverse flow also, i.e., not only should business strategy determine IS resource planning, but IS resources should be considered for their potential impact on business strategy [12].

Gerstein and Reisman [8] noted that the split between the IS specialist and the manager is the major hindrance to an organization's adoption of computer technology for competitive advantage. This split arose because of the seemingly independent concern for technology and the functioning of the business by the IS specialist and the manager, respectively. The obvious result has been a breakdown in communication. Gerstein and Reisman recommended that IS be viewed as a separate “business entity” within the organization. They assume that, as a separate entity which depends on the rest of the organization for “business”, IS is forced to consider the business interests of the rest of the organization and not be narrowly focused on technology per se. This idea has also been suggested and further developed by McFarland and McKenney [18] in their “business within a business” concept and by Zmud et al.’s recent work on “the information economy” [29].

McFarland and McKenney were among the earliest to recognize the new strategic role of IS. Their strategic grid classifies firms according to the strategic impact of existing applications as well as those under development. Although this framework was originally proposed for determining the required level of senior management attention in managing corporate IS, others have suggested it as a framework for identifying strategic IS applications. Its value in identifying strategic IS is doubtful, since it is basically a classification framework. Furthermore, the method seems to suggest that the strategic value of IS is an after-the-fact analysis of current and in-process applications. It fails to account for situations where the strategic importance of IS is recognized, but where appropriate applications are neither currently available nor in-process. Its value lies in providing a framework for a snapshot of the status and role of IS, both currently and in the near future, if there is no intervention by management. This explicit consideration of IS's role in business is an important step towards utilizing it for competitive advantage.

Perhaps the most frequently cited framework for use in the strategic management of IT is the Porter competitive forces framework [21]. He used an industry structure framework to determine appropriate strategies for a firm. This consists of five competitive forces: new entrants, suppliers, customers, substitute products, and competitors. A good strategy should enable an organization to do one or more of the following: erect barriers against potential new entrants, change the balance of power in supplier relationship in favor of the firm, increase switching costs for customers, and change the basis of competition among rivals in the firm's favor. In addition, Porter defined three generic strategies which firms can undertake: cost leadership, differentiation, and focus.

Parsons [20] was the first to suggest that the strategic importance of IT can be assessed within the Porter framework. McFarland [16,17] developed five questions that could be asked to search for opportunities. Cash and Konsynski [7] used it to evaluate the strategic importance of inter-organizational systems. Bakos and Treacy [2] built their research perspective of IS and corporate strategy around the same framework, although they considered internal efficiency and the business portfolio as well. Vitale [27] also evaluated the risks of strategic IS within the Porter framework. The popularity of Porter's framework [28] makes it a convenient and useful tool for communicating the potential of IT to managers and practitioners.

More recently, Porter [22] has described how a firm's value chain can be used as a framework for identifying opportunities for competitive advantage. A firm's value activities fall into two broad categories: primary and support. Primary activities are those involved in the physical creation of the product, its marketing and delivery to customers, and its support and servicing after sale. Support activities provide the inputs and infrastructure that allow the primary activities to take place. These are linked together to form the firm's value chain. Competitive advantage in either cost or differentiation is a function of this chain.

Porter and Millar [23] described how the value chain framework can be used as a basis for identifying strategic IT applications. IT is permeating the value chain, transforming the way value activities are performed and the nature of the linkages among them. It also affects competitive scope and the way products meet customer needs. Every value activity has both a physical and an information processing component. Every one creates and uses information. IT enables a firm to better coordinate its activities and thus gives it greater flexibility in deciding its breadth of activities. Porter and Millar proposed the use of an information intensity matrix to assess IT's role. The matrix evaluates the information intensity of the value chain against that of the product. They suggested that IT will play a strategic role in an industry that is characterized by high information intensity in both the value chain and the product.

Rackoff et al. [25] used a strategic thrusts framework for identifying strategic IS. Strategic thrusts are “major competitive moves made by a firm”, Five were postulated: differentiation, cost, innovation, growth, and alliance; they are targeted at suppliers, customers and/or competitors. IT can be used to support or shape the firm's competitive strategy by supporting or shaping competitive thrusts. They described a strategic IS (SIS) planning process which was successfully applied at one of GTE's divisions. The five phases of the process can be synthesized into the two major activities: introducing top executives to SIS concepts and generating SIS ideas among information management and corporate planning staff. The idea generation meetings consist of tutorials on competitive strategy and SIS, applying SIS concepts to actual cases, reviewing the company's competitive position, brainstorming, discussing and evaluating SIS opportunities, and detailing SIS blockbusters.

Benjamin et al. [3] proposed a strategic opportunity matrix for identifying IT opportunities. They suggested that IT can be used for strategic purposes either in the competitive marketplace or in internal operations. Uses can either focus on traditional products and processes, or on changing the firm's current way of doing business. One contribution of the matrix is its explicit consideration of internal operations as a possible area for strategic IS applications: most of the literature assumes that the sources of competitive advantage are external rather than internal. They also addressed the issue of top management vision and support as a prerequisite for success in using IT for strategic applications.

Ives and Learmonth [9] developed a model for identifying IS to improve customer service. The model has four main phases (requirements, acquisition, stewardship, and retirement) and it is postulated that all customers go through these phases. Thus organizations can adopt the customer's point of view and evaluate how IT applications can better meet customers' needs.

In one of the few empirical studies in the area, King et al. [13] reported on the specific ways in which information resources were actually used, the planning and decision-making processes that were being used to identify and select strategic IT opportunities, and the organizational factors which facilitate or inhibit the different uses of IT. They also distinguished between strategic uses of information and of IT. They found that the most common applications of IT were for cost competitiveness, customer service, and product and market differentiation. In contrast, the most common applications of information were for customer service, market segmentation, and supplier relations. They also found that 55% of respondents reported either “irregular” or “no formal process” for identifying opportunities for the application of IT. The lack of appropriate planning, lack of top management support, difficulty in assessing tangible benefits and other priorities were major inhibitors. On the other hand, the primary facilitators were strong technical support and expertise, leadership in IT, competitive pressure, financial position, and extensive computer facilities.

In summary, the SIS literature suggests that the following factors facilitate the identification of strategic IT applications:

1. Alignment of IS and business strategic planning

2. Improved communication between managers and IS specialists

3. Explicit consideration of IS's role in the business

4. Competitive pressure

5. Analysis of internal operational needs

6. Analysis of market/customer needs

7. Existing strength in IT

8. Extensive computer facilities

9. Management vision and support.

## 3. Methodology

The newness of research in this area and the relative lack of empirical studies so far suggests that an exploratory research design using published company materials may be appropriate. Most of the current literature utilizes individual cases as support for their arguments. They seem to have made no attempts to analyze the cases collectively to see whether the evidence as a whole supports the assertions. Patterns of occurrence are more meaningful than a single instance, which may be just a random event.

Exploratory studies of this nature seem to have the support of the panel and participants of the Harvard Business School's 75th Anniversary Colloquium on current challenges in IS research. In summarizing the discussion on the research needs of the strategy-IT linkage, Ashenhurst [1] said, "The field is too new, unstructured, and ill-defined at present to permit effective use of more formal data collection methods or refined applications of statistical methodologies." On a broader front, Pfeffer [24] observed that the neglect of documentary analysis and archival data in organizational research has contributed to "unresolvable ignorance" in the development of organizational theory. Detailed analysis of published cases have also been fruitfully utilized as a method for studying strategy formulation processes [19], an area very similar to the focus of this study.

The analysis of published materials employs the method of content analysis $[10,26]$ . This requires the researcher explicitly to define, structure, and state the coding scheme before embarking on a review of the documentation. From the review of literature, we will see if the nine factors identified above are important in facilitating the strategic use of IT applications. The factors serve as a coding scheme for reviewing the case materials. In addition, we describe the processes and other factors which emerge as a result of reviewing the actual cases. Findings can serve as testable hypotheses for subsequent studies.

Although this approach is appropriate for this exploratory study, we recognize its limitations. First, the published materials must adequately discuss how each organization identifies IT applications. Much of the material discusses the impact of IT applications without describing the process: these are not relevant here. As a result, only 14 cases have sufficient information to be included. Another problem with all materials is that they tend to be after-the-fact rationalization. Thus, what is reported may appear more systematic and logical than what actually occurred. We mitigate this by using information from as many sources as possible. Finally, qualitative research tends to suffer from being rather subjective. To minimise this, the coding of the cases was kept separate from the analysis. All coding was completed before any analysis was performed.

## 4. Case Samples

Most of the literature on strategic uses of IT cites anecdotes from cases. These provide a starting point for locating appropriate material. A further search was made for relevant published materials from: Business Week, Forbes, Fortune, Information Week, and the Harvard Business

School Case Service. From these, 14 cases were identified as relevant and having sufficient information. These cases are briefly described in the Appendix. The descriptions focus on the process whereby each firm comes to use IT for competitive advantage.

The most important factor driving the use of IT is internal efficiency needs. For 11 of the 14 cases, IT is initially used to improve internal operations. This is particularly true of those companies in the airline and information industries, where computers are a necessity for day-to-day transactions. It is interesting that the same operational transaction processing systems are later adapted for strategic purposes. Three variables seem to be mediating in this process. One is the experience and expertise gained in managing these operational systems. Problems in these systems are sorted out as they are implemented within the firms. Enhancements are made to meet internal user needs. Newer technologies are incorporated to make them more flexible and easier to use. Then, someday some person recognizes that these internal systems can be used, with some modifications, to enhance the achievement of the firm's business plans: this could be an internal organizational member or an outside consultant. Such a recognition often coincides with new environmental opportunities or threats to the organization. This “precipitating event” is the second mediating variable. For the airlines, it was the threat of travel agents building a system that might increase their bargaining power relative to the airlines. For Merrill Lynch, it was a decline in the attractiveness of stock investments due to an increase in interest rates. For McKesson, it was the low and still falling profit margins that triggered consideration of IT. The idea is implemented when management supports and devotes the necessary resources to implement it.

The above finding suggests that a strategic application of IT is likely to be more successful if the firm has gained expertise from using a similar system. The chances of success are increased, because implementation problems can be anticipated. Benefits from existing use can be clearly demonstrated and potential users have a live, tangible system with which to experiment. This suggests that a firm's existing set of IT applications can be a potential gold mine for accomplishing strategic goals! A firm starting to consider the strategic use of IS in its business would do well to look at the potential of its existing systems.

The literature implicitly assumes that fresh ideas are required and new systems are needed to enable a firm to use IT for competitive purposes. There is no doubt that new systems will be required on many occasions. However, the development of new systems involves substantial time, effort, and costs, and the risk of failure is higher, because of the relative lack of experience in using and handling these systems before exposing them to outsiders. Further, the impact of such a failure is likely to be significant in at least two respects. First, accomplishing strategic goals would be slowed; the importance of these goals to the overall business and the availability of contingency plans affect the significance of delay. Second, where such systems involve customers, suppliers, or other outside parties, the firm's reputation could be tarnished. There could therefore be a direct impact on the bottom line if customers switch to competitors and/or suppliers' bargaining power is increased. This may also substantially increase the difficulty of implementing further strategic IT applications involving external parties. Therefore, existing systems should be evaluated for their strategic potential, while considering new systems that might enhance the firm's competitiveness.

Another interesting finding is the lack of support for competitive pressure as a factor for the use of SIS. Only two of the firms use IT applications as a direct result of competitive pressure. This does not give credence to the persistent call in the literature to use Porter's competitive model as a basis to identify opportunities for IT. One plausible explanation is the lack of knowledge of competitive strategy models in the 1970s, when most of the systems in the sample were implemented. Another explanation may be that most of the cases represented the first to use IT in their industry. Subsequent users of SIS may be motivated to do so because of competitive pressure. Combined with the earlier findings, this suggests that early users of SIS are motivated by their internal expertise in IT, while later users are driven by competitive pressures.

The next finding concerns the integration of IS and business planning. From the cases, the firms which explicitly considered the strategic role of IT were new start-up businesses rather than established ones. Federal Express understood, from its inception, that it must rely on IT to succeed. Gannett's vision for USA Today seems to be founded on the availability of communication technology. These examples demonstrate the potential contribution to corporate strategy when the role of IT is specifically considered as part of the business planning process. The literature does not address this issue clearly. It is full of various models that seem to focus on a one-shot initial evaluation of IT for competitive purposes without addressing how it could be incorporated into the overall strategic planning process.

A related observation is that none of the ideas for strategic uses of IT came from the IS department or from their interaction with operating management. This seems to confirm the contention that IS professionals do not interest themselves in the businesses that they are supposed to be supporting. A plausible hypothesis is that better results may be obtained if the use of IT is evaluated as part of the firm's strategic planning process rather than depending on initiatives from the IS department alone.

More established firms seem to rely on outside consultants to introduce fresh ideas. In three of the cases, the consultants suggested ideas which elevated the importance of IT to a business strategy. This suggests a potentially significant role for outside consultants as instruments of change, in this case, to help firms introduce strategic applications of IT. However, the key implication is that strategic uses of IT can be found through systematic evaluation of the business when there is understanding of such use. Although in these three cases, consultants were instrumental, there is no reason to believe that internal organizational members cannot be just as innovative, if exposed to the concept and use of SIS. Thus it seems likely that understanding of the strategic uses of IT in an organization will lead to its greater use for strategic purposes.

Finally, the study confirms the importance of customer needs and management vision/support as important factors in the strategic use of IT. American Hospital Supply's system was initiated because of customer complaints of incomplete orders and late deliveries. Digital Equipment was similarly motivated because of inappropriate equipment configurations for customers, that were discovered only near to the time of delivery of the equipment. The airline reservation system implementation was driven by the travel agents' plans to develop one of their own.

Management vision and support seem to be the catchword for successful implementation of any innovation in organizations. Very few organizational members will pursue an innovation when the initial idea is not encouraged and management seems unsupportive and unwilling to devote resources to allow the idea to develop more fully.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

Our analysis of the 14 cases found that existing systems have been important for organizational deployment of IT for competitive purposes. Although new ideas are often needed, organizations should take a closer look at their portfolio of existing applications for potential extension into the competitive marketplace. External precipitating events also prove to be important in IT organizational decisions. Managers should take advantage of environmental threats and opportunities to introduce useful innovations, such as the use of IT in their organizations. Consultants and organizational members exposed to the strategic potential of IT are more likely to consider it as an alternative solution for competitive needs. For start-up businesses in our sample, there are indications that considering IT will result in its greater use for strategic purposes. Customer needs were also found to be important reasons. Lastly, management support and an environment conducive for innovation facilitate an organization's intent to use IT in the marketplace.

Although our findings must necessarily be tentative because of the small sample size, they represent insights not considered important previously. These findings supplement the literature and form the basis for more systematic investigation in this area of growing importance.

Appendix: Brief descriptions of 14 cases used in the study

## 1. American Airlines and United Airlines

Both the Apollo (United Airlines) and Sabre (American Airlines) systems provide on-line reservations for flights, hotels, car rentals, and other travel related services. United Airlines is also using the Apollo system to help travel agents with their office automation requirements. These systems provide increased revenue to the developer airlines in various ways. Flight schedules are displayed to give the developer airlines advantage in terms of bookings, a fee is charged for bookings on other airlines and information obtained from the system is used to determine pricing and other marketing strategies.

It is important to note that these systems were originally defined to meet the airlines' internal data processing needs. Every scheduled air carrier must store and immediately update a tremendous amount of information. It must keep track of the flights it provide, the variety of fares applicable for each flight, the number of seats available for sale in each fare class for each flight segment for every day within a reasonable period into the future. A carrier also needs to maintain records of passenger reservations. In short, computers contain the inventory of airline services.

It is thus not surprising that American Airlines was using computers to track reservations as early as 1953! The market potential of such systems first came to the fore in the early 1970 when travel agents got together to try to develop an industry-wide reservation system. Recognizing the potential shift in the balance of power, airlines got together to try to develop a reservation system that would be more acceptable. When that arrangement failed, both American and United Airlines utilized their experience with computerized reservation systems and their facilities to develop the Sabre and Apollo systems, respectively.

## 2. American Hospital Supply Corporation (AHSC)

AHSC used their ASAP (Analytic Systems Automatic Purchasing) system to help its customers automate their ordering and inventory control. By placing order-entry terminals in hospitals, AHSC has made ordering and tracking of orders easier for its customers. This increases their customers' switching costs. It is interesting that the system was initially developed in the 1960s to solve the health-care distributor's operational problems of incomplete orders and late delivery, especially with one of its major customers, Stanford Medical Centre. As newer technologies became available, the system was enhanced and its application broadened.

## 3. McKesson Corporation

Another health-care distributor's system, Economost of the McKesson Corporation, helps pharmacists and drugstore operators keep track of which products are selling, when to reorder, how to place orders automatically, and even have their orders delivered with the price and shelf location tagged on the products. The pilot project was conceived in the late 1960s by Wilson Nolen, then Vice President of Operations. He was convinced that computers could help McKesson cut costs, as well as bring in more business. However, the pilot project had limited features and after Nolen left the company in 1973, the project did not proceed much further.

In fact, in 1975, McKesson was considering the viability of continuing in the health-care distribution business, because of low and falling profit margins. The consultants who were hired to make recommendations suggested that McKesson continue with the health-care distributorship but warned that the business would thrive only if the pilot ordering system be upgraded into a full business system. McKesson heeded the advice and Economost was reborn and enhanced. One of the consultants was later hired by McKesson to become its Vice President of data operations.

## 4. Federal Express

Federal Express started their overnight package-delivery business in 1973. Right from its inception, Federal Express invested heavily in technology and automation to provide better control of the business, reduce handling and billing costs, and provide accurate and timely service to customers. One such application is the “cradle to grave” monitoring of packages.

As with other automation projects, it was conceived when the company was formed and implemented when there was enough volume to justify it. This is perhaps a unique case, because this application of technology was identified as key to the business' success at its inception. In particular, the role of IT was considered, along with other physical handling technologies. The primary driving forces were cost, efficiency, and customer service. Its success in capturing and maintaining a large share of the market is due, in large part, to this vision of its founders.

## 5. Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC)

DEC uses an expert system, XCON, to improve its marketing and provide better system configurations. Because of the tremendous variety of possible configurations of its equipment and the difficulty of ensuring that each system was appropriately configured, DEC was faced with a problem of rework of manufactured configurations at or near installation time. In addition to customer complaints and expense, the rework lost revenue through deferral of invoicing. XCON captures the expert rules used by the most knowledgeable design and field service engineers. This has reduce the time required to fill orders and increases accuracy, enhancing DEC's image as a quality supplier.

## 6. Merrill Lynch

Merrill Lynch offers a broad range of services to meet the financial and investment needs of individuals, corporations and institutions. In the mid 70s, the firm was looking for ways to expand and diversify, particularly because of the decline in attractiveness of stocks investment caused by rising interest rates. Among the list of ideas submitted by the Stanford Research Institute was one in which an account holder can buy and sell stocks, earn interest on the balance, write checks, and use a supplied credit card. The idea was supported by management, then Merrill Lynch had to take another three months out the various technical and legal issues. Although the data processing needs of the Cash Management Account was complex, Merrill Lynch had excess computer capacity to handle it. It also linked up with Banc One to provide the checking and credit card facilities, since only a bank could legally provide those services. The innovation was so successful that Merrill Lynch captured 90% of the market and there was no competition for five years. However, no enhancements were made to the system, and with competitors offering similar accounts its share of the market dropped to 50% in 1984.

## 7. Banc One

Merrill Lynch's collaborator in its CMA account, Banc One, also used IT for pursuing its strategy. However, unlike Merrill Lynch, which relied on one main idea, Banc One's management was convinced that IT could play a significant role in all areas of its business. As such, it has invested heavily on computers and IT since the 1960s. The expertise it gained allowed it to complete the conversion of acquired companies' systems in four months. This is important in absorbing and controlling acquired businesses, especially when the concept of acquisitions was the cornerstone of Banc One's strategy. The expertise helped Banc One to acquire 22 banks in Ohio by 1985. Its expertise in IS has also made Banc One attractive as partner to other financial institutions in offering innovative services to the public. Merrill Lynch's CMA is a good example; another is its processing of credit cards issued by other financial institutions. This capability made the Ohio-based bank one of the three largest processors of VISA cards in 1985.

## 8. Citicorp

Citicorp, the largest bank holding company in the nation, is credited with leading in two areas: automated teller machine system, and its Global Transaction Network (GTN). The ATM idea grew out of "Project Paradise", a project launched in the 1970s to streamline Citicorp's internal data processing operations. The ATM system reduced the cost of processing low margin consumer transactions in branch offices. This project was also instrumental in establishing the strategic role of IT in Citicorp's business. GTN is a worldwide financial information telecommunications systems that provides up-to-minute investment information to 140 cities around he world. Interestingly, GTN was initially motivated by the need to structure and connect Citicorp's internal systems worldwide to corporate headquarters for control and support purposes.

## 9. USAA

When Robert McDermott took over as President of the USAA insurance group in 1969, he was “horrified” by the mountain of paper used by the company. That began a search for ways to run the insurance business without, or with much less, paper. The result was a series of systems that automated policy and claims processing. As expertise with IS accumulated, its applications were widened. In 1979, a system was developed to free the company from the use of outside claims adjustors. In 1981, a plan was adopted to develop an integrated system for all the company’s property and casualty insurance lines. By this time, it was clear to the company that its expertise in IT will be a strategic factor in its ability to compete in the insurance market. In 1985, USAA was the sixth largest auto insurer and the tenth largest home insurer in the nation. It has assets worth \$4.1 billion and premiums of \$1.5 billion.

## 10. Dow Jones

Dow Jones is the first national newspaper to use satellite transmission to produce and distribute its published product. The Wall Street Journal (WSJ). As a result, production time and costs were reduced. It also enabled Dow Jones to introduce European and Asian versions of the paper, thus increasing its competitive scope. Dow Jones has also put the data it collects into a computerized database. This has resulted in its ability to introduce new products, like the Dow Jones News/Retrieval Service. The innovations have been possible because of a long-term commitment to using IT in business. Although internal efficiency was the initial motivation, the resulting expertise and experience has enabled Dow Jones to use IT in what is essentially an information business.

## 11. Gannett Corporation

Another newspaper company that uses satellite transmission is Gannett; it produces the USA Today. However, unlike the established WSJ, the use of this technology was espoused by Gannett's management when it considered launching USA Today. Strategic use of communications technology was expected to be key in producing a general interest national newspaper: it would save labor as well as allow the paper to be simultaneously printed in all of Gannett's printing plants. It was later to be used to print and distribute the paper in other parts of the world. Thus, IT was central to fulfilling the vision of USA Today. It is perhaps fair to conjecture that USA Today would not have been conceived without the availability of sophisticated communications technology.

However, in Dow Jones' case, the WSJ had been produced successfully long before communication technology became available, and although its use enhances the Journal's competitiveness, it is perhaps not the cornerstone of the Journal's success.

## 12. Dun and Bradstreet (D&B)

D&B has diverse holdings ranging from the D&B Credit Report and Moody's Investor Services to A.C. Nielsen, the company that compiles television ratings. As with other companies in the information business, D&B had to turn to computers to manage its "tons" of data and improve internal efficiency. As IT became more sophisticated and capabilities to manipulate data were made easier, management realize that its computerized databases was a potential "gold mine". Data could be recycled and repackaged to increase revenue. Furthermore, unlike other resources, data is not diminished through repeated "mining". For example, the data in the single credit report was repackaged into 30 different products. The increased revenue has turned this successful company into a very successful one.

## 13. McGraw

Over the years, McGraw Hill has diversified from a publishing company into an information business, with its acquisitions of Standard & Poor and Data Resources. However, IT began to play a more important role in its business only recently, when Joseph Dionne took over as CEO (1983). His vision to put the old-line publishing empire on-line has critics complaining about his preoccupation with databases and his “high-tech blinders”. He brought in consultants and used the resulting recommendations to put the company on the high-tech track. McGraw Hill is now computerizing its editorial operations, selling price quotes, news and financial data, and has innovated with the “electronic bookshelf”. This puts data about McGraw Hill’s publications onto a national bulletin board, which can be accessed by anyone with a microcomputer and modem. More information about specific publications can be obtained and orders placed using credit cards for payment. Although specific data are currently not available to assess the success of these new innovations, there is no doubt that the computer age has dawned at McGraw Hill.
