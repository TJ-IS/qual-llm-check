# Expert Systems for Small Businesses

Gholamreza Torkzadeh and S. Subba Rao
Information Systems and Operations Management Department,
College of Business Administration, The University of Toledo,
Toledo, OH 43606, USA

Small business contribute significantly to the economy. New information system technologies, like expert systems have the promise of improving the efficiency and competitiveness of small businesses. This paper reviews the issues related to application of expert systems in a small business and discusses their capabilities and limitations. The role of decision support systems and integration of DSS and expert systems as a useful tool for small businesses is also examined. Further, the issues concerning the selection of expert systems for a small business are described.

Keywords: Small business, Expert systems, Expert system applications, Expert system shells.

## 1. Introduction

Small businesses represent a significant portion of the economy. According to a publication of the Executive Office of the President [9] in 1984, 94% of all U.S. manufacturing enterprises have less than 100 employees, but they provide over four and a half million jobs. According to the Small Business Administration of the United States, 97% of all American firms are small businesses and they account for 43% of the GNP [28]. Though they play a significant role in the economy, the problems of small businesses have not been given the same importance as for large businesses. Two trends have been emerging in the development of computer technology: constant increase in its power and capabilities, and steady decrease in the costs of obtaining it. In business offices and factories, personal computers or terminals are becoming as commonplace as telephones [16] [21]. For the first time, and for a modest investment, this technology has left the specialized data processing departments and arrived at the desks of professionals and managers. Technology that was only within reach of larger companies a few years ago can now be employed by many of the smallest, thereby increasing their competitive advantage [23]; an advantage sought by small firms through the use of computer technology [27]. Even the tiniest of firms should be able to take advantage of computer-based information system and microcomputer advancements [7].

In the past, there has been concern about the success of computer information systems in smaller organizations compared to larger ones. Especially, there were reservations about the lack of resources, lack of formalized systems, and shorter management time frames of the smaller firms [8]. Today, this concern seems less plausible. Hardware and software no longer require an overwhelming fixed cost. Advice for good application development is more readily available across the counter in the computer store [24], reducing the need for consultants or a computer expert on the payroll.

A natural extension of the computer based information systems has been in the direction of decision support systems (DSS). The technology was previously adopted by large business; small business can now make use of the full potential of DSS because of the advances in microcomputer technology. In the application development area, for example, computer based DSS can be utilized to assist small businesses in planning their financial futures [17]. Trends in computing toward increasingly flexible, easy to use computer hardware and software will probably continue into the indefinite future [4] and help in the adoption of DSS by small businesses.

A very significant development resulting from the merger of artificial intelligence (AI) research, problem solving and DSS has been that of Expert

Systems. Harmon and King [15] contend that expert systems will change the way businesses operate by altering the way people think about solving problems. More information will be gathered, synthesized, and put into useful form more rapidly than has ever before been possible. It is argued that this technology will play a major role in reshaping traditional notions of what organizations are, and how they are managed [19]. It is our contention that recent advances in hardware and expert systems software, along with declining prices, can have a beneficial effect on the small firms. Therefore, this paper focuses on expert systems, their capability and benefits for small businesses. It also provides guidelines for selecting and using an expert system.

## 2. Expert Systems and Their Applications

An expert system makes use of expertise that has been gathered from human expert(s) about the solution of a specific type of problem or class of related problems. Expert systems can help people solve problems for which expertise is in short supply, or is not well distributed, or is not available around the clock. It has been considered as the most successful area of AI [18]. Unlike many other areas, expert systems provide a commercially viable technology that can be used in a cost effective manner for real world problems.

Expert systems for business are programs that "contain" a condensed version of human expertise in a particular specialized area or domain; the examples are: financial planning, market planning, product innovation, inventory management, vendor selection, production scheduling, and quality control. Expert systems are excellent methods of presenting and transmitting an individual and/or collective experience in specialized areas of business; they capture, preserve and make available the experience that certain talented individuals within an organization have amassed over their careers.

Expert systems technology is argued to have greatly expanded the applicability of the computer [14] by handling complex tasks beyond the capability of conventional software. Expert systems can enhance the usefulness of a DSS interactive query facility in solving unstructured or semi-structured problems. For example, DSS technol ogy has been utilized to assist small businesses in their decision making process; e.g., in planning their financial futures. By greatly multiplying the power of computers to interpret data and make decisions, expert systems are capturing vital knowledge in every corner of business and making the knowledge more widely accessible.

## 2.1. DSS and Expert System Applications for Small Businesses

Expert systems combine knowledge of a particular application area with an interface capability, to enable the system to reach a level of decision making performance comparable to that of human experts in some specialized area. Most existing expert systems are being used as independent computerized systems advising users on a specific problem area [32]. They are considered to be intelligent DSSs [6] or fit the generic DSS framework.

Interacting with a DSS system requires much greater users knowledge of the problem area than for an expert system environment, where the user does not have to know much detail about the domain. There are expert systems like 'Financial Advisor' already in the market; for these the user does not even have to know the meaning of "cash flow." In that sense, they allow a domain expert to enter information through an intermediary [11]; i.e., they simply "automate" the intermediary.

Integration of expert systems and DSSs is a viable endeavor [12] [22] [31], providing a powerful tool for business decision making. In this capacity, expert systems represent one of the most exciting new developments in information technology. The needs and potential for expert systems exist in virtually any company.

The benefits of DSS/ES integration can be realized along several dimensions: expert system or DSS contribution, and the synergy resulting from the DSS/expert system combination. Turban and Watkins discuss these benefits under the categories of database, models, interfaces, and overall capabilities. They contend that the combination of expert system and DSS is likely to produce better results than either approach independently.

Expert systems are probably most effective in a business when they are integrated with mainstream data-processing operations. The idea of DSS and expert system integration as a practical tool for decision making process is attractive to small businesses. Some of the newer developmental tools of expert system possess DSS capabilities. For example, Knowledge Workbench, M.1, GURU, and some others provide environments for expert system development in conjunction with a database management system. Some of these tools, e.g., GURU, are capable of interfacing expert system with database management system as well as a spreadsheet, graphics, communications, and word processing. A comprehensive review of expert system building tools is given in [13]. Invariably these newer tools are capable of various computations and even system simulation (e.g., KEE and DSIM), making them useful for a variety of tasks.

## 2.2. An Example of an Expert System for Small Business

Consider a job shop situation where a production scheduler or supervisor resorts to his/her experience. One of the significant obstacles to improved factory performance is the complexity associated with constructing and maintaining good production schedules [30].

Figure 1 illustrates processes involved in a typical production planning/scheduling situation; based on this one can develop an expert system. Evaluation of alternatives, depicted in the conceptual model, depends to some extent on experience and rules of thumb used by the expert, in this case, the expert is an experienced production planner/scheduler. Experience is particularly important in deciding variables like "move time", "waiting time", and schedules. The expert will typically have access to a database that holds all the basic and relevant information about machines, materials, cost, etc. The expert system will capture the experience and problem solving abilities of the expert and make them available to others. In addition to acting like an expert consultant, the system can also act as a training aid.

## 3. Expert System Development...Shells

The trend in industry and business is toward in-house creation of expert systems that act as a corporate memory and consultant. This trend is likely to increase as automation is further introduced into businesses and industry; these automated systems will have a simulation of human expertise. Large businesses and industry can afford to develop in-house expert systems; but small and even medium size businesses may not have the resources to build their own.

There are three ways of developing an expert system. One is using an AI language like PROLOG or LISP. These are better able to handle concepts and ambiguity than traditional highlevel languages such as COBOL, FORTRAN, or PASCAL. But they are harder to learn and this may be the major reason for avoiding AI. The second method of application development is the use of a commercially-available package or shell. A third is to use the former to find the best way (prototype) and then code conventionally (e.g., in PASCAL).

In the last few years, several software packages, "shells", for building expert systems have become available commercially. This is an expert system development tool consisting of two stand-alone pieces of software: a rule set manager, used to specify, modify, and compile a rule set; and an inference engine capable of reasoning with rule sets built with the rule set manager. A shell is an expert system without the knowledge base. Figure 2 depicts principal components of expert systems and expert system shells.

Shell systems run on personal computers; their low prices indicate a potential for growth of this technology and make them attractive to small business. Recognizing the market potential of low-cost shells, many expert system suppliers have been turning away from a custom-made approach. Suppliers cater to companies who wish to buy easy-to-use shells and train their own nontechnical employees to produce their expert systems. Guterl provides some ideas about cost and training time per person.

Although expert systems will continue to be built on a variety of hardware, the new reality is many will be built and/or executed on personal computers. Expert systems shells for personal computers have traditionally been appreciated as ideal for exploration and experimentation, parparticularly for customers just beginning to work with expert systems technology. Recently, there has been tremendous growth in the marketplace for personal computer based shells. As Martorelli [25] notes, this is mainly due to steady growth in the power, flexibility, and ease of use of personal computer based expert system shells.

An advantage of the “shell” approach is its generic nature. Many different applications can be developed using the same shell. Its use will expedite applications experience and learning of concepts, and will take staff-months rather than staff-years. Without the expert system shell, each application would have to be built from the ground up.

As Basden [1] states, working within the constraints of such shells leads to a clearer understanding of which features are necessary compared to what would have been obtained by working with more versatile software. In short, these packages are very appropriate tools for constructing prototype systems especially, in small organizations.

For discussions of expert systems as they relate to specific areas, companies, commercial applications, and evaluation of different tools, see also [2] and [20].

## 4. Considerations for the Selection of Expert Systems

During selection and implementation of expert systems, four categories of issues need to be considered for both small and large businesses. While large businesses can afford the luxury of experimentation, small businesses cannot. Therefore, capital costs should be given more importance by small businesses.

## 4.1. Technical feasibility

An expert system will be feasible in a business when:

\- sub-optimal solutions are acceptable

\- occasional wrong solutions are tolerable.

\- heuristics are necessary to solve a problem

\- judgement in selecting relevant information is necessary.

\- explanation of problem structure is necessary.

\- application requires intelligence/intelectual activity.

\- telephone test is possible; expert should be able to solve the problem over a phone call.

An expert system will not be feasible when:

\- application can be solved algorithmically.

\- application requires natural intelligence.

differentiation among meanings is required.

\- intuition is required.

## 4.2. Venture Justification

An expert system will be justified for a business when:

\- business plan matches technology.

\- investment opportunity exists.

\- technology impacts flexibility, quality, & productivity.

\- it can be financially justified.

## 4.3. User Acceptance

User acceptance plays an important part in the success of an expert system implementation. There are two factors: (i) user open-mindedness and attitude toward the new technology and a reasonable expectation of its capabilities; and, (ii) user belief that expertise to assist in development is available; e.g., training programs exist.

The greatest gains of expert system applications have been achieved by users who do not expect the computer to be a thinking machine [5]. Rather, they regard an application as a repository of available expertise to help them do their job better.

## 4.4. Connectivity

Most applications have one prerequisite that hampers expert system development: communication with a central database. Expert systems must interface with existing databases and computer programs. This is a mandatory [29] and vitally important feature. Often organizations have information that has already been loaded into their database, and if they are to use expert systems, they must recognize that the knowledge base is partially embedded in prior applications. Expert systems must be able to access this knowledge in some uniform way [10]. Connectivity is a particularly important issue when the system has to cut across different information system groups. Many packages, while offering basic interfacing ability for Lotus 1-2-3 or dBase, may still require considerable effort to enter data into the expert system. However, some packages that run on personal computers have integrated database and communication capabilities which make them particularly useful for cross functional applications (e.g., GURU).

## 4.5. Features Expected of an Expert System

Ideally, an expert system should include the following features:

\- reliability and accuracy.

\- ability to justify and explain results, and the reasoning behind them.

\- ability to generate multiple recommendations, with qualifications if necessary.

\- ability to accept problem descriptions in lay terms.

\- ability to disregard irrelevant data.

\- ability to check problem descriptions and input for consistency and accuracy.

\- ability to recognize a problem outside the system's domain of expertise and to decline offering a solution.

\- ability to offer inductive or deductive reasoning behind any question.

\- ability to modify the knowledge base easily using a built-in editor.

\- forward or backward chaining.

\- knowledge representation: either rule-based, frame-based, or example-based.

\- numeric or logical operators.

\- uncertainty logic; e.g., using fuzzy logic or probability methods to represent the degree of confidence the user places on the information.

\- ability to save facts introduced during a session. Other significant features are: ease of use, by both user and developer; ease of maintaining the existing systems, and adequacy of documentation.

It is not necessary or advisable to buy the most powerful expert system. From the point of view of a small business, it is always best to try out and buy the cheapest system with those features needed in the business. More powerful systems often execute more slowly and invariably are more difficult to learn and use.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

Expert systems are perhaps the most widely discussed area of artificial intelligence. They are adept at tackling problems with repetitious, limited domains that require heuristic problem-solving due to scarce knowledge or uncertainty. Expert systems are being considered as ways to improve efficiency and productivity. They offer useful features not available in conventional decision support systems.

Constant increase in the power and capabilities of computer technology and steady decrease in its costs have provided a good opportunity for its introduction into small businesses. The technologies are now available for microcomputer hardware systems. As a consequence of their lower cost, the risks are far less than in using high-end development environments.

Expert systems can help small businesses in increasing their efficiency and/or reducing their costs. However, it is important for these organizations to have an application oriented approach and realistic expectations. It is important to ask the question: “what do we want from the technology?” rather than “what can it offer?”

For a small firm to be successful in applying expert systems, it is appropriate to consider issues related to technical feasibility, venture justification, user acceptance, and connectivity. Questions should be concerned with implications on present and near future applications, and when such technology is applicable. It is advisable to take the prototyping approach; the initial system is a prototype for testing the applicability and acceptance. Small businesses can benefit from a shell; it provides ease of development, ease of use, time and expenses reduction, and it is generic in nature.
