# The Changing Data Processing Environment in Saudi Arabia

Jawaid Abdul Ghani and Sulaiman Al-Sakran
College of Industrial Management, King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, Diaharan, 31261, Saudi Arabia

The rapid growth in microcomputer sales has affected data processing in many ways and in many countries. In order to study the effects of this growth in a Third World environment, a survey of 200 organizations was conducted. Three major user segments were identified: non-users, microcomputer users, and mainframe users. It was found that during the period 1982–84 an important change occurred, in that the number of organizations using microcomputers surpassed those using mainframe computers. Companies using microcomputers were found to be smaller, younger, and more likely to be in the trade/service sector. The majority of companies in this user segment lacked a formal data processing department. They generally did not employ computer professionals and depended on ready-made rather than in-house development of software. There were no differences in the overall portfolio of applications of the two user segments, though the number of applications was fewer among micro users. This paper discusses the growing use of microcomputers in the small business sector and the implications of these results for educators, equipment vendors, and researchers.

Keywords: Microcomputers, Data processing, Developing countries, Saudi Arabia, Small business.

## 1. Introduction

Major changes in information technology directly affect the data processing environment. The most significant development in the 1980s has been the rise of the microcomputer and with it the rise of end user computing. In the U.S., microcomputer sales increased from 0.6 million units in 1981 to 6.6 million units in 1986 [16]. This resulted in a compound annual growth rate of 12%, compared to a compound annual growth rate of only 4% in mainframe computers [1].

With the vast increase in microcomputers has come a corresponding increase in the number of end-users – from less than four million potential users just ten years ago to about forty million today [7]. This has particularly affected data processing managers, who now find end user computing one of their key areas of concern [6].

The microcomputer has also affected the international computer industry. This has been fuelled by the rise of low-cost, high quality production capabilities in the newly industrialized countries of the Far East. However, despite the increasing importance of the international computer industry, very few studies have been reported that describe the trends in this market segment.

The purpose of this paper is to explore the effect of recent developments in information technology on the data processing environment in a

Third World country – namely Saudi Arabia. A survey of about 200 organizations was conducted in order to identify the extent of computer use, the characteristics of the data processing department, and the changes in computer use over time.

## 2. The Computing Environment in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia is, in many ways, unique. The Kingdom possesses the world's largest known resources of crude petroleum. Its growth rate and per capita income are among the highest in the world [3]. In the 1970s, the Kingdom embarked on a massive industrialization program. Over 200 billion dollars were spent on infrastructure and economic development during the ten year period 1975–85 [12, p. 43]. Traditionally, the economy has been dominated by the oil and public sectors; however, the Government has encouraged the growth of the non-oil economy, particularly the private sector. As a result, the number of private sector establishments more than doubled during the period 1979–83. In order to encourage the development of local industries, multinationals are allowed to operate in the Kingdom only as joint ventures with local companies. The important role played by international companies in the Kingdom is indicated by the fact that, in 1983, joint ventures accounted for 25% of the total number of private companies in the Kingdom and 33% of the total private capital investments [11, p. 104].

The Kingdom possesses abundant capital resources but is short in indigenous human resources. This has resulted in a heavy dependence on expatriate workers, to the extent that two-thirds of the labor force consists of foreign nationals [11, p. 84]. This has also lead to a situation where capital intensive processes are encouraged. Advanced technology, particularly computers, are therefore employed wherever possible.

Generally, the public sector has been mainframe oriented, while the private has been mini and micro oriented. It is estimated that private organizations own only 30% of the total installed mainframe computers, but 75% of the total installed mini and micro business computers [2]. The growth in the private sector has thus resulted in higher growth rates in mini and microcomputer sales. Annual computer imports rose from about \$50 million in 1979 to \$250 million during the bocm year of 1982, and settled to around \$180 million in 1985. In contrast, the microcomputer market, and to a lesser extent the minicomputer market, has witnessed a high growth rate. It is estimated that microcomputer sales jumped from \$7 million in 1982 to \$35 million in 1985 [15]. Note that, as described earlier, the same trends of high growth in microcomputer sales exist in the U.S.

## 3. Research Design

A survey was conducted of various private and public sector organizations located in the Eastern Region of Saudi Arabia during the period January 1986. A convenience sample was used, guided by listings of manufacturing and service organizations. Personal interviews were conducted with senior managers, and, where available, with the most senior data processing staff member. A total of 208 completed questionnaires were obtained. A profile of the respondents is shown in Table 1.

Despite the lack of a proper sampling frame, the sample appears to be fairly accurate in representing industry. For example, the percentage of joint venture firms, the industry breakdown, and the breakdown by year of establishment are very similar to official census figures.

## 4. Market Segments

In order to assess the nature of data processing activities, respondents were asked to specify whether their organization owned a mainframe and/or a microcomputer. Respondents were also asked to specify the year when each type of computer was first introduced.

Overall, 34% of the companies were not using computers, 14% were using only mainframes, 36% were using only microcomputers, and 16% were using both mainframes and microcomputers. In order to assess the changes over time, the data was analyzed by year of introduction of the computer into the organization. Results are presented in Table 2, and the trends are plotted in Fig. 1. Thus, cut of the total sample of 208 companies, 67 had been established before 1975. In 1975, 5% of these companies were using mainframes and none were using microcomputers. By 1982, the number of companies in the sample had increased to 185. Thirty-two percent were using a computer, 20% were using a mainframe, and 18% were using a microcomputer. During the period 1982–84 the number of companies using microcomputers surpassed those using a mainframe computer.

An analysis of the data reveals that companies owning both mainframe and microcomputers displayed characteristics very similar to those owning only mainframe computers, but this was different from those owning only microcomputers, probably due to the different level of investment and commitment necessary for using mainframe versus microcomputers. We therefore break the users into three groups: (i) those not using computers, (ii) those using microcomputers only, and (iii) those using mainframes and possibly also microcomputers.

In order to identify characteristics which differentiate between user groups, a step-wise discriminant analysis was performed. The variables which were found to discriminate most significantly between the groups are: (1) company size, as measured by number of employees, (2) company age, and (3) industry, as measured by its percentage in trading and services (rather than in manufacturing or government).

Mainframe users as compared to microcomputer users are more likely to have formal data processing departments and experienced computer professionals on their staff. Mainframe users also make higher use of in-house software development and of external software houses. Both mainframe and microcomputer users depend on off-the-shelf ready-made software. It was also found that the great majority (80%) depended on expatriate employees for managing the data processing operations.

Thus it seems that the micro users are implementing the same types of applications as are the mainframe users. However, their data processing is on a smaller scale, with less qualified staff and less formal organization structures.

## 6. Discussion

It is clear that the Saudi data processing environment has undergone a major change in the last few years. The decreasing cost and increasing power of microcomputers, together with the growth in the private sector, has created a large and growing market segment consisting of organizations that depend solely on microcomputers for their data processing needs. It appears that similar trends exist elsewhere. A study of small business organizations in the U.S. found that 60% owned microcomputers while only 22% owned mainframe computers [13]. A study of small business organizations in Taiwan found that among organizations using computers, 61% used microcomputers and the rest used mini- or mainframe computers [14].

Unfortunately the literature on the impacts of the microcomputer and end user information technology seems to have focussed exclusively on the large corporation [5.8,9,10,17]. Most of these studies have drawn samples from lists of large corporations or from lists of data processing managers who are likely to be found in larger organizations. In these organizations, the microcomputer has complemented, rather than substituted, the mainframe computer. The key concern is with the planning and management of end user computing, which is one, but not the only, job of the data processing manager. Similarly the literature on the management of data processing has focused on the large organization, i.e. those that have formal data processing departments and mainframe computers. There is certainly a need for research pertaining to the smaller organizations that depend solely on microcomputers for their data processing needs. For instance, we need to identify their problems and to identify the principles of data processing management that are applicable to them. It is also not clear whether, and at what point, these companies will need to make a transition to mainframe computing, and what will be the problems in making this transition. This research is important, not only for developing countries, but also for the U.S., where small businesses account for 97% of the total number of business firms, and for 43% of the gross national product [4].

Another result that emerges is that the micro-computer user segment is staffed mostly by non-computer professionals. This has implications for both computer vendors and educators. Computer vendors will be increasingly dealing with customers who have a minimal technical background. These customers will be depending on the vendors, not only for equipment and software, but also for help in setting up and managing the data processing operation. Academic institutions, in Saudi Arabia in particular, cannot supply the vast number of computer professionals needed by industry, at least not in the short run. The solution is for academic departments other than computer science, particularly management, to provide a certain minimal level of computer training, so that their graduates can partly fill the increasing data processing needs of industry.
