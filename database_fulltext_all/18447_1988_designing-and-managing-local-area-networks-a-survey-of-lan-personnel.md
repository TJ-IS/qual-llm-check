# Designing and Managing Local Area Networks: A Survey of LAN Personnel

Robert A. Schultheis and Robert Klepper
Management Information Systems Department, Southern Illinois
University, Edwardsville, Illinois 62026, USA

A survey was conducted of planners of 45 local area networks (LANS) of personal computers used for office work found in a large metropolitan area. Usable responses were obtained from the planners of 27 of the LANS, a response rate of 60%. The planners were asked to rank 79 LAN planning criteria and to identify problems they encountered in managing the LANS.

Twenty-one planning criteria were identified by planners as important or very important in the design of LANS. Five planning criteria were identified as unimportant by the planners. The planning criteria considered important varied between general purpose LANS and LANS primarily serving special applications. The planning criteria considered important also varied with LAN size.

Few problems were encountered by the planners in this survey and the amount of time the planners felt should be committed to managing the LANs was modest. Installation problems were the most frequently cited.

Keywords: Local area networks, LAN management, LANS, Office LANS, LAN design, Department LANS, LAN problems.

## 1. Introduction

Each of the last few years has been billed by one forecaster after another as "The Year of The LAN". While there has been no discontinuity in the growth of LAN installations that would signal that "The Year of The LAN" has arrived, the number of installations of local area networks (LANS) for office applications has increased steadily over the past few years [22]. Current market surveys indicate that the rate of acquisition of LANS will increase markedly in the near future [7]. Predictions for long-term growth in the installed base of LANS are almost universally sanguine [2].

The increased demand for LANs has been accompanied by an increase in the number of firms offering LANs, and this has led to greater diversity in the products and features available to personnel responsible for LAN planning, installation, and management. Thus, the difficulty of planning and designing local area networks has been compounded by the increased diversity of LAN offerings. The literature on LAN selection and management, much of which appears in the trade press, is conditioned by this diversity. The tone of the literature is cautionary with references to problems in LAN selection, installation and ongoing operations and management.

But what are the experiences of LAN designers, managers and users? Is the selection of LANS and their operation fraught with perils and difficulties, or has this technology been assimilated smoothly? There is, today a fairly large installed base of LANS [2]. Experience in the planning, selection, installation and management of LANS has grown. Surely the accumulated experience generated from this installed base can provide information useful for reducing the complexity of the LAN selection process and the burden of managing LANS in the future.

In fact, almost no research has been done on how LANS are actually selected and the problems that have been encountered with them. This study was motivated by the lack of systematically gathered evidence on experiences with LANS. What are the characteristics of the installed base of LANS? How are LANS being used? What factors guide LAN selection? What problems are most frequently encountered? The purpose of this study was to tap the experiences of those who have already installed LANS to provide helpful information to those who follow.

## 2. Related Literature

In the last three years, numerous articles have appeared on networks generally and local area networks specifically. However, there have been few articles which focus in a comprehensive way on LAN selection criteria, and only one of these is based on a research effort as opposed to the experiences of the authors. A common theme in the advice given on LAN selection is that technological issues should be secondary to user needs and the organizational objectives for which the LAN is planned [8,11,10]. The literature suggests many factors that may be used in the LAN selection process [3,8,15].

In one of the few studies of installed LANS, Datapro Research Corporation surveyed LAN users among subscribers of Data Communications magazine [2]. A total of 300 users were asked to rate their LANS in terms of nine categories of performance: ease of installation, ease of adding or removing devices, reliability, transmission speed, performance in heavy traffic, performance of software, vendor maintenance service, vendor technical support, and overall performance. The users were also asked why they selected the LAN they chose and which factors were most important in their selection. The most important selection criterion found was the features of the LAN. This factor was mentioned by more than half of the participants. Unfortunately, the survey did not ask participants to specify which features were most important in their section. Vendor recognition or vendor loyalty was the second most important selection factor, listed by 17 percent of the participants. Price appeared to play a small part in the selection process, being mentioned by only 10 percent of the respondents. The Datapro study concluded that the LAN users were basically satisfied with the LANs they had selected. In fact 82% of the respondents indicated that they would recommend their LANs to other users.

The literature pertaining to LANs suggests that LAN management issues are also very important. User training, maintenance, and security are aspects of LAN operations which are claimed to demand management attention if a LAN is to function smoothly.[19].

## 3. Purpose of this Study

While there has been much advice on LAN design and management, there have been few studies of actual experience with LANS. The purpose of this study is to investigate the characteristics of LANS installed in offices, to identify the criteria that guided their selection, and to inquire into the problems that were encountered in their installation and operation.

## 3.1. Definitions

Local area network. for the purposes of this study, a local area network is defined as a private communications network primarily composed of personal computer workstations located within a single room, floor, building, or campus [18]. The local area network must operate outside the regulated environment of federal and state regulatory agencies [4]. Furthermore, the primary use of the local area network must be for office, as opposed to factory, applications.

LAN personnel. LAN personnel are defined as those persons within organizations who are primarily responsible for the planning, installation, and/or management of local area networks.

## 4. Study Method

The LAN population for this study is comprised of personnel from organizations located in a large Midwestern metropolitan area. Persons in these organizations who belonged to either a local microcomputer managers association or a local information center managers' group were asked to identify the office LANS in their organizations and the persons responsible for their planning, installation and management. A questionnaire asking about the characteristics of installed LANS, LAN selection criteria and LAN management issues was prepared. The questionnaire was examined by a LAN special interest group from a chapter of the Microcomputer Managers Association and revised. The questionnaire was also pretested on LAN personnel in two organizations that were not part of the sample population. The questionnaire was then mailed to the LAN personnel responsible for the LANS identified in the target organizations.

The 79 planning criteria included in the questionnaire were assembled from a search of the literature. Planners were asked to rank these criteria in terms of their importance, using the IANS they had designed as their point of reference. The questions pertaining to LAN problems were open-ended questions in which participants listed and described the problems encountered in several problem categories.

## 5. Study Results

End-user support staff in 73 organizations were contacted in the second half of 1986. They reported the existence of 45 LANS in 25 of the 73 organizations. The persons who were primarily responsible for the planning, installation and management of these LANS were identified and a questionnaire was sent to each. Twenty-seven usable returns were received from 24 persons representing 18 organizations. The response rate represented 72% of the organizations with LANS and 60% by the LANS identified. It should be noted that this method of obtaining a sample undoubtedly resulted in omitting some LANS in the organizations surveyed. End-user support personnel may not know about all LANS, particularly in large organizations.

Repeated contacts were necessary to obtain responses from individuals in some organizations. The results of the study seemed invariant to the initial willingness of the survey participants to respond. Comparisons were made of data collected from persons responding promptly and persons responding only after several follow-up contacts. Those responding promptly reported data on 17 LANS and those responding after one or more follow-up contacts reported data on 10 LANS. There were no statistically significant differences (p ≤ 0.10) between the two groups when compared on 31 of the 32 LAN characteristics surveyed. These characteristics included media, topology, size, departments served, peripherals attached, and types of software used. Only one characteristic, a catch-all category called “other office software” was found to be significantly different. Had even greater effort been made to increase the response rate, it appears unlikely that the results of this study would have differed from those presented here.

## 5.1. Respondent Characteristics

The participating organizations represented a variety of industry groups including retailing, food and beverages, apparel, medical supplies, primary metals, accounting, financial services, education, and public utilities. The number of people employed by the participating organizations ranged from 300 to 63,000, with a median of 3,400 people. The organizations earned gross revenues ranging from \$900,000 to \$7 billion, with a mean of \$560 million.

The 24 survey respondents varied in the number of years of LAN experience they possessed. The range was from one to five years, with a mean of three years. The participants were asked to rate the computer literacy of LAN users at the time the LAN was installed on a scale of 1 to 5, with 1 being the lowest. The mean for the total survey group was 2.9; thus, the LAN planners rated users as being about average in computer literacy.

## 5.2. LAN Characteristics

LAN technology was generally new to the participating organizations. Of the 27 LANS included in the study, 15 (55.6%) were installed in 1986, 8 (29.6%) were installed in 1985, 4 (14.8%) were installed in 1984, and 1 (3.7%) was installed in 1983.

While a variety of LAN brand names were included in the survey, Novell local area network software was the most frequently encountered (40.7%) followed by IBM PC-Net (22.2%), IBM Token Ring (14.8%), Easy LAN (7.4%) and four others that were each less than 5% of the sample.

Two basic types of LAN media were represented in the survey sample, coaxial cable (37.0%) and twisted pair cabling (48.2%). One of the coaxial cable users also used fiber optic cable in their network. All major LAN topology types were represented with the bus being the most common (40.7% of the sample) followed by star (33.3%) and ring (22.2).

The workstations attached to the LANs in this survey were largely IBM personal computers, IBM XT'S, IBM AT'S or IBM clones. There were two instances in which non-MS-DOS or non-PC-DOS workstation were used: Macintosh computers attached to an Apple Talk network, and both IBM-PC's and Apple IIe's connected to a Nestar network.

The mean number of workstations attached to a single LAN was 13.3, with a range of 1–95 workstations. Because one LAN had 95 workstations, the median number of workstations per LAN -- five -- is a better indicator of central tendency. In addition to personal computers, a variety of other equipment was attached to the LANS or to the personal computers on the LANS. As one would expect, the predominant type of peripheral equipment was printers (see Table 1).

Of the 27 LANS included in the survey, 16 (59.3%) served a single department and 11 (40.7%) served more than one department. The application software installed on the LANS consisted of the major types of software typically used an personal computers in offices: data base software (48.2% of the sample), word processing software (44.4%), and spreadsheet software (37.0%). The most frequently cited motives for acquiring the LANs were to share data (55.6% of the sample) and peripherals (33.3%). Other motives were to implement E-mail (7.4%), share applications (7.4%), and experiment with a LAN (11.1%).

The respondents also rated some factors as “unimportant” or “not applicable” in designing the sample LANS. The responses to these two categories were grouped together under the title “unimportant”. Five factors were rated as “unimportant” or not applicable by the planners of two-thirds or more of the sample LANS. These factors are shown in Table 3.

Very few of the LAN applications in the sample required voice or video transmission capabilities or encryption capabilities. Thus, it is not surprising that these criteria were considered unimportant for the design of most of the LANS in this sample. Existing telephone cable plant and gateways to external networks were also rated unimportant in the design of the majority of the sample LANS.

The respondents rated the technical capabilities of the vendor more important than any other vendor characteristic. The quality of vendor maintenance and vendor financial viability were also important or very important selection criteria. LAN designers considered various types of vendor training to be the least important of the vendor characteristics studied.

Providing connections to mainframes was the only communications criterion which was given a high priority by a majority (51.9%) of the designers. Providing connections to other LANS was an important or very important consideration for 44.4% the LANS studied. Provision to connect to external networks or databases or to remote terminals were much less important.

Unlike the Datapro sample, planners rated a number of cost factors as important or very important for the LANS studied, particularly hardware and software costs. The cost of cabling was considered important or very important in the case of 51.9% of the LANS, use of available conduit was important in only 37.0% of the sample, and the ability to use current wiring for only 11% of the sample. Considering the importance given to the cost of cabling in the literature, the relatively low importance given to it in the survey sample suggests that this LAN criterion may have been overemphasized.

In general, security was not a great concern in the LAN applications in this sample. Only concurrency protection at the file level was considered important in more than 50% of the surveyed LANS. Three criteria: encryption, and record and field level concurrency protection were considered unimportant in the design of half or more of the LANS.

The survey included a number of resource sharing criteria, and the results bear out the general assertions on the importance of resource sharing in LAN design. The ability to share hard disks, data, programs and printers were all considered important LAN planning criteria. The sharing of communications peripherals, however, was not an important design consideration, receiving a rating of important or very important in only 6 (22.2%) of the LANS studied.

Locating printing services near users and providing immediate printing service were considered important or very important criteria by most of the LAN planners. Providing users with print results in one-half day or in one day were considered too slow by the planners of most LANs.

The planners were asked to rank a number of criteria pertaining to file transfer speed and file contents. Data and program file transfer speeds do not appear to be important planning criteria for most LANS. All but one speed criteria were rated as unimportant by most of the planners of the sampie LANS.

The content of the messages sent over a LAN is a major determinant of hardware and cabling selection. In fact, much of the baseband-broadband issue of years past has rested on the type of data which a LAN was required to handle. Numeric and text data message content were important design factors for a majority of the designers. Graphics content was important for fewer (48.2%) of the sample LANs. At the other extreme, voice and video messages were considered unimportant for 24 (88.9%) and 23 (85.2%) of the LANs, respectively.

The software capabilities of a LAN would appear to be important planning criteria, and the survey results bear this out. The ability to use an operating system with the same or similar commands to the one in use before LAN installation, the ability to run existing application software, and the design of the LAN to allow new application specific software to run were important or very important design criteria for half or more of the sample.

The ability of a LAN to utilize existing workstations and current printers and hard disks was important or very important for 50% or more of the LANS. However, the ability of the LAN to use existing boards and modems was considered unimportant for nearly two-thirds of the sample LANS.

The ability of the LAN to accommodate frequent moves was an important or very important design consideration for nearly half of the sample LANS. In the relatively clean office environments represented in this study, protection of the LAN from dust, dirt, and electromagnetic interference did not appear to be very urgent.

Ease of use and LAN speed were judged important or very important for most of the study LANS. Having a LAN manager, being able to isolate and identify LAN problems, and having the latest LAN technology were all important design criteria for a high percentage of the study LANS.

To gain perspective and summarize the design factor data, the eleven general categories of design criteria were examined for their relative importance in LAN design. The number of instances in which design criteria in each category were considered to be (1) very important or important (V/I), (2) somewhat important (S), and (3) unimportant or not applicable (U/N) were found and expressed as a percentage of all possible responses in each category. The results are presented in Table 4. The total percentages for any category may not equal 100% due to rounding.

The “general” category refers to a collection of criteria that is comprised of the ease of adding workstations and peripherals, the ease of use, speed of the LAN, problem isolation capabilities, the state of LAN technology, and LAN management. This collection of general criteria top the list in designer ratings. The general category of criteria, along with vendor criteria, software criteria, and resource sharing criteria were each rated very important or important for 60% or more of the LANS. Criteria pertaining to communications, printing services, and file transfer speed were much less important considerations, being rated as important or very important for only one-third or less of the LANS.

## 5.5. LAN Problems

Planners were also asked to describe the problems they encountered with the LANS they had planned and installed. Six categories of problems were identified (see Table 5).

Installation problems occurred in slightly more than half (52%) of the sample LANs, with 7 instances of hardware problems, 6 of cabling, and 4 of application software.

Just less than half, or 44%, of the study LANs had problems with the application software. However, these problems were all single occurrence problems that differed greatly in nature between LANs. The problems reported in all of the remaining categories were also single occurrence problems.

## 5.6. Results By Application Type

Data were also gathered on the predominant application or applications running on each survey LAN. On the basis of these applications, the LANS were divided into two broad groups: general purpose office LANS and LANS that served specific application needs. There were 10 LANS classified as “general application LANS”. These LANS ran word processing and spreadsheet software as the dominant applications. There were 17 LANS classified as “special application LANS”. Special application LANS were primarily dedicated to the support of a specific application; examples include inventory, scheduling, loan processing and servicing, and financial statement preparation. Special application LANS were larger than general application LANS and planners of these LANS appeared to be more concerned about such criteria as the size of data files, security, concurrency and certain vendor characteristics. LAN management was also a greater concern for the designers of special application LANS than for the designers of general application LANS.

There were twice as many workstations configured on the special application LANs as there were on the general application LANs; 17 workstations and 8 workstations, respectively. Three of the special application LANs (18%) were provided with tape backup for LAN data while no general application LANs had tape backup facilities.

The ability to process medium and large data files and the ability to process medium and large program files were each reported to be important or very important criteria in 10 special application LANS (59%), but were so reported in only 4 general application LANS (40%).

Security and data protection were more important considerations in the design of special application LANS that in the design of general application LANS. File password protection, LAN access protection and file directory access protection were important or very important design considerations for 59%, 53%, and 47%, respectively, for the special application LANS versus 30%, 40%, and 30%, respectively, for the general application LANS. The ability to lockout users at the record or field level was an important or very important design consideration for 35% and 29%, respectively, of the special application LANS as opposed to 10% and 10%, respectively, of the general LANS.

Concurrent use of a LAN was a more critical design issue for special application LANs than for general application LANs. The ability to handle one to five concurrent users was an important or very important design consideration for three-fifths of the LANs in both application groups. However, the ability to handle six to ten concurrent users was important or very important in the design of 7 of the special application LANs (41%) as compared to only one general application LAN (10%).

There were some differences in concerns about vendors between the two groups. Designers of special application LANS considered it important or very important that the vendor offer up-to-date technology in 13 (76%) of the cases in the sample versus only 4 (40%) of the general application LANS. However, the designers of both types of LANS shared concerns on vendor technical and financial resources. Vendor technical capability was a high priority design consideration for 15 (88%) of the special application LANS and all (100%) of the general application LANS. Also, vendor financial viability was an important or very important concern to the designers of 70% of the general application LANS and 88% of the special application LANS. Vendor start-up training was much more important in the design of special application LANS than general application LANS. Start-up training was an important or very important design consideration for ten of the special application LANS (59%) in the sample and only three of the general application LANS (30%).

That managerial duties for LAN operations be clearly defined and delegated was an important or very important design consideration for 15 of 17 special application LANS (88%) in the sample. However, in only 5, or 50% of the general application LANS was this criterion considered important or very important.

The proportion of respondents reporting installation or operational problems with the sample LANS was twice as high for special application LANS as for general application LANS. Installation problems were experienced in eleven of 17 special application LANS (64%) versus 3 general application LANS (30%). Seven special application LANS (41%) incurred operational problems versus only two general application LANs (20%). While there were differences in the incidence of problems between the two groups, there were no recognizable patterns in the nature of these problems.

The time required for on-going LAN management appears to be higher for special application LANS than general application LANS. The number of usable observations on the time required to start-up and operate the sample LANS was small, but the results suggest that there were no real difference in the time required to set up and start the general application and special application LANS. However, designers of special application LANS reported the equivalent of a half-time person on average for their ongoing management and maintenance versus only an hour a week, on average, for the general application LANS. The dedicated nature of the special application LANS and the need to keep these LANS operating reliably probably accounts for the observed differences between the two sample groups.

## 5.7. Results By Number of Workstations

The sample LANs varied considerably in the number of workstations attached to them. The data were analyzed to detect design considerations or other LAN characteristics that varied with the number of workstations. For this purpose the sample was divided into two groups: LANs with fewer than 10 workstations, and those with ten or more workstations. To facilitate discussion, LANs with less than 10 workstations are called “small” LANs. There were 17 small LANs in the sample. LANs with ten or more workstations are termed “large” LANs. There were 10 large LANs in the sample. The mean number of workstations in the small LAN group was 3.6, and the mean for the large LAN group was nearly 30.

The number of workstations is related in some fairly obvious ways to certain aspects of LAN design. For example:

1. As one might expect, large LANS were designed to handle greater data volume than small LANS. Fifty per cent of large LANS utilized coaxial cable versus only 12% of small LANS.

2. About 40% of small LANs had two or more printers while fully 70% of large LANs had multiple printers.

3. Small LANS were more frequently standalone installations than large LANS. Four small LANS (23%) had gateways to a mainframe and none had bridges to other LANS. Five large LANS (50%) had gateways and 2 (20%) had bridges.

4. Small LANS were also more department specific. Only four small LANS (24%) served more than one department while 7 large LANS (70%) served two or more departments.

5. With more workstations there is the possibility of more workstation moves, and the ability to move workstations was an important or very important design consideration for seven (70%) of the large LANs, but only six (35%) of small LANs.

There were no substantial differences in the general office applications running on the LANS of different sizes. Seventy per cent of the large LANS and 60% of small LANS were special application LANS.

The designers of large LANS were more concerned about vendors and technology. Having up-to-date technology was deemed important or very important in the design of nine large LANS (90%), versus only 4 (24%) of the small LANS. A financially viable vendor and a technically competent vendor were each important or very important in the design of all (100%) of the large LANS in the sample. Vendor technical capability was also important for the design of nearly 90% of small LANS. However, vendor financial viability was important or very important for a lesser proportion (11, or 65%) of small LANS. Large LANS represent a larger investment than small LANS. There is more at stake, and perhaps this accounts for the greater concern for the latest technology and the strength of the vendor.

Large LANS with more components and interrelationships between components might be expected to experience more problems, and they did. Large LANS had a higher incidence of reported installation and operational problems: 70% of large LANS experienced installation problem versus little more than 40% of small LANS. Also, the designers of 50% of the large LANS reported operational problems versus 24% of the small LANS. However, there was no pattern to the reported problems and no specific aspects of LAN components or component interrelationships that generally presented more problems in large LANS than in small LANS.

There were only a few usable observations from this survey on the time spent in LAN set-up and operation; usable data were collected on seven small LANS and 5 large LANS. The results conform to one's expectations: there is a need to commit greater managerial time to large LANS than small LANS. Large LANS took, on average, more than 3 times as many person-days to set-up than small LANS and nearly the equivalent of a half-time person to manage and operate. Small LANS took an average of only 2 hours per week for managerial oversight.

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

The LANS surveyed were predominantly new, small in numbers of attached workstations, general in purpose, not connected to other LANS or to mainframes, and served a single department. LANS primarily dedicated to serving a special application had a higher mean number of workstations and were more frequently connected to mainframes or other LANS than general application LANS.

The dominant motive for installing LANS of all application types and all sizes was to provide file and peripheral sharing. The devices attached to the LANS were primarily workstations and printers. Database, spreadsheet, and word processing were the primary categories of software used on the LANS. The special application LANS ran software dedicated to their specialized functions.

The criteria considered important or very important for the design of special application LANS varied from those for general application LANS. The designers of special application LANS showed greater concern for the processing of medium and large files, medium and large program files, security and concurrency protection, up-to-date technology, vendor start-up training, and the management of LANS than the designers of general application LANS.

The criteria considered important or very important for the design of large LANS varied in some ways from those for the design of small LANS. The planners of large LANS considered the ability to move workstations much more important than the planners of small LANS. Large LAN planners were also more concerned with having up-to-date technology and a financially viable vendor than the planners of small LANS.

In general, the designers surveyed in this study found few problems with the LANs in their organizations. Large LANs users and those using special application LANs experienced more installation and operational problems than users of small LANs and general purpose LANs. The need for LAN management was expressed less by LAN designers than expected. Large LANs were found to require more management at startup and during operations than small LANs.

Overall, the adoption of LAN technology appears to be an orderly process. The problems encountered are relatively few in number and the burden of LAN management is modest.

## 7. Recommendations

The study results suggest several tactics which can be used to improve the success of LAN design, and implementation:

1. Choose a LAN which provides strong vendor support, including maintenance and technical capabilities, especially during the initial installation of equipment and application software.

2. Experiment with a general purpose LAN to gain experience before pursuing special purpose LANs and their applications. Let program, data, and peripheral sharing be the primary LAN functions initially, and experiment with concurrency controls.

3. Choose a LAN which is designed for the software you expect to use on the LAN, which provides some means of isolating and identifying LAN problems, which supports text and numeric processing, and which supports at least file level concurrency protection.

4. Keep LAN hardware and software costs down by choosing a LAN that can utilize existing software or network versions of current application software, existing workstations, and existing peripherals.

5. Focus on users by choosing a LAN which is easy to use, is fast, and can run software using the command sets of software which users already know. Locate peripherals close to users.

6. Choose a LAN that is flexible, allowing quick and inexpensive changes in the location of workstations and peripherals.

7. Assign responsibility for the management of the LAN to a LAN user to provide a single contact person when trouble occurs and to insure that effective LAN procedures are followed.
