# Key Human Resource Issues in IS in the 1990s: Views of IS Executives versus Human Resource Executives

J. Daniel Couger

College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado at Colorado Springs, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

Key human resource (HR) issues in IS for the 1990s were identified by separate Delphi studies of IS executives and human resource executives in Fortune 500 firms. Three rounds of survey of participants were used to move opinions toward consensus. For IS executives, acquiring a stronger business orientation was the top ranked issue. For HR executives, better HR planning was the top ranked issue. Six issues were ranked in the top ten by both groups: however ranking differed considerably. The article discusses survey methodology, analyzes results, and provides conclusions.

Keywords: Delphi technique, Human resources, IS management.

## 1. Introduction

The personnel staff functions for IS organizations are typically handled in one of three ways:

(1) by persons within the company Human Resource Management (HRM) organization, responsible for multiple organizations,

(2) by persons solely responsible for IS but reporting to the HRM director, or

(3) by HRM persons reporting within the 15 organization.

A Delphi survey was conducted of HR executives in categories two and three during the summer and fall of 1986.

The purpose of the study was to:

(1) identify the ten most important issues in management of HR in the 1990s,

(2) determine the relative importance of these issues,

(3) ascertain the degree of agreement about these issues.

The Delphi technique was used to collect the information and to combine the opinions of the HR executives who participated. The study was patterned after a 1986 Delphi survey of IS executives [1]. This paper compares the results of the two surveys.

Why is it important to compare these views? There are two principal reasons:

(1) HRM is supposed to be a support organization, aiding other departments in improving the quality of HRM in those departments. Increases ingly, IS management believes that it is not receiving proper support from the HRM organization, as evidenced by the number of IS executives who have acquired their own HRM personnel. However, this paper is not directed at determining the degree of disparity between the views of IS executives and HRM executives in category one above. Instead, the emphasis was upon organizations were HRM personnel either work directly for IS or are assigned full time to IS (categories two and three). Therefore, the following is the more important reason for comparing views of IS and HRM executives.

(2) HRM personnel, because of training and experience in their speciality, could be expected to have a perspective quite different from IS management. This different perspective may provide a special insight on HR problems that will need attention in the 1990s. It is of value, therefore, to obtain the opinions of HRM personnel whose speciality is application of their discipline to IS.

## 2. The Delphi Methodology

The Delphi methodology focuses on two aspects: identifying the key factors and attaining a consensus about their relative importance.

In round one each participant was asked to identify the six most important issues in HRM for IS in the 1990s. Each respondent was asked to provide a rationale for including each issue and also asked to rank them. Responses were then consolidated into a comprehensive list, according to mean ranking.

In round two, the consolidated list was mailed to the first round respondents. They were asked to evaluate their original rankings against the consolidated list of issues and rationale and then to rank their top (20) issues. This process enabled participants to re-evaluate their opinions in the light of the additional information provided by their peers. The result was a movement toward consensus. A new aggregate ranking was developed.

In round three, participants received the aggregated list of the top 20 issues. They were asked to provide a final ranking of these issues, after comparing their previous rankings with the aggregated list; they were asked to tank their top 10 issues and give the remaining issues a rank of zero. The results were combined and analyzed.

Fifty-seven IS executive responses were received in round one and 43 in round three. Twenty-one HR executives participated in round one and 21 in round three. All participants were from Fortune 500 firms.

## 3.4. Median Score Ranking

The rank scores contributed by the respondents were required to be whole numbers and all ten ranks had to be assigned. This approach does not allow a participant to say, for example, that the first rank issue is much more important that the second, and that the second is only slightly more important than the third.

Medians and interquartile ranges are more suitable measures of central tendency and variance, respectively. Medians also provide the advantage of not being as affected by extreme values as arithmetic means. An analysis was done to see if using median rank scores changed the final issue ranking.

Tables 1 and 2 provide the median rank scores and the quartile rank scores for the last round of the final group. Note that the same issue that made up the mean rank score top 10 are included in the median rank score top 10 for both IS and HR executives. The order also did not change for either group. The major apparent difference in using the median scores for rankings is in the relative importance. For IS executives, issue one received a median ranking of 10, while the next closest issues (two and three) had median rankings of seven. For HR executives, issue one had a median ranking of 10 while the next closest issues (two and three) had median rankings of eight.

To illustrate convergence, the 6th ranked issue (emphasizing creativity and innovation) was listed in only 8.2 percent of the responses to round one (Appendix A3). ## 3.5.2. HR Executives

Convergence is illustrated best by the 4th ranked item (increasing productivity/motivation). It was listed in only 26 percent of the responses in round one (Appendix A4). In the final round it was listed in 94.7 percent of the responses. The issue with the least amount of change from round one to round three (issue one: better human resource planning) was listed by only 74 percent of respondents in round one. The Delphi process produced major shifts in views for both IS and HR executives.

## 3.6. Measures of Dispersion

The measures of central tendency (means and medians) and the percentages do not show the degree of ranking agreement. To find out more, we must examine measures of dispersion for the rank scores. Two measures were considered: (1) standard deviations, and (2) interquartile ranges. These measures provide a more precise way to show rank score consensus. A decreasing standard deviation indicates a decrease in the difference, i.e., between participants' score and the group's average score. With perfect consensus, the standard deviation is 0 and successive rounds showed a definite movement in that direction. For IS executives, the major agreement was in issue six (emphasizing creativity) and the least in issue one (acquiring a stronger business orientation). For HR executives, the most agreement was in issue four (increasing productivity and motivation) and the least in issue seven (upgrading recruitment/selection techniques).

The second measure of dispersion is the interquartile range. This is the difference between the third quartile (3Q) rank score and the first quartile (1Q) rank score. For example, for IS executives, the third round 3Q rank score for issue one is 10 (Table 1). Its first quartile score is 8. The interquartile range is therefore 2 (10 minus 8). Perfect consensus is an interquartile range of 0. Issues two and four also had ranges of 2. The issue with the widest range was retraining personnel, with six. As with the standard deviations, there is a movement toward perfect consensus. Again, in the final round there was most agreement about the ranking of issues one, two, and four and least about issue three (retaining personnel). For HR executives (Table 2), the same results occurred for issue one (better human resource planning) which had an interquartile range of 2 (10 minus 8). There was a tie for the issue with the widest range. Issues seven, eight, and nine all had an interquartile range of five.

Substantial consensus was achieved; it should not be assumed, however, that further rounds would have led to more consensus. The consensus demonstrated here should be sufficient to make the ranging credible.

The preceding analysis and discussion is based on the group that completed all of the ranking rounds. The author believes that this is the appropriate group for analysis, because it eliminates changes in rankings that are due solely to participants dropping out of the study. The larger groups, however, still played a part in the study. It was the ranking of issues based on the larger groups' mean scores that determined the order of presentation of the issues and rationales in the next round. It was also this ranking that was the basis for feedback to the respondents on how they compared with the groups' ranking.

## 5. Conclusion

It is comforting to learn that HRM executives, whose primary assignment is with the IS organization, have considerable agreement with IS executives on the ten key HR issues for the 1990s. The mapping of responses showed that IS and HRM executives agree on six of the top 10 issues.

On the other hand, it is important to recognize the differences between the two groups. Some of the issues identified by HRM executives need more attention by IS management. For example, the issue “improving leadership and management skills”, the second ranked issue for HRM executives. IS managers may be “wearing blinders”, unaware of how they compare in these skills to other managers in the firm. HRM executives are trained to assess these skills and have a broader perspective through seeing them applied in more than one organization.

It is reassuring to see that HRM executives recognize some of their own shortcomings. Their second ranked issues was “updating recruitment/selection techniques”. IS executives did not list that issue among their top 20 issues, indicating either that they are satisfied with the present approach or that they are unaware of how much this approach should be improved, or both.

Only one other issue listed in the top 10 by HRM executives was omitted from te top 20 by IS executives: "improving approach to career path planning". That result is surprising, in light of continually changing factors in the field that should dictate reevaluating career paths. Just one example would appear to justify this issue being among the top 10 for IS executives—the shift of resources resulting from the explosive growth of end-user computing. A number of IS personnel need to be looking at career paths in user organizations that are developing their own systems.

One issue, listed by IS executives, provides an excellent statement of policy for this topic area – “use a holistic approach to people management”. A plan that integrates the issues revealed by these surveys will enable holistic people management. If issues are viewed separately, the efforts to implement the key human resource issues would be expected to accomplish far less than an integrated, collective approach which keeps holism as its central focus.

ISSUE: Reward principal contributors.

RATIONALE: The need to acquire people qualified in technical specialities and to develop dual career paths for specialists.

RATIONALE: The need to develop an accepted set of professional standards and encourage employee adherence.

ISSUE: Manage more effectively, contractors and contractor employees.

RATIONALE: The need to motivate and manage contract personnel and make more effective their interaction with company personnel.

ISSUE: Better mental health provision.

RATIONALE: The need to develop programs for effectively handling drug/alcohol abuse, emotional/mental problems, etc.

RATIONALE: The need to develop programs and train managers to recognize symptoms of stress/burnout to prevent their occurrence.

RATIONALE: The need to plan for managing "cottage" workers and personnel in off-site locations.
