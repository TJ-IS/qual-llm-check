# Local Area Networks: The Network Managers' Perspective

Ravinder Nath

Department of MIS & Decision Sciences, Fogelman College of Business & Economics, Memphis State University, Memphis, Tennessee 38152, USA

With the proliferation of microcomputers and a need to interconnect them, local area networks (LANs) are gaining popularity. Issues facing LAN managers have not yet been adequately investigated due to the recent emergence of this technology and lack of prior experience. This paper investigates current practice in the introduction and management of LANs. Thirteen LAN installations were studied. The manager in charge of each was interviewed and asked the same set of predetermined questions in a structured approach. Each manager was then asked to complete a questionnaire on their perception of the many facets of the network. The interview and questionnaire elicited answers to managers' perceptions of the benefits and drawbacks of the LAN, its perceived impact on users, justifying LANs to management, and the significant issues facing network managers in implementing LANs. The results and implications for the LAN manager are discussed.

Keywords: Local area networks, Management of local area networks, LAN managers.

## 1. Introduction

"I am really pleased with it. If I go somewhere else, I would really want to push to get something like this. I think it has got tremendous advantage," said the controller of a manufacturing company. The manager of the mortgage department of a large bank added, "I love it. I wish we had one years ago. It has made our lives much easier." They are praising local area networks (LANs). As personal computer (PC) usage increases, so does the need for their interconnection. This is achieved by virtue of a LAN, which is defined as: "a system of communications equipment, including computers, printers and data storage devices linked together in a network to serve a community of users confined to a small geographical area," [7]. According to Business Week [3], the number of LANs installed worldwide in 1987 was expected to hit 220,000, up from 52,000 in 1984. This represents a phenomenal increase in a period of about three years. As the LANs span a wide spectrum of organizational functional areas and their numbers increase, so does the need to better understand and manage them. In [6], the job description and duties of a LAN manager are articulated along with the prerequisite training requirements; these suggested requirements are not based upon any empirical research, but rather on subjective observation.

The future of end-user computing hinges upon the ability of the functional manager to manage end-user computing resources efficiently, and LANs are becoming a vital and integral part of these. The area of end-user computing has been extensively investigated [1,2,4,8]. But, not much has been attempted in determining current practice of LAN use and network manager's perceptions of its: (1) benefits and drawbacks, (2) potential impact on the user and the organization, (3) management and support issues, and (4) justification to management.

## 2. Methodology

In order to capture LAN managers' perceptions concerning these issues, a study was conducted of thirteen LAN installations. Data for this study was collected in the summer of 1987. A two-pronged approach was undertaken to collect the necessary data. First, each network manager was personally interviewed and the interview was tape-recorded. All managers were asked the same questions. A list of the questions asked during the interview session is given in Table 1. Second, each manager was asked to fill out a questionnaire, consisting of three parts.

(1) LAN Environment. This elicited information about the environment in which the network is functioning. Information were collected on the department, functional area served by the network, type of applications run, number of buildings covered, whether or not the LAN is connected to any other network, etc.

(2) LAN Characteristics. This was designed to collect information on features of the LAN, such as: whether the LAN is baseband or broadband, the network topology, transmission medium, types of devices attached, and the number of network users.

(3) Perceptions of the LAN. This measured respondents' perceptions and attitudes towards the LAN. Their perceptions of the user's productivity, attitude, and participation, along with the issues of data security and accessibility, network reliabil-

## 3. Research Findings

The findings will be presented using two modes. Relevant quotes of LAN managers pertaining to specific issues will be introduced whenever necessary.

## 3.1. LAN Applications

Each manager was given a list of activities which could be performed using the LAN. They were requested to check only those activities which they performed using the LAN. The leading activities (see Fig. 1) according to their descending frequency count are: word processing, data entry, file transfer, transaction processing, inquiry/response, spreadsheets, graphics, and electronic mail.

Most LANs have a data base capability on their central server. The users added, deleted, modified, and/or generated reports from the data base, depending upon their authorization status. Therefore, the activities: transaction processing, inquiry/response, and data entry appear with high frequency. Ninety percent of the LAN managers reported word processing as one of the LAN activities. Since no data were collected on the intensities of these activities, it is difficult to ascertain which activity dominated the network usage.

## 3.2. Benefits

Benefits of the LAN, as perceived by the LAN managers, fell into the following categories:

1. Extension of PC Technology - Many managers felt that, since they already have a number of PCs in their organization, the next natural thing to do was to tie them together. According to one LAN manager, "...we needed to use PC technology and needed multiuser capability and LAN was the obvious way to do that." "We had PCs and needed to share information between PCs," said the LAN manager of a large manufacturing company.

2. Resource Sharing – Ninty-three percent of the managers agreed that the LAN allows sharing of peripherals, data, and disk storage space in an efficient manner. “We have a significant amount of data in this department: a lot of people (users) need it for different reasons. So a network provided a perfect solution,” said a netowrk manager for a manufacturing company. “…there was a need to share data,” said another.

3. Simplicity - There was almost a consensus that a LAN system is easier to maintain and manage than a minicomputer or a mainframe. "It is a real simple system to maintain and very simple to train people on, and it is real efficient," said one manager of an accounting department in charge of the LAN.

4. Cost Effectiveness - Some managers perceived a LAN as more cost effective than a minicomputer for the kind of applications they were interested in. "(LAN is) extremely cost effective on a per user basis and essentially it was to replace a minicomputer," said the manager of the funds management division in a large bank.

5. Transparency – A significant advantage of the network turned out to be its transparency, i.e., most users were not even aware of the presence of the LAN. Comments by one manager really reflect this: "employees have a knowledge of the standalone software packages and beyond that, the LAN is basically transparent to them." Another manager added, “we have written our applications in such a fashion that we insulate the user from the network totally.”

## 3.3. Drawbacks

In spite of the numerous benefits, managers saw some areas of concern. Prominent among these are:

1. Security – The comments by a manager of an auditing agency bear out the fact that adequate hardware and data protection schemes are indeed needed when installing a local area network. "In a LAN having a single storage unit, the danger of fire or electric storm leading to a loss of data is the main problem."

2. Lack of Networkable Software – Many LAN managers were genuinely concerned with the lack of PC software capable of running in a LAN multiuser environment. "Some software packages are not networkable," commented one manager of a large property development company.

3. Need for a LAN Technician – Most LAN facilities in this study have a maintenance contract with an outside agency to deal with problems in the LAN. Nevertheless, comments from many managers suggested the need for having an in-house LAN technician, if the network is moderately large and/or complex. “(We)...need to designate a LAN technician,” said one manager. Commenting on the potential drawbacks, another manager commented, “we need someone to maintain the system adequately and professionally.”

## 3.4. Advice to Other LAN Managers

Each LAN manager was asked to advise other LAN managers based upon his/her own experiences. Responses to this question yielded the following advice:

1. Designate a Person in Charge of Maintaining the LAN - Contrary to some people's perception, a LAN does require maintenance. One manager said: "... designate a person to be in charge of the LAN and allocate a good bit of that person's time, possibly all of it, (to the network) depending upon how big the network is." Some managers mentioned the necessity of having a person in charge of training network users. In essence, the consensus seemed to be towards establishing a resource person capable of providing users with help in the area of network maintenance, training, keeping the data base "clean", and managing passwords and security features of the network.

2. Select the Right Software – From the interviews it became apparent that many are dissatisfied with the lack of availability and the poor quality of networkable software packages. "I will advise the manager if he/she is going to use a DBMS, to be very careful in picking the right DBMS, because none of them that I know of handles multiuser (environment) in a flawless way, as they come off the shelf." This situation is likely to improve as vendors bring out newer multiuser versions of software packages.

3. Plan – “Spend a lot of time initially studying your people. Find out the type of work they are doing,” said the manager in charge of a large LAN in an accounting department. This manager’s advice actually applies to all information systems projects, whether installing a new computer system or developing an application. So planning again turns out to be important.

4. Maintain Control over the LAN – To protect data and information from unauthorized users, it is necessary to establish sound security features in the LAN system. A manager of the engineering department of a health care facility stated, “maintain good control over the network. Don’t allow the users to get too smart as far as the system architecture goes.” These comments were reflective of his concerns that some user find out the security system of the network and thus intentionally or unintentionally contaminating the data.

## 3.5. Justification of the LAN

None of the managers in our sample performed a formal cost-benefit analysis to justify purchasing a LAN. They found it almost impossible to quantify the "soft" benefits and thus considered a formal cost-benefit analysis a waste of time. "There are no numbers out there to adhere to," said one LAN manager. This points to the fact that LANs are a new phenomena and there is a lack of experiential data.

Justification for the manager of a mortgage department was easy as evidenced by her comments: “(justification) was easy. We do some specialized management reports. It was taking 10 people at least 40 man hours twice a month to prepare them. Now it takes us 30 minutes.” The manager of an auditing agency said, "it hopefully makes us more efficient."

## 3.6. Hurdles and Stumbling Blocks

When each manager was asked the question, "what do you see as the most significant hurdle keeping your department/organization from reaping all the benefits of the LAN?", responses were diverse and revealing. Upon examining the interview data, four areas of concern emerged: lack of networkable software, opposition from MIS/DP department, noncompatibility of equipment, and lack of network related training.

The lack of networkable PC software has been discussed before. The lack of support or direct opposition to local area networking by the MIS/DP department was considered to be a major stumbling block by many LAN managers. The comments of a manager in a major distribution company, where centralized computing has been the rule for many years, reflect this: "it has taken two years to get these kinds of people (DP) to realize that PC has a place (within the organization). We are finally beginning to win these people over."

Equipment diversity, in terms of incompatibility, was cited as another major hurdle. Devices manufactured by one vendor may not interface with a LAN manufactured by another without significant cost for interfacing devices. This suggested an urgent need for vendors to adopt the same hardware and network software standards.

Lack of training was another problem area for many managers. They felt uncomfortable in relying totally on vendors and outside consultants for advice and counseling on LAN related activities. They felt a strong need to strengthen their knowledge base in technical and managerial issues relating to local area networking. This, they thought, would allow them to conceptualize and manage the evolving LAN technology better. One manager's comments summarize the dilemma: "(one hurdle is) my lack of training. I do not know what it (the LAN) can do. There is a lot that I don't understand and I do not really have the time to get into it." These comments are revealing, considering that most managers in charge of LANs do not have a technical background, unlike their counterparts in MIS or DP today. Perhaps better training programs should be designed for functional area managers.

## 4. Summary and Conclusions

It appears that the local area networks are still in their infancy. Many companies are experimenting with LANs and trying to find ways which will lead to a better understanding of them. Here we have attempted to provide potential LAN managers with guidelines in managing the LAN. The research has significant implications. Specifically, a LAN manager must pay attention to the following issues if he/she wants to have a successful LAN:

\- Pay special attention to the applications software; many LAN managers have found that most commonly-used stand-alone environment software packages are not adequate for a multi-user LAN environment.

\- Designate a person in charge of the LAN. Depending upon its size and complexity, there is a need for a full- or part-time LAN technician. This person will be responsible for trouble shooting network hardware, software, and data bases.

\- Be security conscious. Invest in equipment to back up important data and files to retain it during a catastrophic accident, whether natural (lightening, floods, earthquake, etc.) or man made (tampering, theft, fire, etc.). Build sufficient safeguards in the system to protect data from unauthorized use. Also, introduce audit-trails whenever necessary.

\- Educate yourself. The vendor o. outside help may not be available. Many managers do not even know what the network can or cannot do. Such ignorance can lead to missed opportunities in developing better and innovative applications to improve the bottom line of the enterprise.
