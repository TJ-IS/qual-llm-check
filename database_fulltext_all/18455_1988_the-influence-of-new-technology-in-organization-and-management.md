# The Influence of New Technology in Organization and Management

Nikitas Assimakopoulos

Organization Division, Agricultural Bank of Greece, Athens, Greece

New technology influences managers in the way thus obtain, analyse, and transmit information. Our experimental study showed that important changes occur in the areas of information used for decision making and associated ideas about the kinds of problems can be solved, the locus of decision making authority, and managerial job characteristics. Analysis of interviews and case examples are given.

Keywords: Communications, Computers, Decision, Information, Management, Organization, Practice, Service, Systems.

## 1. Introduction

Recent technology improvements have made changes in computer usage. Perhaps the clearest evidence for this is seen in the growth of “end user computing”, that is, the use of computing equipment by people who will actually use the computer generated information to further some aspect of their work. In a study of “seven major organizations”, Rockart and Flannery [16] found end user computing to be growing at rates of 50% to 90% per year. They cite another study showing that in some companies, end user computing now utilizes 40% to 50% of the companies’ computing resources. Concurrently, sales of personal computers (inherently oriented toward the end user) have grown at a phenomenal rate, from \$5.7 billion in 1981, to \$15.6 billion in 1983, and a projected \$30.2 billion in 1987, with most of those sales going to business [8].

Why such growth? Because along with personal use come critical advantages over traditional information technologies. Speed: Access to information is no longer subject to the delays inherent in a centralized system that must serve an entire organization. Flexibility: Unique applications become feasible because they do not require the time of an organization's always overworked programming experts. Innovation: Prepared software is inexpensive and new programs can be tested without threatening the schedules or operations of complex mainframe systems. Cost: It is economically feasible to distribute these new technologies widely within organizations. Finally, there is the all important factor of ease of use. Unlike traditional computer systems, the new information technologies do not require users to have extensive computer training.

The capabilities of these new technologies have been widely discussed in both the popular and professional press. Among the most prominent are information transfer capabilities (including electronic mail) that speed the movement of information between individuals; information retrieval capabilities that allow people to find specific pieces of information in large data bases; personal processing (including word processing and data analysis); and electronic activity management that increases the ability to manage time, project and personnel [14].

Given the rapid change in computer technology it is not surprising that there as been relatively little research on the personal and organizational impacts of the new computers. Olson and Lucas [12] have attempted to set a research agenda in this area. They propose 18 types of changes that could result from the new technologies. Ten of these changes refer to organization wide impacts: e.g., "Automated office systems, especially communications functions, can affect the number of sociometric links, and the volume of communications upward in the hierarchy". Four of the changes refer to impacts on clerical workers: e.g., "Automated office systems can affect the role identification and stress of office workers, especially secretarial and clerical workers". Finally, four refer to possible impacts on managers: e.g., "Automated office systems, especially communication functions and personal applications, can affect managers' perceptions of the degree of rationality, flexibility, and free space of their work". They do not, however, present any data to support these propositions.

Some research has been attempted in a few areas where impacts might be expected. Both Zuboff [21] and Walton and Vittori [13] have provided case study evidence for “de-skilling” of clerical and professional jobs. Zuboff shows, for example, how the job of pilot of a modern airliner has changed from one requiring considerable physical and intellectual skill to one in which the pilot primarily monitors a computer. She also shows how a bill collector’s job changed from one requiring great personal independence and skill to one that was almost totally routine and monitored by computer. Walton and Vittori give similar case examples from the insurance and telephone industries.

Gutek, Bikson and Mankin [4] used questionnaires and interviews to study the impact of office automation in 55 offices. They found evidence of improved office productivity, but little evidence of any actual reductions in labor costs. Their respondents also reported improvements in the quality of their work and of their work lives.

Finally, in another recent study, Carter [2] used questionnaires to assess the impact of computers on a sample of newspaper organizations. Her complex set of findings concern interactions between organizational size and use of information technology. For example, she found that greater use of the technology was associated with more centralized decision making in large organizations, while it was associated with more decentralized decision making in small organizations. She also found that functional diversification, in the form of new personnel, was associated with higher levels of technology use, regardless of organizational size.

One of the most under-researched aspects of the new information technologies is their impact on the work life of managers. This lack is surprising because these new technologies are particularly well-suited to provide information relevant to one of the most important aspects of managerial life - decision making. Historically, this matter has been of considerable interest to researchers. During the 1960s and 1970s, a great deal was written about the potential impacts of Management Information Systems (MIS) and Decision Support Systems (DSS) [7]. While many wonders were predicted for these systems, a widespread view is that a substantial number of them were failures (Lucas [6], Mintzberg [9]).

It is crucial to realize that many of the faults of those older systems may be corrected by the new technologies. For example, Mintzberg [10] and Targett [17] discuss the weakness of MiS. First, there are limitations on the formal system, that is, the data are not sufficiently “rich”: too much non-qualitative information is missing. This can be remedied at least partially through modern electronic mail and more sophisticated data gathering and data management techniques. There is also the tendency to “over-aggregate” information. This can be remedied in a modern system by allowing a manager access to data at whatever level is needed, not just a single preprogrammed level. Information is often too late. A modern system that can provide “instant” access via a desktop terminal is much more timely than periodic reports from a distant data processing unit. Finally, Mintzberg notes that some formal information is unreliable. Will this problem be exacerbated by a technology that encourages the development of “private” data bases? Or will the problem be ameliorated because more people will be using, and thus scrutinizing, data from existing archives? With the exception of this last issue, it seems reasonable to assume that the new technologies will lessen the difficulties cited by Mintzberg.

This negative view of older information systems is tempered by research that indicates that they had some positive consequences. As an example, Vergin [18] discusses several changes that he observed in 11 firms using management information systems in the 1960s. Prominent among these were decision making changes. In particular, he cites the ability to obtain data that were previously impossible or uneconomical to obtain, the ability to coordinate and integrate previously independent organizational units, the development of a broader view of the firm, and the development of computerized models to aid decision making. A second area Vergin discusses is a definite trend toward centralization in decision making. Finally, he discusses the increased importance of the data processing unit in the firm that results from the increased importance of its function.

In sum, we see that information technology does affect managerial behavior, that its promise has not been fulfilled, and that new information technology may solve some of the problems inherent in older systems. We also find a dearth of specific research on the managerial consequences of those new computer technologies. With these issues in mind and our experience from a recent research of Problem Structuring in a hospital [13], we designed an exploratory study to assess how new information technology might affect managerial behavior. Before discussing our methodology, however, it is necessary to articulate the specific model of managerial behavior on which we based our efforts.

Managers lead hectic work lives and are continually involved in decision making. An organizational systems view of this process is described in Mintzberg, Reisinghani and Theoret [11] and Assimakopoulos [1]. They characterize decision making in organizations as including three central phases (identification, development and selection); three sets of supporting routines (decision recognition, search design, selection routines); and six sets of dynamic factors (e.g., scheduling delays, failure recycles). The systems they describe do not have fixed stages. Rather they are a set of elements subject to change, sensitive to new information, and rich with feedback loops. Revision and recommittment is continuous.

A complementary individual-oriented view of decision making is presented by Weiss [20] in her studies of the use of research data by managers. Her view is that managers are continually making "small" decisions on a day to day basis, that each decision is constrained by the ones that went before, and that policy is largely determined by the direction of those day to day decisions. Information affects the process mostly through its impact on the manager's perspective during each small decision making activity.

Another important influence on our research perspective is the view that managers lead hectic lives with many competing demands. Mintzberg [9] reports on several studies that indicate that managers rarely have as much as a half hour of uninterrupted time, and that their activities (reading reports, meetings, telephone conversations, etc.) tend to be extremely short-on the order of 2 to 15 minutes duration. Within this busy life managers must perform a balancing act among the often competing demands of being a good supervisor, meeting the needs of their superiors, helping the company, and advancing their careers.

In sum, our view of managerial life is one of continual decision making that is taking place within a fast paced context of competing demands. New computer technology seems particularly well-suited to helping managers who lead this kind of a work life. It is a technology that can produce specific detailed information, quickly, and on an as-needed basis.

Surprisingly, there is skepticism about whether the time saved by the new technology can be used to good advantage. Curley and Pyburn [3] describe cases in which decisions were made to not provide computer access to managerial and professional workers because of a belief that time saved would not result in any advantage to the company. The belief in these companies was that freezing up time for managers would simply give them more time “to play golf”. On the other hand, Quilland, Rockart, Wilde, Vernon and Mack [15] have documented a strong belief among managers that computers can be an extremely useful aid to their work. In their study of corporate use of personal computers, enthusiasm for the technology was widespread.

Do computers change how managers behave? Does more time and more information translate into better decision making? Does any improvement in decision making result in tangible advantage? These are all important questions about which there is little data.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Sample

Twenty-two middle managers in eight large well-established organizations constituted the sample for this study. Smaller organizations were not included because of our belief that size differences could make for a very different dynamic of computer use, due to differences in factors such as role distribution and communication patterns, with one exception all organizations were located in Athens, Greece. Out of the 22 managers 12 personally used computer equipment, while the remainders had a staff person to do the "hands-on" work. No attempt was made to separate "direct" users from "indirect" users of the equipment. This was because of our belief that the critical variable is not who touches the machine, but rather who controls requests for information and access to data. Twelve of the 22 managers used personal computers, 12 used electronic mail, 16 used a mainframe computer via a work station in the office, two used a minicomputer through a workstation, and three made personal use of dedicated word processors. The median number of computer applications (e.g., spreadsheet, data base access) used was two packages. In terms of their computer training, most (13) received some form of informal training, such as counselling from a friend in the office showing them what to do. Thirteen received some type of formal training from their company, nine had read books as a form of training, five had taken outside courses, and only four received any training from a vendor. (Many respondents reported more than a single type of training). Comments about training varied greatly, with generally positive feelings expressed about company provided training, and very negative statements about equipment documentation and vendor support.

## 2.2. Procedure

Instrument development. A questionnaire was used for all data that could be put into a forced choice, or very short answer, format. Personal interviews were used only for questions that demanded more open-ended responses. Questions were based on decision making as a primary focus, and in the belief that decision making must be understood in terms of a more general organizational context. It also seemed essential to learn how (indeed IF!) our sample of managers used their computer equipment and precisely what hardware and software they had.

A preliminary set of questions was developed and tested on a sample of nine vendors of office automation equipment. The reason for this choice was the assumption that vendors know the people to whom they are selling quite well and could give us some insight into how managers were using their equipment. Unfortunately this reasoning was mistaken. Aside from a general sense that our questions were reasonable the vendors were not very informative. The vendors did, however, make quite clear the lack of training available to users of computer equipment. Questions about training were therefore added to the questionnaire.

Interviews. Each of the 22 members of the managerial sample was interviewed at his/her work site using an open-ended interview schedule. The schedule consisted of 18 substantive questions. Table 1 gives a list of topics covered. While the interview schedule was followed closely, the interviewers were free to probe and ask additional questions as needed to clarify points and get respondents to give examples. We conducted all the interviews; they took from 60 and 90 minutes to complete. Each interview was tape recorded and transcribed.

The interview transcripts were used in a two-step process. First, we read all transcripts. This yielded both a sense of which questions were successful in eliciting useful information, and a set of rather striking examples of particularly successful uses of new computer technologies. The second step was the identification of eight issues which the data addressed most effectively. A trained person rated each of the eight issues for each respondent. Each set of responses was checked by us. All disagreements were resolved through discussion.

## 3. Results

Other than the question about who actually uses the equipment, the largest percentages of affirmative responses were found for the effects of computers on the types of information used, decentralization of decision making, and changes in characteristics of the respondent's job.

## 3.1. Changes in Information Used

The most dramatic impact was a change in the managers' sense of the kinds of problems they can solve. Several respondents reported that their new equipment gave them an ability to take on totally new tasks. These were important jobs that greatly benefited their companies, but simply could not be done without the new technology. Case 1 illustrates this quite clearly.

Case 1 - Overfilling in the food processing industry. A major problem for the food processing industry is the necessity of overfilling containers. A company making tomato sauce, for example, must make sure that its 12-ounce jars contain at least 12 ounces of product. Due to inevitable variations in filling equipment, the company must calibrate its machinery to overfill. At a company we visited the fact of overfilling was recognized but considered to be an unimportant problem, which in any case, could not be remedied. In fact, there were no accurate estimates of the costs of overfilling because making such estimates would have been useless from a practical standpoint. By the time appropriate data were assembled and the calculations made, different production runs would be in process, and machinery might have been modified for other purposes. When the manager of "operations analysis and financial control" got a personal computer in his office, he realized that he would look at the costs of overfilling and did so, almost as a trial use of his machine. He found that the costs were much higher than he or anyone had imagined. This led to a new procedure to monitor overfilling by means of an automated production monitoring procedure. Once obtained, the information was quickly distributed to relevant people via electronic mail and facsimile technology. The company has saved a considerable amount of money from this new process. The computer transformed the impossible into the possible, and changed people's beliefs about how business should be conducted.

A second type of impact is that some managers changed their standards for the minimum amount of information they require to make decisions. Once they realised what is possible to be done by a computer, they became unwilling to settle for less information as new directions for decision making have opened. Case 2 illustrates the point.

Case 2 – Information requirements in the insurance industry. In the insurance industry there is a concept called the “combined operating ratio”, which involves the relationship between a company’s premium income and its profitability. In theory this figure should be calculated frequently for various product lines (auto, commercial, etc.) and continually used for planning. In a company we studied the combined operating ratio was not used in this manner because appropriate data were not routinely available. The use of a personal computer allowed managers to begin making more frequent combined ratio calculations. They believed it was important to do so and acted accordingly, both for their own purposes and in reports to their superiors. The superiors also believed such calculations were important and were pleased to receive the new information. They now expect to see combined ratio information frequently and have come to rely on it. In essence, the computer has helped cause a fundamental shift in beliefs as to what information is essential.

## 3.2. Locus of Decision Making

In this sample the advent of the new computers was generally associated with some decentralization of decision making. It appears that new computer technology makes adequate decision making information available at lower organizational levels, while leaving higher levels with the ability to monitor those decisions. Thus, in a sense, the total effectiveness of the organization is increased, with more decision making information available at lower levels, but with no accompanying decrease at higher ones. Case 3 illustrates this.

Case 3 - Branch office decision making in the insurance industry. The insurance company we studied was in the midst of an effort to increase responsiveness to customers by giving branch offices more underwriting authority (i.e., the right to take on new insurance policies). An important element of this effort was the use of computers to process information and keep the home office informed of business transactions while at the same time giving the branch offices sufficient computing power to enable them to make decisions. But while decentralization was the norm, we did find several examples of centralization. The same insurance company also enhanced its centralized strategic planning efforts. Motivated by the availability of relevant information, a group of executives formed an ad-hoc strategic planning group where none had existed before. This can be interpreted as a centralization move because prior to the implementation of the new information processing equipment, such decisions were either distributed throughout the organization, or simply not made at all.

## 3.3. Changing Managerial Job Characteristics

A number of managers discussed specific changes in their jobs that had, at least partly, resulted from the new technologies. In some of these, the manager was able to delegate what were basically routine analytic tasks to their staffs, leaving more time for such “managerial” activities as decision making and thinking. Typically this was a situation in which the manager has been understaffed and was forced to take on clerical and professional tasks. This is similar to a finding [14] that knowledge workers spend a large percentage of their time on support tasks which detract them from more creative and productive work.

Two other cases, while perhaps unique, suggest some of the more dramatic changes that could occur. In one of them, a manager and his unit became widely known throughout the bureaucratic machine in order to improve their ability to use one of the government's mainframes computers for interactive data analysis and graphics. People from other departments began to ask him to help with their analysis problems. When he did this his unit charged the other units for this service. His unit now occupies a significant portion of its previously underutilized time "selling" its expertise internally to other departments.

The other interesting case is that of the manager in the food processing case. A combination of access to interactive mainframe computer capacity and personal computers allowed his and his unit to move from analyzing reports sent from factories to developing computer programs that factories use to monitor themselves.

## 4. Discussion

The interviews reported in this paper suggest that the new technology can affect people's beliefs about the minimal acceptable information needed to make decisions. It has been widely recognized that people seldom have the time or will (not to mention the cognitive capacity) to always make an optimal decision. Rather, they usually compromise, that is, satisfice. The extent to which one does not optimize can vary greatly, depending on the importance of the decision and the cost of obtaining relevant information. The computer's greatest power is that it can lower the cost (in terms of time, money, and effort) of obtaining information. Thus, one might expect profound changes in the type and amount of information that is considered "good enough" for solving a problem. No single shift in beliefs about a discrete piece of information may be dramatic, but the cumulative effect of those changes on an organization could be considerable. A related issue is the matter of beliefs about the types of problems which can be addressed. Computers are likely to change not only beliefs about information needed to solve old problems, but also beliefs about what problems can be solved. This dynamic can also have long range consequences for organizations.

The literature seems to suggest that computers can have both decentralizing and centralizing effects on decision making. The interviews reported here suggest that the preponderant, but not exclusive, effect is to decentralize. It is also clear that both effects can take place simultaneously in different parts of the same organization. The critical question is: What circumstances lead to centralization or decentralization? Several of the managers in this study suggested that computers enhanced already existing trends in the organization. If so, what is the nature of the interaction between the capabilities of computer technology and other forces for organizational change?

Our data indicate that managers often do different tasks as a result of access to computer technology. But what happens to the work they did before? Do they do it in addition to their new tasks? Does some one else do it? Is it automated? It seems reasonable to assume that all of these possibilities may occur in varying combinations. Over and above the implications for individual managers is the cumulative impact of these changes on their organizations. Research must address the impact of those changes on organizational structure, goal setting processes and productivity.

## 5. Closing remarks

While changes brought by computer technologies will affect organizations in a multitude of ways, it is important to recognize that a number of variables will mediate their impact.

The work of Kanter [5] suggests that there will be great individual variability in how managers exploit their new ability to obtain and analyze information. Her research was aimed at understanding what differentiates creative from uncreative middle managers. She found that creative managers act in a particularly entrepreneurial mode. Among other things they are particularly important using information to analyze problems or build a case for their position. Following this lead, we believe that the effects of computers may well differ for creative and noncreative managers.

Similarly, computers will have a differential impact on different organizations, or on a different sub-units within organizations. The research of Gutek, Bikson and Mankin [4] argues convincingly against a “technological determinist” view of computers. This new technology will not inevitably bring about well defined changes in all organizations in which it is implemented. We also know that as a function of personality, management structure and organizational culture, organizations differ widely in their capacity to adjust to change.
