# Company-Wide Documentation Planning \*

John H. Major

Academic Computing Services, University of Wisconsin-Eau Claire, Eau Claire, WI 54702-4004, USA

A reference manual is not a user's manual. A tutorial is not a recipe. And none of these is a standard operating procedure. Each has its own function and design, and all have a place in a company's set of documentation. Research has shown that workers must glean, from mounds of printed material, crucial information specific to their tasks and that they must do this quickly to meet task-related deadlines. Companies must provide their workers with the information they need, when they need it, and at a level they can comprehend. This can be done through a company-wide documentation plan that properly designs and implements recipes, tutorials, standard operating procedures, reference manuals, and user's manuals. Such a plan has met with success for the internal documentation needs of a computer center with over 70 employees, most of them part-time, and for the external documentation needs of a clientele numbering well over 5000.

Keywords: Documentation, Planning, Technical writing, Tutorials, Reference manuals, User's manuals, Standard operating procedures, Taxonomy, Audience analysis, Business writing.

## 1. Introduction

Writing and editing technical manuals is what I do for a living... and I knew they were full of errors, ambiguities, omissions and information so completely screwed up you had to read them six times to make any sense out of them... These were spectator manuals. [19, pp. 34–35]

If one is to write, one must believe – in the truth and worth of the scrawl, in the ability of the reader to receive and decode the message. [25, p. 77]

Researching the writing of readable texts for the adult worker touches on such topics as reading comprehension [3,15,16,18,24], audience analysis [1,7,21,22], information retrieval [9,12], reading strategies [2,4,8,14,20,23], adult education [5,13], and typography [10,11].

"Job success depends in part on the ability to absorb crucial information from large amounts of print quickly. Productive use of limited time requires the workplace reader to employ a conscious process for reading" [17, p. 234]. Much research [6,15,22], dating from the late 1940's, has been completed on measuring the readability of text by objective formulas. These formulas use sentence length, number of syllables per word, and dictionaries of familiar words, but do not define the actual features of texts which make them easy or hard to read [6].

Thomas Sticht, in his chapter on “Understanding Readers and Their Uses of Text,” indicates two classes of reading tasks: (1) the reading-to-do reading task, in which information is looked up, used and forgotten, and (2) the reading-to-learn task, in which information is extracted and retained to be drawn upon at a later time. Writing text for the reading-to-do class therefore needs to concentrate on different reader skills than writing text for the reading-to-learn class. He suggests that “students’ experiences with textbooks and learning in school may lead to the development of school-like, topic-oriented manuals, when in fact, workers want job-related, performance-oriented guidance” [22].

Can we provide our workers - technicians, managers, and clerks -- with the information they desire when they desire it and at the level they desire it? Can we keep them from becoming bogged down in information they do not want? Can we keep them informed on the topics they do want? With a well-designed documentation plan, we can.

First we must agree on a taxonomy for documentation, so we know what it is we are talking about. Then we need a plan to implement the concepts suggested by the taxonomy.

## 2. Taxonomy

What is a recipe? a tutorial? a standard operating procedure? a reference manual? a user's manual? Are they related one to another or are they separate and distinct documentation types? Perhaps these are just interchangeable terms for the same thing.

## 2.1. Recipe

A recipe is a short, step-by-step, procedural, "how to" handout written for personnel who need to perform a specific task NOW.

The structure of the recipe is step-by-step: begin at step 1 and continue until the last step. The reader of the recipe is defined as inexperienced;

however, the reader may, or may not, be experienced with other procedures. In other words, we, as writers, have no idea of the readers' general background, for example, in the area of computing: can they log on? can they create files? etc. We do know that they are reading to do. Another problem we face is where to begin our procedure: do we detail the log-on sequence? do we explain the need for some sort of user ID, etc.? And how do we define (should we define?) the specific tasks readers want to perform in order not to include unnecessary options? Perhaps some of the answers to these questions can be found in the definition of another term.

## 2.2. Tutorial

A tutorial is a teaching tool, hardcopy or online, written for personnel inexperienced with a given topic who want to learn about that topic in order to do something LATER.

As with the recipe, we don't know what to assume about the background knowledge of the readers in the audience; we can, however, assume ignorance of the topic and focus on taking the reader from the known (some sort of an analogy) to the unknown (a basic understanding of the topic). We do not detail every option; we, instead, provide a foundation upon which the reader can build (through practice and the use of reference manuals). This journey then dictates the structure of the tutorial, concept by concept, simple to complex. The readers are reading to learn. They should go through the tutorial, which supplies specific examples, once or twice to become familiar with the subject and then put the tutorial aside. Because of the journey-like structure, tutorials make poor reference manuals; however, they make wonderful training aids.

## 2.3. Reference Manual

A reference manual is the definitive statement on a given subject, detailing all the minute options available, written for experienced personnel who want to perform a specific subtask of generally a much larger job NOW.

Reference manuals should be written for the reader who is experienced with the subject of the manual as well as its prerequisites. Whereas the tutorial is structured to lay a foundation and then build upon that foundation, becoming increasingly complex, the reference manual is designed for ready access to the specific detail being sought. Reference manuals are not by definition 1...o...n...g. It is quite possible that the tutorial will be longer than the reference manual, since the tutorial must provide explanations that the reference manual does not. The tutorial is organized by concept, the reference manual perhaps alphabetically. The reader of a reference manual reads to do as opposed to the reader of the tutorial who reads to learn. The key to success is the reader's ability to "absorb crucial information...quickly."

## 2.4. Standard Operating Procedure

A standard operating procedure is a step-by-step, procedural, when to guide written for the technician.

Just as the recipe is a how to guide to performing a task, the standard operating procedure (SOP) is a when to guide to performing a series of tasks. Rather than standing along and taking the reader from the known to the unknown like the tutorial, standard operating procedures are grouped together and take the reader from 7:00 am the first day of the year to midnight of the last day of the year, often referencing recipes to explain the how tos. SOPs also handle the processing of those impromptu demands that always arise by "scheduling" them at certain times and referencing available recipes for more details.

## 2.5. User's Manual

A user's manual is a summary of all related resources, such as available services, software and hardware, written for all personnel who might use those resources. It serves as a system of pointers to more detailed information.

Since the user's manual points to all available documentation and guides the reader through a general background first, we, as writers, can begin to make some well-grounded assumptions about the level of experience our readers have when they reach a given page. They have either read the previous material, or chosen not to. We can assume they are familiar with it. For example, the University of Wisconsin-Eau Claire Academic Computing Services User's Manual has a one page summary of batch processing fairly early in the manual, referring to a "Tutorial on Batch Processing" and an "Execution Report Reference" as well as several vendor manuals. From this point on, it is assumed that the reader is familiar with batch processing and that no further explanation need be given. We can now reference these resources as additional information or as prerequisites in any documentation dealing with batch applications. The user's manual provides a common thread running through all the other supporting documentation, as indicated in the presence to the Academic Computing Services User's Manual:

This user's manual is general in nature, designed to introduce you to academic computing resources at UW-Eau Claire. It contains summary descriptions of all serivces, software, and hardware, as well as maps, sample forms, and the location of resources. Specific details on particular resources are covered in additional documentation all of which is cited.

## 3. The Plan

Recipes, tutorials, standard operating procedures, and user's manuals.... Must all procedures and topics be supported by all the different forms? Does this plan mean four or five times as much documentation? How does it all come together?

## 3.1. Inventorying Available Documentation

Every company has, at the very minimum, documentation in the form of recipes. Some of these were formally produced, others are scattered about the company on scraps of paper or stashed away in notebooks or posted on bulletin boards. They may be called notes, tutorials, user's manuals, or by some other name. The first step of the plan inventories the documentation that exists and categorizes it according to the preceding taxonomy.

## 3.2. Designing the User's Manual

The second step of this company-wide documentation plan is designing the user's manual. The user's manual should define both the scope and sequence of the documentation plan. Because it is the set of necessary pointers to further information, it should be the first place personnel go for information. It is not enough to document information, it is necessary to provide access to it – not only access to the documentation, but also access to the specific information within the documentation. A well-planned user's manual is a big step in gaining access to that needed information. The user's manual should briefly describe each topic being covered and then reference additional documentation. Some of this documentation will already exist (and be inventoried, see step one). However, some of this documentation will, for the first time, be identified as necessary. Regardless of whether it exists yet or not, it should be referenced from the user's manual. Step three, "Supporting Documentation," covers the non-existent documentation.

The top heading for the user's manual should contain the name of the chapter printed on the outer edge of the page, far right on right pages and far left for left pages. All other documentation (recipes, tutorials, standard operating procedures, and reference manuals) supporting this chapter should contain the same heading. In this way, all supporting documentation dovetails with the user's manual. In addition, supporting documentation has a second level heading, printed directly below the chapter name, consisting of the title of the specific document.

## 3.3. Writing Supporting Documentation

The user's manual should reference not only existing documentation, as inventoried in step one of this plan, but also needed documentation that has yet to be written. This third, and final, phase of the plan covers the creation of documentation as identified in step two and the revision of existing documentation to meet the standards of the taxonomy and to ensure consistency with the newly developed documentation.

Whereas the design of the user's manual defines the scope and sequence of the documentation, this step concerns the detail of documentation. Supporting documentation must be written for the appropriate personnel and in the appropriate format according to the taxonomy above; short, step-by-step recipes for inexperienced personnel who want to perform a specific procedure; tutorials for the personnel who want to learn about a given topic; step-by-step, "when to" standard operating procedures for the technician; and reference manuals, detailing all the available options. for the experienced personnel.

## 4. Conclusion

For this plan to work, company personnel must feel that their documentation needs are being met. Therefore, the appropriate documentation must be available to the personnel that need it.

\- All personnel should be sent a copy of the user's manual. Since it points to all services, equipment, and documentation related to a given resource, the user's manual should be the focus of all training and consulting. Training sessions should begin with the summary of the topic found in the user's manual, making reference to the manual at every available opportunity.

\- It is equally important that the documentation referenced by the user's manual be accurate and appropriate for the reader. Support personnel should refer to it often when consulting. This will help build personnel confidence in the user's manual as a set of pointers to available documentation as well as in the specific documentation that it references.

\- Documentation must be kept up-to-date. As soon as something changes in the supporting documentation, that change should be announced through an update to the user's manual.

Adopting a new taxonomy in and of itself is not enough to successfully fulfill the documentation needs of a company. However, adopting a company-wide documentation plan founded upon a taxonomy that takes into consideration the needs of all company personnel goes a long way toward fulfilling that goal.
