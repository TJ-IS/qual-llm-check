# An Empirical Investigation of DSS Usage and the User's Perception of DSS Training

Peter P. Mykytyn, Jr.

Department of Information Systems and Management Sciences, University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, Texas 76019, USA

Financial analysts, financial managers, and other finance specialists participated in a laboratory study to examine the perceived value of specific DSS training with DSS usage, the decision process, and task structure. Measures included the frequency of DSS features used, the number of syntax errors made, comfort with and the use of a computer terminal to improve decision effectiveness, and the use of a specific DSS to enhance decision making. Forty-nine participants received DSS training, were randomly assigned to a structured or semi-structured task, and conducted analyses. Results showed that DSS users see a strong positive correlation between their training and the use of a DSS to improve decision making. Significant relationships were also noted between perceptions of training and task structure differences.

Keywords: DSS, DSS training, Task structure, User perceptions, User performance.

## 1. Introduction

Watson and Hill [33] defined a decision support system (DSS) as an interactive computer-based system which provides a user with easy access to decision models and data to support semi-structured and unstructured tasks. Often, however, a DSS is identified by its characteristics. These include flexibility and adaptability to change in the environment and decision making approaches of users, ease of use by non-computer personnel, and use of models and traditional data access methods [31].

These characteristics may be important to a decision maker if the DSS is to be useful and judged successful. A DSS will not be deemed a success in the eyes of the user, DSS designer, or senior organizational management if it is not used effectively to support decision making. Alavi [1] noted that decision makers recognized the benefit of DSS but were concerned about ease of use. Although DSS design can affect its ease of use, the quality of end user training may also be important. If the training is poor, or perceived as poor, or is not appropriate for the type of task faced by the decision maker, the overall success of that DSS and of future DSS efforts could be affected.

Proper training in the use of the specific DSS is essential [19]. A specific DSS allows a decision maker to deal with a particular problem or set of related problems. A DSS generator, on the other hand, provides a capability to develop a specific DSS quickly and easily [31]. Both Alter [2] and Ginzberg [9] have stressed the importance of training in the effective use of DSS, emphasizing its importance to job-related tasks or decisions. Fuerst and Cheney [8] also found that training is an important consideration in determining DSS utilization, and Chervany [6] noted that training expe rience could impact the decision making process.

Two training-related issues, addressed by this research have not been reported. The first is the issue of the user's perception of training in the work-related environment. That is, once the decision maker has received training and used a specific DSS in decision making, what is the perceived value of the training? If training is not well received, the actual use of the DSS may suffer, the user's perception of its value in the decision making process may be lower, and, ultimately, the task may not be completed successfully. Second, and perhaps more important, how does task structure affect perception of the training or use of the specific DSS or its components; e.g. the terminal. It may be necessary for organizations to incorporate training methods consistent with specific task structures.

## 2. Background for Study

## 2.1. Task Structure

Task structure can be defined by the degree of judgement exercised by a decision maker [15]. In general, most problems can be classified into one of three categories. At one extreme are programmed or structured decisions or tasks [26]. They are ordinary, recurring, have definite procedures established for dealing with them, algorithmic in nature, and thus easily programmed for a computer. Nonprogrammed decisions or tasks occupy the opposite end of the spectrum. These are the unstructured tasks, being novel and eventful; compared to structured tasks, they lack clearly defined procedures. Such tasks often demand significant judgement on the part of the decision maker, even in identifying the exact nature of the problem; in contrast, structured tasks require little judgement. Keen and Scott Morton classified task structure along a third dimension. They defined semi-structured tasks as those which are programmable and algorithmic on the one hand while necessitating decision maker judgement and analysis on the other.

Several researchers have indicated that a DSS is applicable in semi-structured tasks or decisions [10,13,20], due to both DSS capabilities and the process of decision making. A DSS provides support by combining modeling and analysis flexibility with appropriate judgment to arrive at a solution. Task structure is considered an important factor in decision making and has been the subject of much research [3,18,21,27,29]. It is also an issue in both theoretical and practical applications of DSS design and its usage [17,24].

## 2.2. DSS Training

The effective training of end users in the use of information systems has received considerable attention. Many researchers have concluded that instruction, affecting the use of the system, should therefore be given to all persons associated with the DSS [7,22,34]. Clearly, an end user must understand the system and be able to guide and interact with it; this will probably include understanding the system's purpose, entering and manipulating data, and knowing how to correct mistakes. In typical computer-based applications, such as inventory control, end users are primarily concerned with data entry and report generation. With DSS, end users can develop their own applications using a DSS generator and conduct analyses to support decision making using a specific DSS.

DSS training may therefore include instruction on both DSS generators and specific DSS. DSS generator training would emphasize syntax and its general capabilities, procedures for the development of specific DSS, and instruction related to the purposes and ways of employing the analysis features of the generator. Training on a specific DSS would provide decision makers with the knowledge for a particular problem or application. Rockart and Treacy [23] found that managers enjoy the ability to view, change, and manipulate data in ways that are meaningful to them; further, respondents to a survey found that many of these features were easy to learn and well received [19]. A corollary to this is that many systems fail because of inadequate training.

Another aspect of training is its effect on decision making. Chervany noted that training experience may influence the way a manager would make a decision. Sloan [28] reported that one organization commits resources, including time and money, to training and considers the evaluation of those training results as extremely important. This is consistent with Van Zwieten's [32] observations, which stress that a manager's success depends increasingly on the analysis of data to make effective decisions. It would therefore follow that any effective use of DSS is predicated on knowledge and ability – a direct result of effective training and, perhaps, the perception of that training.

Different training methodologies exist, including: seminars with participant interaction, lectures without participant interaction, workshop with different degrees of hands on training, and computer-aided instruction. One study [12] examined how decision maker performance was affected by training and user cognitive style. If decision makers perceive the quality of DSS training differently based on different task structures, then task context training may be essential. Ginzberg indicated that task context training is necessary to provide the user with knowledge to produce and use that output in the context of a given job function.

## 3. Research Methodology

The purposes of this study were to evaluate DSS training from the perspective of DSS usage and perception by decision makers faced with either a structured or semi-structured task. Due to its exploratory nature, no research hypotheses were formulated. Rather, if significant differences were found, it would suggest that additional research examining types of training for different tasks or situations may be necessary.

## 3.1. Operational Definitions

In this study, a decision maker was faced with analyzing a financially-oriented structured or semi-structured task using a specific DSS. Two dependent measures assessed decision maker performance: the total number of DSS features used (FEATURES) and the total number of syntax errors made using the specific DSS (ERRORS). Several variables also measured a decision maker's perception of training and the specific DSS: comfort with using the computer terminal (COMFORT), use of the terminal to improve decision effectiveness (TERMINAL USAGE), sufficiency of general FCS-EPS training (GENERAL TRAINING), sufficiency of task-related FCS-EPS training (TASK-RELATED TRAINING), and use of the specific DSS to enhance decision effectiveness (MODEL USAGE). FCS-EPS is the DSS generator which was used in this study.

The first dependent variable is frequency of use of DSS features (FEATURES). Fuerst and Cheney found that system usage is an appropriate dependent variable. Is there any relationship between the analysis capabilities of a specific DSS used by a decision maker and the perception of training received on those features? Further, is this relationship affected by task structure? If a relationship exists, the training method may not be appropriate. In this study, a feature was defined as the correct use of any of the six analysis commands of FCS-EPS: sensitivity, step, scan, row, target, and impact.

The second dependent variable was the total number of syntax errors (ERRORS) made while using the specific DSS. No research has been reported on the relationship between errors committed and the users' perception of training. However, it might be expected that a DSS user who makes mistakes would perceive the training to be inappropriate and poor. This could affect subsequent acceptance and use of the specific DSS, the DSS generator, or other DSS efforts. Of course, as proficiency increased with continued usage, the number of errors would be expected to decline. However, a decision maker may never reach that point, due to lack of DSS acceptance and thus its rejection. The number of errors was operationally defined to be the sum of all syntax errors made when attempting to use the analysis commands.

The measures of perception – COMFORT, TERMINAL USAGE, MODEL USAGE, GENERAL TRAINING, and TASK-RELATED TRAINING – were determined by administering a post test questionnaire to each participant after completion of the experimental session. The instrument (see Appendix) attempts to determine the participant's perception of the areas through a set of questions using a Likert-type scale. COMFORT, TERMINAL USAGE and MODEL USAGE deal with the specific DSS and its peripheral, which is an integral component of the support system. It would be inappropriate for DSS trainers, user managers, or other responsible individuals to emphasize one component at the expense of others. Since task structure is an integral part of this research, it was considered necessary to separate a user's perception into general aspects of FCS-EPS, GENERAL TRAINING, and TASK-RELATED TRAINING.

## 3.2. Participants

A total of 56 finance managers, financial analysts, controllers, and other finance specialists were identified as potential subjects for this study and randomly assigned to one of two finance tasks. All were screened to ensure: computer/terminal familiarity, academic and/or job related experience in finance, and no prior experience with FCS-EPS. Screening of potential subjects was necessary to ensure as much homogeneity as possible, because Kasper and Cerveny [14] found that previous computer/terminal familiarity was a significant factor in the success of initial end user applications.

A total of 49 subjects actually completed the training and experimental session with unexpected meetings and illness accounting for seven subjects not able to participate. The average age of all participants was 33.6 years with a range of 25 to 50. They had an average of almost three years experience in their current positions, and the average time employed with their current organizations exceeded five and one-half years. All subjects had received at least a bachelor's degree, either in accounting or finance; several had received a master's degree.

## 3.3. Tasks and DSS Models

Klein [16] noted that financial modeling is a common application of DSS in industry. Therefore, two financially oriented tasks were selected for the research. The first dealt with short-term cash forecasting and was identified as a structured/management control task within the Gorry and Scott Morton framework. The second task was concerned with long-term financing (stocks or bonds) and was identified as a semi-structured/management control task. Each task was selected from a finance casebook [25] and further adapted through suggestions of finance faculty and senior students enrolled in a capstone finance case course, two pilot studies, and detailed analysis of another finance expert.

## 3.4. Pilot Studies

Two pilot studies were conducted to strengthen internal validity. As a result of suggestions from participants in the initial session, some procedural changes were instituted and incorporated in the second. After this session, no new changes were made.

## 3.5. Training Session

A training session consisting of $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours of classroom instruction and one hour of hands on training was provided to all participants. The classroom instruction consisted of presentations on DSS concepts, computer logon, terminal access to existing FCS-EPS models, and its analysis/managerial features. Each participant was provided with a training manual and other materials that aided in the explanation, purpose, and use of the six analysis features. Familiarity with these features and the different ways in which they could be employed were reinforced by examples. Questions about each feature, other aspects of FCS-EPS, or the training were encouraged. This portion of the training compares favorably in length and content with that provided by the vendors of FCS-EPS.

The second session consisted of completing ten requirements based on a sample FCS-EPS specific

DSS using the analysis features presented during classroom training. Participants were provided with the requirements and correct output; however, the correct analysis feature was not provided. Participants were expected to format the analysis command on their terminals to achieve the correct output.

The use of the training manual with its numerous examples is similar to Grace's Interactive Training Manual (ITM) [11], intended to provide instruction in operating a specific DSS and using it to solve specific problems. In this portion of the training, participants constructed the analysis command(s) necessary to achieve the correct output for each requirement; then they compared their output displayed on the CRT with that presented as correct. Thus, participants developed familiarity in the use of the language by iteratively working with the sample specific DSS and each requirement. They also became familiar with the CRT, the keyboard, and its functions. This latter exposure was important since, even though they were experienced with computers and terminals in general, they may not have been familiar with the particular CRT and keyboard used in the study. Questions concerning correct syntax, appropriate commands, etc. were encouraged during this portion of the training. In this manner, participants practiced all of the features of FCS-EPS and became familiar with them prior to the experimental session, minimizing learning effects during that session. To ensure maximum integrity for the actual experimental session, the sample DSS used during the hands on session was in no way similar or related to the actual cases or specific DSS.

## 3.6. Experimental Session

The experimental session was conducted in a computer terminal room reserved for the study. Each person received the $3\frac{1}{2}$ hours training during the morning prior to conducting the afternoon experiment.

To begin the session, verbal instructions were read from a script. These indicated that each participant: was to work alone; could take up to three hours to complete the task, as necessary; could use the applicable specific DSS and all training materials to aid them in the analysis; could not use calculators; and could not ask questions about the case and/or DSS. At the conclusion of the task, the participant was escorted to a class-
sroom to complete the post test questionnaire.

## 4. Results

Cronbach's alpha, a measure of instrument reliability, was calculated for the statements in the post test questionnaire, prior to conducting analyses concerning participant perception. A value of 0.8640 was computed. This exceeded 0.80, a minimum value recommended by Brown [5]. The post test questionnaire thus possessed a high degree of reliability.

Initial statistical analysis employing zero order correlations was conducted to determine the extent and direction of relationships between the performance measures, FEATURES and ERRORS, perception measures, COMFORT, TERMINAL USAGE, and MODEL USAGE, and each of the training-related measures of perception. Correlations for the structured task are shown in Tables 2 and 3 for the semi-structured task. FEATURES was the only performance measure not significantly related to either of the two training measures for either task. Apparently each participant followed unique approaches to DSS usage consistent with individual decision making styles and processes and the particular task. Curiously, a negative association was found between FEATURES and GENERAL TRAINING for the semi-structured task. A possible explanation for this result may be that participants became frustrated with the nature of the task and employed features on a “trial and error” basis, thus considering the training to be inappropriate.

As would be expected, a negative association was found between ERRORS and each of the training measures. Persons who committed fewer errors judged the training to be of greater value. In only one instance, that of ERRORS and GENERAL TRAINING for the semi-structured task, was the association significant (p = 0.01). Although it is obvious that DSS should be designed to minimize frequency of errors, these results emphasize the importance of worthwhile training. If a user makes mistakes, the training may be blamed; then future systems may carry the stigma, thus jeopardizing their potential for success.

The first measure of perception, COMFO. was positively related to both the training variables. Participants felt quite strongly about this relationship with significant findings for GENERAL TRAINING and TASK-RELATED TRAINING (p = 0.0003 and p = 0.0001, respectively) for the structured task, and with p < 0.005 and p < 0.05, respectively for the semi-structured task.

Given these significant findings, however: "What is the implication to DSS and to DSS training?" Simply stated, user training must include instruction about the terminal in addition to obvious training on the DSS. A user must feel comfortable with its use, since it forms an integral part of the dialog component of the DSS. If the terminal is not appropriate, is difficult to use, or detracts in some way from the DSS, decision makers may not use the DSS at all.

TERMINAL USAGE, a second measure of user perception, showed that the use of the terminal improved decision maker effectiveness with GENERAL TRAINING and TASK-RELATED TRAINING (p = 0.0002 and 0.0005, respectively) for the structured task, and p = 0.02 for the semi-structured task. These results show the importance of both general and task-context training for DSS users. Since the terminal forms an integral part of any DSS, it must complement the decision maker.

The last measure, MODEL USAGE, showed that a specific DSS enhanced decision effectiveness during analysis of the assigned task, with results for GENERAL TRAINING and TASK-RELATED TRAINING for the structured task of p = 0.0003 and 0.0001, respectively and for the semi-structured task (p = 0.03). Two implications may be drawn from these results. First, senior management and persons responsible for end user training may wish to reconsider a popular practice of letting end users fend for themselves [4]; the information center may provide a good organizational function to counter this. Second, strong support was provided for applicability of a specific DSS to support decision makers faced with a structured task. Since DSS has long been associated only with less structured tasks, thought should be given to making such support aids available to those facing structured tasks.

Univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was also conducted to determine mean differences by task of several of the perception variables. ANOVA results are presented in Table 4. Descriptive statistics by task for the perception variables are given in Table 5.

Significant differences were noted for TERMINAL USAGE and MODEL USAGE. Persons assigned to the structured task felt strongly about the use of the computer terminal to improve decision effectiveness (p = 0.02) and the use of the specific DSS to enhance decision effectiveness (p = 0.006). The importance of the terminal as a component of a DSS was suggested by Sprague. Possibly less defined problems require a decision maker to exert considerable effort to define the problem and determine appropriate analysis. This may conflict with the use of the terminal, specific DSS, or other computer-related peripherals, affecting the decision maker's perception of their value. This result would support the concept that it is important to ensure that DSS and related components provide support in generating solutions and in understanding the problem.

No significant differences were noted for either GENERAL TRAINING OR TASK-RELATED TRAINING. Apparently persons viewed their perceived value on a DSS generator and a specific DSS to be independent of the task structure. ## 5. Discussion

Results from this study indicate that decision makers have high regard for DSS training and associated components of specific DSS. These include feeling comfortable with a terminal, with a positive attitude toward its improving decision effectiveness. Participants felt strongly about the value of training for applications supporting a structured type of task.

On the other hand, less conclusive results were noted between the training measures and related DSS perception measures for the semi-structured task. It may very well be that decision makers faced with a many-faceted, less-structured task become so involved with it that it may overshadow how a decision maker feels about the training. So much effort is expended in this type of task that it might tend to diminish the worth of the training itself.

The study employed only one type of training method, combining classroom with hands on. No differences were noted by task between how decision makers perceived the worth of both training measures. However, other methods, such as computer-assisted instruction, are becoming increasingly popular and they should be investigated also.

Increased attention should be given to the use of sample specific DSS and various scenarios associated with them as part of training. It should include providing trainees with correct output for particular requirements and the capability to work with them iteratively using a CRT and the sample DSS. This may provide for a more positive attitude, which could lead to better decision making.

Finally, although the study was exploratory, some very interesting results were found, particularly in perception differences by task structure. It may be worthwhile to study organizations that use specific DSS and conduct formal user training, examining how end users view these situations. Furthermore, it would be of interest to investigate other decision situations than finance; e.g. the marketing function includes similar activities, such as sales and production analysis, and even new product planning.
