# Differences in Analyst's Attitudes towards Information Systems Development: Evidence and Implications

Brian L. Dos Santos \* and Stephen R. Hawk \*\*  
\* Krannert Graduate School of Management, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907, USA

\*\* Stuart School of Business, Illinois Institute of Technology, Chicago, Illinois 60616, USA.

Many studies have concluded that one of the causes of systems development failures is that systems professionals have a technical orientation, lack knowledge of human needs and motivation, and are machine oriented. This paper presents evidence that systems analysts attitudes vary considerably and although some of them have a technical orientation, others emphasize the behavioral and socio-political aspects of development. Q-methodology was used to study the attitudes of thirty analysts with varying backgrounds from eight different organizations. Three groups of analysts emerged, each with a distinct attitude. One group is characterized by its emphasis on user needs and is less concerned with the technical aspects of systems development. Another group is characterized by its emphasis on the technical aspects of systems development and project management issues. A third group is characterized by its emphasis on the socio-political aspects of systems development. The study also attempted to find similarities in the backgrounds and experience of analysts with similar attitudes. The implications of these findings for information systems management are discussed.

Keywords: Information systems development, Systems analysts, Systems developers, Analyst attitudes, Analyst differences Implementation failures.

Recently, the systems professional has come under increasing scrutiny, as a possible cause of these failures. It has been suggested that system professionals subscribe to overly technical and economic design ideals [16,18], adhere to theory X [4], are viewed by their peers as technicians rather than managers [8], lack knowledge of human needs and motivation [13] and are machine-oriented [17]. Thus, past research suggests that is professionals pay attention to technical and methodological issues and ignore social, political and psychological issues.

Implicit in past research is the assumption that IS professionals have a relatively undifferentiated attitude or orientation towards systems development. Hence, proposed solutions to development problems include; use of a socio-technical approach to systems design [4,5], separation of social system design responsibilities from technical system design responsibilities [19], and formal training of systems analysts in behavioral concepts and communications techniques [8].

There are two problems with the proposed solutions. First, they are based on the assumption that a technical orientation is always undesirable, that it is too limited under all circumstances. However, many development projects may require just such a technical orientation [7]. Second, as a consequence of these generalizations, proposed solutions to the problem attempt to overcome this narrow attitude or orientation via education and training programs. These solutions are expensive, largely unproven and their benefits can only be realized after a long time.

Although IS professionals have been compared to non-IS professionals [8, 15], they have seldom been compared to one another. An exception is the study by Hedberg and Mumford [13] that compared system designers (systems analysts by the definition used here) from Sweden and England. They found designers from Sweden to be more theory-Y oriented, while those from England were more theory-X oriented. While past research provides insights into the general nature of the problem and suggests solutions, a focus on the differences in attitudes among systems analysts may provide a basis for refining these solutions and the consideration of alternative solutions. In the short run, identifying differences can help in the assignment of analysts to projects where their strengths (relative to other analysts) are most likely to be needed and their weaknesses will cause few problems. In the long run, it can help customize education and training programs to the needs of individual analysts. This paper presents the results of an exploratory field study that sought to identify differences in analyst attitudes towards systems development. In addition, the study also sought to identify analyst characteristics that may be the cause of differences in analyst's attitudes.

## 2. Study Objectives

The notion that individual differences among humans is one of the major causes of differences in their behavior is not new to IS professionals or researchers. The impact of individual differences on the behavior of users has been extensively investigated [31]. Analysts differ from one another in many respects, including their educational backgrounds, length of data processing experience, specific development experience, etc. These individual differences can result in differences in attitudes towards systems development, which could affect their behavior during the development process. Further, differences in their behavior can affect the outcome of a project.

The primary objective of this research was to identify differences in analyst's attitudes. A secondary objective was to try to identify characteristics common to analysts with similar attitudes, while being different for analysts with different attitudes. Thus, the two objectives of this study were:

1. To identify differences in analyst's attitudes towards systems development that could affect the outcome of development projects.

2. To identify similarities in the background and experience of analysts with similar attitudes and differences in backgrounds and experience of analysts with different attitudes.

## 3. Methodology

A field study was conducted using the Q-methodology developed by Stephenson [27,28,29]. Q-methodology was chosen because attitude differences identified using this methodology have been found to be indicative of behavior [6]. In this study, statements that express opinions on various aspects of systems development were constructed and subjects were asked to rank these statements in order of their importance to the systems development process. Their ranking of these statements is indicative of their attitudes. Thus, for example, subjects ranking statements dealing with technical issues higher (i.e. more important) than those dealing with socio-political issues, have an attitude favoring a technical approach to development. Factor analysis was used to identify groups of developers with similar statement rankings. In order to identify developer attitudes, statement rankings of developers in each group were used to construct a composite statement ranking for the group. Differences in analyst's attitudes were assessed by examining differences in statement rankings across groups. Statement construction, selection of subjects, the procedure for rank ordering the statements and the analysis and interpretation of results are described in greater detail here.

## 3.1. Statement Construction

The objective in constructing statements was to generate a list of statements that represent opinions on various aspects of systems development. Theories, models and empirical results from the IS literature that relate to the outcome of IS development efforts, and suggested solutions to development problems, were considered in generating statements. From the literature it was determined that the following factors are thought to influence system development efforts: user/analyst communication [3,9], individual differences among users [31], user expectations and attitudes [12], technical capabilities of the development staff [11], systems that alter the balance of power in organizations [23], etc. Similarly, proposed solutions to help overcome development problems are: getting users involved in the systems development effort [14], obtaining top management support [21,22], educating aspiring analysts in the social and political aspects of system development [18], improving analyst's managerial skills [21,24], etc.

A list of statements was generated after carefully reviewing this literature. From this list, thirty-three statements were chosen so as to provide as representative a coverage of IS development issues and solutions as possible. Five professional is analysts reviewed the chosen statements, to assess their representativeness and understandability. It has been suggested that statements selected in this manner produce a set of statements that approximate the complexity of the phenomenon being investigated [6]. These statements were intended to be representative of the population of possible statements, rather than being comprehensive in their coverage of IS development issues and solutions [6]. The statements used in this study are presented in Appendix A.

## 3.2. Data Collection

In selecting subjects for this study, the objective was to choose analysts who were likely to have different attitudes towards is development. Since most of the earlier studies generalize about systems analysts [4,8,18], the literature provided little guidance in the selection process. Based upon the solutions proposed to overcoming the narrow orientation problem it was expected that educational backgrounds, length of development experience, length of data processing experience and the type of development methodology used were likely to affect analyst's attitudes towards systems development. Eight regional organizations employing different development methodologies were chosen to participate in this study. The chief is executive in each organization was asked to select subjects from his organization with different backgrounds and experience. Totally, thirty subjects were selected. Subjects ranked the thirty-three statements on the strength of their agreement with the statements. Each statement was typed on an index card and the cards were presented to a subject. Subjects placed a specific number of cards into each of nine categories, based upon the strength of their agreement with the statements. The procedure used to help them rank statements in this manner is referred to as Q-sorting and is described in detail in [b]. Fig. 1 was used to guide them in this process and shows the number of cards placed in each of the nine categories. A researcher was present during the entire process to give instructions and to answer questions.

After ranking data were collected, subjects were asked to fill out a questionnaire that requested information concerning their data processing and development experience, and their educational backgrounds. Data on analyst's experience and educational backgrounds were gathered since education and training have been suggested as solutions to the "technical orientation" problem. In addition, information about the development process and the organization was gathered. Use of a development methodology may influence attitudes by defining the role played by systems analysts in IS development. Lieberman [20] provides evidence that role characteristics can affect the attitudes of role occupants. He suggests that people need to have attitudes consistent with their actions.

Development processes employed by each organization in the study were determined to be formal or informal. A development process was considered formal if there was strict adherence to phases (without iteration), including authorizations after each phase and enforcement of standards during each phase. An informal process allowed iteration between phases and paid less attention to standards.

## 4. Analysis

For each subject's statement rankings, values from one to nine were assigned to each statement based upon the subject's ranking of the statement, as shown in Fig. 1. In order to group subjects having a similar ranking of statements, the rankings were subjected to principal components analysis (PCA). In this analysis, each of the factors extracted may be thought of as a hypothetical subject, with each subject's loading on the factor being indicative of the similarity of that subject's ranking with that of the factor ranking. Since factor loadings represent the correlation between a subject's ranking and the factor, the loadings were used to identify subjects with a similar ranking to the factor. Loadings in excess of 0.44 or less than -0.44 indicate that a subject's ranking is similar or opposite to that of the factor, respectively, at the 0.01 significance level [26].

## 5. Discussion of Results

In addressing the first objective of this study, the factor ranking of statements was of particular interest, since they reflect the attitudes of subjects defining each factor. The characteristics of subjects defining a factor were of interest in addressing the second objective. The factor loadings in Table 3 show that (p < 0.01) ten subjects uniquely identified factor A, ten subjects uniquely identified factor B and four subjects uniquely identified factor C. The large number of subjects defining each factor (particularly factors A&B) increases the reliability of the results [6].

The normalized scores in Table 3 and the SED's on pairs of factor scores were used to determine which statements were ranked differently by the subjects defining the three factors. The ranking of statements, particularly those ranked differently, were carefully studied to understand how the attitudes of subjects in each of the three groups differed. The attitudes of subjects in each of the three groups is discussed below.

## 5.1.1. Group $A$

The subjects defining factor A appear to be oriented towards the needs of users, when compared to those defining factors B&C. They are also more concerned than the other groups about changing user requirements resulting from user area turnover (29). The subjects defining factor A also view the development process as an important means for obtaining user acceptance. ## 5.1.2. Group B

The subjects defining factor B are oriented towards the system technology and project control. They give prominence to statements 2, 8, 10, 11 and 33. Statements 10 and 33 show a concern for project control issues, while statement 11 emphasizes the importance of technical issues. They also emphasize the need to use structured techniques (32) and to freeze specifications (25), indicative of an orientation towards a traditional life cycle approach to systems development where each phase is completed before the next one begins.

Factor B subjects are less concerned with addressing important user needs (22), and gaining top management support (7) than factor A subjects. They are also less concerned that development efforts receive resources on the basis of business priorities than the other two factors (6) and show less concern for behavioral issues such as user expectations (15). Further, this group places less emphasis on requirements specification with an orientation towards freezing requirements prior to design and programming.

## 5.1.3. Group C

The subjects defining factor C are oriented towards the socio-political aspects of systems development. They give prominence to statements 1, 6, 20, 21 and 27. They place greater emphasis on user attitudes (1), use of steering committees to manage projects (6), is commitment (20), political skills (27), the placement of analysts in user areas (21), and are concerned about user expectations (15).

These subjects appear to have a stronger orientation towards the life cycle approach than factor B subjects. In addition to placing importance on the use of structured techniques and freezing specifications, they do not see the need for prototyping (4), nor do they see the need to split large projects into smaller ones (12). They are also less concerned with problems arising from turnover in the IS area (10), and place less emphasis on technical issues than factor B subjects (11).

While this group emphasizes commitment and support from important individuals, i.e. user attitude, is commitment, political skills, they do not emphasize the development process as a means of remedying potential problems. These subjects place less emphasis on technology than factor B subjects. They display less concern for changing requirements and are less convinced that user involvement helps requirements specification than either of the other groups.

## 5.2. Distinguishing Characteristics

The purpose in examining data on subjects and their organizations, was to identify characteristics common to the group of subjects defining each of the factors as an initial step towards determining why subjects have similar attitudes. From Table 5, it appears that the length of system development and project leadership experience and education are not characteristics that differentiate one group of subjects from another. However, the development methodology employed appears to be a differentiating characteristic. Ten of the fourteen subjects whose previous development experience required a strict adherence to a life cycle approach to systems development, had attitudes favoring such an approach (factors B&C). Six of the ten subjects from organizations employing a less formal approach had attitudes represented by factor A. In addition, three of the four subjects working in a less formal environment who favor a life cycle approach had been with their employer for under one year and their prior experience was with organizations employing a life cycle approach. A procedure described by Gibbons [10, p. 328] was used to determine that the association between development methodology and analyst attitudes was significant (p < 0.05) when these three subjects were omitted from the analysis.

## 6. Conclusions

This study suggests that analysts attitudes do vary. More importantly, it suggests that the assignment of analysts to projects could have an effect on project success or failure. Factor A analysts appear to be better suited to the development of systems supporting less structured problems where an evolutionary development methodology such as prototyping is preferred [25]. The attitudes of factors B and C analysts appear better suited to larger projects, supporting problems of a more structured nature, where their emphasis on determining and freezing precise specifications, and on project management issues and problems, is necessary.

In a field study of systems development using the prototyping methodology, Alavi [2] found that designers experienced difficulty in managing and controlling the design process. Alavi also suggested that frequent changes in requirements could frustrate analysts. These results may explain some of her findings. It appears that precisely those analysts least likely to be frustrated by changing requirements, are most likely to neglect project control and management issues. It also indicates that analysts better suited to managing and controlling the design process (factors B and C) might be more easily frustrated by changing requirements.

This study also suggests that there is agreement across groups on the importance (or unimportance) of some issues. For example, none of the analysts placed much emphasis on the user interface. This could cause problems since inadequate resources may be assigned to this facet of a system when resources are constrained and tradeoffs have to be made. Since most organizations develop a variety of different types of systems, it is important that they have analysts with different attitudes. Based upon the preliminary results presented here, this may be accomplished by hiring analysts from organizations using development processes different from their own or by exposing different is groups within their organization to different development processes. If project assignment decisions are to be based upon analyst's attitudes, further research is needed to develop an easy to administer instrument to identify attitudes.

These results, while encouraging, must be interpreted carefully. This methodology was used to study analyst's attitudes and the results are representative of the population of analysts from which those in the study were chosen. Since the analysts chosen had a wide range of backgrounds and experiences, the results may be fairly representative of systems analysts in general. However, these results should be verified with randomly selected subjects.

This study did not attempt to determine how pervasive each of these attitudes are, i.e. what percentage of the analyst population belong in each group. This is an important issue that needs to be addressed in a future study. Another important issue not addressed here is whether analyst's attitudes change, and if so, what causes changes in attitudes. The study does provide evidence that there are important differences in analyst's attitudes and these differences may be useful in matching analysts' attitudes to project characteristics.

## Appendix A

## Statements

1. Prospective users of a system should have positive attitudes/perceptions towards the new system. If they do not, it could cause significant problems during development.

2. Users should be active participants on the project team during the system development phase. Such involvement helps with the determination of a system's requirements.

3. Projects should be monitored and evaluated frequently in terms of their ability to satisfy current user needs. Not doing so could lead to the development of a system that does not meet user needs.

4. Evolutionary development of systems, via the use of prototypes, helps develop useful systems and is a good development methodology. This is particularly true for systems where user needs are not definite and can be expected to change after using the system.

5. Good communication between users and is analysts is necessary so that analysts understand users needs and users understand what analysts are proposing.

6. An organization wide steering committee should monitor project progress and assign resources (personnel) to projects based upon business requirements (priorities).

7. Top management support for a project is important. By making subordinates (users) aware of their support, they help overcome some potential user problems.

8. Users should be active members of the project team during the system design phase, where the exact specifications for the system, in terms of inputs, processing and outputs, are laid out.

9. Users should have confidence in the system analysts. Without this confidence, many problems will arise during development.

10. Turnover in IS development staff either through attrition or re-assignment causes problems. Bringing staff onto a project in mid-stream increases costs and delays completion.

11. The technical competence of the IS development staff is important. Working with unfamiliar technology always seems to result in unforeseen problems that affect the project development effort.

12. Large projects should be avoided by splitting them up and working on a portion at a time. This way we work on a number of smaller projects that we can complete and turn over to the users in a short period of time. Large projects have a way of going on forever and always seem to run into problems.

13. Projects should have benefits that are measurable (quantifiable). Then it is much easier to get solid management support. Also, it becomes easier to evaluate the project in terms that management understands.

14. It is better to have systems initiated by users than by IS staff. It results in far fewer problems in dealing with users.

15. Users should have realistic expectations of what the system is to deliver. That way they are not disappointed and are happier with the system.

16. Is analysts should follow a system after it has gone into production to help ensure that it is used as intended, and to iron out any problems that may arise.

17. There should be reviews and walkthroughs of the system with users at many points in the development process. This helps users understand what the system will provide and makes analysts aware of any omissions.

18. Carefully laid plans are necessary to implement needed changes for a new system to function effectively. Without such plans, the best system is headed for trouble.

19. Turnover among top management, either through attrition or re-assignment is a problem. New management might not support the project. Also, organizational changes resulting from management change can cause other problems for analysts.

20. The is development staff should be committed to the project. They will then provide their best resources and given it their best effort to make it a success.

21. Organizationally, analysts should be in the user areas, so that they are more familiar with the user's function. This helps communication and better requirements definition.

22. Projects should address "real" and "important" business problems. Then it is easier to generate management support and user cooperation.

23. The user interface to a system is important. What the user sees is probably as important as anything else that the system does.

24. Users should be properly trained before the new system is turned over to them. If not, operational and morale problems could result.

25. At some stage system design needs to be frozen so that programming and implementation can begin. If not, we could forever be iterating through the analysis and design phases and the system will never be built.

26. Users should be an integral part of the project development team. This gives them a sense of ownership of the system and generally makes life easier for the analysts.

27. The project leader/analyst needs to be politically adept. Political skills are as important as any other skills to a project leader.

28. The “urgency” of a new system is an important determinant of project development success. An urgently needed system is able to pull together users, management and IS development staff with a good deal of goal-oriented motivation.

29. Turnover in the user group, either through attrition or re-assignment can result in changing requirements for the new users as well as a lack of commitment on their part.

30. The number of different users of a system complicates system development. Dealing with the demands of different users and personalities can cause many problems.

31. Different personnel should be involved in the analysis and programming phases of development.

32. The use of structured techniques in analysis, design and programming is essential. They shorten development time, and reduce both development and maintenance costs.

33. The project leader/analyst should have good managerial skills. Lack of such skills can severely affect project development.
