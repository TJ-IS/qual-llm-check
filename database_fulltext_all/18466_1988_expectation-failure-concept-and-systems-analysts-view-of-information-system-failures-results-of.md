# Expectation Failure Concept and Systems Analysts' View of Information System Failures: Results of an Exploratory Study

Kalle Lyytinen

Department of Computer Science, University of Jyväskylä, SF-40100 Finland

The paper presents a new concept of information system (IS) failure called an expectation failure. An IS failure is defined as the inability of the IS to meet a stakeholder group's expectations. Results of an empirical study focusing on systems analysts' perception of information system failures are presented. The study shows that systems analysts' perceptions can be largely accounted for by their professional and structural position in the systems development process and associated interests. This supports the view that a perception of IS failure is largely stakeholder-dependent.

Keywords: Information system failure, Stakeholder analysis, Systems design, Implementation.

## 1. Introduction

The issue of information system failure has received notable attention in the information systems (IS) literature. Among others Lucas [19,20], Brooks [5], Morgan and Soden [26], Markus [23], Markus and Robey [24], and Keen [14] have increased considerably our knowledge of IS development pitfalls. These scholars have shown that problems with IS are wide-spread and pervasive. Moreover, the problems are many and no single feature can describe the phenomenon entirely.

Despite the large number of the studies the research on IS failures has suffered from two weaknesses. First, the notion of IS failure has been poorly articulated [21] and the majority of studies fails to take into account the richness of the phenomenon. Second, the empirical investigations into IS failure have studied the phenomenon mainly from a managerial viewpoint. Hence they have offered inadequate models of IS failure and considered too little the environment and types of IS failure [22].

This paper has two goals. First, it will shortly outline a new is failure concept called an expectation failure. We show how the concept can overcome some limitations in the earlier failure research and provide a sounder basis for empirical investigations. Second, the paper reports results of an empirical study that was informed by the new failure concept. We investigated systems analysts' perceptions of is failures which were obtained using a structured questionnaire. The data was analyzed to uncover how systems analysts read and interpret problems in is. The study provides new information on the frequency of different types of is failures and their relative importance for systems analysts. Moreover, it offers some insights into how systems analysts perceive, think of and tinker with its problems. The study shows that systems analysts' perceptions differ from those of users and management. Their perception is determined by systems analysts' professional role as rational problem solvers and technical experts.

## 2. Expectation Failure

The expectation failure represents a pluralistic and political account of IS failure. It defines an IS failure as a gap between stakeholders' expectations expressed in some ideal or standard and the actual performance. The concept is pluralistic, because it assumes that IS failures depend on the plural values that stakeholders behold. It is political, because it suggests that most failures are resolved (and often caused) through political means such as compromises, bargaining, truce and bribery. Therefore, it suggests a more in-depth analysis of a multitude of aspects that render some IS problematic, including identification of IS stakeholders, analysis of failure types, and tracking down failure reasons.

## 2.1. Stakeholders

The concept suggests that an IS failure concerns one or several groups of people called stakeholders who have a vested interest in the IS. The interests originate from a personal, or a group advantage that accrues from controlling important material or organizational resources [8]. These interests shape values that define desirable features in the IS and how these features are to be obtained. These interests are actualized in everyday situations through expectations that express stakeholders' dynamic "going concerns" with the IS $^{1}$ .

The IS stakeholders can be identified by several criteria such as stakeholders' view of IS, type of relationship to IS, depth of impact, internal or external, etc. Typical stakeholder groups often include (but not always) systems analysts, various classes of end-users, sponsors, external users (e.g. customers), legislators etc. [21].

## 2.3. IS Failure Reasons

A vital task in any IS failure is to discern a pattern of events and arrangements that make an IS problematic for a stakeholder group. These we call IS failure reasons and they can be defined as generic images or schemes (theories in use [3]) which stakeholders draw upon to explain occurred pitfalls. We can divide the principal IS failure reasons into four classes:

1. information system,

2. the environment of the IS,

3. information systems development (ISD), and
4. the environment of the ISD.

The first class of reasons covers features of the IS: hardware, software, prior design decisions, documentation and so on. The second class includes features of IS environment: user's characteristics, organizational characteristics and so on. The third reason class consists of systems development features: design methods, tools, managing principles and organizational arrangements. The fourth reason class embraces a wider environment of systems development such as user and designer population characteristics. In each of the four classes we can further recognize one of several types of elements that have been found to cause IS problems. These elements are shown in more detail in Table 3.

## 3. Research Method

## 3.1. Research Subject and Goals

To assess the impact of stakeholders' expectations on perceived is failure pattern and to explore the validity of the expectation failure concept systems analysts' perception of is failure was selected as a research subject. Based on the pluralistic failure notion we hypothesized that the choice of a different stakeholder group influences the frequency and types of is failures observed. This follows from the systems analysts' professional position [16], educational background, organizational status and career prospects which all make systems analysts distinct and unique stakeholders. Accordingly, many studies have witnessed that systems analysts have a high loyalty to their profession, and share largely the same professional values and attitudes [18]. By the same token, many scholars in is have argued that systems analysts pursue their own interests that differ from and often conflict with that of other stakeholder groups [17,24] – especially users and management.

Our study differs somewhat from many other investigations published on IS failure, since most studies have focused on user [19] or management experience [1,28] and attitudes about the IS $^{2}$ . Instead, we tried to obtain insights into difficulties that systems analysts find in their work by classifying and analysing is problems that systems analysts report. This could clarify how systems analysts reason about difficulties encountered, what sorts of images and models they draw upon in this process, and what, if any, differences systems analysts exhibit in this process compared to other stakeholders.

## 3.2. Research Method

To achieve the research goal we used a questionnaire (Appendix 1) to solicit systems analysts' perceptions of IS problems. We did not attempt to inquire directly the espoused fit between analysts expectations and perceived reality, because this would make data gathering cumbersome and analysis of the data more difficult $^{3}$ . Therefore, a questionnaire was made using the expectation failure concept and its typology of failure types and reasons. The questionnaire served as an instrument to collect data about most common and important failure types and about significant schemes analysts apply to explain the failures. We believe that reports of these categories also tell about systems analysts' expectations about IS and how these expectations have been achieved.

The research method differs from those used in many other failure studies which have used either a mailed questionnaire [1,19,28] or laboratory experiments [6,19]. Instead the questionnaire was filled in on site after a lecture. The research was carried out in three organizations (A, B and C). The number of respondents was 34 analysts. In Appendix 2 a more detailed description of the sample is given.

The advantage of the research method was that we could control the response ratio and also how the respondents interpreted the questionnaire (which was important as we wanted the replies truly to reflect their expectations). The method had obvious disadvantages: the number of respondents was small and covered only three organizations. Therefore we can only make weak generalizations about the findings, and they may indicate a rather skewed pattern of systems analysts' perception. The data collection method may also introduce bias due to "halo-effects" and anchoring of opinions.

## 4. Research Results

In what follows we shall discuss three findings: how often see systems analysts is fail, what sorts of failures they perceive, and what reasons they bear upon to explain them.

## 4.1. How Many Information Systems Fail?

The first question we wanted to probe was: How often do systems analysts encounter is failures in their work (questions 4 and 5) $^{4}$ .

The data included both qualitative and quantitative data on failures and their commonality. On the qualitative level the data included characterization of types of systems that had failed. These were by and large common administrative applications such as customer files, product inventories, accounting systems, invoicing systems, production control systems and so on. One reported failure was an installation of an application generator (!). Thus, none of the failed systems represented any advanced solutions. Some answers focused on critical system functions that mostly produce the problems. Here all respondents told system interface design to be the most critical task.

## 4.2. How Do Information Systems Fail?

Next, the systems analysts expressed their opinion about the commonality of various types of systems failures (questions 7 and 8). This was used to clarify what they meant by “IS failures” and what concerns they had about the IS. The replies are summarized in Table 5.

The questions measured both the commonality and the relative importance of a failure type. We believe that these two measures may vary independently and that they describe distinct aspects of the phenomenon. Some is failures may be common, but not necessarily fatal, whereas some others may be “dead or life” issues, but quite rare. The former feature was measured by the number of observations per failure type. The latter was measured by averaging the rankings given by the analyst to each failure type that he or she had picked. In ranking 1 meant the most important and 3 the least important.

In IS development failures (Table 5a) the two measures yielded the same three failure types. As systems analysts are mainly engaged in design activities these three failure types can be regarded as the most substantial ones. They were: general lack of goals, process characteristics, and the self-image of systems design. In the light of the expectation failure concept we can read these three failure types to show those principal features of IS design that contradict with systems analysts' expectations and their image of 'good systems work'. These center around the need to have accurately defined problems, to have well-structured and systems analyst controlled design process, and to have clear communications about user needs and problems. Thus, systems analysts' seem to hold a highly rationalistic image of IS design [4,10,11] and the data corroborates results of earlier studies [18] that systems analysts want to maintain highly separate and professional values.

Not surprisingly, in IS use problems (Table 5b) systems analysts' opinions were more evenly distributed and all failure types received lower rankings, too. The same three IS failure types were not found to be the most common and most substantial. This result is obvious in two ways. First, many systems analysts feel that systems use is somewhat peripheral to their professional concerns and their answers can be much more "random". Second, the use problems depend more on the type of application and use situation which can be very different.

The three most common is use failure types were (in this order): complexity problems, conceptual problems, and people's reactions. The most common is use failures reported here differ from Lucas' [19] findings of users' perceptions. In his study people's reactions was found to be the most common is use failure. Thus, systems analysts' primarily focus is in areas that are at the core of his or her technical expertise: the inherent technical complexity of the applications. This is a cause for concern because application complexity increases software maintenance demands, technological evolution, and and so forth. On the other hand, the notification of people's reactions and conceptual problems indicates that analysts are not necessarily unaware of the social dimension of systems' use (contrary to conclusions in [9.19]).

The three most highly ranked is use failure types were (in this order): conceptual problems, data problems, and people's reactions. Thus, the more substantial the failure type the more it is socially conditioned. The message here is clear: whenever analysts confront severe problems they are mainly determined by how users relate to the IS.

Differences in the measurement can be summarized as follows. The most common problems like e.g. application complexity, are usually “muddied through” by the analysts in their attempts to maintain stable computing environments. On the other hand, the social use problems exert drastic impacts on the whole computing arrangement and must be resolved without delay. This explains the mentioning of data problems in the severe failures. Wrong or incomprehensible data render the application immediately useless.

## 4.3. Why Information Systems Fail

Again, we tried to identify the five most common reasons for is failure as well as to rank them (Table 6). The five most common reasons mentioned were (in this order): decision making, contingency, work-based, user-based, and implementation reasons. The five most highly ranked reasons were (in this order): decision making, organizational, technical and operational, contingency reasons, and work-based reasons. The frequency distribution is here quite skewed.

Some conclusions can be drawn when we look at Table 6. First, the most common way to explain the failures is based on the social side of IS (such as decision making, contingency and work-based). Second, when we look at the most important suggested reasons the picture changes: we can see a shift in focus to more technical aspects of system design. Thus, though systems analysts recognize the crucial impact of the social system in producing IS problems, in their view the most crucial reasons lie in inadequate technical designs.

However, it is interesting to observe which three reasons are common in the two measurements. These are decision making, contingency, and work-based reasons (moreover, both measures find the decision making based reasons to be the most important reason) and they hence identify systems analysts' main "theories-in-use" [3] to explain IS failures. The theories explain failures by three major factors: (1) a lack of detailed functional specifications (or the incapability to state them), (2) inadequate conceptual and methodological means to understand users' work, and (3) insufficient understanding of and control over contingencies that affect the development process [2]. They state that IS failures accrue because designers lack control over the design process and its results, they have inadequate knowledge of control variables and they have poor skills to manipulate these variables. In consequence, this prevents systems analysts to act as competent professional experts and to solve real users' problems. Instead, analysts are compelled to live in a much more uncertain, ambiguous and conflict laden environment.

This point is strengthened by the observation that systems analysts find users and organizational implementation to belong to the five most important reasons for is failure. The former signals that the analysts do not possess sufficient organizational status and psychological skills to cope with organizational change problems. In consequence, designers many times feel that users are a threat that hinders them to work properly.

## 4.4. How to Remove IS Failures?

We tried to shed light on approaches that systems analysts suggest to solve is failures they confront by asking their personal opinion about the means to cope with the problems. It was expected that by analyzing the replies we would learn something of how analysts judge the chances to improve their working practices, what types of measures they prefer in so doing and how reflective they are about their own work.

We obtained many answers. Only five respondents did not reply to this question. However, the answers were very dispersed and could not be interpreted easily. The general conclusion from the answers is this: systems analysts do not necessarily think reflectively about their own work and this is difficult for them: many answers did mix means and goals or mainly mentioned some desirable goals such as “improve productivity”. Many answers were also ambiguous in that they prescribed abstract principles such as “improve documentation” or “have a clear time-table” instead of offering concrete solutions.

The results are of course preliminary and only hint to some key images applied in analysts' answers. Also some reservations must be made to their validity, because it was not always easy to interpret and code the results.

Despite these caveats, the analysis does indeed reveal some interesting results. In general, systems analysts tend to suggest process improvements (methods and tools) instead of structural improvements (organizational arrangements, architectures etc.). They also emphasize short-range, controllable reasons and neglect the impact of long-range and uncontrollable reasons. Thus, analysts' immediate and unconscious focus seems to be more on "technical fixes" than on improving the larger social environment and values of systems development. In this sense, the range of issues raised by systems analysts does not coincide with some research results which emphasize user involvement [12,13], project group composition [29], and project management politics [7] as conditions for successful systems design. As Table 7 shows, the replies also contradict with findings obtained earlier in this study. For example, though methods were not seen by the analysts as the major reason for IS failure (although decision-making based reasons also imply some information requirements methods) most suggestions for improvement concern this failure reason class. This indicates a still more rationalistic bias than the answers that concerned types of IS failure.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

In this paper we have explored the viability of the IS expectation of failure concept. This was accomplished by surveying systems analysts' perceptions of IS failures. The study shows that systems analysts' view of failed systems largely coincides with their stakes with the IS. Accordingly, their view differs from the users or management view.

Systems analysts find problems in defining exactly what the IS should do, in clearly communicating users' needs and in maintaining a rational image of the development process. They also face difficulties in managing the complexity of applications, in understanding users' problems and in anticipating users' reactions. Analysts prefer to explain the failures in procedural and rationalistic terms. Reasons mentioned for failure include inexact development goals and specifications, inadequate understanding of users' work, and inadequate understanding of system contingencies. Thus, systems analysts' view of IS failures is highly concomitant with their professional role in and expectations of the IS development process which conceives IS design as an overtly rational act, involving high technical and professional competence.

The study involved a small sample, so the results cannot be easily generalized, though the same pattern emerged in all organizations included in the study. The message for the research into systems development is clear: we need more theories and methods that help systems analysts to understand and anticipate social aspects of systems development and use.
