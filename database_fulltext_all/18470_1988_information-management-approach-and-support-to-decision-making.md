# Information Management Approach and Support to Decision-Making

Christopher K. Carlson

Department of Decision Sciences, School of Business Administration, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA 22030, USA

Linkages exist between the information management approach and support of the organizational decision-making process. The premise of this article is that utilization of the advanced forms of the information management approach is better suited to supporting the decision-making process. The research reported here confirms that subject program managers desire information management approaches that support rather than replace their own judgment. Organizational requirements most desired by managers from their data system support are highlighted. However, there are fundamental differences between the views of the top level managers and their operational managers.

Keywords: Information management, Information management approach, Decision-making support, Decision support framework, Automation techniques, File processing, Management information systems, Decision support systems, Organizational information requirements, Federal program management.

## 1. Introduction

The research reported here was undertaken as an investigation of information management approaches at a U.S. federal agency. The focus was on the effect of information system automation technique in support of management decision-making. It has been assumed that more advanced forms provide better support for decision-making. Our work attempts to investigate the linkages between the approach and its support of decision-making. In our study, the approach ranged from file processing providing statistical reporting, through management information system (MIS) having data base applications, to decision support systems (DSS) exhibiting direct involvement in the decision-making process. The general claim of most IS professionals is that utilization of more advanced approaches will be shown to provide considerable support to better decision-making.

The research arena was the community development organization and programs of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). This organization has inplace automated data processing (ADP) systems supporting its programmatic activities, which cover the gamut of the evolution of automation techniques from file processing systems, through MIS, to the current 'state of the art' DSS.

It has been suggested that management styles and attitudes towards program data and information affect both organizational requirements for information processing support and the specific information management approach utilized to support program management activities [3,4,12]. The linkage between organizational behavior and information management approaches, particularly the use of DSS, has been conceptualized and investigated by Alter [1] and Keen and Scott-Morton [6]. They (following Robert Anthony's pioneering work [2]) define a 'framework for decision support' differentiating information needs for three types of managerial decision-making.

They also contend that the computer is best used in an organizational setting when it supports the management decision-making process. This is accomplished by

(1) assisting managers in semi-structured tasks,

(2) supporting managerial judgment, and (3) improving effectiveness rather than efficiency of decision-making.

The focus of the research was on investigating the validity of these contentions. The following process was defined to achieve this; users were asked:

A. To state their level of need for data system support to management decision-making. They were then asked to provide a measure representing their view of data system support for unstructured versus well-structured problems.

B. To provide a measure representing their view between supporting or replacing managerial judgment.

C. Their view regarding support to office effectiveness as opposed to office efficiency.

Our first hypothesis was that user responses would show a positive relationship between the requirement for management decision-making support and their view of data systems as mainly supporting unstructured (versus well-structured) problems. The second hypothesis was that user responses would show a positive relationship between the requirement for support to management decision-making and the view that data systems should support, rather than replace, managerial judgment. The third hypothesis was that user responses would demonstrate a positive relationship between the requirement for decision-making support and the view of data systems as supporting office effectiveness.

The investigation of these hypotheses was achieved by administering a complex survey instrument that first determined a respondent's need for data system support to the management decision-making task, and then attempted to relate this requirement to the three process elements of Keen and Scott-Morton.

## 2. Research Methodology

Three types of information management systems are utilized at HUD's Office of Community Planning and Development (CPD): (1) File Processing, (2) Management Information Systems, and (3) Decision Support Systems. The Office of Urban Rehabilitation (OUR) and its rehabilitation loans and grants program activities are supported by a twenty-year-old file processing system. The Office of Block Grant Assistance (OBGA) and its Community Development Block Grants (CDBG) program are served by a nationwide MIS. OUR and its newer Rental Rehabilitation program are supported by the Rental Rehabilitation MIS. The Office of Urban Development Action Grants (OUDAG) and the Urban Development Action Grant (UDAG) program are served by the Action Grant Information System (AGIS) which provides both management information and decision support system (DSS) capabilities.

The Rehabilitation Loans and Grant data system provides detailed data concerning Rehabilitation Loans by geographical area and by property and loan recipient characteristics. Summaries are provided for the total program and by program activities. Data are stored in records (one per loan) held in a simple ‘flat file.’ The system’s major characteristics are

\- automated processing of approved loan forms,

\- statistical reporting on individual program,

\- routine, repetitive data processing, and

\- data accuracy from automation as compared to manual processing.

FORMS/CPD was designed and developed as a grant tracking system to support planning and control functions at HUD Regional and Area (Field) Offices and also provide Headquarters CPD Program Management with information to administer the CDBG program. It is oriented to supporting information needs at both field and headquarters management levels. The system is an example of the MIS approach in that its major characteristics include

\- use of data bases and the database approach,

\- data flow integration to avoid data duplication and data redundancy,

\- remote entry by terminal and telecommunications,

\- ad hoc, random inquiry capability,

\- enhanced flexibility in data handling for both routine operations and spontaneous requirements, and

\- Data hierarchies, or 'layering,' moving from general, summary level to detailed, specific information.

The Rental Rehabilitation Cash and Management Information System (C/MI) was developed to serve as an electronic funds transfer and MIS for OUR's Rental Rehabilitation program. The system is used to disburse and manage program funds, and collect and report project and tenant data. The C/MI system is directly geared to program implementation and delivery, and provides a complete linkage to Departmental financial management and accounting systems. It has the same MIS characteristics noted for FORMS/CPD above.

The AGIS system provides administrative and management information support in tracking and monitoring projects and grant funds for the Urban Development Action Grant (UDAG) program. It is a multi-purpose information, monitoring and DSS serving HUD Headquarters and field offices. AGIS provides for direct interactions and support of the operations and processes of the OUDAG program office. Direct support to managerial decision-making is provided by the grant application databases and the Selection Round and On-Line subsystems. AGIS has the following elements of a management support mechanism that are characteristic of a DSS:

\- quantitative analysis techniques and tools,

\- management decision-making support,

\- 'what if' capability,

\- direct, and intermediary, control by decision-maker,

\- support to nonprogrammable decisions,

\- real-time analysis capability, and

\- contingency planning features.

The above data system implementations provided a case study basis to our research. The research considered each of the three approaches and their value in program management. The different characteristics of these information management approaches were highlighted in designing the survey instrument.

The classification of the three approaches was used to establish the case study framework. Initially, a small set of open-ended interviews was conducted with the executive management personnel of the three program offices to clarify which variables should be measured to determine information requirements and mission effectiveness [9]. As a result of these interviews, a survey instrument was prepared to gather data from the personnel working at non-clerical levels within each of the three program offices (a total of 92 individuals). The survey instrument is shown in Appendix 1.

## 2.1. Survey Instrument Pre-Test

The instrument was pre-tested on a 20% random sample (19 individuals) of the total survey population, stratified by office and management/supervisory level versus nonmanagement/supervisory level. The sample was drawn by using a table of random numbers.

To insure confidentially of responses, a 'blind' survey collection mechanism was employed so the respondents and their responses would never be connected. Respondents completed the survey questionnaire and returned it to a contact person indicated for their office. The survey questionnaire was returned to the contact person, who removed the cover letter (which would identify the person responding) before returning the completed instrument to the author. The contact persons were also asked to make a follow-up call to improve the response rate.

The survey questionnaires were hand-carried and personally given to each potential respondent with the request to take the time necessary to answer the questions and return the instrument to the contact person. This method was successful in obtaining a 100% response rate on the survey pre-test. The results and analysis were used in refining the final survey questionnaire.

The survey instrument pre-test was accomplished on the 20 percent random sample prior to the full instrument data collection activity. Statistical analyses were then undertaken to refine the final instrument. Since the pretest results were good, the instrument only needed slightly improved formatting and alignment of columns, questions, and response areas. Minor flaws in the column heading labels were corrected and an improved form was prepared. The pre-test thus did not significantly affect the outcome, with changes being cosmetic or for clarification purposes.

## 2.2. The Full Survey and Its Statistical Analyses

Following the pre-test, the survey instrument was sent to the balance of the survey population (80 percent or 73 individuals) at the non-clerical level in the three offices. Fifty-four responses were received for a response rate of 74 percent on this phase of the survey. Combined with the initial 19 responses of the pre-test, this resulted in a total of 75 responses of the 92 individuals, with a final response rate of 82 percent. Since the survey instrument had only slightly changed, the two survey files were combined in the final statistical analyses.

Once the survey responses were obtained, the responses were coded and entered into a response data file. Several statistical analysis programs were developed using SPSS, the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. These included

\- Simple Statistics: Mean, Mode, and Standard Deviation

\- Cross-Tabulation Analysis

-- Correlation Runs Across the Subcategories or Variable Groups from the Survey Instrument

\- F-Test and T-Test Analyscs.

The responses were statistically analyzed to assess attitudes towards information management and the specific data system being utilized, as well as perceptions regarding mission accomplishment and data system effectiveness [4,11].

After the written survey analysis, an open-ended survey questionnaire guide (see Appendix 2) was developed for face-to-face interviews with a selected subset of the survey respondents: the 17 senior management officials of the three program offices. These results were of a qualitative nature and focused directly on interaction between organizational requirements for information management and mission accomplishment. The analyses of the survey responses and interview data were directed towards hypotheses testing, determination of the implication of the tests, and explanation of findings.

## 3. Results

The major hypothesis of this study was that the computer is best used, in the view or perception of program managers, when it supports the management decision-making process.

The first specific hypothesis was that:

(A) survey responses would have a positive relationship between the requirement for management decision-making support and the view of data systems as mainly supporting unstructured (versus well-structured) problems.

The second specific hypothesis was that:

(B) survey responses would show a positive relationship between the requirement for support to decision-making and the view that data systems should support, rather than replace, managerial judgment.

## The third specific hypothesis was that:

(C) survey responses would demonstrate a positive relationship between the decision-making support requirement and the view of data systems as supporting office effectiveness.

Table 1 presents the results of Pearson's correlation statistical runs made to investigate the specific hypotheses. As indicated on Table 1, these statistical data show that the result for hypothesis A is counter to its statement; that is, there is a negative correlation between the requirement for direct support to management decision-making and scores on the 'well structured versus unstructured problems' spectrum. Hence, hypothesis A was rejected.

The results for the second hypothesis indicate a positive correlation of .21 at a significance level of .04 (further, the Tau c value is also positive at .18 with a significance of .02). Thus, the second hypothesis was confirmed.

The testing of the third hypothesis does not meet a desired significance level of .05. Thus hypothesis C is rejected on the basis of not meeting the needed significance level.

## 3.1. Qualitative Interview Findings

In support of the general hypothesis of the study, the major findings from qualitative interviews with the seventeen (17) senior officials and managers in the three program offices covered in the study are:

\- The majority of the interviewees (14 of 17) agreed with the statement that the computer is best used when it supports the management decision-making process.

\- The same majority concurred that the computer should be used for direct support to decision-making on semi-structured tasks (particularly for special analysis runs, ad hoc evaluation and analyses, project grouping analysis reports, etc.).

\- This majority thought that an ADP system should be used to support management judgments (especially in the formulation and structuring of the problems on which the judgments were to be made, or as a testing device for their judgment or intuitions).

\- A majority (10 of 17) did not see ADP as basically used for well-defined tasks to provide past or current status information, but rather as valuable for its data reduction, analysis, and presentation capabilities.

\- The majority of interviewees (14 of 17) did not think that ADP was only appropriate to routine, repetitive work activities, but saw its value to analyses, ad hoc inquiry responses, and 'what if' types of questions.

## 4. Conclusion

The quantitative study results indicate that the hypothesis of employment of advanced information management approach as supporting the management decision-making process was not confirmed. Qualitative interviews with senior managers were undertaken to obtain explanatory information. These interviews and the results obtained were supportive of the the hypothesis.

The disagreement between the quantitative and qualitative results may be due to differences in perception between the senior managers and their subordinates. Mumford has indicated that values, technology views and work ethics are different for top managers and workers in organizations [10]. Although disturbing from the standpoint of this study, other researchers have also indicated that there may be differences in value sets between managers and their work force [5,7].

The results of this study suggest that program management officials look first for information management support that will take care of all aspects of their program data, produce recurring reports, and handle collection of data involving routine, repetitive processing [11]. Once those initial requirements are satisfied, an advanced information management approach can be considered to support the management based activities of, for example, goal tracking, planning and reporting (characteristics of the MIS approach). It is unclear whether the managers surveyed in this study desire to employ an information system to support their decision-making activities more directly. They clearly do not want systems to replace their own judgment.

Implicit in our findings is a lesson for ADP support personnel. The implication is that system developers should direct their efforts to finding ways to provide desirable characteristics of the advanced information management approaches – how to make a system work to support decision-making and enhance office effectiveness [11]. Most of the agency officials surveyed and interviewed view their job as how to deliver their program effectively. At the same time, they were concerned with operational efficiency, perhaps due to declining staff levels and increasing work demands.

Another suggestion for improving information management utilization is in establishing a program of management education and training in ADP applications for information management. This could improve understanding of the possible uses of information management to support management decision-making. Most of the interviewed management officials had not considered that an information system could support unstructured or only semi-structured problems, while contributing to strategic planning and qualitative analyses. They apparently agreed that a system can support both effectiveness and efficiency in an office; they view ADP systems as supporting day-to-day office work activities and departmental requirements, but they do not consider it aiding in unstructured, nonquantitative issues. IS support personnel must show users what else can be provided through ADP systems support.

This study is only one step in a continuing effort to deliver better information systems. The issue here is how to make effective use of information technology once basic, routine, repetitive tasks have been served. Mechanisms that would improve support to management decision-making and office automation are required. These should be built into the information management systems support activities.

## Face-to-Face Interview Questions

1. Do you feel that there are management or leadership style differences between your program office and the other two major program offices? For instance, what are some of the differences as you see them?

2. Has the leadership style of your office influenced the type, or development, of the ADP system which supports your office? Please elaborate.

3. What aspects of the office, or your own, style are there that may have hindered the use of ADP technology to support your program? Why, or why not? And, if none, what style aspects have particularly aided the employment of ADP?

4. Has the ADP system positively contributed to your organization's effectiveness? Efficiency? Mission accomplishment? How so, particularly?

5. Has the ADP system held back, or negatively affected, accomplishments or effectiveness. What specifically?

6. One view in the ADP arena is that the computer is best used when it supports the management decision-making process. Do you agree with this? Why, or why not?

7. What features or capabilities are missing from your ADP system that would support your decision-making? How would these be used by you?

8. Should this support directly assist you on semi-structured tasks or problems, as opposed to well-defined ones? How would this be of aid to you?

9. Should the ADP system and its outputs be used to support your own judgments? In what way could this be done?

10. Should a computer system basically be used for well-defined tasks to provide past or current status information? Why, or why not?

11. In your view, is an ADP system only applicable to routine, repetitive work activities? Why?

12. Is an ADP system only appropriate in situations lending themselves to more efficient operations? What about your own decision-making process?

13. Can an ADP system be used to improve the effectiveness of your decision-making? In what ways?

14. Please make any other comments or observations that may have occurred to you.
