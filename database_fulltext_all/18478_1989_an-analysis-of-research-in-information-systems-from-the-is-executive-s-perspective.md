# An Analysis of Research in Information Systems from the IS Executive's Perspective

Varun Grover

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Rajiv Sabherwal

Department of Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Florida International University, Miami, FL 33199, USA

While it is imperative for IS research to address the issues of concern to practitioners in the field, it is arguable whether this is being adequately done. This paper examines the extent to which research over the past decade has addressed those issues important to practitioners. A list of 26 key issues is used to categorize 858 articles published in four major IS outlets. The amount of research on each issue is then compared with the importance rating of the issue by practitioners over the decade 1977–86. The study reveals a definite gap between what the IS executives consider important and what is being researched. It also demonstrates that IS researchers tend to concentrate on narrow technical issues rather than broad managerial issues. The implications of this study strongly suggest that IS researchers should pay greater attention to the concerns of practicing managers.

Keywords: IS research, Key issues, IS management priorities, Research priorities, IS journal orientation, IS research trends.

## Introduction

Recently, IS researchers have exhibited an inclination to explore and evaluate the progress of the IS discipline. On the one hand there have been investigations into the nature of IS research and $[4,5,7,9,16]$ to assess the extent to which the discipline has attained a distinct and significant identity. On the other hand, IS researchers have demonstrated a keen interest in identifying the issues considered important by practitioners, as may be seen from the numerous studies $[1,2,6,10,14]$ conducted with this objective over the last ten years.

As another step in self-evaluation, it explores whether we are doing the “right” research in IS. On a philosophical note, some may object to the assertion that IS research should address the current needs of practicing managers. The pragmatic orientation of IS research however, is evident in its objective of systematically investigating the development, operation and impact of an information system in an organization [11]. Further, the terminology and concepts used in IS research to date, reflect this orientation. For example, more often than not, “information” is equated to “computer-based” something and “success” is equated to use. Keen [13] describes this strong relationship to practice as healthy and prescribes that IS research point towards practice. The fact that many others subscribe to this view is evidenced by the significant influence the Dickson et al. [6] study, identifying practitioner issues, had on major research programs in the country. Nevertheless, it is the view of these authors that the relationship between practical issue and IS research should exist and be strong.

The objective of this paper is to assess the extent to which the published IS research concurs with the apparently changing importance of the key issues. The following questions are addressed:

1. How has the attention of IS researchers changed over the decade 1977–86?

2. How does this shift in focus compare with the change in importance, as viewed by practitioners, for each issue?

3. How has the relative importance of managerial/technical issues changed over the last ten years?

4. How does the profile of publications on the key issues vary across IS journals?

## Background

A number of articles have systematically identified the issues that are important to the IS practitioners. Various methodologies, such as questionnaire surveys, interviews, and Delphi studies, have been used to elicit the issues considered critical by general managers, IS executives, etc. For each of these studies, the year of the study, the data collection methodology the nature of respondents, sample size, and most important issues, are shown in Table 1. The studies by Martin and Hartog and Herbert are not directly comparable with the other three and are based on a dissimilar set of respondents. However, the issues raised in the 1980 study map reasonable well to those in the 1983 and 1986 Delphi studies. Moreover, all three used SMIS membership. These studies are thus compatible and may be assumed to capture the relative importance of issues in three distinct time periods. Branchaeu and Wetherbe have compared the three studies and examined the shift in importance of 26 key issues over these time periods. These issues, listed in Table 2, were used to classify the IS research published in the period 1977–1986.

## Methodology

## The journals selected

The choice of journals for the study was based on past citation studies [5,7] and a study of IS journals [8]. In a citation analysis to study the knowledge utilization among MIS researchers, Hamilton and Ives used MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, and Data Base as the core MIS journals. Moreover, these journals were found to be among the top ten in a study of MIS journal communication [8], and were three of the only four journals $^{1}$ in the top ten in which the MIS articles constituted more than half of all published. In a recent co-citation analysis, however, Culnan and Swanson, considered MIS Quarterly and the Proceedings of the International Conference on Information System (ICIS) as representing the MIS field. As they pointed out, and also documented by Cougar [3], ICIS Proceedings have gained prestige as a leading publication outlet for MIS researchers. In the present study, therefore, three core MIS journals, MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, and Database, and the ICIS Proceedings, were chosen for consideration. However, because Database is non-refereed, although individually analyzed, it was not included in aggregate statistics.

## Research method

The two authors carefully read the rationales and discussion for the 26 issues in the three papers [1,2,6] to get a clear understanding of these issues. Then, working independently, the authors examined the titles of the 858 articles. For each, the extent to which it contributed to each of the 26 issues was rated using a four point scale. "3" indicated that the reviewer was very confident that the article addressed the issue, "2" meant moderately confident, "1" implied unsure, and "0" meant the reviewer considered the article to be unrelated.

The ratings of the two authors were then added. 288 articles which had a total score of 0–1 were rejected. Abstracts of the 256 articles receiving a total score of 2–4 on an issue were studied to determine whether it should be included into the issue or not. This resulted in the rejection of another 47 articles. 314 articles which had a scores of 5-6 were directly accepted. If an article was seen as contributing towards more than one issue, as with 78 articles, it was divided equally among these issues, and the appropriate fraction counted towards each issue. This ensured that all the selected articles contributed equally towards the results. The 523 articles that mapped onto one or more issues were distributed among the four journals as shown in Table 3.

The above procedure is similar to that employed by Hamilton and Ives [8] who looked at the table of contents to decide which articles were relevant to IS. However, here there was a need to map the articles onto 26 categories, and it might not have been accurate to depend solely on the title of the article. Therefore, to reduce the margin for error, the title was used only in case of very high degree of confidence (article issues scores of 0–1 or 5–6) $^{2}$ . To further establish the validity of the mapping process, a random selection was made of 35 articles from the 314 with a score of 5–6 and 15 articles from the 288 with a score of 0–1, six weeks after the initial exercise. The abstracts of these 50 articles were then read by each author, without considering earlier ratings. By following the previous procedure, it was found that only 3 (of 35) articles mapped onto different issues, and only 1 (of 15) article that had been rejected earlier was found relevant. The consistency is thus high (92 percent), especially considering that test-retest reliability is also factored into this figure. This establishes the validity of the earlier exercise.

IS is a pragmatic field and thus research in this area should respond to practitioner needs. To estimate the extent to which IS research does address issues key to IS executives, Spearman's rank-order correlations were run between the perceived importance and publication rankings for each of the three periods. A significant (at 5% level) correlation between the two ranking was obtained for 1981–83. However, the coefficients were not significant (even at a 10% level) for the other periods. On observing these negative results, the authors proceeded to explore if this was because of the time taken by researchers to research, and publish, on the issues important to practitioners. This assessment of whether publications lag behind the practitioners was made using rank-order correlations between 1980 perceived importance and 1981–83 publications and between 1983 perceived importance and 1984–86 publications. Neither correlation was significant. Similar negative results were found on doing rank-order correlations to see if publications lead practitioners.

An interesting question was the extent to which the change in importance ranking between periods paralleled the change in publication rankings between the same periods. This was studied using Chi-Square statistics to test for independence between the increase/decrease in issue importance rankings and publication rankings. The hypothesis for whether changes in issue importance rankings lead or lag changes in publication rankings were also tested. None of the Chi-Square statistics were significant at the 10% level. This implies that the changes in publication percentages are independent of changes in the criticality of these issues as considered by the IS executives. This result, along with the negative results obtained from rank-order correlations, shows that IS research is not consistent with the practitioner needs.

## Trends for the 26 issues

For every year in the 1977–86 period, publication for each issue was computed as a percentage of the total publications selected for that year. Twenty six regressions were then run to see how the percentage publications for each issue changed with time. The F-ratio was observed to see the overall significance of each model. Table 5 illustrates the results of the regressions. It can be seen that six issues resulted in significant results (at 5% level).

Of the issues that yielded significant results, two - IS Role and Contribution and Aligning the IS Organization - showed negative beta values indicating that in the last ten years, publications on these issues have been decreasing. On the other hand, four issues - Using Information Systems for Competitive Advantage, Organizational Learning, Artificial Intelligence and Computer Graphics showed a significant (at $5\%$ level) positive trends.

In the light of the change in importance ranking as perceived by practitioners, two of these results were unexpected. Firstly, the increase in importance of IS Role & Contribution has not been matched by publications in the area which have been falling. Secondly, Aligning the IS Organization with that of the enterprise has been increasing in importance, especially since centralization vs. decentralization issues are coming to the forefront with the advent of end-user computing and telecommunications. However, research in this area has declined.

As opposed to the above disconcerting trends, there has been a significant increase in publication percentages for Using Information Systems for a Competitive Advantage, Organizational Learning, and Artificial Intelligence. Each of these issues has also increased in perceived importance over the last ten years and the trends in publications are thus in the same direction. The fourth issue for which publication percentage has significantly increased – Computer Graphics – increased in prominence from 1980 to 1983 but has since been considered less important.

Decision Support Systems has been by far the most researched IS topic in the last ten years. Maybe the positive effect of this research has resulted in a decline in its importance to practitioners. However, while it is no longer seen as a key issue, research on this topic continues on surprisingly unabated, and in fact, exhibits an increasing trend. Possibly due to the heavy investment IS researchers have made in this area, the “fad” continues.

Measuring IS Effectiveness ranked second in publication order. This has been a major problem for IS executive as perceived importance studies have consistently indicated, but it has reduced in importance during the last ten years. According to Branchaeu and Wetherbe, this may be due to a title difference ${}^{3}$ between the 1983 and 1986 instruments, or because of frustration due to failure to tackle the issue. However, general managers still view this issue as highly important and ranked it fourth, and continued research attention is well-justified.

IS Human Resources is another issue which has received consistently high research attention. As Branchaeu, and Wetherbe point out, this could be the reason for its decrease importance to IS executives. There has been a steady rise in the production of qualified IS personnel and, moreover, turnover rates for IS professionals have fallen considerably, further explaining the decreased importance of the issue. However, the reduced prominence of the issue does not seem to have affected the research efforts directed at this issue as it ranked fourth in publications is 1984–86 and only twelfth in perceived importance in 1986. Perhaps IS researchers and teachers have a natural bias towards exploring new ways of training IS personnel.

IS Role and Contribution was the fourth most published issue in the last decade; it also ranked fourth in executive importance in 1986. There is, however, a marked difference between the trends in perceived importance and publication percentages. While the importance of this issue to IS executives has increased dramatically over the last ten years. The attention it has received from the researchers has reduced, as may be seen from its negative regression coefficient and its publication ranking. This issue involves both improving the understanding of IS role and also marketing the IS function to top management; maybe the difficulty in researching such a global issue is the reason for the divergence between practitioner and publication rankings.

Strategic Planning for IS has, over the last ten years, been consistently viewed as the most important issue by IS executives. It has received considerable research attention and has been among the ten most researched topics in each of the three periods – 1978–80, 1981–83, and 1984–86.

End User Computing has decreased in importance since 1983. This may be because IS executives now have more experience in this area and the issue has also benefited from considerable research. However, research on this issue continues to increase, despite its declining importance, possibly due to inertia.

Software Development has received the seventh highest research attention in the last decade. Its importance to IS executives has dropped considerably since 1983 but it continues to be highly researched. While the decline in importance of an issue so crucial to IS is surprising, the continued high levels of research on the topic reflect its specific and relatively well-defined nature $^{4}$ .

IS Alignment in the Organization is another issue on which the researchers and practitioners have exhibited startling discord. While the importance of this issue to IS executives has been increasing over the last ten years, the attention of researchers has been decreasing, as may be seen from its negative regression coefficient and the decline in its publication ranking.

Telecommunications, which ranked eleventh in the 1986 Delphi study has been the ninth most researched issue in 1977–86. Apparently, the proliferation of technologies and standards that facilitate communication links have contributed to the increased importance given to this issue by both practitioners and researchers.

Using Information Systems for Competitive Advantage, the tenth most researched issue in 1977–86, is another issue on which practitioners and researchers concur. The increasing research attention given to this issue compares well with the dramatic increase in its perceived importance.

## Notable absentees

Of the ten 1986 issues seen as the most important by IS executives, four (Organizational Learning, Data as a Corporate Resource, Developing Information Architecture, and Integrating Technologies) are not among the ten most researched issues in 1977–86. None of these issues is among the ten most published issues for 1984-86 either. Moreover, of these issues only Organizational Learning has exhibited an increasing trend in publication percentage.

To see how the percentage publications addressing managerial issues have changed over time for each journal/proceedings, four regressions were run. The results of these are shown in Table 9. All the beta coefficients are found to be negative but were significant (at the 5% level) only for MIS Quarterly and Information and Management. This indicates a decrease in percentage publications addressing managerial issues. Moreover, on looking at the constants and coefficients for the four publication outlets, it appears that Information and Management started off with the greatest orientation towards managerial issues but exhibited the greatest shift towards technical issues. Data Base is on the other extreme. It started off with the least orientation towards managerial issues but exhibited the least shift towards technical issues. It would thus appear that the orientation of the four journals towards managerial and technical issues would not be parallel over time. A Chow Test was conducted to see if this is indeed true. It showed that the hypothesis that the four journals have a similar orientation with respect to percentage of publications about managerial issues can be rejected (at 10% level).

## Conclusion

A close look at the results of the study indicates that the issues seem to fall into three distinct groups:

Group 1: Includes issues for which research has apparently fallen short of the perceived importance. This group includes IS Role and Contribution and IS Alignment in the Organization – for both issues, importance has increased but research has declined. The group includes Strategic Planning for IS, which has remained the most important issue, but for which the research has decreased and also Data as a Corporate Resource, Developing Information Architecture, Integrating Technologies, and Organizational Learning, for which importance has increased considerably but research has not kept pace.

2: Comprises issues on which there has been considerable research, and possibly as a result of this effort, the issue has become less important. However, while the importance has decreased, research on the issue has continued unabated. This group includes Decision Support Systems, End User Computing, Software Development, Telecommunications, and IS Human Resources.

Group 3: Represents the normative ideal. It consists of issues for which importance and publication percentages have both increased at a comparable pace. It includes Measuring IS Effectiveness and Using Information Systems for Competitive Advantage.

Branchaeu and Wetherbe have indicated that some of these issues are broad and global in nature, from which several other narrow and specific issues evolve. Our findings suggest that IS research has fallen short of perceived importance in the case of global issues. On the other hand, on the more narrow and specific issues, there has been a significant amount of research, but this seems to have considerable inertia, as it continues even after the issue has decreased in importance.

In summary, this study indicates a disconcerting gap between what the IS executives consider as important and what is actually researched. To reiterate the specific findings:

(a) Comparison of publication rankings with issue importance rankings and of their changes demonstrated that they are independent of one another.

(b) Two key issues - IS Role and Contribution and Aligning the IS Organization - which have increased dramatically in importance to practitioners in the last decade, were found to receive decreasing research attention.

(c) While managerial issues have been increasing in importance to IS executives, the research on them has been slackening and research on technical issues has been increasing.

(d) IS research on global issues has not kept pace with their importance to executives. On the other hand, specific issues continue to be highly researched even after they are no longer considered key by executives.

(e) A comparison of the journals indicate that Data Base and ICIS Proceedings have been oriented more towards specific, technical issues compared to MIS Quarterly and Information and Management. The study also showed that all the four publication outlets exhibit an increasing concentration on technical issues.

The study suffers from some drawbacks: it considers only four publication outlets and utilizes mapping based on abstracts rather than full articles. However, within these constraints, the findings are strong and surprising.

In an 1985 colloquium on IS research, McFarlan [15] discussed some barriers to IS research. A majority of IS researchers come from a data processing background and the result is a lack of skills in business policy, organizational behavior, organizational learning, and management of technology. Moreover, many IS doctoral programs unwittingly stress technology and narrowness. This situation is only worsened by the bias that universities have towards quantitative and structured studies of smaller problems rather than qualitative investigations of complex, messy problems.

It thus appears that IS research has focused on narrow, technical issues that are probably easier to research and may even be more likely to be accepted for publication. Also, after the researchers make initial investment in a specific issue, it becomes a reason to continue research, even after it falls in importance. “Publish or perish” pressures and (perceived) journal preferences seem to have a major impact on IS research, rather than criticality of issues. Setting priorities for future research has been a major objective of the several studies that have identified key issues. Isn’t is the responsibility of IS researchers to utilize these findings – in order to become more relevant and useful to practicing managers? Herein lies a major challenge for future IS research which we cannot afford to ignore.
