Decision theory is a formal basis for considering human decision making. It has typically focused on humans and even if the decision-maker is assisted in the process, it is assumed that the assistance is provided by another human. However, in the computer age the decision-maker is assisted more often than not by a computer. Hence in this paper we explore the rationale for an integrated human-computer information processor and consider the information processing capabilities of the human and the computer within a formal model of decision-making. The analysis for the computer is done assuming it has at least the capabilities of a decision support system.

Keywords: Decision making, Decision support systems, Information processing, Human-computer information processor.

## 2. The Human and Computer Information Processing Capabilities

## 2.1. The Human Information Processor

The human information processor can be viewed as operating in a manner suggested in Fig. 1. The language capability and the senses directly interact with the environment, and the acquired information is processed utilizing mental skills. The memory contains facts and problem solving information that has been learned formally or through experience.

It should be noted that this model of an information processor is not radically different from that proposed by Newell and Simon [15]. Fig. 2 represents this view of the information processing system. From these, it is clear that the receptors and effectors in the Newell and Simon framework correspond to the language and senses used to receive and communicate information in decision problems. The primary difference is that we make the assumption that mental skills are what make the processor an effective decision-problem solver, and these skills are utilized to relate what is observed in the environment to what is held in memory. In this paper, the processor is considered primarily as hardware which alone cannot achieve anything, but is dependent on mental skills. However, the capabilities of the processor determine how complex an analysis can be performed.

The limitations of the human information processor arise from the limited capabilities of the three components. To begin with, the limited attention span, as well as the inability of humans to process large volumes of information, result in selective selection of environmental cues on which to base decisions. This feature of humans could lead to ignoring important information, while placing undue importance on other information [8].

Human memory, which plays a major role in decision making, affects it in several crucial ways. First, one cannot store all the information pertinent to a problem domain. Additionally, retrieval is affected by the vividness or recency of stored information, rather than its relevance to the problem at hand $[16]$ . Secondly, memory also contains the past experiences of the decision-maker; however, in utilizing it, two problems arise: humans cannot access all past processing information, since several important parts may have been distorted or simply forgotten; also what has been “learned” by the individual about an outcome in the past may be wrong due to judgmental bias $[8]$ .

Additionally, the computational skills of humans are limited. This results in making simplifying assumptions about the problem, or utilizing heuristics to solve it. Although the utilization of heuristics aids in solving the problem faster, they also lead to biases in judgement, especially when making probabilistic judgments $[18]$ .

It may be argued that a formal decision setting may not require heuristics, since a vast array of computational models can be used to analyze the variables affecting the decision problem. However, solving the problem by hand using the models may be a very time-consuming job. If time is an important constraint in the decision problem, one may not have time to characterize the problem adequately. In addition, cost and accuracy are important, and both of these suffer when humans alone solve problems.

## 2.2. The Computer's Capabilities - Decision Support Systems

A DSS can be viewed as an information processing system that is imbedded within a decision making system [1]. Following Bonczek et al. [1], a generic DSS is viewed as having three principal components: a language system (LS), a knowledge system (KS), and a problem processing system (PPS) (Fig. 3). The PPS is software corresponding to the mental skills of the human. The language system provides the communication link between the human information processor and the computer. It is characterized by the syntax furnished to the humans, and the statements, commands, or expressions that the human may use. The LS also contains the response language.

The knowledge system contains knowledge relevant to solving problems in a problem domain. This typically includes large volumes of facts or data, subsets of which are relevant to making good decisions for a particular problem. In addition to facts, the KS contains problem solving information as well as models pertinent to problem solving in the domain.

The PPS forms the link between the human queries interfaced through the LS and the information available in the KS. It is the PPS which has the problem solving capabilities and provides such functions as information collection, model formulation, problem recognition and analysis.

![](/api/attachments/QHTQHASX/fulltext/images/59635cf315547d2a7e7f982352926b7483da170f6b0f26885250f4ce8485da67.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Structure of a Decision Support System.

In the above description, the issues of knowledge presentation, language processing, etc. are not considered. Though these are important issues the focus here will be on DSS capabilities which enhance human decision-making.

Two features of interest are memory capabilities and speed of processing, both of which are constantly being improved. The result is that the DSS can solve problems of increasing complexity faster and also form a reservoir of information due to its storage facilities. Thus the KS of a DSS provides a repository of facts that a human typically is not capable of remembering. Utilizing a DSS would also eliminate biases when humans utilize only their memory. In addition, humans can utilize the computer's processing capabilities to save time and obtain accurate solutions. When complex models are utilized, the computer's processing capability outstrips that of the human.

One of the major advantages of utilizing the computer in the decision process is the elimination of those biases that arise when decisions are made under time pressure; thus biases arising from utilizing heuristics such as availability, representativeness, and adjustment and anchoring could possibly be eliminated by utilizing computers in the decision process, thus improving the decisions.

## 3. The Integrated Human-Computer Information Processor

Computers are being utilized increasingly in a decision making capacity; evidence of this is the proliferation of expert systems. A possible reason for this is their performance. In analyzing the performance of MYCIN, Harmon and King [7] attribute its superior performance to four factors:

(a) A detailed and comprehensive knowledge base.

(b) The fact that it considers every known possibility.

(c) It never jumps to conclusions, and asks for all key pieces of information.

(d) Constant updating, based on new information that specialists may not remember in their diagnostic procedure.

In spite of its capabilities, there are three features which make computers unattractive as decision-makers. If a certain amount of risk is involved, as in medical diagnosis, there is a tendency for the computer to perform an exhaustive analysis, as in the MYCIN case. Thus, if there are cost and time constraints, the system may not be in a position to make a decision, because there is insufficient data. By incorporating the human in the process we can improve the overall system. First, since the human and computer can perform actions in parallel, the total information available for decision making can be increased within the same time interval. Secondly, the human, by assuming the risk, can make a decision, even though sufficient information may not be available to make a decision with certainty. An additional argument for incorporating the human in the decision process in domains such as medicine relates to the legal liability issue. The human may not be willing to assume the risk when an expert system makes the decision and all he/she has to do is implement it. This could be one of the reasons why expert systems are not being readily accepted in the medical field. Finally, computers are not yet able to be creative.

Creativity has been characterized by Bruner [2] as “An act that produces effective surprise.” He goes on to elaborate on three kinds of effectiveness.

(a) Predictive - the discovery of laws or relations which allow the prediction of phenomena.

(b) Formal - determining the ordering of elements in such a way that one sees relationships that were not evident before.

(c) Metaphoric - connecting domains of experience that were previously apart.

In order to be creative, one has to go through the creative process: its stages appear similar to the decision process. Johnson [9] identifies the stages as:

(i) Preparation - where the individual collects information or prepares for the creative activity.

(ii) Production - when ideas relevant to the problem are generated.

(iii) Judgement - evaluation of the ideas prior to selecting a creative solution.

Though a computer can assist the creative process, for example, in the preparation and judgement stages, its capability to be creative would be restricted to the ingenuity of the programmer. However, in the preparation stage the computer could provide information to the human that may not be stored in the human memory. In the judgement phase, one can effectively use the computer to answer 'What if?' questions. Thus several ideas or creative solutions can be evaluated before a final judgement is made. This is routinely done in computer-aided design.

One area in which computers have their greatest limitation is in considering novel situations; here human creativity comes into play. Typically the person searches past experience in diverse areas in order to cope with the situation; computers on the other hand cannot cope with this, since one cannot program what one has not encountered. Thus, creativity in the form of metaphoric effectiveness still seems to belong to humans.

Given the complexity and unstructuredness of decision problems, it is most effective to have the human and computer combine information processing capabilities. This leads to the concept of the integrated human-computer information processor.

In considering an integrated human-machine information processor, one can visualize three scenarios. Fig. 4 depicts the framework in which the present work is conducted. The human is considered to interact directly with the environment and has to input the relevant information to the computer. The computer has historical information as well as problem solving information that can be utilized in solving the problem.

## 4. Model II

The decision problem in our framework is defined by eight elements [13,14]:

$$
\boldsymbol {D} = \left\langle X, \Phi , D, \omega^ {*}, A, \left\{\boldsymbol {M} _ {a} \mid a \in A \right\}, c, r \right\rangle ,
$$

where X is the set of possibly mutually exclusive states, defined as

$$
X = \Theta \times Z,
$$

where both $\Theta$ and Z are finite, and $\Theta$ represents the unobservable and Z the observable characteristics of the environment, or $\Theta$ represent the hypothesis, and Z represents the results of the experiments conducted to verify the hypothesis.

A is the set of available “initial” (information-gathering) actions or experiments.

$\Phi(\Theta, z)$ represents the joint distribution. For each $z \in Z$ the marginal distribution $g(z) > 0$ , is given by

$$
g (z) = \sum_ {\theta \in \Theta} \Phi (\theta , z) \quad \text { for } \quad z \in Z.
$$

For each $a \in A$ there is a finite signal space $Z_a$ and a conditional density function $h_a: \Theta \times Z_a \to [0, 1]$ ; where

$$
h _ {a} (\theta , z _ {a}) = P r (z _ {a} | \theta);
$$

that is, $h_{a}(\theta, z_{a})$ is the probability of observing $z_{a}$ given $\theta$ , with action or experiment a chosen.

$M_{a}$ is the information structure associated with action $a \in A$ .

Letting

$$
Z = \prod_ {a \in A} Z _ {a},
$$

the information sets $M_{ay}$ are taken to be of the form

$$
M _ {a y} = \left\{\left(\theta , z\right) \in \Theta \times Z \mid z _ {a} = y \right\} = \Theta \times I _ {a y},
$$

where

$$
I _ {a y} = \left\{z \in Z \mid z _ {a} = y \right\}
$$

where $y \in y_{a} = \{1, \ldots, n(a)\}$ is the set of information signals which can be observed on performing action a. Therefore $M_{a} = \{M_{a1}, \ldots, M_{a,n(a)}\} \forall a \in A$ .

D is the set of available (final) decisions.

$\omega^{*}: X \times D \times R \rightarrow R$ is the payoff function (the inclusion of the third variable allows for the effect of the cost of information-gathering on payoffs).

$c: A \rightarrow R_{+}$ is the cost function; $c(a)$ is the cost of utilizing action $a \in A$ .

r is a positive integer representing the number of information-gathering actions that can be taken before a final decision is made.

The decision problem is visualized as follows. The decision-maker's payoff depends upon the true state of the world, $\hat{\theta}$ , the (final) decision (d) taken, and the costs (c) incurred in acquiring information. The decision-maker (d-m) can take $r$ information-gathering actions before making a final decision, and with each information-gathering action (or experiment) there is a finite set $(Y_a)$ of possible signals (or results). If the d-m performs experiment a and observes $y$ from $Y_a$ , then the true state $(\hat{\theta})$ is an element of $\Theta \times I_{ay}$ . With this information in hand, the d-m must decide whether to choose another experiment from the available set, $A$ [thereby obtaining a more accurate prediction of the value of $\hat{\theta}$ , but also incurring additional costs, $c(a)$ ], or a final decision from $D$ , based on the information already obtained.

We can see, then, that a decision strategy which begins with an experiment must specify (for each possible outcome; i.e., each y in $Y_{1}$ ) which additional experiment (if any) will be performed, or which final decision (d) will be chosen. A complete strategy for the solution of the problem must then specify each of these choices for each of the possible steps. For each such strategy, one can determine an expected gross payoff and an expected cost of information-gathering; and from these two expectations, one can find the expected net payoff, the difference between expected gross payoff and expected cost. Our assumption is that the goal of the decision-maker is to find a feasible strategy which has an expected net payoff at least as high as any other feasible strategy.

Since the capabilities of the human and computer are analyzed based on the impact on the payoff for the decision-maker, the form of the expected gross payoff is:

If $\pmb{B}_q = \Theta \times I_q$

then for $B\in \pmb{B}_q$

$$
B = \Theta \times I \quad \text { where } \quad I \subseteq I _ {q}.
$$

The expected gross payoff can be represented as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Omega (\sigma) = \sum_ {B \in B _ {r + 1}} \sum_ {(\theta , z) \in B} \Phi (\theta , z) \omega (\theta , z, d, t), \\ \qquad = \sum_ {I \in I _ {r + 1}} \sum_ {z \in I} g (z) \sum_ {\theta \in \Theta} (\Phi (\theta , z) / g (z)) \\ \qquad \times \omega (\theta , z, \delta (I) t), \\ \qquad = \sum_ {I \in I _ {r + 1}} \Pi (I) \sum_ {z \in I} P r (z / I) \sum_ {\theta \in \Theta} P r (\theta / z) \\ \qquad \times \omega (\theta , z, \delta (I), t), \end{array}
$$

where $\Pi(I)=\Sigma_{z\in I}g(z)$ and $B_{r+1}$ is the information structure resulting from performing r information-gathering actions ( $B_{1}=X$ in the framework).

## 5. The Human and Computer Capabilities: A Formal Analysis

The analysis in this section utilizes Model II. It will be seen that the information-processing limitations of humans impact the payoff as a result of incorrect assumptions of the probability of events occurring, given specific information and incorrect perceptions of the information generated by an information-gathering signal.

## 5.1. The Human

Because the unaided human decision-maker has essentially three sets of information-gathering actions, $A_{h}^{1}$ can be considered to be of the form:

$$
A _ {h} ^ {1} = A _ {h e} \cup A _ {h m} \cup A _ {h c},
$$

where the set of actions are:

$A_{he}$ performed in the environment.

$A_{hm}$ that utilize human memory.

$A_{hc}$ that is utilized to perform computations.

## 5.1.1. Environmental Actions

Actions performed on the environment can generate a wealth of information. However, because of limited attention capabilities, a human may not be able to assimilate all the information received. In order to reduce the amount of information to be considered, humans typically select only a subset of the information observed; this feature is called selective cuing. In addition to this, lack of attention could result in humans not observing all the relevant information. The following example illustrates the result of selective cuing and inattention

Let

$$
\begin{array}{r l} Z = \left\{z = \left(z _ {1}, z _ {2}, \dots , z _ {4}\right) | 0 \leq z _ {i} \leq 2 \quad \text { for } \right. \\ i = 1, \dots , 4 \}. \end{array}
$$

Suppose the result of an action $a$ is the signal $y = (y_{1}, y_{2})$ which results in

$$
I _ {a y} = \left\{z \in Z \mid z _ {1} = y _ {1} \quad \text { and } \quad z _ {2} = y _ {2} \right\},
$$

however if as a result of selective cueing or inattention, the human only observes $y = (y_{1})$ , then

$$
I _ {a y} ^ {s} = \left\{z \in Z \mid z _ {1} = y _ {1} \right\}.
$$

This implies that

$$
I _ {a y} \subset I _ {a y} ^ {s}.
$$

This obviously results in

$$
M _ {a y} \subset M _ {a y} ^ {s},
$$

which would result in

$$
M _ {a y} > M _ {a y} ^ {s}.
$$

It is evident that if ‘a’ is an action that is performed in a strategy then the final partition obtained in the selective cueing case is going to be coarser then if all the information were considered, and this would probably reduce the net payoff (as in [10]).

## 5.1.2. Actions on Memory

The actions performed on memory are

(a) retrieval of data,

(b) retrieval of knowledge (techniques for interpreting data as well as manipulating it).

Two problems arise in the performance of these actions, related to the nature of human memory and how the probability of $\theta$ given z is perceived: size of memory and distortion of information in memory.

Human memory can store only a finite amount of information about a problem domain, both in terms of the data and the knowledge required to solve the problem. Tests have shown that information stored and utilized are based on the vividness of the information [16]. This then influences the perception of $Pr(\theta \mid z)$ , since for a given signal, a vividly recalled event would be considered highly likely.

Information stored in human memory is often distorted as time progresses. It can also be lost as a result of 'forgetting'. This implies that for some events $Pr(\theta \mid z) \to 0$ while for others it tends to one, this skewing of information may not necessarily be valid given the marginal distribution $f(\theta)$ . Thus in a given $B \in B_{r+1}$ one may associate $Pr(\theta_1 \mid z) > Pr(\theta_2 \mid z)$ even though it is not true; which would then bias the final decision taken towards $\theta_1$ .

The selective retention of information in memory also affects the Z space considered by the human for problem solving. As a result of forgetting or selective retention of information one would consider only some $Z'$ such that

$$
Z ^ {\prime} \subset Z,
$$

where Z is the correct space to be considered. As a result of this, one may consider only those actions which give information about $Z'$ . For any information-gathering action a, the impact of considering only $Z'$ is that,

$$
I _ {a y} ^ {\prime} = \left(I _ {a y} \cap Z ^ {\prime}\right) \subset I _ {a y}.
$$

Ignoring the presence of some of the $z \in Z$ could lead to incorrect decisions as the final decision is based on I.

## 5.1.3. Computational Actions

Computation implies using models or algorithms to solve problems. It could entail using concepts in probability and statistics. The computational skills of humans are limited, and as the complexity of the computation increases, both the time spent on computation and the inaccuracies in the results increase. In order to simplify the problem, humans generally use heuristics. These, while simplifying the problem, could also lead to biases that could be avoided. Here we focus on three common heuristics utilized when probabilistic judgments have to be made, namely, representativeness, availability, adjustment, and anchoring [18].

Representativeness is a heuristic that humans use to determine the probability of $E$ 's being a member of class $G$ . In making the judgement, humans use similarity or representativeness with other members of the class as criteria. However, in making this judgement they tend to ignore base rate information. Also there is a tendency for humans to make a decision about the value of $\theta$ given some $z$ , even though $z$ has no bearing on $\theta$ . That is, even if $Pr(\theta | z) = Pr(\theta)$ , the judgments made given $z$ generally assume they are not equal.

The availability heuristic is tied to the vividness criteria. In making a judgment about the frequency of an event, one tends to judge events higher that can be recalled easily, and these are generally those events which are vividly remembered. Within the current theoretical framework if $\theta_{1}$ and $\theta_{2}$ are two events which are equally likely given Z and if $\theta_{1}$ is an event which is vividly associated with z then, even if $Pr(\theta_{1}|z)=Pr(\theta_{2}|z)$ , this heuristic would lead to $Pr(\theta_{1}|z)>Pr(\theta_{2}|z)$ being utilized.

Besides affecting the posterior probabilities of $\theta$ , the availability heuristic can also affect the information inferred from an observed signal. The error in information inferred occurs as a result of availability leading to illusory correlation, that is the overestimation of the co-occurrences of two events. This would imply that on observing, for example, signal $y_{1}$ , the decision-maker also infers $\hat{z}_{2}$ , in which case the information inferred from $y_{1}$ is

$$
I _ {a y _ {1}} ^ {\prime} = \left\{z \in Z \mid z _ {1} = y _ {1} \quad \text { and } \quad z _ {2} = \hat {z} _ {2} \right\},
$$

since the actual partition resulting from $y_{1}$ is

$$
I _ {a y _ {1}} = \left\{z \in Z \mid z _ {1} = y _ {1} \right\} \supseteq I _ {a y _ {1}} ^ {\prime}.
$$

By considering $I_{ay_{1}}^{\prime}$ , one excludes the states in $I_{ay_{1}} - I_{ay_{1}}^{\prime}$ from consideration; which would generally lead to incorrect decisions.

Adjustment and anchoring occurs when a solution method starts with an initial estimate and adjusts it while progressing toward a solution. However, the adjustment may be insufficient, thus biasing the solution towards the initial estimate. Within our framework this could be perceived as starting with an estimate of $Pr(\theta \mid z)$ that is lower than the true value and as further experiments are performed $Pr(\theta \mid z \cap z_{1} \cap z_{2} \ldots \cap z_{n})$ would be perceived as having a lower value than if one had started with the correct value of $Pr(\theta \mid z)$ .

## 5.2. The Computer

The computer performing environmental actions is not considered here, though computers analyzing environmental information from sensors is not uncommon.

Based on this assumption, the computer actions can be characterized as

$$
A _ {c} ^ {1} = A _ {c m} \cup A _ {c o},
$$

where $A_{cm}$ denotes the set of actions primarily performed on computer memory. This would be mainly retrieval of both data and problem solving information. $A_{co}$ is the set of computational actions available to the computer.

Although the memory of a computer is finite, its capability to store voluminous data in a particular problem domain outstrips that of the human. The banking and airline industry can be seen as examples in which this capability of the computer is utilized. Also computer memory gives no distortion or forgetfulness over time, nor does vividly have any role in retrieval. Thus if the input information is correct, it will be stored as such. Therefore, for any action $a \in A_{cm}$ one can define a function $f_{a}$ such that

$f_{a}:\Theta \to Z$ such that

$$
M _ {a y} = f _ {a} ^ {- 1} \left(I _ {a y}\right) \times I _ {a y} \quad \text { for } \quad I _ {a y} \subseteq z.
$$

Clearly, given the computer's memory and computational capabilities, heuristics such as representativeness, availability, and adjustment and anchoring need not be utilized for problem solving, thus eliminating the biases which result from utilizing them.

The effectiveness of the computer to aid the human is dependent on utilizing its memory capabilities as well as making sure that the correct problem solving techniques are implemented. Although vividness and forgetfulness are not characteristics which the computer utilizes to retrieve information, one could get incorrect results if only part of the relevant information needed for problem solving is stored. Thus, if $Z'$ is the information stored instead of Z, where $Z' \subset Z$ , then the result of utilizing $Z'$ instead of Z is identical to that of selective retention in the case of humans.

Although researchers have implemented systems with rudimentary learning capabilities $[11,12]$ , a major drawback in current computers is its capability to learn as humans do. This implies, that the problem solving techniques which are implemented should be correct or else the computer will consistently give incorrect results. This within the model translates to making sure the function $f_{a}$ is accurate. If $f_{a}$ is not accurate then either

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {(a)} & M _ {a y} ^ {\prime} = f _ {a} ^ {- 1} \big (I _ {a y} \big) X I _ {a y} \subset M _ {a y}, \\ \text {(b)} & M _ {a y} ^ {\prime} = f _ {a} ^ {- 1} \big (I _ {a y} \big) X I _ {a y} \supset M _ {a y}. \end{array}
$$

In the first case one would not be considering states which are relevant, in fact it is possible the true state itself might not be considered when making a decision. In the second case one would be considering states which need not be considered, this could lead to the need for performing other, possibly costly, information-gathering actions to obtain additional information. In such a situation a human, given his/her learning capabilities would consistently perform better than the system or a human-computer information processor (assuming the human does not realize the errors being made by the computer). However, given the inherent capabilities of the human and computer and assuming the correctness and completeness of the information and problem solving techniques stored in the computer, an integrated human-computer information processor will certainly be able to complement each others skills to solve decision problems more efficiently and effectively.

## 6. Conclusion

The limitations of the human information-processing capabilities not only lead to the incorrect determination of $Pr(\theta|z)$ , but can also affect the perception of $I_{ay}$ , which in turn affects the gross payoff. It is argued here that, by utilizing the computer's memory and computational power, it is possible not only to obtain accurate values of $Pr(\theta \mid z)$ , but also to undertake rigorous analysis; this may not be feasible if humans solve the problem unaided.

By utilizing a DSS it may be possible to eliminate some of the biases that arise in human decision making as a result of human limitations. However, it is possible when constructing expert systems, etc., the specialists' heuristics are also incorporated and thus the system could include the expert's biases. To a certain extent these might be eliminated in the process of constructing the system as one formalizes the experts knowledge.

Often, when expertise from several different domains are needed to solve a problem, a group is formed. The individuals comprising the group would have the requisite capabilities to solve the problem. However, even in such situations not only the group members individually, but also the group as a whole could be assisted by a computer. Issues relating to such support is addressed by researchers in Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) (for example [3]).

In spite of its capabilities, one major limitation of the computer is that it generally utilizes historical information and knowledge for problem solving; however in a dynamic environment, novel situations could arise which require the coupling of the historical information with the ingenuity of the human. Thus an integrated human-computer information processor would be a more effective decision-maker than either one acting individually.
