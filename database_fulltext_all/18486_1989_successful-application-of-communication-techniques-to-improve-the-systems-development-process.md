# Successful Application of Communication Techniques to Improve the Systems Development Process \*

Robert P. Bostrom

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

Inadequate system requirements specifications reflect ineffective communication transactions between system users and developers. Today, effective communication between developers and users is more important than ever as organizations redirect resources to the development of decision/expert and communication support systems and to helping users develop their own systems. This paper reports on an exploratory study which tested the use of the Precision Model, a generalized communication model that draws upon a set of communication behaviors to facilitate effective communication between users and developers. These behaviors were incorporated into a general format for running design team meetings. The findings indicate that the new meeting format improved the communication between users and developers and enhanced their ability to develop shared, accurate and complete system requirements. In addition, the use of this format led to a reduction in the number of and length of meetings. It also demonstrated that developers were better able to develop and maintain rapport with users and that team members felt more productive and satisfied when meetings concluded. This research identifies specific behaviors and guidelines that can be used to improve the requirements definition process in any systems development project.

Keywords: Systems development, Communication, Meetings, Communication skills/behaviors, User-designer relationship.

## Introduction

It is still too often the case that Computer-Based Information Systems (CIS) are developed behind schedule and over budget and that they do not satisfy their users. After 30 years of experience in building these systems, why do these troubles continue to arise? Frequently, system requirements are not stated accurately and/or completely. If the requirements specifications are erroneous or incomplete, the resulting system cannot be expected to perform satisfactorily! In addition, errors caught or requirements discovered in later phases of the system development process can result in the failure to meet cost estimates and completion deadlines. Montazemi's [42] recent study demonstrates that inadequate assessment of user requirements can have these same negative impacts (i.e., errors, failure, etc.) on small companies as well as large ones.

Yet, there has been relatively little empirical research on the process of Information Requirements Analysis (IRA), and there are few practical, well-formulated guidelines for helping system developers and users obtain information requirements [18]. This paper reports on an action-research study in which improvements in the IRA process were made through the utilization of a set of practical communication behaviors. The premise is simply that the use of effective communication behaviors will enhance the ability of (1) users to specify requirements, (2) developers to elicit and evaluate requirements, and (3) both groups to communicate effectively with one another. In addition, it is argued that these communication behaviors will complement the successful application of any requirements determination strategy or methodology such as prototyping or structured systems design approaches.

Over twenty years ago, Churchman and Schainblatt [15] highlighted the importance of communication and mutual understanding between a specialist and a generalist. During the ensuing twenty years, many authors have stressed the significance of effective communication between CIS developers and users [2,9,11,16,22,28, 29,31,32,39,48,50]. Effective communication has been offered as a solution to many of the problems encountered in the CIS development process, especially in the requirements definition phase.

Today, effective communication between developers and users is more important than ever. Organizations are redirecting CIS resources. They are moving away from traditional transaction processing systems to Decision-Expert (DSS, ESS) and Communication (CSS) Support Systems. With the creation of DSS-ESS and CSS, systems professionals are modeling the decision-making and communication processes of individuals, as opposed to functional processes such as payroll processing or general accounting. This type of modeling requires a more demanding set of communication skills to “get inside someone’s head.” In addition, many CIS personnel are helping users implement their own systems designs rather than performing both design and implementation functions. The phenomenon typically referred to as end-user computing places CIS staff in consulting and training roles which demand effective communication skills $[11]$ .

While many authors stress the importance of effective communication in the CIS development process, few provide empirical evidence or concrete suggestions. The study reported here tested the use of the Precision Model $[40]$ , a generalized model which draws upon a set of behaviors to facilitate communication. These behaviors were incorporated into a general format for running design team meetings. The system being designed was a large, integrated Human Resource Information System for a large multi-campus university. Findings were based on comparisons of experiences during requirements specification for the first phase of the system, when the Precision Model was not used, with experiences while using the Model to specify the requirements for additional phases. The results indicate that the new meeting format improved the communication between users and developers and enhanced their ability to develop shared, complete and accurate systems requirements.

Why should we be interested in more effective design team meetings? Simply put, because meetings are a very time-consuming feature, and, as numerous studies have borne out, a major time-wasting feature. Mosvick and Nelson [43] reviewed a number of studies on meeting productivity and found that anywhere from 25% to 80% of a manager's time is spent in meetings. In addition, they estimated that "Over 50 percent of the productivity of the billions of meeting hours is wasted... causing many companies to lose the equivalent of 30 man-days and 240 man-hours a year for every person who participates in business conferences" (p. 4). They estimated that ineffective meetings cost one Fortune 500 company a total of 71 million dollars a year.

This article begins with an overview of the communication perspective of CIS development, a description of the research setting and a review of relevant previous research. A description of the Precision Model and the meeting format developed follows. This in turn is followed by the presentation and discussion of results. The paper concludes with an outline of the implications of the results obtained.

If users and developers framed the world similarly, they would probably have few problems in communicating with one another. However, personality differences between users and developers are well documented in the CIS literature [17,24,32,33]. Further, these two groups speak different “languages” [30]! Users have domain specific knowledge and use the vocabulary of their domain, whereas developers are familiar with information requirements methodologies used to extract domain knowledge from users and use the vocabulary of systems development. The ability to integrate these diverse views into a shared, accurate and complete model of the desired system is essential to successful CIS projects. The process of establishing shared models is largely one of communication. Achieving mutual understanding through effective communication behaviors helps to promote shared meaning.

In general, researchers and practitioners have directed their energies towards specific methodologies to aid in CIS design, rather than focusing on communication in the design process. Communication processes should be regarded as an integral part of systems design. They would not replace system design methodologies, but complement them. For example, Davis' [18] contingency framework for selecting information requirement methodologies can be enhanced to include the use of effective communication patterns. Strategy selection is based upon the uncertainties associated with the requirement definition process such as certain characteristics of systems, as well as user/developer characteristics, as indicated in Figure. 1.

It is, therefore, necessary to include the use of effective communication patterns in the description of effective requirements determination (Figure 1, Box II). The argument is simply that effective communication will improve the successful application of any information requirement determination methodology. The study reported here explored the use of communication behaviors to support various elicitation methods.

## Research Setting

This study was carried out while I was an action-researcher involved in the implementation of an integrated Human Resource Information System at a large multi-campus University. The integrated system provides information to multiple departments (Payroll, Personnel, Budget, etc.) by using a single logical database. I played a dual role as both a researcher and the project manager reporting to the Treasurer of the University (the major user area). My tasks included gathering information through group as well as individual interviews, negotiating to resolve inter-departmental issues, formulating systems requirements and facilitating organizational changes.

Prior to my involvement, the University had made three attempts to implement this system over a thirteen-year period; all three attempts failed. The most recent failure employed a database-oriented design utilizing a well-known database management system. It cost approximately one million dollars. This system was implemented and subsequently replaced as a result of the first phase of our successful effort. (A detailed comparison of the successful and failed efforts is presented in Bostrom and Kaiser [10].)

One of the major reasons why the earlier efforts were unsuccessful was that the initial information requirements were inaccurate and incomplete $[10]$ . Users had difficulty articulating their models and their needs. Instead of helping users build good models, developers got some ideas from users and then constructed the system with little feedback to users. When questions arose, the developers tended to fill in missing information based on their models rather than eliciting information from users. As a result, the final models embedded in the system reflected the developers' perspectives rather than those of users.

In the successful effort, we applied the contingency framework discussed by Davis [18] and Nauman, et al. [45]. The primary information requirements determination strategy used was prototyping. A prototyping design approach was utilized to implement a purchased software package. Secondary strategies included deriving requirements from the existing system (using data flow diagrams) and utilizing system characteristics (using Socio-Technical Analysis). Utilizing of these approaches led to the development of good user models and needs statements. Use of these approaches also improved the feedback/dialogue between users and developers.

While we were successful in “getting the requirements right” during the first phase of our project, our efforts were time-consuming and often frustrating. We often rehashed the same material “for the tenth time” in design team meetings. Most of our problems and frustrations resulted from ineffective communication. For example, data flow diagrams helped developers documented and provide feedback about users’ models of systems, but this technique provided little help in eliciting the information needed to construct models or to run a design team meeting.

After the first phase of the project had been concluded, I was trained in the Precision Communication Model (PCM). Using PCM techniques, I developed a meeting/interview format for conducting meetings and interviews. We felt that improving communication patterns using the Precision Model and the new meeting format could reduce the amount of time spent in the design process while improving the effectiveness of users and developers in generating requirements specifications. We decided to test this working hypothesis by using the new meeting format during the implementation of the remaining Phases of the Human Resource Information System.

## Previous Research

Previous research indicates that communication is important in the design of a CIS. Symptoms of ineffective communication during the requirements definition process are consistently associated with user dissatisfaction, while effective communication is associated with improved productivity. Edstrom [22] found that ineffective communication during any of the phases of system development is negatively correlated with system success. Salaway's [49] study of conversations between the designer and user indicates that this form of communication is error-prone and exhibits dysfunctional characteristics that could have far-reaching consequences. For example, a Savant Institute study showed that 56% of the errors in installed systems resulted from poor communication between the user and the developer in defining requirements and that such errors were the most expensive to correct [1]. In studies of programming productivity [51,58], communication was found to be one of the most important factors positively correlated with increased productivity. Boland [6,7] empirically demonstrated that different interaction processes produce requirements statements that differ significantly in quality and implementation potential.

Investigations of the skills required to develop systems underscore the importance of communication in CIS development. Much of the early research in this area focuses on the technical skills required $[2]$ . As more recent research clearly documents $[59]$ , a systems developer must still have technical skills, but they are no longer sufficient to meet the requirements of the job. This latter research reflects the evolving role of the systems developer and the emergence of a new set of skills $[5,14,31]$ which are usually referred to as “people”/generalist and business skills. The ability to effectively communicate is an essential element of the “people” skill set. The importance of communication skills has been documented for the development of traditional systems [31], new types of applications such as expert systems [21,23] and for new roles such as that of an information center consultant [11].

DeBrabander and Edstrom [19] suggest that in certain situations, a third party should be used to bridge the gap between developers and users. DeBrabander and Thiers [20] provide empirical evidence to justify the use of this strategy. In most cases, however, a third party is not cost-effective or necessary. As the research reported in this paper demonstrates, developers and users can be trained to use practical, simple and effective communication behaviors.

Although the importance of communication is clearly recognized, the research cited above has not resulted in the formulation of specific guidelines for achieving effective communication between the developer and the user. Most authors fail to provide specific suggestions for linking theory and research data with practice. For example, the skills research cited above focused on somewhat vague concepts such as the ability to communicate and listen. Specific behaviors for implementing these concepts are not provided. Guinan and Bostrom [26] point out that behavioral reference disciplines that focus on communication provide theory and discussion but few empirically-based guidelines.

However, previous research has identified what some of those behaviors might be. Boland's [6,7] and Salaway's [49] work suggests the importance of using communication behaviors to establish specific frames to facilitate the exchange of relevant information. Salaway trained developers in an “organizational learning” interaction methodology. Her results demonstrated that the new interaction methodology successfully generated more valid information with improved error detection. Thomas and Carroll's [52] and Vitalari and Dickson's [55] research suggests that an outcome frame should be established, i.e., the users' attention should be kept focused on specific goals or objectives. Thomas and Carroll found that the establishment of goals accelerated the problem-solving process directed toward formulating a complete set of systems requirements. Vitalari and Dickson found that highly rated developers, as compared to low-rated developers, exhibited more specific goal-setting behavior and tended to use an output-oriented analysis process, whereas low-rated developers used an input-oriented analysis process.

Research by Thomas and Carroll also suggests that specific language patterns such as meta-comments and metaphors are important in developing a successful CIS. Guinan and Bostrom [27,28] studied the general communication behavior of high- and low-performing developers. Their investigation revealed that while eliciting system requirements from a user, highly rated developers outperformed their low-rated counterparts in achieving shared meaning, establishing and maintaining rapport and keeping the user's overall satisfaction with the interaction high. They also used specific communication behaviors such as outcome frames, metaphors, and metacomments more frequently than their low-rated counterparts. Moreover, the level of use of these communication behaviors could be used to discriminate between high and low performers.

## Precision Model and Meeting Format

Many of the successful communication behaviors identified in the previous section are found in the Precision Model. The Precision Model, which was developed by McMaster and Grinder [40], is a generalized communication model that outlines specific behaviors and procedures for generating effective communication in a business setting. The Model was one of the first applications of Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP) in a business context. NLP synthesizes research in neuro-psychology, linguistics, artificial intelligence, cognitive psychology and other fields in an attempt to understand the process of human information processing and communication. The Precision Model was developed by modeling effective patterns of communications in a business context.

The classes of communication behaviors used within the Precision Model to ensure effective communication and the development of shared explicit linguistic models are frames, procedures and pointers. Each class of behavior will be discussed separately. A discussion of how these behaviors can be integrated into a format for running meetings follows.

Frames

The following example will help you understand the concept of framing. Try to answer the following question without skipping ahead to the answer. Pay attention to the thinking process you are going through.

What is the ordering in the following numbers?

## 8,5,4,9,1,6,3,2

Most of you were probably looking for a numeric sequence, a pattern of thinking installed during childhood. Although some people have found numeric sequences, they are not readily apparent. Try another perspective, look for another type of ordering. In fact, the numbers are in alphabetic order (i.e., eight, five, four, nine, etc.). Looking for a numeric or alphabetic sequence are examples of applying frames to a situation to assign meaning. The meaning of any event or situation depends upon the frame in which it is perceived. For example, a glass of water can be seen as half-empty (frame: degree of emptiness), half-full (frame: degree of fullness) or something to drink (frame: usefulness).

The term “frame” is used here to refer to a person’s internal contextual definition that creates meaning. When the frame is changed, the meaning is changed. For example, Tversky and Kahneman [53] found that people’s perception of decision problems and their evaluation of probabilities and outcomes shift in predictable patterns when the same problem is framed in different ways. Reframing is a technique for changing the way a person frames and understands events in order to change the meaning of the events [3]. When the meaning changes, the person’s responses and behaviors also change.

In the information requirements definition process, users and developers apply frames to analyze the current system and design the desired system. The framing process can lead to problems unless steps are taken to avoid them. For example, the selection of frames is more heavily influenced by recent events than by events in the distant past [18]. Thus, an information need experienced recently may be given more weight than it deserves. Effective questioning on the part of the developer can overcome a user's tendency to rely on recent experiences. Developers must realize that every question they ask creates a “mini-frame,” that is, it directs the thoughts i.e., the framing process, of the user who is being questioned in a particular way. For example, the questions “Can you get me this information by Friday?” and “What do we need to do to get this information generated by Friday?” establish different frames. The latter presupposes that the task can be done.

Frames are established and manipulated through verbal language patterns. For example, to establish or use the as-if frame, the following lead-in words or phrases could be used:

Let's suppose that...

If you were to...

Can you act as if...

Pretend that I am...

Three types of framing behavior are included in the Precision Model: outcome, backtrack and as-if. When a developer establishes an outcome frame, he or she is keeping the user focused on specific goals or outcomes such as what the user wants, or the objective of a meeting/interview. An example of establishing an outcome frame for a design team meeting would be: “The outcome for today’s meeting is to develop a rough draft of the academic personnel action form for the new system.” Establishing an outcome involves answering the question “What’s the point of this meeting?” or “What do we want to accomplish in this meeting?” For a more detailed discussion of the use of the outcome frame in the systems environment, see Cashman and Bostrom [12].

In contrast to the outcome frame, the backtrack frame is more relationally oriented. It refers to statements made to verify what someone has said or experienced. The listener is verifying that he/she understands the speaker's frame of reference. It should serve as an indicator of the quality of understanding or listening that exists in the interaction. It may be used to review the progress made in a meeting or interaction. Examples of backtracking include: "Let me summarize what we have decided so far..." and "Glen, you are saying we need to include both retirement and title information on the new form?" In the meeting context, backtracking is particularly useful in establishing and maintaining rapport and in helping to determine if the group has a shared understanding.

The as-if frame is used to manipulate frame boundaries to increase the quality of the information exchange. Its purpose is to establish a frame in which people can access information or take actions which would otherwise be unavailable because of the way they are framing the situation. An example of an as-if frame is: “Current policy will not allow us to eliminate any approval signatures from the form. Let’s assume we can change current policy. What would we do?” The as-if frame is a simple type of reframing and, like all reframing behavior, it is critical to the creative process [3,40]. There are different types of boundary shifts and ways to establish an as-if frame. The example above was used to get people to shift from a frame in which certain constraints exist to one free of those constraints. Other examples of as-if frames are:

1. Time shift - "If it were six months from now and the system is installed, what are its major benefits?

2. Person shift – “Can you act as if you were the user? How would they respond?”

3. Context shift – “Imagine that the primary user walked in and asked you for three more alternatives. What would you say?”

4. Information shift - "Let's suppose you had that information. How would you use it?"

There are many other techniques that can be used to manipulate frames within a group to increase the quality of the information exchanged and resources available. Most of them built upon the basic ideas of framing and reframing discussed in this section. For example, brainstorming establishes the following frame: (1) delay evaluation of ideas (criticism is ruled out); (2) all ideas are useful (no need to filter comments); (3) quantity breeds quality (the more ideas the better); and (4) use others' ideas to stimulate your thinking. Most of these techniques combine both creativity and problem solving. Many can be applied to systems development tasks. Van Gundy [54] provides an excellent inventory of these techniques. Von Oech [56,57] also provides a number of useful suggestions.

## Procedures

Procedures in the Precision Model are sets of verbal statements or questions which allow the communicator to both guide the direction of the communication and to ensure that the discussion stays within the established outcome frame. Two of the five procedures developed by McMaster and Grinder [40] are relevant here: the evidence question and the relevancy challenge.

Although the outcome frame is one of the most powerful organizing devices available to a systems developer, it is ineffective unless the specific characteristics of the desired state have been defined. The evidence question is the procedure for developing high-quality information defining the characteristics of a desired state, i.e., answering the question: "How will you know when you have achieved the outcomes?" The specific form of the evidence question can vary – any expression which elicits a description of the characteristics of the outcome state is adequate. Some variants are:

\- What evidence would lead you to conclude that (an outcome) is achieved?

\- What specifically would you see, hear and feel that would let you know that (an outcome) was achieved?

One of the major problems in meetings and interviews is that participants often start discussing irrelevant topics $[43]$ . Relevancy challenges are used when the information being received does not seem to be relevant to the current discussion or desired outcome. The challenge is usually phrased as: “Thank you for that question/input, I’m having trouble fitting that into [state outcome currently focused on] (point to outcome or agenda item to anchor). Could you make that fit for us?” If the speaker can demonstrate the relevance, then the discussion goes on. Otherwise the irrelevant topic is dropped or dealt with in another fashion (e.g., put on a list of issues to be discussed). The relevancy challenge is only useful when a clear outcome frame has been established.

## Pointers / Getting Specific Information

Pointers are cues or probes to elicit specific information. They are a primary strategy to overcome vagueness. Ogdin's [46] work on the use of vague and specific language patterns suggests that the two patterns elicit significantly different responses. Vague language provides us with the opportunity to fill in our own meaning. The listener draws the assumptions he or she believes are most appropriate. For example, the sentence “Computers are very useful." is extremely vague. It tells us little, and so we are forced to derive our own meaning from it.

Pointers are request for more specific information that combat general and vague comments. They may take one of many forms:

\- Could you be more specific?

\- What exactly do you want an information system to do?

\- Which costs specifically are you talking about?

In each of these examples the questioner is trying to elicit specific information. He or she is trying to understand precisely the way the other person views the situation.

Specificity is crucial to the accurate identification of requirements. DeBrabander and Thiers' [20] concept of a semantic gap between developers and users highlights the problem. The expression "semantic gap" refers to the fact that different people assign different meanings to words. Therefore, when vague language is used, it may mean different things to each of the parties. For example, a "productive" system may be one that updates files efficiently, as far as the developer is concerned, while a user may consider a "productive" system to be one that gets a simple report out by 8:00 a.m. If vague language such as "productive" is used, and more specific information is not requested, the questioner must fill in gaps based on his/her model of the world. "Filling in" creates an opportunity for miscommunication and misunderstanding, while asking "What do you mean by a productive system?" uses a pointer to reduce the semantic gap.

The greater the semantic gap, the more communicative effort is required by developers and users in order to establish semantic equivalence. DeBrabander and Thiers [20] found that reducing this semantic gap improved effective communication between developers and users. Use of pointers to probe for more specific information is the key to reducing the gap and to developing semantic equivalence and mutual understanding.

## RUNNING THE MEETING

Fig. 2. Meeting format.

in Figure 2. Figure 2 depicts a meeting as a systems change process. A meeting transforms the present state to a desired state by accomplishing a set of specified outcomes. These are accomplished by using resources (people) to carry out a set of action steps (agenda). A summary of this guide for running meetings follows.

In this guide, a meeting is divided into three stages: the pre-meeting, the set-up for the meeting, and running the meeting. The pre-meeting phase is the most critical. The most frequently reported meeting problems deal with poor preparation, e.g., no outcomes and/or agenda [43]. Before the meeting, the systems developer defines meeting outcomes and the evidence for each outcome. Once the outcomes are defined, the participants (i.e., the resources needed to achieve outcomes) to be invited are identified and the meeting agenda (i.e., steps necessary to achieve outcomes) is established.

People are invited to a meeting because of the resources they bring. The meeting leader, i.e., the systems developer, needs to make sure he/she has access to those resources both before and during the meeting. This is accomplished by establishing and maintaining rapport and a positive mental state (i.e., a resource state) on the part of participants. Rapport is the synergy, mutual trust and respect developed within the group. Without rapport, effective communication is unlikely $[26,47]$ .

Once rapport is established, participants can obtain information more readily, probe more easily and communicate more freely [47]. Backtracking and outcome frames are particularly useful for maintaining rapport, while the as-if frame is very useful for keeping people creative and resourceful. Other techniques for developing rapport are discussed in Richardson [47], Moine and Herd [41], Guinan and Bostrom [27], and Guinan [28].

At the beginning of a meeting, it is also important to share the outcomes and evidence for each outcome with the group. This is usually done through flipcharts or handouts. The goal is to make sure each person understands the desired outcome and how the group will know the outcome has been achieved. For example, a meeting might start with the following on a flipchart:

Outcome: To develop a preliminary draft of a personnel action form.

Evidence: A rough draft of a form created on the blackboard.

Once the flipchart has been viewed, the meeting leader makes sure each participant agrees with the outcome and the evidence stated. This meeting set-up phase establishes a specific shared frame of reference for the entire group, which will help keep the group focused and productive.

A number of the communication behaviors described in the Precision Model are useful in running a meeting. Relevancy challenges and pointers are used to ensure high-quality information exchange. When the information a participant contributes does not appear to be related to an established outcome, a relevancy challenge is used to get the participant to tie in the seemingly irrelevant information to the outcome frame. Using relevancy challenges to keep the meeting focused is critical because getting off the subject is the one of the largest time wasters in meetings $[43]$ .

Pointers or questions to obtain more specific information are used whenever participants use vague or low-quality language. Pointers also provide a means for building “shared frames” by requiring that all terminology used be precisely defined. As-if frames are used whenever it is necessary to change frame boundaries to increase the quality of information exchange. Backtracking is useful in clarifying what the group has accomplished, to verify objections and to establish and maintain rapport. At the end of the meeting it is also important to briefly review the meeting and to state each point that requires future action.

## Results

The Human Resource Information System (HRIS) was implemented in five phases over a five-year period. The meeting format described above was used in all interviewing and meeting sessions that took place after the implementation of Phase I. Systems developers involved in the project were trained to use the new meeting format. Users were taught how to use the meeting format during actual design team meetings.

A typical design team meeting had 6–14 participants and lasted 1–5 hours. The results were generated by comparing our experiences during the development of the first phase (when we did not use the Precision/meeting format) with our experiences while using the Precision/meeting format to develop the requirements for the second phase. Approximately 125 design meetings utilizing the meeting format took place during the second phase. Research data were gathered through post-meeting interviews and observations. I recorded my observations, and other system developers and users involved in the system development process were asked to compare their experiences in Phase I and Phase II meetings. The findings reported here summarize our experiences in utilizing the Precision-based meeting format.

## Specific Findings

The findings are summarized in Table 1 and are stated in terms of the improvements in the requirements specification process in Phase II. For example, finding 1 states that fewer and shorter meetings were sufficient to develop requirements in Phase II (and in later phases). It appears that the use of the entire meeting format and, in particular, the use of the outcome frame and evidence and relevancy procedures permitted the accomplishment of requirements determination in fewer and shorter meetings.

The success of the first phase of the project was largely attributed to the use of design team meetings. However, a lot of time was spent in meetings. Our new meeting format solved this problem in subsequent phases. Meeting times were reduced by 25 to 60 percent. The formulation of desired outcomes prior to the meeting contributed significantly to this reduction in meeting time. Once outcomes were defined, evidence questions were used to develop specific evidence criteria for each outcome. Relevancy challenges were then used during the meeting to maintain direction, as well as to provide probes for exploring seemingly unrelated issues. Goals were accomplished in shorter periods of time by focusing on outcomes.

A marked improvement in the ability of the developers to fill in information in users' frames of reference during requirements specification was detected in Phase II. Failure to develop shared frames had previously resulted in the developer's/user's filling in missing information from his/her own frames but operating under the assumption that this view was shared by the other. The result was an inability on the part of the developer to specify the requirements accurately. By using pointers, developers and users explicitly asked each other to explain the terms they used, which prevented "filling-in" and facilitated the development of “shared frames.” Backtracking provided a major means of ensuring that a shared understanding existed. Backtracking required that developers present the proposed requirements to the users for their verification. If the developer had filled in from his/her own understanding, corrections were provided by the user. Increasingly accurate and complete requirement specifications resulted.

The use of pointers, backtracking and the relevancy procedure resulted in the generation of high-quality information during the requirements definition process. Having high-quality information led to increased accuracy, completeness and relevancy in the design specifications. The gathering of high-quality conceptual information also reduced the effort required to translate specifications into a logical design. Further, very detailed logical designs facilitated the development of physical design specifications.

A problem often encountered in the project's first phase was a lack of rapport or even mistrust between developers and users, which was not surprisingly since the three previous attempts had failed. The communication behaviors used made it easier for developers to develop and maintain rapport with users. All aspects of the meeting format contributed to this finding. The purpose and the intent (outcomes) of the developer were known and open for discussion. The developer was not automatically expected to assume an expert role. S/he was interested in the resources the user brought to the meeting and the project. Backtracking reinforced this concept by providing auditory evidence that the developer understood and shared the views of the user. These behaviors helped to promote an environment of mutual respect and trust.

More creative solutions to information-processing problems were devised in Phase II. Use of the outcome and as-if frames, helped overcome limited thinking reflected in statements such as: "We have always done it this way." "It's policy" and "Financial considerations require ...." The development of well-formed outcome frames (which were positively stated and obtainable through available resources) focused resources on the system goals and objectives. Generating creative solutions to obtain the desired system state is a key to developing good requirements specifications. One of the processes involved in generating creative solutions is the ability to look at the situation through different frames, e.g., to act as if the policy could be changed. Utilization of “as-if frames” in design team meetings provided a simple technique for creating a context in which users and developers were encouraged to access information and ideas that would otherwise not have been available.

The design teams in the study included managerial and clerical personnel at different levels in the university. The meeting format established a shared cooperative frame (i.e., shared outcomes and evidence) which facilitated information sharing. The focus on obtaining high-quality information through the use of relevancy challenges and pointers encouraged and facilitated the resolution of disagreements. This emphasis on high-quality information exchange within a cooperative frame resulted in an increased amount of input from clerical personnel and the establishment of an atmosphere that encouraged constructive conflict and effective collaboration. This increased sharing of information resulted in a more effective group problem-solving environment.

Design team members repeatedly expressed feelings of accomplishment and satisfaction following meetings. These positive feelings resulted primarily from the articulation of specific outcomes and evidence criteria to be used to gauge the productivity of a meeting. Feelings of accomplishment and progress are extremely important in large projects. While there is no direct evidence that such feelings improve the specification process, I believe that the attitude of the design team is a critical factor in the development of high-quality information. Our evidence indicates that people with positive attitudes are productive and motivated team members. Most of us have experienced the unproductive effects that one negative participant can have on a group. Having feelings of accomplishment after a productive meeting increases the probability of members having positive, productive attitudes in subsequent meetings.

## General Discussion

The Precision Model was developed by modelling effective communicators in business settings and contrasting their behaviors with those of ineffective communicators $[40,41]$ . This comparison resulted in the identification of the essential behavioral patterns that lead to effective communication. Our own experience was that developers performed certain patterns (e.g., used pointers) very well, but used others (e.g., setting outcomes)

poorly. The magnitude of the improvements resulting from the use of all these behaviors surprised us. We did behaviors that we did well before even better because we now explicitly understood what we were doing, whereas the behaviors were sporadically successful before. We got the biggest performance gains from utilizing the new behaviors and integrating the entire Precision Model into effective tools such as the meeting format. Performance improvements continued throughout the project, as we modeled our behavior to identify more effective patterns.

The use of these communication techniques can have positive consequences, negative consequences or no consequences at all depending on the governing variables that guide their use. These governing variables are illustrated in Figure 3. The first and essential step in communication is establishing rapport and resourcefulness. Without rapport, no technique will work well. It is very difficult to establish rapport without having certain beliefs and assumptions. Within the rapport frame, you need to have an outcome. If your behavior is not directed toward an outcome, you have no way to determine if that behavior is relevant or not. Once you have well-defined outcomes, you can then select from a wide variety of specific techniques (e.g., as-if, pointers, etc.) that are designed to get specific results.

Figure 3 illustrates that a hierarchy of frames must be present for a specific behavior to be successful. That is, a person who holds particular beliefs or assumptions and who has established rapport and an outcome can then successfully use communication behaviors such as the as-if frame. The important beliefs/assumptions which guided our use of communication behaviors were:

1. Users know what they want and need but have a very difficult time expressing it. As developers, our job is to help them make what they want and need explicit. Communication and the quality of the interactions between developers and users is assumed to be important in this assumption.

2. People (including users) are valuable resources. As developers, we need to respect and utilize these resources.

3. Win-win situations are preferable to win-lose situations. We directed our resources toward establishing outcome frames where all parties (developers, different user groups) got what they wanted. Attention was focused on resolving outcome incompatibilities and satisfying others' as well as our own desired outcomes.

4. High-quality, valid information is essential to success. High-quality, valid information described what the person or group is actually experiencing. It may a conflicting opinion, a negative emotion, etc. This assumption implies creating an environment in which information is freely exchanged.

5. There is no such thing as failure, only feedback. We focused on learning and on obtaining the desired outcomes. Feedback information was taken in and adjustments were made to our understanding of where we needed to go next (learning), instead of dwelling on how we had failed or succeeded.

Our successful application of communication behaviors was in part due to these governing beliefs/assumptions, which guided how we operated.

From these assumptions it followed that one of the outcomes we desired was maintaining good relationships with users. Developers must balance the accomplishment of task outcomes with the need to support people and on-going relationships within the design team. For example, the developer needs to know when not to invoke a relevancy challenge and simply let a member express him/herself. The communication process occurs between individuals who exhibit a wide range of emotions. The meeting format does not attempt to reduce the communication process to a slick set of procedures. I believe that the meeting format represents a powerful approach to systems design. However, obtaining an outcome should never be given priority over the development of the team or maintaining the respect and resourcefulness of team members.

Training people in communication techniques is not enough. People also need to be taught that the use of these techniques must be guided by certain beliefs/ssumptions. Salaway [49] demonstrates one approach for developing both beliefs and behaviors. Like Salaway, we found that users and developers easily pick up and use the communications behaviors and that formal training programs provide the best vehicle for helping developers and/or users adopt these beliefs and behaviors.

Implications For Systems Development Methodologies

I agree with Davis [18] that the use of an information requirements determination strategy and appropriate methodologies (e.g., data flow diagrams) will enhance the abilities of developers and users to develop shared, accurate and complete requirements specifications. The first phase of the action-research project, which was briefly described in the Research Setting section, supports Davis' hypothesis. We also found that the use of these techniques facilitates dialogue between developers and users. These improvements result primarily from utilizing the techniques to structure the content of the communication. However, they alone do not make it effective. Effective communication behaviors improve the successful application of any strategies and methods. Our new meeting format was used to formulate requirements in subsequent phases of the project. Since there were no other major changes and we continued to use the same determination strategy and methods, increased success in requirements determination can be attributed to the use of the improved communication techniques.

The meeting format outlined in this paper can be utilized with any methodology or technique used to develop any type of system (traditional, expert system, etc.). The communication behaviors included in the meeting format deal with the form or process of communication transactions, not their content, and thus they are applicable to all communications transactions. This makes the behaviors very useful to other systems personnel such as managers and information center consultants.

However, some system development methodologies explicitly recognize the importance of effective communication more than others. For example, Peter Checkland's "Soft Systems Methodology" attempts to achieve better communication by focusing on processes which help give users and developers a "rich picture" of the situation [13]. Socio-technical Systems (STS) development approaches [8,44] recognize the difficulties that arise from poor communications in a group seeking to develop an information system. STS methodologies are designed to achieve a consensus among a group which may come into the process with different beliefs, expertise, etc. An STS approach was used in the case reported in this paper. Winograd and Flores [60] outline a potential design approach which focuses primarily on the use of knowledge-based processing and communication techniques embedded in computer tools. The Winograd and Flores “language action” approach to systems development is primarily based on speech-act theory. A number of other authors have applied language theories to information systems development [25,35,36,37,38].

Most system development methods, rely implicitly or explicitly on the presence of a facilitator (systems developer, knowledge engineer, etc.) who is familiar with development methods, but not the user's knowledge domain. The facilitator's job is to use the methods to elicit requirements from the domain. Many argue that such knowledge elicitation can yield good results, no matter what elicitation technique is used if the facilitator is good. Our research indicates that one key component of good facilitation is communication [27,28]. Therefore, training facilitators in the meeting format/communication behaviors outlined in this paper would be very useful.

## Conclusion

The results of the action-research study reported here indicate that substantial improvement in the abilities of developers and users to “get the requirements right” will result from training them to use effective communication behaviors. In this study, communication behaviors were drawn from the Precision Model $[40]$ and integrated into a format for running meetings and conducting interviews. Results indicate that this format is particularly useful for design team meetings. Other benefits resulting from the use of the meeting format were: a reduction in the number of meetings and their length; developers were able to build and maintain rapport with users more easily; and team members felt more satisfied and productive at the end of design team meetings.

The research presented in this paper was of an exploratory nature. As a result no conclusive statements about causal relationships can be made. However, our findings support the claim that effective communication is important in the requirements definition process. This paper identifies a number of simple, practical communication behaviors that lead to effective communication between developers and users. A recent more rigorous study provides further support for the claim that communication and the specific communication behaviors discussed in this paper are important [27,28]. More research is needed to identify additional critical behaviors, patterns and skills.

Training is the best mechanism for improving the communication skills and productivity of systems developers. I have carried out a number of communications workshops tailored to the needs of particular companies. Salaway [49] describes another applicable training approach. Some commercially packaged training programs also focus on this type of training for technical professionals [24]. If training is not possible, I strongly suggest using the techniques and tools as best you can. (You are your own best researcher.) This paper and other relevant papers [12,46,49] and books [34,40,41,43,47] are your best starting points.

The importance of communication is and will continue to be a key element in the successful diffusion of computer technology. I strongly urge IS researchers and practitioners to investigate the applicability of the communication perspective presented in this paper to their environments.
