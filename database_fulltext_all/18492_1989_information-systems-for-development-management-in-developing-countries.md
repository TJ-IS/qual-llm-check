# Information Systems for Development Management in Developing Countries \*

Chun Kwong Han

Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge, UK

Barry Render

Roy E. Crummer Graduate School of Business, Rollins College, Winter Park, FL 32789, USA

A large-scale distributed information processing system, designed by native human resource, was implemented for planning and control of development projects in a developing country in Asia. The system coordinated the activities of a number of central government agencies involved in development management and facilitated use of information technology for project management at the local level. The case demonstrated that information technology can be employed to promote decentralised development and maintain centralised control simultaneously in developing countries.

Keywords: Information technology impact, Systems development, Development management, Malaysia.

Eight development plans have been published, each with objectives consistent with the requirements of the changing nature of the Malaysian socio-political-economic situation. Guided by these national plans, major development initiatives had been mounted by both federal and state governments in the last three decades. The need to coordinate these initiatives and ensure that the implementation of projects conformed to planning schedules drawn up at the federal level, meant inevitably that some forms of information systems were required through which the federal government would be kept updated as to development progress.

The RED (Rural Economic Development) Book System is a manual information system developed in the early 1960s following the creation of the Ministry of Rural Development. This comprehensive planning and monitoring system was based on the military style of security operations. Projects were identified on maps and charts that were updated from time to time to show progress. However, stated development goals were not achieved and development programmes failed to satisfy Malay aspirations, resulting in a racial riots of May 1969. In the aftermath, the government charted a new course to socio-economic development, labelled the New Economic Policy (NEP) which aims at poverty eradication and restructuring of society. The levels of planned public sector expenditure were rapidly increased and there was an upsurge in development activities, but these were some 20% shortfall in the first decade of the NEP. Administrative restructuring resulted in the dropping of the RED book plans and the subsequent conversion to the computerized Project Monitoring System (PMS) in 1978 to handle the increased number of projects.

In the early 1980s, a new administrative style emphasizing efficiency and productivity was adopted. A rationalisation of the administrative structure occurred with the closure of many statutory bodies and public enterprises that were poor performers. Development administration was to be improved, and a 1981 study undertaken by the Cabinet Committee On Steps to Speed Up the Implementation of Development Projects [4] found various limitations in the information systems for planning and controlling of development projects by government agencies. More specifically, some of the problems were that some projects were not reported by implementing agencies; incomplete, inaccurate and late reporting; and the systems run by central agencies not being properly coordinated. In order to resolve these problems, the Cabinet Committee made the following recommendations:

The Ministries/Departments with the co-operation of the Implementation Coordination Unit will design a complete system or use existing systems for the purpose of planning and controlling development projects as well as making necessary decisions.

All Central Agencies' information systems for controlling the implementation and evaluating the performance of development projects are to be integrated. In this regard, the SIMKOM system of the Treasury, the Project Monitoring System of the Implementation Coordination Unit and the data collection activities of the Economic Planning Unit is to be integrated so that a common information system will be used. Central and all other agencies are encouraged to exchange information among themselves.

To implement the above recommendations, SETIA, acronym for (S)ystem (E)economic Planning Unit (T)reasury (I)implementation Coordination Unit (A)ccountant General's Department, was developed with the objective of providing a common database with a common data source, and end-user facilities to government agencies for planning and controlling development projects. The high priority accorded to the use of IT in development management is reflecting in Paragraph 98 of the current Fifth Malaysia Plan (1986–1990) [3], which stated that:

The use of computer and other electronic technology in the planning and implementation process will be further intensified. The use of these facilities will allow for the reduction of costs and delays through the setting up and use of common data collection, processing, and dissemination centres. Online facilities of the ICU will be linked with all Federal Ministries and State Development Offices.

## 2. System Description

Thus SETIA is based on the concept of an application system in which all procedures of the

Federal Government machinery related to the creation, budget allocation, implementation progress reporting, and the financing arrangements of federally funded development projects are automated. It is a first attempt at integrating four existing information systems: Project Planning System of the Economic Planning Unit (EPU), computer-based Information System of the Economic Planning Unit (EPU), computer-based Information System of the Treasury (SIMKOM), Project Monitoring system (PMS) of the Implementation Coordination Unit (ICU), and the Federal Accounting System of the Accountant General's Office.

## 2.1. System Components

The SETIA system was developed to support the Federal Government's machinery involved in development project management. It contains subsystems for registering projects, preparing budget allocations, and reporting physical and financial progress of projects.

## 2.3. Hardware

SETIA is implemented on an IBM 3083, a general purpose computer used by the Prime Minister's Department community with 5, 6, 10 and 34 terminal located in SERU (Socio-Economic Research Unit), PWD (Public Works Department), EPU, and ICU respectively. Since the development of SETIA, some 80% of the computing power has been dedicated to SETIA. Thirty-four terminals in ICU have direct access to the SETIA system.

## 2.4. Software

The CLIO Data Base Management System supplied by Groupe Syseca of France is the software package used for the SETIA Database. Besides the CLIO DBMS software, software packages for the data entry, information query, and security sub-systems are being acquired.

## 2.5. Secretariat

The SETIA Secretariat is located at the Management Information Division, Implementation Coordination Unit, Prime Minister's Department. The Division consists of three sectors: Projects Analysis, Computer Services, and System Development with 16, 12, and 21 officers respectively. Nearly all the staff in Project analysis, ten programmers and analysts in System Development and some $5\%$ of the operators' and supervisors' time in Computer Operations are dedicated to the SETIA, as it is the first priority project of the ICU.

## 3. System Development and Management Processes

The concept of SETIA as a planning and control system for the management of development projects at the national level was first mounted in late 1981, following the Cabinet Committee's recommendations. A historical perspective of its development in terms of the systems development life cycle is now described.

## 3.1. Justification

Since the 1950's, development projects have consistently failed in their implementation. A number of factors, including poor statistical databases, lack of administrative will, and inadequate feasibility studies, have been identified as the causal agents. Development projects were controlled and monitored by several agencies simultaneously, giving rise to duplication and redundancy in data gathering and information processing activities; e.g., the Project Monitoring System (PMS) of ICU and SIMKOM of the Treasury were collecting almost the same type of data. The need for a coordinated system to effectively monitor and control the development projects was critical. The Justification Statement was in the form of the 1981 Cabinet Report.

## 3.2. System Development Processes

## 3.2.1. Information and Processing Requirements and Specifications

Three forms (called S1, S2 and S3) are used for recording input data. Form S1 is used to register development projects that have been approved by EPU for implementation. Filled out only once in the life of the project, S1 constitutes the basis for the creation of projects. It is a 2-page document containing details on project number, ministry, department, implementing agency, purpose of development, location, scope, year of initiation and completion, cost breakdowns according to land, building, equipment, consultancy services, management expenditures, local versus foreign costs by year, sources of funds, and types of financing.

Form S2 is a computer printout produced by SETIA; it is sent once a year to ministries, departments, and agencies who will forward their budget requirements for the following year to the Budget Division of Treasury. It contains basic project details, as well as specifics on status of implementation, actual and budgeted expenditures for the 5-year period, current and next year budget estimates, and forecasted expenditures for the remaining years. Form S2 is sent back to the SETIA Secretariat by the agencies, with information on the approved allocations that will be updated on the database.

Form S3 contains data on implementation process with respect to physical projects, beneficiaries, disbursements of payment, problems in implementation, and data of initiation and completion of project activities. This form is filled out every four months by all agencies planning the projects.

The outputs of the SETIA System include general and primary listings from S1, S2 and S3, online queries, analysis, and reports. A general listing of any items in S1, S2 and S3 can be outputted according to user requirements. A primary listing is a regular report produced for other purposes, such as verification, queries, and turnaround documents. Online queries are made by data control clerks to reported offices in order to verify data. The Project Analysis Officers can also make queries so that appropriate action can be taken.

Reports are prepared at three levels: Executive Summary Reports for the Prime Minister and Chief Ministers; Management Reports for Chief Secretary and Director-General; and Detail Reports for project managers, such as Public Works Department Engineers and State Directors and Education. The formats of the executive summary and management reports are free and aim at providing macro-level analyses, whereas the detail reports represent a turnaround document highlighting project problem areas where currency of information is of vital importance.

## 3.2.2. Hardware and Software Decisions

The IBM 3083 on which the SETIA Database was to be implemented was already available in the Management Information Division. The DBMS package required for the SETIA project, however, had to be well-proven in handling large database, allow for online updating from multiple locations, and easy to understand and use not only by technical personnel but also by non-data-processing users. IMS (Information Management System) and QBE (Query by Example) in use in the Project Monitoring System of ICU were found unsuitable for SETIA, as the former requires technical background and substantial training for effective use, while the latter does not support concurrent updating from two or more remote terminals.

The CLIO DBMS was chosen from amongst database management system packages offered by six vendors. The SETIA Project DBMS Evaluation Committee composed of representatives from Treasury, ICU, EPU, Accountant General's Office and MAMPU (Malaysian Administrative and Management Planning Unit) was charged with the responsibility of recommending the appropriate system. In September 1982, a questionnaire was sent to the six vendors, requiring them to return details on their companies, their DBMS user base, operating environment, installation and maintenance procedures, training facilities, costs, terms of contract, technical features of the DBMS, security, integrity, recovery, extent of data independence, and data dictionary. Out of the six vendors, four were short-listed for detailed evaluation. The seven evaluation criteria and their weights were: (i) available features – whether it could run under MVS and CICS (20%), (ii) support data dictionary and non-procedural languages (18%), (iii) willingness to run benchmark test at company expenses at the Computer Centre, Prime Minister's Department (16%), (iv) training (14%), (v) consultancy (12%), (vi) publications (10%) and (vii) cost (10%). Of the six vendors of RAMIS II, IDMS, DATACOM, DB/DC, ADABAS, TOTAL, and CLIO, the last four were on the shortlist and were selected for the benchmark test. The criteria were installation, query language/report generator, reliability/efficiency, and data dictionary. The basis of the final choice was the score assigned by the seven members of the Evaluation Committee to CLIO, ADABAS and TOTAL/MANTIS. The final decision to choose CLIO was based on the US\$184,000 price offered by Groupe Syseca. It may be noted that this low price was a consequence of the French government assistance programme to the Government of Malaysia.

## 3.2.3. Systems Development and Programming

The SETIA System was officially launched on March 1, 1984 with the issuance of a General Circular. With its official launching, the SETIA Secretariat concurrently performed the tasks of collection of data on development projects, systems development and data entry.

Data on the Fourth Malaysia Plan (1981–1985) projects were collected from 24 Ministries and their departments in various states. The SETIA Secretariat visited all the agencies to explain the concept of the new system and the procedures for filling out forms S1, S2 and S3. By the end of 1984, this exercise was completed and information on 19,294 projects was obtained.

At the same time, work on systems design and database development began. The development work is a new experience, owing to the unique nature of the application and Malaysia being a first-time user of the CLIO DBMS. However, with the help of CLIO consultants, the SETIA Secretariat was able to develop the SETIA database system in a year. By April 1985, the development work was completed. Data entry was started at once. This activity continued until August 1985 when a functional database containing information on 19,294 Fourth Malaysia Plan projects, reclassified to 5,570 projects within 1,200 programmes under the current Fifth Malaysia Plan, was formed.

## 3.2.4. Creating And Updating Mechanisms

Various procedures have been instituted to update project data in the SETIA System. The need to update data arises due to correction on project data in input forms S1, S2 and S3, changes to project cost and budget allocation, abandonment and postponement of a project, etc.

## 3.2.5. In-House Development and Consultancy

SETIA, which is an enhancement and extension of the Project Monitoring System, was developed by the in-house systems development team of ICU; its members were drawn from existing systems personnel without the benefit of previous experience with large-scale systems. Consultancy on customizing the CLIO programmes to meet the requirements of the SETIA system, and training in the use of the CLIO DBMS were provided by the vendor.

## 3.2.6. Implementation and Future Plans

The facilities allow access to and input of data to the SETIA Database; these enable the agencies to update and use the information on their projects. ## 4. Managing the User/Computer Interface

Organization mechanisms used in the progress of systems development and on-going implementation and review include a high-level steering committee, chaired by the Director-General of ICU; its membership comprises the director-generals of EPU, Treasury, and the Accountant General's Office. This steering committee formulates policies to guide implementation of the SETIA concept. A technical subcommittee made up of representatives from various technical groups of central agencies also meets regularly to discuss the technical aspects of systems development. Ongoing review and planning of the activities of ICU's Division of Management Information, including SETIA activities, a weekly affair involving the director-general, deputy director-general, and the three sector heads.

When the distributed system is fully operational on a nation-wide basis, the various agencies are expected to ensure that the SETIA tasks at their locations are carried out smoothly. They are to collect the data required for forms S1, S2, S3, and verify their accuracy. Such data are sent to the SETIA Main Database every month via the terminal/PC located at the agency. The agencies will then use monthly-updated information in their subdatabases to plan and control the implementation of their development projects. A variety of training programmes has been organised by the SETIA Secretariat on a nation-wide scale to orientate the users to the new system. These programmes, each of two to three days duration, focused on the basics: database access, updating procedures, and report generation.

## 5. Resource Requirements

The development, implementation, and ongoing maintenance of the SETIA System have been an exercise whose developmental and operational costs have been embedded in the development costs of ICU under Development Purpose P7, Item 13, Computer For National Operations Room. The original approved budget for the SETIA project was US\$3,500,000, that is one-third of the amount under the Fourth Malaysia Plan, with the remainder under the current Fifth Malaysia Plan. This allocation was meant for the purchase of computer equipment to be supplied to the user agencies as well as software for the SETIA system. To date, some US\$470,000 has been spent for the DBMS software and computer equipment for the various central agencies, ministries, and state development offices. About US\$180,000 is expected to be spent on the maintenance of the distributed processing system for the period ending 1990. Thus, the direct outlay for hardware, maintenance, and software required for the SETIA project is now estimated to be in the region of US\$660,000.

The systems development work has been performed on the existing IBM 3083; the distributed processing operations involve linking the ICU's IBM 3083 with the computer facilities in the Chief Executive Information System Network in the Confidential Office of the Prime Minister. This enabled the equipment supplied by the ICU to the various ministries to be used for SETIA purposes; the opportunity cost of computing facilities may be justifiably reflected in the original SETIA budget of US\$3,500,000. In other words, had the SETIA project started from scratch, it could be reasonably assumed that the estimated costs for all hardware and software would have been about budget. However, implementing SETIA largely on existing facilities has resulted in substantial savings, besides optimising the use of already available computing resources. Further, as all the systems design and development work on SETIA has been done by existing staff of ICU, the human resource cost accrued to the project is estimated to be some US\$700,000 in the 1984–86 period. The projected total human resource cost at the end of 1990, when the distributed system will be completely operational and gearing itself for the new Sixth Malaysia Plan (1991–1995), would amount to some US\$1,600,000. Hence, the best estimate of total costs of developing and implementing the SETIA project, which will stretch over a seven-year period, should be the order of US\$5,100,000.

## 6. Assessment and Conclusion

Seen in the context of information systems for the development management in developing countries $[1,2]$ , the development of the SETIA system beginning in 1981 can be described as a entrepreneurial exercise. Not only has user participation been effective in the various phases from strategic design to testing and implementation, but interagency cooperation and linkages have been forged among the four central agencies. This has been facilitated by the strong support for the

SETIA project from the very highest level of the Malaysian government. At this point in time, with the distributed processing system being recently installed, it is somewhat early to form judgement on its medium-to-long term impact on the process of national development and as a vehicle for decentralised development. Nevertheless, SETIA can be expected to impact development management in wide-ranging ways, although it is a technically simple transaction processing and management control system.

In the immediate term, by inputting data and the stages of project planning, yearly budgeting, project monitoring, and project payment, and consolidating all data into a single database, integration and coordinated project management are achieved. In this way, data are gathered only once and the information generated can be used by all four central agencies concerned, as well as other agencies requiring information on development projects. This is relieving the burden of the reporting agencies, as the SETIA System is not only a reporting system but also an application system that incorporates several means of control. By providing end-user facilities at both the national and local levels, an environment for learning and experimenting has been created. Not only has this encouraged user-driven applications based on dBase III and Lotus 1-2-3 to be developed, but also effectively decentralised the process of managing development to the local level, where the direct impact of computerisation can be felt. More recently, with the launching of MFRAH-DUA, an IT-based version of the old RED Book System, information on individual projects have been processed on microcomputers located at State Development Offices. The distributed information so generated are then integrated into the SETIA Main Database at ICU, which is made possible by the networking of computer lines.

In the longer term, a generation of development planners and administrators raised in the use of new information technologies will have been created. The consequence of this will have a powerful impact on the methodologies, efficiency, and effectiveness of development management.

Finally, given the five-year cycle of Malaysia's development planning, the success of the SETIA project should be measured at the end of the development plan period, starting in 1990, when the system is operational on a nation-wide scale for the full Fifth Malaysia Plan period. Its contribution will be in making national and organisational goals assessable. Of course, the ultimate measure of success, which could be far from quantifiable, may best be seen in the extent to which governments in developing countries adopt the SETIA System as a success model for which their own national-level development project information systems may be fashioned.
