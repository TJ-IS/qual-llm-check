# The Cloze Procedure: An Assessment of the Understandability of Data Processing Texts

Ronald A. Guillemette

Department of Management, University of Texas-El Paso, El Paso, TX 79968-0544, USA

Introduction

Readers often find data processing written materials difficult to understand. Poor writing style can adversely accept reader performance with and acceptance of textual materials. A number of methods have been used to measure or predict readability; the cloze procedure is a psycholinguistic technique which measures the extent to which a particular audience understands specific textual material. An exploratory cloze investigation of introductory COBOL textbooks and the ANSI COBOL Standard indicated that they all have readability problems for a significant number of students; cloze results also correlated with comprehension tests and readability formula estimates.

Keywords: Cloze Procedure, Readability Formula, Readability, Textbook Evaluation, Documentation.

Documentation: A Managerial Perspective

Documentation can serve as a means to control overall software development costs by providing an enhanced basis for software planning and control, reducing entropy in software development and maintenance activities, lessening the risk of duplication of effort, and facilitating the sharing or reuse of system resources and technology [7], [55], [67].

Increased managerial attention to documentation, e.g., through requirements for anticipatory documentation, the use of inspections and other review activities [29], and the establishment and enforcement of documentation standards [13], may result in lower costs by indicating potential problems early in the development cycle and by reducing uncertainty in the communication of software details.

Readability Problems with Data Processing Materials

Many readability problems with computer-related technical material have been noted by readers, including: poor organization and accessibility; confusing writing style, layout or graphics; system rather than reader task orientation; few relevant, clarifying examples; and inaccurate, incomplete, or extraneous details [18], [69], [70].

Existing documentation may be so incomplete or inadequately written that the desired software cannot be properly evaluated, implemented, or used [27], [31], [52]. A significant number of software errors have been attributed to unsatisfactory written materials [5], [28]. Documentation problems may result in a need for software redesign and recoding [37] or more extensive audit and control procedures [2], or contribute to higher operational and maintenance costs [35], [46]. Additional reader-related costs include extended search/retrieval activities, lower retention of the material, inefficient or neglected use of software functions and features, increased errors in following described procedures, and protracted user assistance and training activities [36], [41].

## Readability Assessment Techniques

Management and control of documentation activities are facilitated by availability of information concerning the suitability of documentation for its target readers. The prediction or measurement of reader/text mismatches, particularly in the review cycle of documentation development, can provide useful feedback for revising the material to accommodate the needs of the audience.

A reader/text mismatch can trigger comprehension failure and the necessity of using repair strategies (e.g., ignoring the text, forming a tentative hypothesis, rereading material, or consulting an expert source), any of which bears a cost to the reader, either in diverting attention form from the main thread of discourse or in failing to understand a larger portion of the text [38]. It may ultimately result in related performance problems or in the reader failing to use or ignoring available written materials [63]. A number of techniques have been used to predict or measure the readability (understandability) of written materials [40].

Recent readability analyses have predicted the comparable probable difficulty of selected computer language manuals and data processing publications [44], [45], including a computer language standard [32]. These studies were based on the use of readability formulas, which are regression equations composed of certain linguistic variable counts [39]. Readability formulas traditionally have been validated against measure results reflecting the relative success of reader-text interactions.

Three measures have been widely used by reading researchers: judgments, comprehension tests, and the cloze procedure. Judgments have often been used to rank readability of materials and to provide estimates for various text factors; limitations of using judgments include feasibility constraints in reliably rating a sufficiently large, comprehensive range of materials [15], [68]. Comprehension tests, particularly multiple-choice tests over standard reading passages, traditionally have been used in formula validation; criticisms of tests include time-consuming development and validation efforts, possible bias in question formulation, and the effects of subject guessing [12].

The cloze procedure, a psycholinguistic technique, involves the principled deletion of text and completion of deleted material by a sample of the target audience; the readability of a passage is defined as the extent to which subjects are able to replicate deleted text which is considered to be representative of the words, grammatical constructions, and turn of phrase in the passage [65].

## The Current Study

This paper illustrates the use of the cloze procedure in assessing the readability of computer-related prose. Cloze tests derived from randomly chosen passages from three introductory COBOL textbooks and the COBOL ANSI Standard were used to analyze readability across sources. The paper also compares cloze results against those for other methods of readability assessment, specific comprehension tests, and certain readability formulas.

## Background

## Readability of College Textbooks

The selection of textbooks in data processing courses is an important activity that has received little attention in the literature. Textbooks and other written materials constitute a primary interface between students and the attainment of data processing knowledge [16]. Readability of a text is a critical factor which can affect student morale and performance.

In many colleges and universities, students come to introductory level courses with different backgrounds and skills. A mismatch between the text and the student's background and capabilities is likely to discourage the student; it can lead to dysfunctional coping activities, such as memorization or course attrition [43]. Student performance may be adversely affected [49], and the instructor may need to spend more time inside and outside the classroom covering documented concepts and examples, defending textbook selections, or coping with frustrated students [1], [17], [61].

Unfortunately, the choice of many college textbooks is based more on expediency than on explicit criteria for judging “good” textbooks. Textbook reviews are often conducted by the author’s peers, and feedback from prospective students in the subject is rarely obtained. Some editors and publishers do make reference to readability formulas; however, readability formulas do not directly measure the extent to which a particular audience understands specific textual material.

## Readability Formulas: An Assessment

Readability formulas are regression equations, comprised of certain linguistic style variables, that are used to predict potential reader difficulty with written text; certain variables are known to correlate with comprehension difficulty across reading levels [9]. The single best indicator of reading difficulty is vocabulary load, typically measured by rarity or difficulty of words, diversity of words, and word length. A second significant indicator of difficulty is sentence structure; the most common measure of syntactic difficulty is sentence length, which correlates highly with other measures of grammatical complexity.

Readability formulas have been shown to correlate with all three types of basic readability measures. Grade-level estimates can be made without time-consuming subject data collection and analysis; increasingly available automated versions of readability formulas improve reliability and reduce time and effort in measurement.

However, there are a number of limitations in the use of readability formulas to assess difficulty, including:

\* The formulas do not consider certain critical reader-based factors known to impact success in reading, such as intelligence, motivation, abilities, purpose, background, expectations, or maturity [30]. The usefulness of readability estimates depends on the validity of underlying assumptions regarding the target audience (e.g., reading abilities).

\* Formulas concentrate on easily quantifiable stylistic aspects of text and do not consider other important text factors, such as content, organization, format, quantity, density, or use of explanation. Linguistic variable counts are insensitive to unusual patterns or uses of words, which clearly impact readability. Other variables may explain additional variance. Finally, such variables constitute only one aspect of style; they do not, for example, indicate whether a particular writing style is persuasive or effective.

\* Formulas only provide correlational evidence of reading difficulty; formula authors do not claim that simple linguistic variables cause difficulty. A strategy to write or revise to formula frequently fails [22], [59]; an author may still write obscurely while using familiar terms and short sentences.

\* The models underlying the traditional formulas have also been questioned. For instance, semantic and syntactic variables may interact [50], and certain traditional variables might depart from linearity.

\* Other objections include the restricted nature of formula validation in terms of subjects (students) and tested passages (not job-related). Furthermore, traditionally used criterion levels of test scores seem inadequate for the analysis of technical passages [23].

## The Cloze Procedure

The cloze procedure is a special case of the Ebbinghaus completion method, a traditional testing procedure [3]. In 1953 Taylor introduced the method as applied to measuring the readability or understandability of textual material, calling it the “cloze procedure” (derived from the closure construct of gestalt psychology, the human tendency to complete familiar, incomplete patterns).

The cloze procedure, in its most general sense, refers to the systematic deletion of words from a representative text passage and completion of the mutilated passage by a sample of the target audience. A cloze test differs from a fill-in-the-blank test in that construction of the latter typically reflects judgmental deletion of text. Several different variations of the cloze procedure have been used in research and practice; these include consideration of the pattern and frequency of deletions, the types of words deleted, whether the subjects are allowed to read the unmutilated passage prior to presentation of the test, and the scoring of items.

A random, or pseudo-random, deletion procedure is used to develop a cloze test; the predominant method is to use an every-nth-word (mechanical deletion) pattern. This is fairly easy to construct and yields an approximately random sampling of text, including all words, grammatical constructions, and turns of phrase [6], [60]. Subject performance in completing successive blanks have been shown to be statistically independent when there are four intervening terms between blanks [47], [66].

Some researchers note that inclusion of easier-to-compete structural items (e.g., articles, pronouns, verb auxiliaries, prepositions, or conjunctions) adversely affects test reliability and they suggest restricting deletion patterns to lexical (content) terms. The general response to the inclusion of nondiscriminating items in a mechanical deletion test has been to lengthen the test to 50 or more items.

The distinction between pre-cloze and post-cloze testing is in the latter's exposure of the reader to the original passage prior to testing. However, results across methods are generally comparable, and hence the less time-consuming pre-cloze procedure has usually been adopted [11], [57]. Scoring is usually by exact-word replacement; synonymic scoring has also been used, but it requires time-consuming, subjective judgments that provide little differentiation beyond exact-word scoring.

The most common form of the cloze procedure includes the following steps: (1) multiple passages of at least 250 words each are randomly selected from different sections of the textual material to be evaluated; (2) each fifth word from some starting point is deleted and replaced by standard-length underlined blanks; (3) the resulting test form is duplicated and given to subjects who have not previously read the passage; (4) subjects are instructed to complete each blank with the word they believe was deleted; and (5) responses are matched against deleted terms and the accumulated number of correct matches is taken as a measure of subject understanding of the underlying passage.

Cloze results have been shown to correlate with those of other readability measures, i.e., judgments [64] and comprehension tests [12]. There are a number of comparative advantages to the use of the common version of the cloze procedure as a readability measure. Cloze tests are simple and economical to prepare, administer, and score; cloze items are embedded within the text itself, avoiding experimenter effects in test development. Cloze tests have been shown to have high scoring reliability and validity as a measure of the relative readability of alternate passages for specific groups of readers; subject responses can be easily and objectively scored.

Unlike a readability formula, the cloze procedure yields a direct measure of the success an individual reader has in interacting with written material. The efficiency of the reading process depends on the reader's ability to predict text; these hypotheses are based on a number of factors, such as the background of the reader, simplicity and consistency in writing style, and the author's adherence to language conventions. The cloze procedure is sensitive to departures from reader understanding and expectations; e.g., use of unconventional phraseology or obscure, unfamiliar terminology. The degree to which a reader can predict missing text is considered to be indicative of successful communication between the author and the reader. The cloze procedure has been shown to be strongly related to a number of verbal abilities, and many studies report significant correlations with general reading achievement and pretest knowledge.

The Cloze Procedure and Computer-Related Studies. The cloze procedure in data processing research has been most often used in the study of computer program comprehension instead of other measures, such as specific comprehension tests, judgments, program replication exercises, debugging location/modification exercises, and software metrics [19]. These studies have generally used student subjects and relatively short high-level computer language programs [24], [34].

The most ostensive difference in the use of the cloze among these studies is selection of the unit of analysis. Some studies (e.g., [62]) have defined a line of code as a cloze unit; a key limitation of these studies is the fact that no validation studies with other software comprehension measures are reported. The other studies have focused on defining program elements (tokens) analogous to the word in a prose passage; e.g., reserved words, constants, variable identifier names, operators, and punctuation. Several of these studies reported significant findings involving cloze results and specific comprehension quiz scores, e.g., [19], [24].

Few studies have been reported dealing with computer-related prose. A recent study by the author [33] focused on the readability of an IEEE standard on software requirement specifications. The hypothesis that graduate MIS students, given their greater data processing background, would significantly outscore MBA students was confirmed; a moderately strong correlation between cloze results and comprehension quiz scores (0.3911) was observed.

The Cloze Procedure and College Textbooks. A traditional problem in the use of the cloze procedure was the absence of any frame of reference against which the size of a particular score could be interpreted; i.e., whether a score represented an “acceptable” level of subject performance. (In contrast, readability formula scores either directly yield or are readily converted to grade-level estimates.) Cloze investigations, making reference to traditional comprehension test criteria, established minimum cloze scores of 44% for instructional use of the material, i.e., reflecting suitable use with supervised guidance, and 57% for independent use [10]. These criteria have since been shown to be relatively stable across populations [48], including, at the instructional level, college students [53].

The accounting literature provides two examples of how the cloze procedure has been used in assessing the readability of college textbooks using indicated criteria levels. A study of four intermediate accounting textbooks concluded, broadly speaking, that all of the texts were suitable for instructional use; however, readability generally varied significantly within texts, and one text was rated more readable than the others [1].

A second study contrasted readability of two original sources and six textbooks on federal taxation; half of the sources failed to reach the instructional use level, and none reached the independent use level. In fact, two texts were rated less readable than the original sources [56].

## Development and Administration of Research Materials

## Development of Study Materials

A key core course in the DPMA [21] undergraduate computer information systems model curriculum is CIS/86-3 (Introduction to Business Application Programming). Whereas the suggested course description does not prescribe a high-level computing language for the course, it notes an explicit preference for COBOL as the primary programming language among academics and practitioners.

Previous studies employing a number of readability formulas in the assessment of COBOL language manuals [44] and the 1985 ANSI COBOL Standard [32] have suggested that the materials are too difficult for many prospective readers to understand. A related question is whether candidate introductory COBOL textbooks sufficiently improve over the readability of primary sources, in particular, the ANSI COBOL Standard.

Hence, four titles were chosen for the purposes of comparison: three introductory texts on structured COBOL programming (Appendix A), and the 1985 ANSI COBOL Standard [4]. The textbooks were submitted to the author for consideration in course adoption. The Standard is the most critical source of information about the form and interpretation of COBOL programs; the 1985 Standard revision includes a number of enhancements designed to facilitate the implementation of structured programming constructs.

Each source was divided into three sections and one passage was selected from each section with the use of a random number table. The use of multiple passages and their random selection provides for more representative, reliable readability assessment and increased control for experimental and sampling bias. The choice of three passages reflects practical constraints and is consistent with prior readability research.

Each study form consisted of an instruction sheet plus two randomly chosen sets of a cloze test followed by a short-answer comprehension quiz. The instruction sheet identified the purpose of the study, indicated the anonymous, voluntary nature of participation, provided instructions for the test sets, and asked the student to identify any prior exposure to COBOL and which of the two test passages he or she found more understandable or acceptable as a COBOL student.

The beginning of a passage was defined as the logical start of the topic discussion identified through the random selection procedure in order to provide additional context to the study participant. Passages ranged between 250 and 280 words in length. Embedded headings and figures were included with the cloze tests but were not subject to the deletion procedure. An every-fifth-word deletion pattern was used because of the technical nature of the material [51] and for comparability with other cloze studies. The starting point for the deletion pattern was randomly chosen from within the first passage sentence. Passages were typewritten double-spaced with each deleted term replaced by an underscored blank 15 pica spaces long and were treated like words for typing of punctuation and spaces. Excerpts from the instruction sheet and a sample cloze test are provided in Appendix B.

For each of the 12 study passages, the author constructed an 8-item, short-answer quiz. The quizzes were designed to measure inferential knowledge over passage contents, including the comparison and contrast or application of concepts. The questions were independent in nature and sampled content over the entire passage. Examples of passage comprehension test questions are listed in Appendix C.

## Data Collection Procedures

Seventy-four students enrolled in two sections of the CIS/86-3 course in a large urban midwestern U.S. university were each randomly provided a study form to complete voluntarily at the beginning of the semester. The author supplemented written with oral instructions and remained in the classroom to supervise data collection procedures. The study objects were allowed the full lecture period to complete the forms.

Of the 148 test sets, 12 (mostly second sets of the forms) included generally incomplete cloze or comprehension tests and were excluded from further analysis; the subjects in question may have found the cloze procedure relatively fatiguing [8]. However, subsequent analysis showed that sequence had no significant effect over retained tests. In addition, reported prior exposure to COBOL (e.g., in a dropped course) had little effect on cloze results, and accordingly these data were merged with the remaining data for analysis. Scoring was by exact replacement (allowing for minor misspellings). The retained tests numbered over 30 for each of the four text sources, allowing for reasonably reliable results.

## Cloze Tests and Comprehension Tests

If cloze tests and comprehension tests, presumed measures of the reading comprehension of data processing written materials, both measure the same skill, then the correlation between them should be significantly, positively correlated. Since the scores were approximately linearly related, the correlation coefficient was computed: 0.3844, 1-tailed p < 0.001.

Normality of the distribution of cloze and comprehension test scores was verified by approximately linear normal probability plots. Homogeneity of variance was supported for both variables by Cochrans C test probabilities exceeding 0.4. ## Readability Formulas and the Cloze Procedure

Selection of Readability Formulas. Today there are literally hundreds of readability formulas developed for different purposes or ages of readers [40]. However, variations of two traditional formulas have been widely used in research and practice: the Flesh Reading Ease and Dale-Chall formulas. One reviewer concluded there is no "best" formula, but found the Dale-Chall formula [20] to be the most accurate and the Flesh Reading Ease formula [25] to be the most popular among the traditional formulas [39]. The two formulas primarily differ in vocabulary load variables: the Dale-Chall formula uses word difficulty (the percentage of passage words not on a list of 3000 common words), whereas the Reading Ease formula uses word length (syllables per 100 words).

The Flesch Reading Ease and Dale-Chall formulas were designed primarily for the assessment of adult written materials, of which study passages are a subset, and have often been used in textbook assessment studies. The Flesh formula has also been used to investigate technical documents, computer language manuals, and major computing periodicals. More than one formula is used, given the questionable reliance on a single formula [32].

Procedure. For each text passage, the first ten sentences were chosen for analysis. (A sentence is defined, in the context of readability research, as a logically separable unit of thought which may be physically separated by periods, semicolons, or colons.) A microcomputer program was used to provide syllable estimates and to parse each passage into word groups for further analysis. After minor corrective adjustments to the data, the passages were scored for the traditional formulas following originally stated procedures and examples.

## Text Acceptability and Cloze Scores

Readability problems can adversely affect acceptability of technical material [42]. Dissatisfaction with the readability of technical material can negatively impact reader behavior; for instance, the intended reader may decide not to use the text in lieu of alternative sources [63]. One study found a significant relationship between readability of course materials and student retention rate in correspondence courses [43].

It was hypothesized, based on the relationship between acceptability and readability, that preferred passages would have greater cloze scores. Each student was asked to provide his or her opinion which of the passages would be more acceptable or understandable to a COBOL student. Of the 48 stated preferences, 35 were associated with higher cloze scores (binomial test Z approximation 1-tailed p=0.0012). A paired-samples t-test yielded a significant difference between preferred passage cloze scores (mean 24.85, s.d. 6.4) and those for less preferred passages (mean 20.9, s.d. 8) (t=3.58, df=47, 1-tailed p<0.001).

## Discussion

## Readability of Study Materials

All four text sources had cloze means which did not fall significantly above or below the 44% criterion threshold; this finding suggests suitability of use of the materials in supervised instruction. However, there are a number of reasons to treat this conclusion with caution.

First, the observed means approximate the bottom of the instructional use interval (44–57%). In fact, the means of two of the 4 sources fell below the 44% level, and at least one-third of the subjects over each source failed to reach this level. For a significant number of subjects in this study, the materials seem too difficult to use even with close supervision by the instructor.

Second, even though a number of studies support the use of the 44% criterion across reader ability levels, including college students, some argue that the criterion is probably understated for adult readers. As reading ability increases, redundancies of grammar and language become more evident. Hence, more advanced readers should be better able to predict certain missing terms based on context alone, whether or not the base passage is comprehensible [14].

Third, the independent use criterion (57%-above) may be more appropriate than the instructional use criterion in the assessment of these materials. The use of higher criterion levels is advocated when the material is novel in nature, significant interaction with the material beyond available instructional guidance or more extensive comprehension of material is required [54]. These conditions appear relevant to the materials in this study. Many CIS/86-3 students enter the course with little, if any, working knowledge of compiled computing languages and structured programming concepts. The completion of COBOL programming assignments requires considerable time and effort outside of the classroom and intimate familiarity with related textual sources. Moreover, success with technical materials requires a high comprehension level; the production of error-free, efficient code depends on a mastery of documented computing language concepts.

An examination of the unadjusted (57%) independent use criterion data in Table 1 reveals that the cloze means of all tested text sources significantly fail to achieve this level, indicating a mismatch between the materials and study subjects.

The readability formula data in Table 2 further corroborates the evidence of a reader-text mismatch. The Flesch means of the passages are comparable in difficulty to those found for academic journal articles. For a rough estimate, chronological grade level of the reader may be taken as a surrogate measure of actual reading level. However, in many cases chronological grade level may significantly overestimate reading ability.

In addition, many reading experts suggest that the reading level of novel material should be below the ability level of the reader.

Although the study data indicate readability problems with all of the text sources, they also show some sources are more difficult than others. For example, the ANSI COBOL Standard consistently ranked lowest in understandability across cloze results, comprehension test scores, and readability formula estimates, and many of these were statistically significant differences with one or more of the textbooks. It is interesting to note the significant cloze differences were found despite the comparably higher redundant style and greater proportion of (easier) structural deletions associated with the ANSI passage cloze tests.

## Selected Criticisms of the Cloze Procedure

An author can “write to cloze” by adopting an intentionally redundant writing style; higher scores may reflect more the successful processing of linguistic patterns and cues than greater understanding of the evaluated passage. Some studies suggest that cloze results reflect local redundancy and/or are sentence-bound. Consider the following excerpt from an ANSI Standard study test passage:

"The generic terms identification-division, environment-division, data-division, procedure-division, source-program, and end-program-header represent a COBOL Identification Division, a COBOL Environment Division, a COBOL Data Division, a COBOL Procedure Division, a COBOL source program, and a COBOL end program header, respectively."

Most adult readers could complete conventional cloze deletion patterns on this sentence without any understanding of COBOL language structure or making reference to prior context.

Mean cloze scores for alternate tests over the same passage may significantly differ. Many, if not most, cloze studies use single-form cloze tests over passages; the assumption is that a mechanical deletion pattern will result in a representative sample of text, no matter what the starting point. An example from the study can illustrate the point. The mean score associated with one ANSI passage test exceeded the 44% correct threshold; however, comprehension test results over the passage did not reflect improved understanding over those for the other ANSI passages. A subsequent investigation of cloze items in the test revealed a disproportionately high number of (easier) structural items relative to the passage as a whole (and the other cloze tests). Greater reliability of cloze tests may be obtained by: (1) increasing the number of items in the test, and/or (2) using multiple cloze tests over a passage.

The cloze procedure is a measure, not a predictor of readability; nontrivial effort is required in testing sufficiently large enough samples of readers. In many cases, cloze results may not generalize beyond the sample.

Conventional cloze tests usually contain non-discriminating items, the inclusion of which are questionable from the standpoint of classical test theory [58].

Much cloze research has focused on exact-word-scoring using every-fifth-deletion patterns; however, these deletion patterns or scoring procedures may not be appropriate for some types of materials, and insufficient criterion-related research has been conducted with other variations of the cloze procedure.

## Summary

Readers often find data processing written materials, such as application software documentation, difficult to understand. Readability problems with documentation typically result in significant direct and indirect costs to the organization.

This paper introduces the cloze procedure to the information systems literature. The cloze procedure provides an objective, cost-effective alternative to the use of judgments and comprehension tests in testing the suitability of specific textual materials for a particular sample of readers. A cloze test draws upon the reader's ability to assimilate information in a passage, reading and reasoning skills, and background. Unlike readability formulas, the cloze makes no assumptions about the ability levels of readers or the relative importance of factors accounting for difficulty in reading.

This study illustrated the use of the cloze procedure by examining the readability of three introductory COBOL textbooks and the ANSI COBOL Standard for college students. Results indicated differences in readability among the text sources, with the ANSI Standard more difficult to understand. However, a significant number of responses with each source failed to reach the criterion reference level associated with suitability for use with supervised instruction, and none of the cloze text means approached the level associated with suitability for independent study use, a more appropriate criterion for learning novel or technical material. The study also showed that cloze scores significantly correlate with comprehension test results and correspond with readability formula estimates over the passages and that students prefer more readable passages.

The findings of the study underscore the need for documenters and other authors of technical prose to obtain evidence concerning reader-text interactions during the development of written materials. Implementation of the cloze procedure provides one way of operationalizing such input; greater accuracy can be achieved by increasing the number and length of randomly selected text passages, the use of multiple test forms per passage, and the number of subjects in a study.
