# Assessing the Impact of IS Executives' Critical Success Factors on the Performance of IS Organizations \*

T.S. Raghunathan

College of Business Administration, The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

Yash P. Gupta

Department of Management, University of Louisville, Louisville, KY 40292, USA

P.S. Sundararaghavan

College of Business Administration, The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

This study reports the results of a survey conducted in 205 US organizations. The study attempts to relate critical success factors (CSF) of IS managers with performance of IS organizations. It has been concluded that all the performance measures identified in this study are significantly related to the CSFs of IS managers. Relations with and support of the objectives of the parent organization is most significantly related to performance, whereas DP operations is least significantly related to performance. On the other hand, improved user job performance is most significantly related to CSFs, whereas widespread use of IS is least related to CSFs.

Keywords: IS department performance, Critical success factors, System development, Data processing operations, Human resource development, Management control, Relationship with parent organization.

\* All authors have contributed equally in this study. We acknowledge the constructive comments made by four referees on the previous version of this paper.

Martin [31,32], studied 15 large businesses and government organizations and identified six CSFs for IS executives, namely (i) systems development, (ii) data processing operations, (iii) human resource development, (iv) management control of the MIS/DP organization, (v) relationship with parent organization and (vi) support of objectives and priorities of the parent organization. At the same time Rockart [41] operationalized this concept by extensive interviews of IS executives of 9 organizations. Recently, Guimaraes [19] has attempted to rank critical success factors based on their importance. Other significant contributions using the CSF methodology in the area of IS have been made by Boynton and Zmud [3], Rockart and Crescenzi [42], Shank et al. [44], and Zahedi [53]. Several benefits are expected from establishing CSFs. These include:

(i) MIS managers can focus attention on the most important task and prioritize the investment of resources.

(ii) They can be used as a basis for better communication between MIS managers and their subordinates or superiors.

(iii) MIS managers may use them as a vehicle for defining their information requirements.

(iv) Corporate managers may use them as the basis to evaluate the performance of their MIS department.

(v) MIS managers, in general, can use the list of CSFs identified by MIS managers of leading organizations as a preview of areas which may become important in their own organizations.

The concept of CSF's has been used in numerous other areas including corporate planning [22][36], guiding board of directors [14], university planning and IS planning. Applications have also appeared in areas such as small business [12], financial services and hospital administration [2].

Davis [10,11] noted some potential difficulties with CSF methodology. He suggested that managers may inadvertently fail to recall some CSFs, especially when the manager and analyst interviews are unstructured. Other areas which could impair the effectiveness of the CSF approach include (i) bounded rationality, (ii) human ability to evaluate probability and causality, and (iii) the biasing effect of availability of data. Munro and Wheeler [48] suggested an approach which lends itself to structured discussion between the manager and the analyst. This is done by early identification of goals and objectives through a planning process. Hall and Munro suggested the use of cognitive mapping (by employing system dynamics) in developing CSFs of managers. An extensive critique of the CSF method by Boynton and Zmud [3] concluded that any weakness as identified by Davis that may occur using this method can be largely overcome by the careful application of the technique.

Miller and Doyle [35] in a study of financial institutions, found that IS effectiveness is a function of the relationship between perceived importance of the particular aspect of IS and the level of performance. They further suggested that factors related to IS effectiveness can be mapped well onto the CSFs of IS managers. Therefore, one can argue that if IS managers were to manage the IS function according to their CSFs, it should result in an improved IS performance in an organization. Munro and Wheeler seems to corroborate with Miller and Doyle's argument by stating that:

"The IS manager's CSFs, IS performance measures and their desired levels should guide managers in the performance of their responsibilities over the designated period, and facilitate more objective evaluation of their performance."

In spite of the fact that several authors have argued that a relationship exists between IS manager's CSFs and IS functions performance, almost all the studies concentrated in identifying and applying the CSF methodology, however few, if any, have attempted to investigate this relationship empirically. The purpose of our study is to do so. Here we conceptualize the IS function's performance in a multidimensional perspective.

## Research Methodology

## Choice of Data-Analytic Technique

Three data analytic schemes could have been employed in examining the interrelationships between critical success factors and IS performance variables, namely: (i) factor analysis of the two data sets of dimensions reflecting CSFs and IS performance, followed by a correlational analysis of the factors extracted, (ii) separate multiple regression analysis for each CSF, and (iii) a canonical analysis approach. The possible use of the first alternative in pursuing the objectives of this type of study has been questioned by Wimmer [51]:

"Independent factor analyses are fine if the researcher wants factors chosen independently of each other. It is not a good procedure if one wants to explain as much (characteristics) of one data set from the other."

The second alternative neglects a possible interrelationship between IS manager's CSFs; e.g., Martin [32] suggested that management control overlaps with the areas of systems development and data processing operations. Thus in line with Kuylen and Verhallen's [27] observation that “a composite criterion variate may better reflect reality than a single criterion variable in a separate regression analysis”, we have opted to use the third alternative.

## Measurement

Performance of individual information systems has been the subject of investigation for a considerable time. Various approaches to measure a systems performance have been suggested including [7][8]: cost–benefit analysis [49], user satisfaction [25], value added and value chains [38]. However, the research effort in measuring IS organizational performance is sparse. Brancheau and Wetherbe [4] argued that:

"Measurement [of IS performance] has long been a problem for IS executives. While measurement of performance is crucial for sound management, few concrete measures exists for assessing the health of the IS organization. While costs are relatively straightforward to establish, benefits continue to be difficult to quantify. Underlying the problem is the IS profession's inability to establish and quantify the value of information. Meanwhile, measurement continues to be a critical problem as organizations invest more and more in information systems."

Guimaraes and Gupta [21] have discussed in detail the reasons for the difficulties IS managers are experiencing in measuring IS organization's performance. Doll [13] has tried to measure organization wide information systems development effort which is only a part of overall IS functions performance. In the absence of any well researched measures of performance of IS function, we have identified from the literature different dimensions which have been discussed as the most important criteria. They are: profitability [6][16][39], application of IS to major problems of the organization [29], quality of decisions, user satisfaction, and widespread use [50]. The perception of IS managers regarding the achievement of these six criteria is considered as a measure of performance of IS organization. Appendix 1 gives the set of questions used to measure performance. We have also measured IS managers' perception regarding overall IS performance using a single question to validate the construct measured by the above six individual objectives.

Martin's CSFs were used to capture IS managers CSFs for the companies surveyed in this study. Systems Development was measured by the timeliness of response to user needs, efficiency in selecting projects and reliability and cost effectiveness of systems. Data processing operations were measured by systems availability, security, response time, and error-free reporting. Development of human resources was measured by recruitment, retention, employee morale, and career development plans for technical and managerial personnel of the IS organization. Management control of the MIS/DP organization was measured by systems development and maintenance cost control, user involvement and satisfaction, performance as compared to budget, standardization of the procedures, and effectiveness of IS planning. Relationship with parent organization was measured by the involvement of top management and effectiveness of alignment of priorities of IS department with other functional areas, as well as with top management. The nature of the support given by IS organization to the objectives and priorities of IS organization and how the IS organization regards user priorities were also considered.

## Data Collection

A survey questionnaire measuring the constructs using a multi-item 5 point Likert-type scale was developed for data collection. The group of items to measure each critical success factor is given in Appendix 2.

The target population for this cross-sectional survey consisted of 1000 IS executives who were randomly selected from “The facts on the file: Directory of Major Public Corporations” by Stanley Greenfield, which contains about 6000 addresses of various U.S. organizations. The questionnaire, with a cover letter explaining the purpose of the study, was mailed during May, 1986. The first set of 120 responses were received within a span of 3 weeks. A reminder letter was sent to non-respondent organizations and another 85 responses were received. Out of these 205 responses (representing a 20.5% response rate), 199 were usable. To check for any bias between original respondents and those who responded to the reminder, tests were conducted on the basis of sales and industry type. No bias could be identified. ## Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the interrelationship between IS manager's CSFs and the performance of IS organizations.

Rockart suggested that the relative importance given to each CSF may vary, however no suggestion has been made about the relative weighting of each. The linear combination of the predictor set dimensions derived from the canonical analysis can be considered to be an empirically based measure of importance of the CSFs of IS managers. An examination of the loadings of five CSFs indicate that they are significantly related to the IS performance measures used in this study.

The CSF “Relations with and support of the objectives of the parent organization” (with a loading of 0.844) figures most predominantly in the predictor set. This can be explained by the following:

(i) The role of the IS manager is no longer just as the custodian of data with relatively little or no knowledge of business goals nor access to corporate plans. He/She has been charged with the responsibility for developing policies and strategies for information resources of the firm. This includes strategic planning, organizational learning, and organizational alignment. In discharging such responsibility, an IS manager must understand the inter-connections of information flow to the business. These results have been corroborated by a recent survey [23] where aligning MIS with business goals has been ranked second in order of importance by IS managers.

The second most important dimension in the predictor set is human resource development (with a loading of 0.723). One of the plausible explanation for such a high loading is that in recent years an increasing number of organizations are adopting information technology, which in turn has resulted in an increased demand for IS personnel [34]. For example, in 1984 it was forecasted that in organizations with IS budgets of more than \$1 million, the demand for IS personnel would increase by 20% in that year [30]. This increased demand is further exacerbated by the fact that organizations are experiencing a very high turnover among these professionals [17]. For example, in 1985, in some regions of the USA this turnover rate was in excess of 21% [24]. Although not all turnover has a negative organizational implication [46], excessive turnover has been demonstrated to have a negative impact on organizational performance [47].

It is interesting to observe that the loading for systems development (0.584) is relatively small, in spite of the fact that it has been considered by IS executives as part of a crucial triad with planning and aligning IS with business. On the other hand Brancheau and Wetherbe reported that this factor has dropped from fourth to thirteenth in importance for IS executives, which si consistent with our findings. They argue that this could be due to the fact that IS executives are in the midst of transition from managers of technology and applications to fully fledged corporate managers with their focus of attention primarily oriented towards external management issues. This argument has also been corroborated by Benjamin et al. [1]. Other explanations are that:

(i) An increasing number of organizations are using packages which can enhance the speed and reduce the cost of applications development. For example, the U.S. Forest Service developed and implemented a system for one site at a cost of \$150,000 and was subsequently able to install it at over 100 other sites for a unit cost of less than \$15,000. Moreover an added benefit was a decrease in maintenance cost [18].

(ii) The advent of fourth generation languages, such as FOCUS, RAMIS II, and NOMAD, which can accomplish many tasks with less code.

(iii) The development of prototyping approaches which allows IS professionals to build "quick and dirty" systems which are then refined and modified as they are used, in a continuous process, until the system is acceptable to the user.

The loadings for DP operations (0.581) is also very small as compared to other dimensions in the predictor set, in spite of the fact that in some organizations, such as financial institutions, the efficient and effective running of DP operations is critical. Reasons for this are:

(i) An increasing number of organizations are decentralizing their DP operations; i.e. they are implementing the concept of focussed factory (or factory within a factory) introduced by Skinner [45]. This has improved their DP operations significantly. For example, in a decentralized insurance company, senior staff, after careful analysis, recommended an orderly dissolution of the company's \$25 million data center and the creation of 8 smaller diversified data centers over a 30 month period [33].

(ii) There has been a phenomenal growth in end-user computing (more so personal computing) which allows users with relatively little or no knowledge of data processing to develop simple applications [20]. For example, it has been estimated that end-user computing accounts for 10% of the computer capacity of large organizations and it is expected that this will grow to 70% of an expanded capacity by the end of the decade. In some organizations the budget for personal computing is close to or has surpassed the budgets of central computing [26].

The loading for management control of MIS organizations (0.702) is high. This may be due to the facts that:

(i) Increasing proportion of DP budgets are being allocated to maintenance of existing software systems developed by the DP departments. For example, it has been suggested that up to 80% of DP budgets and 75% of DP personnel time are allocated to maintenance, thus leaving few resources for DP departments to develop new systems [5][43].

(ii) There is a considerable backlog of user requests for the development of computer applications. One study found that the invisible backlog of user needs (that is, those which have not yet formally entered the queue of pending user requests) averaged 164% of the declared backlog. The total backlog measured in this study represented 179% of the entire base of installed applications.

(iii) The information services include office automation, telecommunications and data processing. Co-ordination in these three areas is extremely difficult, because they have different technological bases, they are marketed to the organizations individually, and they have different managerial histories and decision making habits associated with them. Managing the islands of technologies to improve IS performance requires proper IS planning.

An examination of Table 7 one can infer that all six dependent variables (Criterion set) are significantly related to the predictor set. Improved user job performance has emerged as the most important contributor to IS organizational performance by contributing approximately 39% of the explained variance. This result is consistent with the results obtained by other authors, who have applied this methodology on a case study basis. For example, Shank et al. found that CSF methodology “has increased the productivity of established staff members, allowed new staff members to become productive more rapidly than in the past, and contributed to the generation of new ideas by staff members at all levels of the corporation”. However it is interesting to observe that user satisfaction represents one of the smallest variances in the dependent variable set. This can be explained by the fact that user satisfaction may not be an appropriate measure of performance. Chismar et al. suggested that the arguments on which the use of user satisfaction as a surrogate measure of IS performance in the literature has several flaws. They stated that, in the context of evaluating the performance of IS, satisfaction may be a necessary condition for economic success of effectiveness and nothing more.

Similarly the loading for widespread use of IS is low though significant. This may be explained by the following:

(i) With the proliferation of personal computing in organization, users have become relatively self-sufficient with respect to their information needs. For example, Lee [28] suggested that only 60% of the respondents of a survey had access to mainframe computing, however they spent significantly more time (3:1) with personal computing than mainframe computing.

(ii) The improved IS organization may not necessarily reflect, at least in the short run, an increase in numbers of people using IS services but may simply mean an improvement in services already provided to the existing user base.

Profitability of IS projects ranks second highest in factor loading in the dependent variable set. This shows that IS managers may still be evaluated on the basis of economic performance (i.e., cost benefit analysis) of their organization.

Application of IS to major problems of organization is also significant to CSFs. This is in line with arguments provided by Wiseman [52] and Porter and Miller, who suggested that an increasing number of organizations are using IS for gaining strategic advantage.

## Limitations

In this research, six indicators of IS performance have been used. Although they correlate with the single performance measure, they may not be an adequate representation of the total performance. Further research should concentrate on developing good, measurable constructs of IS performance. We have used only the CSFs derived by Martin. It may be possible that these factors might have changed or might not adequately represent all the important factors. A series of recent studies indicate that some of the important issues are changing due to the dynamic environment of the information systems field. Hence it may be important for the researchers to constantly update the CSFs and operationalize them to study their relationship with other organizational variables.

## Conclusion

Several methodologies have been suggested to assist IS managers in defining their information requirements, including CSF.

This study related CSF of IS managers with performance of IS organizations. It has been found that all the performance measures identified in this study are significantly related to the CSFs of IS managers. Relations with and support of the objectives of the parent organization is most sigsignificantly related to performance, whereas DP operations is least significantly related to performance. On the other hand, improved user job performance is most significantly related to CSFs, whereas widespread use of IS is least related to CSFs.

## Detailed Exposition of Results

In this study, with five CSFs in the predictor set (independent variables) and six measures of IS performance in the criterion set (dependent variables), five canonical functions (i.e., pairs of canonical variates) were extracted. Multivariate test results, given in Table 4, indicate that the predictor set (CSFs) has a statistically significant impact on the criterion set (measures of IS performance). Table 5 shows that only the first canonical correlate (or function) is significant. The canonical correlation si the amount of correlation between each corresponding pair of canonical variates. The absolute magnitude of the canonical correlation (0.695) is quite high. However, some would rightly argue that it is an inflated measure of the underlying relationship since it is the purpose of the canonical analysis to maximize the correlation between linear combinations of two sets of variables [15]. It is therefore necessary to examine in greater detail the nature of the canonical variates themselves.

Table 6, column 1 provides the canonical weights (W) or coefficients of each variable in the canonical variate for the criterion-predictor sets. The canonical weight assigned to each variable in computing the canonical functions reflect the importance of the original variables in deriving the canonical variates. The variables whose weights have opposite signs would exhibit an inverse relationship with each other and those with the same sign a direct one. However, this interpretation has been criticized as being inappropriate, particularly in cases where the variable sets are multicollinear.

Column 2 shows the canonical loadings or structural correlations (L). These are linear combinations of the variables in each set weighted by their respective canonical weights. Although canonical loadings are considered to be more valid than weights as a means of interpreting the nature of canonical relationships, they are subject to considerable variability from one sample to another. A comparison of relative rankings of the variables based on their canonical weights and loadings show a significant perturbation of the ranks. No definite guideline exists as to what the magnitude of canonical loadings should be in order to show that a strong relationship is in evidence. However, for purposes of comparison, loading values of 0.3 and above are considered to be sufficient for drawing inferences about criterion-predictor set relationships. An examination of the canonical loadings in the predictor set shows that the most important influence on the performance of IS organizations is “Relations with and support of the objectives of the parent organization” (0.844), followed by “Human resource development” (0.723).

The $L^{2}$ values in column 3 are a measure of the variance which the observed variable shares with the canonical variates and can be interpreted like a factor loading in assessing the relative contribution of each variable to each canonical function.

Their values range from 0.105 to 0.706 (average 0.3) and 0.338 to 0.712 (average 0.481) for the dimensions in the criterion and predictor sets respectively.

Stewart and Love's redundancy index provides a measure of the variance in the criterion or predictor set explained by the canonical correlations between the two sets. It is computed for each canonical function by multiplying the squared canonical correlation for the respective function and the average value of the squared canonical loadings across the variables in a given set. The average of the squared canonical loadings is a measure of the variance in the original variables extracted by the canonical variate. Table 7 shows the variance extracted and the redundancy indices for the first canonical function in the criterion and predictor sets. This function alone captures 30% of the variance in the original variables of the criterion set, and 48% of the variance in the original variables in the predictor set. The redundancy index (Table 7) of 0.145 is the shared variable between the first canonical variate and the variables in the predictor set. The shared variance between the first canonical variate and variables in the criterion set is 0.233. Note the asymmetry in the redundancy indices, which is due to the differing number of variables in each set. Overall, the high values of the canonical correlations and moderate values of redundancy indices taken together, suggest the presence of a strong multivariate relationship between personal computing problems and organizational factors.
