# The Impact of Information Technology on Organisational Structures

Janice M. Burn

Hong Kong Polytechnic, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong

This paper describes an effort to assess the impact of information technology on organisational structures. In particular it examines the problem of applying this research to a virtually greenfield environment since little investigation has taken place on Asian companies; also no definitive work presently exists on the global Hong Kong environment, which is unique in Asia, if not the world. At this time, we are able to assess: the analytical framework and overall conceptual approach to the project; use of existing structural analysis models to develop a basic Hong Kong model; characteristics of the basic Hong Kong model and the refinements for a new model; approaches for the measurement of system effectiveness. The project covers an analysis of IT, and IS for the spectrum of computer system users in Hong Kong including local and multi-national firms.

Keywords: Structures, Information technology, Organisations, Impact, Strategies.

“Throughout history, progress in technological endeavour whether based on intuition, trial and error or scientific discoveries has had profound social significance. Perhaps its implications have been most clearly evidenced in the structure, conduct and, in many cases the very existence of work organisations. Although observers are united in their belief that technology and technological changes have been intimately and significantly related to changes in the nature and functioning of work organisations, the particular patterning of such interactions remains, in large measure, problematic.” (Mansfield, [14])

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. The Project

A research project on the “Impact of Information Technology on Organisational Structures” is underway in the Department of Computing Studies Hong Kong Polytechnic.

The aims of the project are:

(1) To identify whether any correlation exists between the Structure of an organisation and its usage of Information Technology (IT).

(2) To measure the effectiveness of usage and identify whether a direct relationship exists between specific approaches adopted by organisations, the organisation structure and the benefits. (3) To develop a framework that can be used by organisations for strategic planning in the development and use of Information Systems/Technology.

## 1.2. The Rationale

There has long been speculation about the effects that IT might bring to bear on organisational

Janice Burn is currently Principal Lecturer at Hong Kong Polytechnic and is course leader for the Masters degree in Information Systems. Prior to coming to Hong Kong 3 years ago she had 2 books published in the UK and recently Paradigm UK published her third text "Strategic Approaches to Information Systems and Technology" intended for Post-Graduate students with a business background. Her current research is focussed on the impact of IT on organisations and also structures but no one consensus of opinion. Indeed one has to exercise great caution when reading accounts or predictions of the effects of IT since the wide range of applications leads to a similarly diverse range of effects, many of which may be in different directions [22].

More recently, concerted efforts have been made in the UK and USA to establish causal relationships between computer technology and organisational structures. Whilst there is substantiation of such effects, work by Boddy and Buchanan at Glasgow [1] suggests that none of these predicted effects are inevitable, and that any changes in structure which accompany the introduction of new technology reflect choices made within the organisation concerned.

The observed consequences have been:

\- there is no structural change

\- the horizontal dimensions alter: there is a deletion of old functions, and creation of new ones.
- the vertical shape alters: there may be a new number of levels in the hierarchy, and a changed balance between centralised and decentralised decision making.

By identifying areas where management can exercise significant structural choices and highlighting the likely effects of any such decision, the value of such research can be found.

This work, however, relates to organisations outside Asia where a number of earlier studies have already classified structural dimensions and related this to IT usage. The nature of Hong Kong organisations and the pace of change in the community is such that it is impossible to relate such work directly to the environment. Yet the strategic opportunities presented by IT merit further study with many documented examples of competitive advantage resulting from effective use of IT. [2,13,21].

Earl (1986) [3] suggests that there are 4 prime reasons for the use of IT as a strategic resource:

\- to gain competitive advantage

\- to improve productivity and performance

\- to enable new ways of managing and organising

\- to develop new businesses

and as a consequence IT and IS should no longer be considered support activities but harnessed to support this firm's strategy and structure.

This indicates that a new research area has arrived, potentially combining at least the bodies of knowledge about information systems, business strategy, organisational behaviour, technology management and industrial economics. Practitioners have been experimenting, consultants developing and academics evaluating and the results, although often preliminary and suggestive rather than robust and prescriptive, are helping to define the area, guide management policy and practice and show where further research is needed [3].

Our project is directed towards the Hong Kong situation and a rationalisation of how best to capitalise on the benefits of applied IT in this environment.

## 1.3. Frameworks for Analysis

Michael Earl 1986 [4,5] identified 3 types of framework for the analysis of IT and IS strategies:

\- awareness

\- opportunity

\- positioning

and suggests that with a workable set of all three we would be creating a three-level complementary set of frameworks for analysis that

\- show what is possible

\- help identify applications

\- guide how to get there.

These are illustrated in Table 1.

Awareness helps to identify where strategic opportunities exist; opportunity provides more detailed techniques or models for analysis or identification of strategic uses of IT; positioning helps to assess the strategic importance of IT and how the IS functions can be managed.

It is not possible in our project to provide detailed models at all three levels but the approach has been to integrate them at a meta level and then set the groundwork by arriving at a micro-level analysis of Awareness models.

At the Awareness level, we concentrate on evaluating the usage of IT in Hong Kong and identifying for specific organisational configurations where proven opportunities exist to exploit IT. At the micro-level further investigation of the models as detailed case studies aims to assess the current and potential impact of IT and hence provide a general awareness of what is currently happening. The Awareness model provides examples by analogy rather than direct guidance for usage.

At the next stage, focus is directed towards the firm's specific context and activities and explores how organisations are using IT to improve productivity and performance; it relates this to an analysis of the structural framework of the organisation. The Opportunity model seeks to develop a way that managers can adopt a radically different perspective of the organisation to derive the most appropriate framework and exploit the strategic advantages of IT.

Finally, Positioning frameworks aim to develop an understanding of how the Information Systems function should be managed. We can make no detailed analysis but seek to identify a situational framework for management which considers not only IT but also factors such as structure, business, environment, management etc. These are to be used in their strategic assessment of how to maximise the potential of IT in the organisation.

## 2. Conceptual Approach

## 2.1. The Modelling Approach

This project obviously requires a large data collection and synthesis operation. Our plan was to complete part of the study prior to the detailed data collection and to define an acceptable model framework for a Hong Kong organisation and hence the parameters which should be used to measure key values of the organisation. This would be used to develop the questionnaire and after analysis of returns, a second iteration would then relate directly to the Hong Kong organisational environment. Follow up with interviews providing the detail, would allow the development of model 3. This would then be refined to produce a strategic planning model. ## 2.2. Existing Structural Models

Available structural definitions offer a variety of classification criteria. Detailed synthesis of this work to derive a composite model was considered but rejected on the grounds that there is no evidence that our organisational sample can be typified according to such classifications: little work has taken place in Asia and little work has related to IT in organisations. The project would thus have to define a unique classification and this would form MODEL 1.

In order to develop this MODEL, Mintzberg's 5 structural forms were used as a starting point. They are:

simple structure,

machine bureaucracy,

professional bureaucracy,

divisionalised form,

adhocracy.

The importance of this basic model lies in the well documented parameters which Mintzberg used and which several other researchers have since applied [15,17,10]. From these, a sub-set were defined for our preliminary model:

## 2.3. Hong Kong Organisations

The organisational environment in Hong Kong is very different from the US or European model. [11,25].

Businesses in Hong Kong are typically small and account for around 75% of the total. A typical example is illustrated by the 1987 government statistics where out of a population of 48,992 manufacturing organisations over 65% employed less than 10 persons and only 3% over 200 persons!

Small organisations are operated as owner-controlled companies with close relatives employed in key positions and are not controlled by formal structures but by obligation networks. Few of these small companies are likely, to fall within the data sample, however, since few are as yet exploiting IT. Many family based companies have acquired subsidiaries and introduced public capital but the style of management is still very much owner centred supported by increasingly distant concentric circles of family members. [25,26] The networks which can result from these family webs are often publicly untraceable since non-public companies do not have to file accounts for public inspection; and even where public companies are involved nominee holdings are permitted and widely used. Thus actual ownership and control can be difficult to trace.

These organisational factors – quite unique to Hong Kong – enforced a more rigorous definition of organisations within the questionnaire. When possible (i.e. for public companies) the data was collected from public records and then used as a validator on receipt of replies.

## 4. Measurements of Effectiveness

The first issue relates to measurement of information systems properties where work by Ginzberg (1981), Turner (1981) and Lucas (1981) [6,27,12] has already suggested system properties that have been shown to be fairly reliable. In particular, Ginzberg introduced the notion of complexity as a way of grouping systems into categories and this has been expanded by both Lucas (13 aspects of information systems) and Turner (5 aspects). This project is not trying to evaluate particular systems but rather the overall impact of IS/IT usage and so organisations will be ranked on their current state of development. This will include an evaluation of the complexity of systems using the Turner model

\- processing organisation,

\- technical complexity,

\- organisational complexity,

\- functional completeness type.

This will also be related to work by Hedberg (1980) and Nolan (1979) [7,18,19] suggesting models of the way organisations use computers, achieving maturity and hence greater effectiveness in the final stage of the model - 3 and 6 respectively. The frameworks are developed from the questionnaires.

A further measure of effectiveness relates to the quantified cost/benefits which have resulted, matched against the overall increase in profitability/productivity of the organisation. This opens a can of worms, since very few organisations are applying more sophisticated techniques than straightforward Payback calculations (based on capital investment and savings recouped through staff reduction). Also, the intensive measurement systems such as Strassman's value Added measurement, IBM's Four Layer Methodology, and Parker and Benson's Information Economics [23,20] relate more specifically to evaluation of specific systems benefits rather than the impact of IT as a whole on the workplace. However, a number of studies have suggested ways to apply cost/benefit analysis and to relate the results to improved productivity over the whole organisation.

Han Chun Kwong and Mohd Zain Mohamed (1985) applied a computerisation index which measured the extent and sophistication of computer usage of petroleum companies in Kuala Lumpur (10 variables with different weightings) and related this to a measure of corporate financial performance (6 measures) and identified strong correlations [9].

Peter Weill of New York University [28] has explored this with regard to IT investment in Utilities using the model of Figure 6.

He relates this to the organisational strategy at three levels; transaction, information and strategic. Whilst input measurement is reasonably easy it requires the organisation to perform some sort of self-tracking to measure the benefits and from there to identify an optimum strategy.

Obviously any real study of measurement will have to take place at an in-depth level with selected organisations. It is hoped, however, that sufficient detail has been inbuilt to the questionnaire to allow identification of overall trends.

A report by A.T. Kearney [8] for the UK government suggested that “Companies lagging in the use of IT are 6 times more likely to have a poor financial performance within their sector than the companies leading in the use of IT” and lists characteristics that can be used to categorise organisations with regard to their effectiveness in implementing IT. The characteristics are fairly easily identified and this approach could be particularly useful if Hong Kong organisations are perceived to have the same characteristics as UK.

## 5. Conclusion

The initial groundwork has already proved invaluable in providing the researchers with a framework which they believe they can use effectively to evaluate the current situation with regard to usage of IT in organisations in Hong Kong. Initial results show that Mintzberg structures can provide a basis for examining Hong Kong organisations and combined with Nolan's Stage Model will allow us to develop a Hong Kong model. This will be examined in detail to identify whether there is any "fit" between the CBIS architectures and stage of growth and the organisational design.
