# Experimental Techniques for Information Requirements Analysis

Oscar Gutierrez

University of Massachusetts at Boston, College of Management, Department of Management Sciences, Boston, MA 02125, USA

Techniques are needed to improve the communication between user and analyst in the elicitation of information requirements. In particular, techniques are needed to enable them to work together in formulating and developing a model for the analysis of problems characterized by unclear statement of requirements. Hence, the need for an experimental approach to systems development. This paper describes some techniques and discusses the rationale for their application to the process of information requirements analysis in the context of an experimental strategy. Guidelines to support strategy formulation are provided.

Keywords: Experimental techniques, Prototyping, System simulation, Repertory grids, Game playing, Delphi method, Opinion questionnaires, Surveys, Pilot studies, Information needs, Information requirements analysis, Strategy formulation.

## 1. Introduction

If the information systems requirements differ from those that are needed to specify a good design, or if the analyst realizes that the problem statement is inherently inconsistent, the specialist faces serious problems. In this case, excellent use of systematic analysis and specification methods is not enough, since the problem is not to find a solution, but to understand and formulate the proper problem. In these circumstances, the specialists must follow alternative analysis methods, that allow them to take a less deterministic view of the application. An experimental approach seems a suitable alternative.

Such an approach is a highly iterative process that involves formulating and implementing a set of guidelines starting from the tentative formulation of the problem or its design goal. It also involves the elicitation of facts based on the proposed action strategy and evaluation and reformulation of the system design on the basis of these results. This continues until the alternatives are verified, and the one that satisfies a particular design objective is selected.

This paper looks more closely into a set of techniques, some of which have been specifically designed as experimental methods of systems development. It also looks at other techniques within the general field that can also be incorporated.

The review is by no means exhaustive. However, it provides the base for an identification of different contexts for which some techniques may be more appropriate. This paper gives an indication of these differences from an analysis of empirical examples and also from an analysis of some of the intrinsic characteristics of each technique.

## 1.1. The Experimental Approach

Prototyping has been widely discussed [12,9,5, 1,6,2,3]; it is amongst a number of supporting techniques that has, over the past few years, received growing attention.

As a result of the increasing variety of tools that are being developed specifically for the purpose of rapid design and experimentation, prototyping has become a leading example of the experimental approach. Sometimes it is termed the approach. Its virtue lies in the fact that it emphasizes “learning by doing” instead of by designing, utilizing immediate feedback to the systems construction process [19].

In this paper, prototyping is not synonymous with experimental strategy. Prototyping is perhaps the most representative method; the one with greatest potential and one that is likely to be supported by others in one or more stages of an analysis and design process. But there are cases where alternative approaches maybe more desirable.

## 1.2. Objectives

The somewhat “contingent” nature of information systems applications suggests that the selection of an experimental strategy be based on the particular needs and conditions of the problem being addressed. For example, techniques should be chosen based on the role, purpose, and skill level of those who are involved in the development and future use of the system. Taking this view, the question of the appropriateness of a technique or its fitness for a given information system problem has to be addressed in more detail.

This suggests a need for consideration of the characteristics of the techniques: what are their features?, and for what context might they be adequate? It is also necessary to take into account circumstances and characteristics of the particular problem. Thus, an assessment of the problem situation should be performed in advance in order to determine what are the objectives and targets of the experimental plan, and what are its criteria for success.

Our work suggests that experimental techniques should not be seen in isolation or as independent processes, for many of them can complement each other. The objective is to define a suitable strategy, consisting of one or a mixture of techniques that suit the conditions faced by particular problems.

## 2. Relevant Techniques

We now discuss some characteristics that make a technique adaptable within an experimental strategy. No attempt is made to give a detailed description of each, merely their salient features. The techniques are representative of the types available. Table 1 shows a summary of characteristics.

## 2.1. Delphi

Delphi is a formal process normally associated with the systematic solicitation and collation of expert opinion on forecasts of future events $[27]$ leading, for example, to the formulation of goals and policies $[7]$ . But there is a surprising variety of other application areas that include budget evaluations, urban and regional planning, and exposing priorities of personal values and social goals $[24]$ . The objective is to arrive at a group opinion on the subject under discussion, providing a basis for design or action.

In the context of user requirements analysis, the technique has been applied to problems where the analyst is faced by a lack of solid information. Perez and Schuler [28] describe its application on a system to support student-related academic administration activities. The objective was for a group of nine university administrators to comment on, validate, and evaluate a set of system objectives and performance indicators. This was achieved by having each individual analyze an initial set of information requirements, and provide independent views of the system's characteristics. The exercise took five iterations; each cycle was complemented with feedback information to all participants about the views of each member. The process helped in completing the set of requirements and clarifying a unified view of systems objectives from an initial, tentative, incomplete design.

A number of features make it possible to adopt the Delphi technique in an experimental fashion. Firstly, it is an iterative process of individual interrogations, usually made in writing or through other means, like online computer media. The process involves continuous evaluation and modification of views over the subject matter, until a group opinion is reached. Applied in the context of requirements analysis, it demands that the participants explicitly state their views, and it can serve to identify uncertainties and problems of these needs. Further iterations can lead to clarification and resolution of uncertainty or, alternatively, to the definition of suitable strategies.

Secondly, it is a rapid means to obtain valuable information from a group of knowledgeable people: those who can provide relevant input to the process, have the highest authority possible, and are committed and interested. In contrast with traditional interviews, which are time consuming, the participants in the Delphi process may remain anonymous and be accessed in a distributed fashion. In this way, it delivers a group view of the problem under study relatively quickly. It is thus instrumental in satisfying one objective of the experimental strategy: group response allows for shared responsibility over design decisions, decreasing the possibilities for questioning individual competence.

Thirdly, Delphi is a search and evaluation process suited to dealing with the most open-ended and creative aspects of systems development (another objective of the experimental approach). By decreasing interaction between participants, it diminishes some of the effects of face-to-face discussions (i.e., persuasion, domination, defense, etc.). On the other hand, it motivates independent thought and the gradual formulation of group solutions.

One problem with the Delphi method is that the outcome may result in compromise by some of the participants, instead of a genuine reflection of consensus by a group. The feedback process may pressure the participants to release original positions in order to terminate the process, and the original position may include a real need that is now unsatisfied or satisfied in a relatively unacceptable fashion.

## 2.2. Game Playing

Game playing, requires the creation of an artificial working environment where a group of users acting as themselves or playing simulated roles are able to experience simultaneous interaction. This is done to gain insights and to determine viable actions with respect to specific information needs of the problem.

Games may be manual or automated. When a game-system is computerized, simulation models are utilized to represent system states. Games may involve the specification of certain rules of communication and behavior. They demand heavy user interaction, normally in the form of teams.

In designing a useful game, the analyst-experimenter attempts to include the relevant roles and activities from the real world, transforming actions into game rules that are part of the model. It may include specific types of procedures, decision making environment, channels of interaction, etc. The environment sets initial conditions for play and is useful in giving an indication of possible alternatives for the exercise.

A desirable condition of game design for eliciting user requirements observes different views, as opposed to using a model of a perfect user; i.e., one who is well informed and knows how to use the available information. It is useful to include a setting based on different assumptions of the affected parties; these can then be explored and altered. Greenblat and Duke [13], proposed the design of “multiple-reality” games, whereby game components (i.e., scenario, decision rules, roles, etc.), are defined on the basis of initial preconceptions of the players about problem conditions. To be useful for requirements elicitation, the process of game design must be one of flexibility rather than one of definition.

Difficulties in creating convincing simulated operational environments, have confined game design mainly to problem-specific applications (e.g., inventory control systems, budgeting, and regional and urban planning). Most games have been used in teaching and to instruct users in the characteristics of a system as in the operational phase.

Games applied in non-academic and non-training usage are more difficult to find. METRO-APEX [13] is a computer-based game originally designed for urban management training, but its use has been extended to urban policy research and data collection and analysis. It attempts to model a coherent view of a city, replacing a narrow technical perspective and making it possible to observe problems in their entirety. The game structure has become more realistic and is intended to provide evidence to support public policy proposals.

Success in the use of game playing depends on departure from mere technical considerations and the need for definition of simple and realistic settings.

Firstly, a game-system allows users to carry out prototype operation of a model that can convey at least some key features of the final system. It is through actual play that users and specialists discover and test working alternatives: play serves as a means of refining and adapting the skeleton system.

Secondly, there are the advantages which are mainly a consequence of play: the possibility of early identification and consideration of real problems that may otherwise pass undetected. In addition to the exploratory nature of the search for facts and insights, this experimental strategy aims at identifying and resolving problem areas and uncertainties early in the process. Davis and Taylor [8], in applying an approach to systems design through gaming, found the technique useful in identifying system inadequacies and design gaps early in project development (the “conceptualization” stage). Their objective was to show the anticipatory potential of gaming, rather than its traditional role of instructional aid.

Thirdly, game playing normally allows evaluation through comparison. Games may be played under existing and proposed conditions. Then results are compared against fulfillment criteria to select the best system [22]. Davis and Taylor include a comparative analysis stage, characterized by game-playing followed by a critique session. This gives the player a basis for identifying differences between an abstract play and current conditions.

## 2.3. Operational Experimentation

Some techniques impose stringent methodological requirements upon the analyst. Others call for the use of specific supporting tools. In contrast, operational experimentation is characteristically very simple. Basically it involves the use of policies, decision processes, or manual procedures to perform field studies without automated tools. To illustrate the process, a simple case is described. It involves the design of a report form to improve the data collection process in a manufacturing workshop.

Management had identified some inconsistencies in the reports of shop-floor workers. A design team was asked to define a way of increasing the efficiency in the data transcription process. It was felt that the core of the problem lay in the complexity of the existing form. The design team needed to find the proper number of key fields in the form to reduce transcription errors to a minimum. To do so, they designed a program of operational tests, in which the workers used an experimental report form for a limited period. The format was modified several times, each changing the number of possible key identifiers. The workers used it under normal conditions for predefined time intervals and the transcription errors were recorded. After several trials, it was found that eight items were satisfactory; then a definitive form was designed.

This case helps to illustrate some of the characteristics of operational experimentation. Firstly, it introduces activities that are put in operation for a limited period of time. It was clear to the participants, the ultimate system users, that the task was undertaken to test a specific aspect of a new system. The main objective is to learn about and to understand what affects the aspects being investigated. This is done by gradually modifying design until it satisfies actual requirements.

Secondly, it deals mainly with manual procedures. The exercise must be implemented within the object system's environment, and the proposed solutions tested with real events and with the participation of the actual users. Thus, the behavior and user response to the system, contribute information and lead to insights into the problem.

Thirdly, the technique is normally limited to a partial investigation of a system, or of a system feature, where there is no clear indication that the solution supports a particular design alternative. An operational test may provide the evidence. However, demands on current resources may be too great, because trials are performed with real-life events. In the same way, the costs might be prohibitive.

## 2.4. Opinion Questionnaires and Surveys

Opinion questionnaires and surveys may be designed to assist the analyst in examining a group's view of particular issues (e.g., views concerning work conditions and preferences regarding degree of automation). This may be done through interviews or other means of data collection.

Opinion questionnaires normally imply a set of predetermined questions for which there are coded or scaled responses. Surveys, which may include the use of questionnaires, cover a wider set of alternatives in presenting questions or issues to a group.

These two techniques differ from traditional ad hoc methods of interviewing and observation in two ways. Firstly, they have a precise focus: they follow a method of systematic data collection from a chosen sample through the use of predefined working models. In contrast, ad hoc methods are less structured and more susceptible to the problems and biases of misconception and poor communication between analysts and end users. Secondly, the purpose is essentially different. By adopting ad hoc methods of analysis, one concentrates mainly on descriptions of current practice (finding out how a particular task is performed). With opinion questionnaires and surveys the analyst attempts to gather, not only factual information but an explanation of why a particular task is being performed.

Thus, these are systematic methods of gathering information about facts, actions, and, most importantly, about attitudes and opinions. If one chooses “description” as the purpose, the user group and the problem are reasonably well understood. One needs to describe them by showing some salient characteristics (e.g., what percentage of time is spent on problem solving activities, and who and how many people are involved in manual transcription). The analyst asks questions with concrete responses that are relatively easy to give. If the analyst seeks an “explanation” of how the system is seen by the users and how change can be introduced, the inquiry focuses on understanding the view and opinions of the participants (e.g., which current activities are most important to users, and which type of technology seems best to replace the manual transcription process).

Mumford [26] describes how a managerial opinion survey was used to investigate the possibility of change in responsibility between secretaries and their managers at ICI's Central Management Office. The origin of the study was the introduction of word processors at the Central Department. A questionnaire-based survey was part of a strategy to help secretaries in the design of their own work system. The results of administering the questionnaire showed the difference in types of information that were gathered. For example, it included questions to describe groups (e.g., the manager's job, the secretary's job, work responsibilities, and functions), and questions that attempted to determine individual opinions and attitudes towards current or future events (e.g., manager's views of present functions of secretaries and personal views of the effects of transfer of responsibility).

Opinion questionnaires and surveys are especially useful in exploratory analyses. While some techniques, like Delphi, seek group opinion through a process leading to consensus, surveys attempt to find global views, and help in determining if there are differences over specific issues.

Surveys normally use a conceptual working model of the future system. The questionnaires are used to discover how the participants formulate and respond to this conceptual model by indicating their views and preferences. This may be compared with other techniques that make use of concrete working models which the users actually exercise.

## 2.5. Pilot Studies

A pilot system is one which, after exhibiting most of the required features of a production system, is normally adopted as a pioneer project. It is designed to help in identifying some potential problems and opportunities surrounding the system's implementation under test conditions. Operation of the pilot system will hopefully yield information about necessary modifications and corrective action. But these are only a few of the possible events that may occur when the system is put into operation. The pilot is thus an access-constrained version of a final system, with limitations on the population it serves, and the load that it handles.

Tests are made using the pilot to provide a first hand experience of the system. They are not necessarily structured, nor are they always geared towards assessing specific criteria; the learning experience may be revealing enough to define the strategy for definitive action.

In a case study, a pilot project was designed to discover some of the problems of exporting existing computer-based systems to four other companies. The new user groups had little previous exposure to on-line computer technology. The pilot system was a single application. It was intended to test and refine an approach to systems conversion and implementation for a total of fifteen operational control systems. The pilot gave a first hand view of many problems surrounding a proposed conversion plan; e.g., it had ignored some aspects of incompatibility of existing systems; and the basis for the involvement of various interest groups had not been considered properly. These issues came to light only through actual systems conversion. The experience allowed the design group to modify the plan in less time and with fewer resources than had originally been anticipated.

As with other techniques, pilot systems display some unique characteristics. For one, they attempt to convey a total view of a system. Tests represent samples that examine all its features.

A potential strength of pilot studies is that they allow concentration on problems along the “periphery” of the system: a model of the system may exist and the application may be well understood, but it may be necessary further to understand or define contextual parts of the system. One can use a pilot for explaining work, behavioral, or environmental aspects.

As an example, a survey may capture a group's view of specific issues of importance to the success of the system; simultaneously, a prototype or a simulation can be applied to discover its technical characteristics. Then, a pilot may be developed to discover some of the problems of implementing the application.

A pilot system can be used to test certain key operational components, such as response time variations, which an untuned prototype cannot test.

## 2.6. Prototyping

Prototyping is concerned primarily with the creation of a simplified but operational version of a system, usually based on incomplete user requirements and definitions. It evolves and grows in size and complexity as requirements are evaluated through practical experience. It disregards technical performance for the benefit of gradually clarifying and understanding information needs.

Prototyping tends to cover a wider range of aspects of systems development. It is a reduced version of a system through which the user is exposed to issues of design, implementation, and operation.

All experimental techniques must fit within a broader development plan if they are to fulfill the objectives of the experimental approach. Emphasis should be placed on attempting to establish conditions which increase the possibilities of understanding the problem. This is opposed to the intention of designing (on a trial-and-error basis), a technical solution to a partially understood situation. The results of any experimental plan must provide specifications for the design of a more definitive system. Dearnley and Mayhew [9] put prototyping in context by showing how it can fit and form a part of a whole system life cycle.

Sound prototypes need suitable tools. Their characteristics can be used to describe their potential in the experimental approach. Firstly, they are operational working models, developed quickly and easily. This allows users and specialists to concentrate on real life situations in order to develop and test recommendations to create systems of best fit.

Secondly, the technique can exploit the potential associated with implementing learning conditions [20]. Prototypes can be used to help inexperienced users to understand the sort of information that might be useful to the analyst; the specialist can also benefit from observing the user.

Thirdly, prototypes are vehicles for user participation in the early project stages [16]. The simplistic and tentative nature of the technique allows users and specialists to communicate under less strain and with a speculative attitude.

Fourthly, prototyping is inherently iterative [4,17]. It begins with an initial implementation of a skeletal version of a part of the system and performs only a limited number of functions. The use of the prototype will hopefully lead to insights into the appropriate tactics to approach a solution that will, after a number of iterations, produce a satisfying system.

## 2.7. Repertory Grids

The repertory grid is a technique devised by the psychologist G.A. Kelly, based on his theory of personal constructs [21]. Every person possesses a framework of “constructs” through which mental models of the environment are developed. This implicit theoretical framework is used by the person to think about and act with respect to events in the world. According to the appraisal, experience from the environment are classified, categorized and responded to. The person evaluates, elaborates upon, or changes views about the world within this system [11].

A full repertory grid has three components; elements, constructs, and a means of establishing a link between the two. Thus, to apply repertory grids we elicit elements and constructs and establish their interrelationships. Basically, a grid shows a classification of events in the environment and how they are related to the individual's abstractions of experience associated with those events. The grids are represented in the form of matrices. Elements are situated at the top of the matrix and constructs (which are bipolar) at the sides.

Data collection follows a process of individual interrogation by the grid administrator or a set of interactions with computer programs. This is continued until a fairly complete system of views and relationships can be obtained.

To set up a repertory grid, it is necessary to specify the underlying purpose or criteria behind the inquiry. By narrowing the perspectives, we facilitate the elicitation process. Typical examples are: evaluating managerial jobs, assessing group performance, and evaluating customer needs. For a comprehensive review of current approaches, see [10].

Our aim in applying repertory grids is to depict individual views of a system; i.e., what it should look like. However, these views should conform to a particular domain. For example, systems can be depicted in terms of the decisions they support, their deliverables, the data that they handle, or the actors involved in their operation and use. Repertory grids require the purpose or underlying criteria of the inquiry to be stated explicitly.

A framework that seems suitable is one based on a method for analyzing systems in terms of unit operations [23]. A system is described as a sequence of unit operations: the execution of activities necessary to fulfill the goals of the organization. A unit operation is an integrated set of activities attached to a major system function. These activities are separated from others by some kind of boundary, such as location or inputs. A unit operation should be able to sustain five types of activities: operational, problem avoidance or problem solving, coordinating, control, and development activities.

An interactive program was developed, following a prototype strategy, in an attempt to combine the use of repertory grids for analyzing requirements in the context of unit operations [14]. The experimental plan followed a staged underlying process, resulting in four development phases:

1. Basic elicitation process: roles and activities.

2. Extending the elicitation process: inputs/outputs.

3. Precedence of activities.

4. Outputs: role maps and functional diagrams.

The analysis produces two types of result: a set of role maps and functional diagrams.

Each role in a unit operation carries out a number of activities, some of which are critical and specific to that role. Others are less important or can also be performed by others. Role maps represent a view of the way in which a user perceives the distribution of responsibility with regard to specific functions.

One of the main objectives is to provide a representation of the flow of fundamental activities (operational activities) and their relationships. This is done in the form of a functional diagram. This is a graphical representation of a unit operation involving all types of activities and their respective inputs and outputs.

The end result of the process represents user views of certain aspects of an existing or future system. This can be incorporated into the determination of requirements. Their major potential lies in the process of requirements elicitation and less within their validation and verification.

As the technique does not make use of concrete models, communication focuses on the use of abstract representations. The validation of these abstract models depends on how a group of individuals agree on the results.

## 2.8. System Simulation

System simulation attempts to give the user an idea of how an application would operate if it were implemented. This is achieved by providing the user with early feedback in the form of examples. The objective is to provide a working model to guide the elicitation and communication of user requirements.

A system simulation is not an operational prototype, because it does not normally work under operational conditions. As such it rather represents a system sketch [15] to assess characteristics of a system not yet in existence, while prototypes tend to develop a whole application model. System simulation looks at the system from the point of view of its components.

System simulation also differs from simulation models used in gaming. System simulation attempts to convey an idea of the structure and features offered by the intended system. It is through users' observations when operating with a simulation that a designer can assess some possible impacts and characteristics of the future system.

A notable advantage of the technique is in a demonstration, by which users can discuss operational features by working through examples. Hopper and Hsia [18] call this approach Scenario-based prototyping. With it, the examples are scenarios, each representing a version of the system or a particular portion or feature of it. A script is prepared to guide the individual. Thus a system simulation has an operational capability, but only for quite restricted input and output sets [25].

Many prototyping tools utilize the concept of scenarios. Through on-line interaction, a designer can create screen formats, menus, and user dialogues, and specify the flow of events between the components of the computer-based system. The user makes use of sample data to go through that part of the application which is being simulated. During the running of a scenario, users and designers can modify the features and flow of the system, so that subsequent simulations will embody the change.

The formulation and structure of scenarios represent suitable working models to run experimental trials. Users can operate a simulation to determine whether their requirements are being understood and incorporated. Errors can easily be corrected and system characteristics iteratively altered.

The main purpose of system simulations is to discover system features that are relevant to an application and to help assess its impact on potential users.

Finally, system simulations depend on user participation. The examples are built for the users and their comments and observations aid in specifying modifications or new requirements. When implemented, they should provide a system with best possible fit.

## 3. Selection of Experimental Techniques

A program of activities cannot be outlined without a previous assessment of the problem (see Figure 1). It should look at issues that fall beyond the system's technical specification. This may involve analysis of the object system's environment, its structure, the current work system, and group and individual job needs.

The link between the assessment and any plan of action must be regulated by an analysis of actual conditions. This is a view of the problem versus the actual possibilities available to the design team. This involves scheduling the plan in the context of other objectives, priorities, resources and constraints.

Guidelines for action that should be embodied in a program of experimental activities consider the following: the purpose of the exercise; the underlying methodological approach; the particular strategy to be adopted; and the technique or mixture of techniques.

## 3.1. Purpose

Based on the aims of the exercise, it is possible to identify different forms by which some techniques can handle specific aspects of the requirements analysis process.

## Operation

Operation implies experiential modes of interaction. Techniques used with this purpose should be intended to test environmental conditions and characteristics of an object or computerized system through actual events. Operational experimentation, pilot studies, and prototypes are operative techniques that rely on the existence of concrete working models.

## Demonstration

System models are developed. Through the use of simulated inputs, processes and outputs, one should be able to provide a form of evidence to users as to how the system would look. The objective is to facilitate analysis and understanding of system characteristics. System simulation, game playing and prototyping can be included here.

## Planning

Techniques used for planning help to elicit, clarify and consolidate system goals. They assist in developing a view of the future by simulating a set of event sequences or system characteristics, thereby indicating the paths to be followed to clarify initial goals. Planning does not necessarily call for a concrete model of the specification. This allows the adoption of techniques that make use of conceptual modes of analysis.

## Consensus Search

Many systems have disappointing results because they fail to consider the varying views and expectations of different interest groups. The objective is to bring these views into the open for discussion and evaluation. This calls for conditions that provide a simulated present or future environment in order to promote interactions amongst all interested parties. Game playing, Delphi and repertory grids can provide such an environment.

## Exploration

The purpose of exploration implies collecting information from the field in an attempt to make a thorough examination of the state of affairs. The objective is to determine if a pattern of behavior or an opinion can be derived from the analysis. Opinion questionnaires and surveys are helpful, especially when the information is widely distributed. Operational experimentation can be useful to explore actual conditions for a variety of options, albeit on a limited scale.

## Motivation

Getting users involved in the development of the working model, may increase their interests in the project. Most techniques assume a form of intervention from users. Some are based on testimony when the user is subjected to conceptual working models: the Delphi method or repertory grids. Others on direct user experience with the model: prototyping, pilot studies, game playing and operational experimentation.

## Prediction

When the effects of a system cannot be tested due to high costs, security, or unavailability of information, a limited system is created to predict the behavior of key variables. This demands the use of a model able to represent instances of a future which is described mainly through probabilistic data. Simulation and forecasting techniques incorporated in the context of a pilot study, a prototype or a game, can be useful.

## 3.2. Underlying Approach

The adoption of an underlying method of analysis is mainly concerned with the definition of the tactics to assemble the set of procedures required for the use of one or a combination of techniques in a coherent experimental plan. These tactics depend on: the nature of the application which may in itself suggest the underlying philosophy most suitable for development; the nature of the inquiry; and the role of computerized systems in the organization, particularly the way they are developed.

Computer-based systems depend on, and most often embody, the underlying philosophies behind the real systems that they attempt to model and control. The way they come to be reflects the principles that govern the manner by which the organization is prepared to incorporate them. The norms, rules and culture of an organization are, to a large extent, determinants of the underlying development methods.

Two underlying approaches can be distinguished:

## Evolutionary Approach

An underlying evolutionary approach assumes a system development effort to be a continuous process of change. A working model is defined initially to capture only essential features of the system under investigation. As better understanding of the problem is gained from the collected information, and perhaps from the use of the working model, it is then modified and made more complex by attending to additional aspects of the application. The developer relies on the experience gained from findings in order to dictate further action.

## Staged Approach

A staged approach considers the formulation and development of a project in the form of sequences of events. A predetermined set of hypothesis-like phases can be put forward. These refer for example, to stages of a proposed set of activities to develop a particular application, or to independent events such as sessions, presentations and game settings that have been scheduled systematically. The developers have a preliminary view of the likely paths that the experimental plan will follow to completion.

## 3.3.Strategy

A strategy addresses the problems concerned with the course that the overall project might follow. This refers to the possible roles that plans of action can take, to make an analysis that will fit with the nature of events in the environment. It is possible to identify three strategies for a project:

## Reactive Strategy

An experimental plan that follows a reactive strategy, is designed to respond to changes that have already taken place. The role of the project is directed at finding a design which will help in identifying characteristics of the new conditions and to provide a solution that will satisfy current affairs. Change in organizational objectives and structures, changes in technologies which themselves lead to changes in power games, introduce different types of uncertainty into the environment. All these are examples of conditions that may lead to a definition of a plan that follows a reactive role.

## Proactive Strategy

A plan that follows a proactive strategy is designed with the explicit intention of exerting change on the environment. Examples here include cases involving operational experimentation in which a designer learns whether or not new programs of activity are effective, and which are modified or discarded on the basis of the evidence made available through operational tests.

## Anticipatory Strategy

While a reactive strategy responds to a need for adaptation, and a proactive to one of active influence, an anticipatory strategy attempts to satisfy a need to foresee events and likely changes in the future. The intention is not of manipulating the environment to reach a given state of affairs. Rather it is one of attempting to understand current events and possible trends from the imperfect information that is available.

## 4. Some Issues that Emerge

The idea of bringing together a number of techniques in support of an overall strategy is not without its problems. Some techniques are based on different principles; most follow different tactics. However, the analyst should regard them as complementary.

Some of the most relevant points are:

## 4.1. The Need to Develop an Experimental Plan

Perhaps the most important issue is that we are no longer concerned with isolated techniques. For each problem, we must discuss the formulation of a strategy and its plan of action. One must attempt to combine the facilities offered by different tools and techniques to form a congruent program of activities.

## 4.2. The Need to Formulate Strategy

While setting up an experimental plan, it is necessary to make clear the reasons or issues that justify its formulation. This provides an opportunity to define the objectives, targets, and tactics of the exercise.

If an argument for the program of activities is not explicitly formulated, it is very easy to conclude that the designers have a cynical approach to their task.

4.3. The Need to Understand Characteristics of the Techniques

If an experimental plan combines two or more techniques, it is vital that the designer understands their characteristics.

Some techniques make use of a tangible model, such as a set of experimental manual exercises or a working prototype, whereas others rely on verbal communication and deal with abstract concepts in questionnaires or interviews. The approaches to involvement and participation are dealt with in different ways too. Different techniques have different scope and potential; this cannot be ignored.

## 5. Conclusions

Some techniques that provide an experimental strategy have been reviewed. An attempt has been made to show some ways in which they fit. There is probably no one technique that matches all the demands and requirements of a system's development cycle, nor even those of a single life cycle stage.

In defining an experimental plan, one must be aware of and concerned with two main aspects: in understanding the features and characteristics of particular techniques in order to be able to define a strategy appropriate to the conditions of a particular situation, and in the categorization and classification of the system's environment so that a suitable plan can be formulated.
