# A Decision Support System for Real Estate Investment Portfolio Management \*

Robert R. Trippi

Sigma Research Associates, La Jolla, California, and United States International University, San Diego, California, USA

Managers of portfolios of sizeable income-producing properties face a series of significant short and long-range planning problems. Many of these can best be addressed by a DSS specifically designed for their needs. This article examines industry factors, design goals, and functions of a system used to improve major real property asset acquisition, improvement, and divestment decisions. Success factors and implications for DSS developers are also discussed.

Keywords: Decision support systems – Real estate, Decision support systems – Investments, Decision-making – Real estate, Decision-making – Investments, Investments – Computers, Investments – Mathematical Models, Investments – Portfolio-computers, Investments – Real estate-computers, Real estate – Investment-computer, Real estate – Investment-mathematical models, Real estate – Investment-portfolio-computers, Computers – Investment analysis, Computers – Real estate industry, Information processing – Investment analysis, Information processing – Real estate industry.

## 1. Introduction

One of the major strategic planning problems facing firms that own and manage portfolios of income-producing properties is that of evaluating properties for possible acquisition, divestment, expansion, renovation, or conversion of use [10, 11,14]. This article describes a specialized decision support system (DSS) that successfully addresses these problems and some of the major design choices made in its development. $^{1}$

Like most others, this DSS can be incorporated into the firm's management information system, with its own database augmenting the organization's other databases. The DSS's database is tightly integrated with mathematical models employing optimization, sensitivity analysis, and other analytical techniques. DSS's are often distinguished by the degree of structure possessed by the activities that they assist with [6]. This DSS can best be classified as one for management activities involving semistructured strategic problems [8]. In the context of Simon's triad of the problem-solving process [12], the Intelligence or search phase for the activities aided by the DSS may be either structured or unstructured, the Design or analysis phase is usually unstructured, and the Choice or action selection phase is usually structured. The DSS is equally applicable for portfolio decisions that are made on a recurring (e.g. see [1]) or ad-hoc basis.

## 2. Background

In order to fully appreciate the function and design of this DSS, it is helpful to understand the unique characteristics of the assets being managed, how they differ from other types of investments, and who manages such assets. Income-producing properties include office buildings, industrial parks, shopping centers, apartment complexes, hotel/motel developments, and projects servicing recreational markets. Management of such assets differs from the more common investment portfolios of exchange-traded assets (stocks, bonds, etc.) in the following ways:

1. Virtually every decision addressed by the DSS is a “megabuck” one. Acquisition and divestment cannot be done incrementally as with most other types of investments, and other decisions, such as improvement and financing, likewise involve discrete alternatives typically involving the allocation of at least several million dollars.

2. The asset is relatively illiquid. Mistakes usually cannot be corrected quickly or without considerable cost to the organization.

3. Transaction costs are high. In a complete turnover of a portfolio of such assets, monetary transaction costs can exceed ten percent of asset value. Thus the organization must be prepared to live with its decisions for some time. Non-monetary transaction costs are also high. An extraordinary amount of senior management time must be allocated for activities related to acquisition and divestment.

4. Management may be either active or passive. The most productive portfolios are usually those which treat the investment as active. Unlike securities investments, the asset may be improved in numerous ways (referred to in the industry as having “upside potential”). Therefore the performance of the portfolio is dependent upon the development and execution of an optimal long-range action plan for each property.

5. Investment performance is markedly sensitive to external environmental factors. In addition to general business conditions, the success of the investment is sensitive to interest rates, inflation rates (e.g. reflected in cost-of-living rent escalation clauses in leases), taxation policies, and local factors such as changes in neighborhood quality and vacancy rates.

Organizations engaged in managing portfolios of such assets include real estate investment trusts, private and public syndication firms, REO (real estate owned) departments of lending institutions, bank and savings and loan trust departments, property developers who retain an ownership interest in their completed properties, and insurance companies with portfolios of properties which they either own or manage in trust for pension funds and other institutions.

## 3. Decisions Addressed

The DSS discussed herein is designed to assist top management with strategic decisions only, and is not intended to replace the operational “property management” function which involves more frequent, recurring decisions. These are generally handled at a lower level of management or contracted to outside firms offering suitable services.

As an illustration, a typical user of the DSS, a developer and manager of regional shopping malls, has an established policy of divesting itself of one of its malls for each two new ones that it builds or acquires. This provides the company with a continuing source of equity capital to finance new construction while eliminating from its portfolio older properties that have passed the peak of their life-cycle equity yield curves. Selection and timing of divestments, rehab candidates, and refinancing alternatives for its portfolio of more than two dozen properties requires almost continuous attention from top management. With the aid of the DSS the firm includes in its annual reports to shareholders and partners a “current value” set of financial statements in addition to the traditional ones. This organization relies heavily on the DSS for the development and revision of a fifteen-year improvement, sale, and reinvestment plan with respect to its real property portfolio. Since these activities can involve millions, tens of millions, or even hundreds of millions of dollars, implementation of a DSS to assist with decision-making has had an immediate and significant payoff to the organization.

It could well be argued that some such problems have a tactical as well as strategic dimension. However, in the present framework tactical decisions may be considered to be those secondary decisions associated with the execution of the fundamental decisions addressed by the DSS. For example, the decision to acquire or divest of a particular property would be a pure strategic decision, but the decision to also participate with an equity partner to preserve capital or to carry back seller financing to enhance marketability would be a strategic-tactical decision. Since these distinctions are not always clear-cut in the real world, we will consider any decision that results in a serious discontinuity in the investment portfolio's total return to be strategic.

## 4. Design Considerations

Computer-aided investment analysis is by no means new to the real estate industry. Many software packages are available that perform investment analysis functions of one sort or another [7,17]. The system discussed here, by virtue of its comprehensiveness and potential for integration with an organization's financial management information system, possesses the elements expected in a DSS for organizations that manage medium to large income property portfolios.

Some of the most difficult design decisions in implementation of this particular application were centered around determination of the appropriate level of structure for the DSS itself. A relatively unstructured DSS might be a skeletal model using a DSS generator such as IFPS, to be augmented and modified by the end-user on an ad-hoc basis. At the other end of the spectrum, most of the real estate investment analysis software packages and spreadsheet templates for personal computers provide a highly structured (and sometimes simplistic) repertoire of capabilities. Realistic models for this type of asset must comprehend many complicated relationships that are not easily described mathematically. Rapid computation of complex formulas in performing sensitivity analyses and optimization is required if real-time interaction is to be maintained. As a consequence, ordinary spreadsheet-type software has generally proven to be an inadequate or clumsy substitute for a specialized DSS for such organizations [3]. The semi-structured approach adopted for this DSS is consistent with the types of problems, predominantly in the strategic domain, to be addressed [13].

The initial implementation of the DSS employed IFPS for construction of a simple model which would answer the types of questions most frequently asked by CFO's (chief financial officers such as financial vice presidents and controllers) of client organizations assigned with portfolio management responsibilities. However, this was soon replaced with a predominantly FORTRAN 77 implementation with enhanced capabilities. Although this is contrary to the trend towards employing DSS generators, there are valid reasons for this approach.

First, the end-users were generally reluctant to modify the model themselves and relied on others for assistance whenever major changes were required, negating a primary advantage of the DSS generator. Second, although many different types of questions could be addressed by the DSS, the fundamental economic relationships in this application are invariant. Most managers of income property portfolios are concerned with the same measures of performance and have a similar viewpoint vis-a-vis the impact of environmental variables. Therefore the model base core need not be organization-specific. This is in contradistinction to the experience of the author and other DSS developers who have worked with managers of portfolios of exchange traded investments. The latter managers often have significantly differing theories of value and inclusion/exclusion criteria which must be satisfied. Thus it was recognized that through careful design a DSS nucleus could be coded in a high level procedural language while retaining the degree of flexibility required through user choice options. Third, since the DSS had the potential for use by more than one organization, maximum portability and minimum expense was desired. It was considered highly advantageous for users not to be required to to acquire an additional costly software package to support the DSS. Virtually every firm managing such investments has access to a FORTRAN compiler on a mainframe or supermini. Fourth, unlike the majority of DSS's [18], specialized optimization routines constitute a major component of this system, and it was apparent early on that continued use of a DSS generator would exact an unacceptable computation time penalty and require other compromises in this critical area.

Although the current development machine is a PRIME 850, customized versions of the DSS in source code form have been delivered for use on other PRIME's and computers as diverse as IBM 30XX and DEC VAX's. The total compiled code has a size of 2.5–4 megabytes (depending on the version and computer). However, the implementation is highly modular (DSS nucleus comprising 21 source code modules) with a largest compiled run module of about .6 megabyte. Since FORTRAN 77 is ANSI standardized, the most significant problem in its use was avoiding the temptation to use attractive manufacturer-specific features. A subset version of the DSS is also available for the IBM personal computer. The most recent multi-user implementations of the DSS provide graphics support through use of IBM-compatible personal computers as terminal emulators and downloading of selected report rows and columns into 1-2-3 format files which can then be graphically displayed and printed using personal computer spreadsheet software. This has effectively extricated the DSS developer from providing for graphics capabilities while maintaining the desired source-level code compatibility across a wide range of computers.

A major step in creation of an effective DSS for the intended application was design of the database that the DSS would draw upon. The DSS database requires an interface to the firm's financial information system. The most modest interface is a manual system of entry and editing of selected data about income, expenses, lease provisions, maintenance contracts, and the like from standard accounting reports and contractual documents for each property into the DSS database. For acquisition analysis, this is the natural mode of operation. For existing investments the interface is substantially automated using a standard "front-end" to the DSS's database with periodic or ad-hoc updates being done without manual intervention.

The DSS accepts in an interactive prompt-response mode the above manually entered data plus managerial inputs such as judgmental estimates of future economic parameters and nonrecurring expenses, definition of the mathematical and logical relationships among the various income and expense items, and instructions as to the specific types of analyses that the system is to perform. This dialog style was chosen in lieu of command language or full screen menu as it makes few learning demands on the end user, yet allows concise presentation of either next-step choice alternatives or data entry prompts, while previously entered data and directives remain visible. Any variable may be specified to be the continuation of a trend line computed by regression analysis, if the appropriate historical data is available, or be related to any other exogenous or endogenous variable stored in the DSS's database. Managers may also provide a set of feasible actions for inclusion in an action table for the property. The action table may contain such prospective future activities as refinancing, expansion, sale of partial interests, or renovation, each of which may have several possible options deemed feasible by management.

## 5. Sensitivity Analysis and Optimization Functions

A comprehensive sensitivity analysis function is designed into the DSS to answer the inevitable "what-if" questions asked by management. In addition, any inputs of an estimated or subjective nature may be specified as random variables described by discrete independent probability distributions. In the risk analysis mode the DSS cycles through each of the feasible values of the random variables, then computes expected-value point estimates and measures of risk (variance and semi-variance) of investment performance criteria by an enumeration of the potential outcomes. With the number of random variables typically kept small in practice, this methodology results in efficient computation of such statistics more precisely than would be possible using monte-carlo or other simulation techniques. The ability to handle uncertainty in a facile manner is a major strength of this DSS, as managerial judgmental assumptions about such exogenous variables as inflation and interest rates are almost always stated with some degree of uncertainty. This is frequently the case also with such endogenous variables as specific operating cost line items, future improvement costs, and vacant space absorption rates.

The optimization routine built into the system employs an efficient specialized search algorithm to determine the most advantageous selection and timing of actions included in the action table. Unlike some other types of investments, real estate investment performance is not uncontrollable, but depends heavily on future actions having an impact on the cash flow stream. The long-range action plan directly affects pro-forma income/expense projection report line items and thus also the IRR (internal rate of return), FMRR (financial management rate of return), and discounted present worth of the investment in the current period. The projection of rental income for each action plan requires entry of detailed lease data as the DSS must comprehend the effects on revenue of often complex annual and end-of-term COL escalations, possible percentage rent lease clauses, and expense passthroughs, not only for the current lease term but also for renewal options which may differ from the initial term's provisions. Typical behavior of one-year cash equity yield, IRR, and FMRR versus age for a particular action plan for a shopping center investment is shown in Figure 1.

The potential actions comprise a set of discrete variables for which there exist many straightforward tests for dominance. In addition, the IRR is unimodal with respect to timings of the optimal actions under all but the most perverse of conditions [15]. From a different perspective, with a rental space demand curve shifting upward over time and cost behavior characteristic of income property, maximization of net present value (NPV) of the cash stream can be framed as a simplified case of a continuous-time investment scheduling problem discussed elsewhere [4] for which the NPV is separable and pseudoconcave with respect to each of the action timings. This result is directly applicable to the discrete-time real estate action planning problem, and this is the only commercial computer-implemented application of this optimization algorithm known to the author.

Each possible set of prior actions X implies a sequence of subsequent system states $S(X)$ for the property following its initial state. With a planning horizon of T periods, for each of the possible states the optimal timings $t_{k}^{*}(S(\cdot))$ , $k=1\ldots n$ , for an n state-change policy are found through use of a univariate search procedure. This is repeated for values of n from 1 to N, the maximum number of periods in which some action can take place. Whenever the relaxed timing problem results in a violation of a precedence constraint such that $t_{k+1}^{*}(S(X))<t_{k}^{*}(S(Y))$ , the solution is disregarded since the best constrained solution for that set of system states will have $t_{k+1}^{*}(S(X))=t_{k}^{*}(S(Y))$ and this has already been considered in an n-1 state-change problem which includes the state $S(XUY)$ . Feasibility and dominance tests applied to the action sets X reduce the number of system states to be considered to a relatively small number M. The maximum possible value of N is M-1, and in practice it is generally unnecessary to consider values of N greater than 10. The complete search therefore represents a very small fraction of the $MT^{N}$ possibilities which would otherwise require enumeration. The discount rate is varied parametrically to arrive at values of IRR and FMRR. This exploitation of the structure of the optimization problem permits solutions to be obtained with modest computational effort. The optimal action/timing solutions for each of the properties in the investment portfolio is an important product of the DSS for long-range organizational planning.

The DSS can provide the mean-variance investment return data with which some optimal risk/return portfolio inclusion decisions using Markowitz-type models can be made (e.g. see [2] and [5]). In practice, however, at any point in time there are usually too few acquisition candidates to make such an exercise worthwhile. In the case of forced divestment from an existing full portfolio, such decisions are almost always influenced more by other organizational considerations such as degree of management attention demanded by the property or partnership liquidation demands.

## 6. The DSS Nucleus and Extensions

The relationships among the principal components of a minimal configuration of the DSS are illustrated in Figure 2. Key reports are the detailed (up to 250 line item) projected income-expense-cash flow pro-forma, the projected income by tenant space report (for up to 600 leases), the projected ROI report, the projected valuation report, the divisional consolidation report, and the optimal action plan. The projected balance sheet report is optional and is not considered of great value for real estate investments in its traditional form due to the increasing undervaluation of fixed assets (at cost less accumulated depreciation) as time progresses [9]. However, a current or market-value accounting balance sheet projection can be constructed by replacing certain items on the balance sheet report with output from the valuation report. Adjustments to produce supplementary corporate balance sheets using market valuation of income property has become popular among organizations with a significant proportion of their assets in income-producing real estate due to the generally consistent appreciation of such assets in recent years.

Using the above information, potential acquisitions may be pitted against one another and against minimal equity yield hurdles or other criteria, and it can be quickly ascertained in-house whether or not acquisition candidates are fairly priced. In addition, optimal short and long-range plans for future divestment, expansion, refinancing, and renovation of currently owned properties can be developed. Using a suitable model for pricing $[16]$ the date of realization of revenues from future sales can also be estimated.

The DSS nucleus can be augmented with additional modules which perform functions specific to a particular organization. Such customization is fairly economical due to the many elements incorporated in the nucleus modules that are common to differing types of investing organizations. Following are examples of problems addressed by organization-specific modules.

1. Acceptance or rejection of offers to purchase ground leases. The organization was the lessee of numerous sites that it had improved with special-purpose buildings. Many of those no longer needed had long-term ground leases at below-market rents and thus had the potential for sale at a profit. However, the holding cost (rent) of such non-productive assets was considerable and favored rapid liquidation.

2. Determination of optimal sale-leaseback parameters. The organization wished to exploit its property management expertise. By master-leasing divested properties and subleasing to individual tenants it is able to realize greater sale prices. This operation essentially converts the asset to one of lower risk and lower return from the standpoint of the purchaser. Its profitability depends on trade-offs between property value and the rental obligations of the master lease.

3. Incorporation of a model of space absorption for an institutional investor in new office buildings. The organization was comfortable with a previously developed model of absorption that had proven accurate in the past and simply wanted it incorporated into the DSS.

4. Determination of ownership share to offer a multiple-project joint venture lending partner. Since the lender would furnish a below-market-rate loan as well as equity capital, the acquired property would show a greater return to this investor than to most others. Part of the value added should be reflected in an increased ownership percentage for the lender.

## 7. Implications for DSS Developers

This DSS, although industry-specific, offers several potentially valuable lessons for DSS developers. The particular management problems to be addressed, and not the convenient availability of tools, should dictate the critical choices for a successful DSS design. In the context of the real estate investment portfolio management activity, there were valid reasons for selecting a high-level procedural language over a DSS generator for continuing development of the DSS. These included avoiding dependence on the presence of proprietary software on every target computer and the high percentage of code devoted to problem-specific algorithms. The contribution of the microcomputer employed as a terminal in recent versions of the DSS, notably in the graphics area, capitalizes on its particular strengths.

Even with a standardized DSS nucleus, it was possible to economically meet the specialized needs of different user organizations through augmentation of the system with custom modules. This approach was effective due to the relatively consistent set of problems faced by real estate portfolio managers regardless of the nature of the investing organization, enabling the development of a common model base incorporated in the DSS nucleus. Choice of the appropriate level of modularity and consistent program interfaces (one of the strong suits of FORTRAN 77) were also instrumental in the success of this design.

Finally, DSS developers should consider seeking out other types of organizations addressing problems similar to those for which a DSS is being constructed. In addition to gaining additional insights into the immediate client's problem set, minor modifications to the completed DSS may enable it to be profitably employed by other firms. In the case of the real estate portfolio management DSS, expansion of its repertoire of capabilities in order to meet the needs of each new user benefited, at relatively low cost, earlier as well as subsequent users of the system.

## 8. Summary and Conclusions

This article has presented a global view of a successful DSS which applies methodologies of risk analysis, optimization, and modern investment theory to the management of portfolios of income-producing real properties. As a decision support and not a decision making system, the DSS's purpose was limited to furnishing critical information related to strategic decisions involving asset acquisition, improvement, and divestment. The DSS's contribution is important for several reasons, including the extraordinarily large monetary implications of virtually every potential action and the complex embedded long-range planning problems that must be solved in the decision process.

Managers enhance their own judgment and understanding of organizational goals with various outputs of the DSS to improve their decision-making performance. Typical users of the system described herein are responsible for portfolios that range in gross aggregate value from about twenty million to over one billion dollars. The DSS has been successful in the sense that most users attribute a considerable portion of their own success in making profitable decisions to the information and insights that it has provided. Factors contributing to this success include the depth of understanding of the problem brought to the portfolio managers by the DSS developer through communication with other organizations facing similar problems and the ability to bring the DSS operational in short order on a variety of host computers.

Since the DSS is used by several different types of organizations with differing asset mixes, there are naturally individual variations to be found from one implementation to the next. However, the elements of commonality have been found to be sufficient to provide for significant economies in adapting the system to the special needs of individual users. DSS developers facing comparable situations, including similarity of analytic perspective across organizations and highly specialized algorithmic DSS content, may likewise find it worthwhile to reexamine some of their initial design choices and to actively seek out other organizations that might benefit from or improve the quality of the DSS developer's work.
