# Key Issues in Telecommunications Planning

William R. King \* and G. Premkumar \*\*

\* Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA \*\* Iowa State University, Ames, IA 50011, USA

Telecommunications has emerged as a critical function playing a vital role in the business strategy of the organization. The deregulation of the industry, evolution of new technologies, rapid growth in the telecommunications market and use of telecommunications for competitive advantage have increased the uncertainty in the telecommunications environment. Strategic telecommunication planning has become a necessity in responding effectively to this dynamic and complex environment. A conceptual model of strategic telecommunications planning is proposed and, based on this model the various planning parameters are identified. An empirical survey of practicing telecommunications managers is carried out to determine the key issues in telecommunications planning, and the differences in planning characteristics across different groups. The results of the study indicate that planning managers mainly concentrate on factors related to “technology” and “cost” and place very little emphasis on “business” oriented factors. The results also indicate that significant planning gap exist for most of the planning items.

Keywords: Telecommunications planning, Strategic telecommunications planning for IS, Planning model, Planning issues, Telecommunications, IS and telecommunications planning.

## Introduction

The merger of voice and data communication and the use of digital transmission are having profound effect on the technology, products, and services offered by the telecommunications industry. The role of the telecommunication function is undergoing a dramatic change in organizations that recognize the need to integrate information and telecommunications systems and realize the potential of telecommunications for gaining competitive advantage. This has resulted in the function growing from a small staff function providing voice communication service to a critical function supporting the corporate strategy of the company [6].

Although McKenney & McFarlan [24] categorize telecommunications as an “island of information”, it is becoming increasingly difficult to differentiate between information systems and telecommunications. A redefinition of the scope of information systems encompassing voice, data, and video communication and having the primary responsibility of providing information and information channels to all members of the organization will provide a more cogent perspective for restructuring the function in the organization. Such an integrated functional structure would provide an organization with enormous potential for tapping its information resources effectively.

The Need for Strategic Telecommunications Planning

Traditionally, in most organizations, the telecommunications department has been primarily dealing with services related to voice communication. Planning was mainly concerned with annual financial and operational planning for the maintenance of the telephone system. This was simple, as there was usually a single supplier, who took care of many of the planning requirements. However in recent years, the telecommunications environment has changed considerably due to:

(a) Rapid advances in technology (digital transmission, integration of data and voice communication, ISDN, and use of optic fiber) [32].

(b) Deregulation of the industry and flooding of the market with a host of new products from different suppliers [7].

(c) Uncertainty with respect to adoption of ISO-OSI standards in the industry.

(d) Growth in office automation, interorganizational systems, distributed data processing, and systems spanning across national boundaries [3,4].

(e) Use of telecommunications technology to gain competitive advantage [6].

These changes in the industry have made the environment complex and dynamic requiring a strategic telecommunications planning process if the organization is to effectively employ these resources [8].

## The Telecommunications Planning Model

There is very little literature in the area of telecommunications management, and hence the relevant literature from information systems (IS) planning was used to develop a conceptual model of telecommunications planning. We felt justified in doing so, as the two fields have similar characteristics and the planning issues identified in the telecommunication literature are very similar to those in IS planning. The model is illustrated in Figure 1.

The model has three levels - inputs, process, and outputs. The primary inputs to the planning process are the organizational objectives and goals; these provide general guidelines and directions for functional level planning. It is a vital input to the planning process that has been stressed by many IS researchers [26,14].

Planning is a process of interacting with various agencies internal and external to the organization to reduce the uncertainty in the environment. Boynton & Zmud [1] identified nine different planning agenda based on a review of various IS planning models. An analysis of the agenda reveal that they can be broadly classified into organizational, market, and technology categories which map onto three major planning factors of the model. While business factors have gained importance in recent years with the emphasis on using telecommunications for competitive advantage [17], the rapid changes in technology have made the assessment of technology and its impact on telecommunication and business functions an important part of the planning process. Since it has to function in an organizational context, the characteristics of the organization influence the operation and hence are a key element in the planning process [20]. The fourth planning factor, external environment, is particularly relevant to telecommunications field, as the industry has traditionally been regulated, with external agencies exerting considerable influence on the industry.

Very few existing telecommunications planning methodologies take into account all these factors. Most of them are based on forecasting of demand using trend analysis and developing a financial and operational plan based on the forecast. The wide variety of choice in the market provide a rich problem space for optimization and the cost savings from such exercises can be substantial. Also, there is a strong need to integrate telecommunication plans with the plans of other functions in the organization. Hence, it is necessary to develop a comprehensive methodology incorporating the requirements from different constituents and taking into account various options available in the market place.

## Objectives and Outline of the Study

The objectives of the study are to identify the “gaps” – input, process, and output – that may exist between perceived importance of various elements of the telecommunications planning models and the actual usage of these elements.

This is accomplished by operationalizing the inputs, process factors, and plan features and using a mail questionnaire to assess the perceived importance of each element and the level of actual use of each element in the respondent's organization. The gaps are then assessed through comparisons of the perceived importance and actual usage of the various inputs, process elements, and outputs and hypotheses are tested regarding their significance.

## Operationalizing the Process Elements

The operationalization of the input and output factors in the model of Figure 1 is rather straight forward. However, the process elements merit some consideration. These process elements respectively relate the business, technology, organizational, and environmental contexts.

## Business Factors

Although the research literature primarily highlights the role of IS in gaining competitive advantage [12,23,28], a careful analysis of most of the best known applications (e.g., American Hospital Supply, American Airlines, or Citibank)

reveal that telecommunications technology was vital for the success of these applications. The two technologies should be considered in an integrated manner to obtain a synergistic effect.

Various strategies may be used to gain competitive advantage – product differentiation, internal cost reduction, improved accessibility to markets, spawning new businesses, and changing competitive scope [30]. Telecommunications may be used to reduce cost of internal operations by substituting slow and costly written communication with fast and cheap electronic communication. Telecommunications may also be used to create a niche or differentiate a product from its competitors, by providing specialized on-line data services (e.g., Dun & Bradstreet), by creating better access to customers through installing terminals in customer premises etc.

Telecommunications can alter the industry structure and extend market boundaries, as the technology has the capacity to change the transaction costs in the market [21]. This results in firms entering totally unrelated businesses based on the availability of a network infrastructure. Firms have entered banking from retailing by providing their own ATM's in the retail stores (e.g., Publix), or travel business from financial services (e.g., American Express).

The presence of an information channel or a network infrastructure seems to be the key determinant of business success in many information-intensive industries. The vast potential available to strategically utilize telecommunications makes analysis of “business factors” a critical element of the planning process.

## Technology Factors

The evolution of technology in the last two decades has had considerable impact on the planning process. The major technology issues facing the telecommunication manager are obsolescence, integration, and connectivity [9]. Organizations can respond effectively to obsolescence through long-term forecasting of technology trends and developing a long-term strategy for acquisition that ensures phased growth. Another major consideration is the integration of data, voice, and video communication, which may cause considerable structural changes in the management of telecommunications. The growth of inter-organiza tional systems, local area networks (LAN), and distributed data bases, make it necessary for integrating telecommunications planning with IS planning. The problems of incompatibility have been aggravated by changes in technology, lack of standardization, and increase in the number of suppliers and variety of products. The new technologies in telecommunications have created the potential for totally different solutions to classical communication problems. For instance, optic fiber increases by orders of magnitude the transmission capacity, making it possible to send large data bases or video images across large distances. Similarly, there is great potential in ISDN, and organizations which are first-movers into this area may probably have substantial strategic advantage [27]. Thus, technology factors play a critical role in the process of determining the telecommunications strategy.

## Organizational Factors

Since telecommunications planning is done in an organizational context the characteristics of the organization influence the planning process. For instance, data and voice communications have necessarily to be integrated to obtain economies of scale [11]. In some organizations, data communications consume at least 50% of the telecommunication expense. Many organizations have considered integrating the two functions for strategic advantage and internal efficiency.

The telecommunications manager needs to be conversant with a broad range of technical skills to handle both voice and data communication, have considerable business acumen to deal with multiple suppliers and evaluate different alternatives, have good communication skills to increase the visibility of the function in the organization, and have the planning skills to plan and operate in a complex and dynamic environment.

The involvement of top management in planning is essential to provide direction and guidelines for planning $[31]$ . It is also necessary to have greater user involvement, as applications such as teleconferencing, voice mail, inter-organizational systems etc., require substantial user input in the design process.

Resource constraints may force plans to be abandoned [10]. Realistic plans based on resource constraints are better than grandiose plans that are not implementable.

Finally, strategies for highlighting the role of telecommunications and increasing its visibility within the organization have to be developed to ensure easier resource mobilization.

## Environmental Factors

The external environment exerts considerable influence on the function as the industry is regulated in most countries. Although the telecommunication industry in the USA is deregulated, regulatory agencies still exert considerable influence. In other countries the strategies have to evolve based on policies in that country. The international exchange of data and information through a global communications network constitutes an important element of world trade and future economic growth. However, countries exert considerable control over transborder flow of information [4]. This importantly affects the operations of multi-national corporations, who must develop strategies and initiate multi-lateral negotiations with foreign governments to get over this serious constraint and ensure a free flow of information within their organization.

Various standards are evolving for products and systems. The adoption of these standards is based on a strong interplay of different market forces that need to be constantly monitored. It is essential that all systems be evaluated against these developing standards to avoid potential incompatibility.

## Research Design

In order to identify the major issues in telecommunications planning, a field survey of telecommunication managers in large US corporations was undertaken. Initially a set of 24 items, measuring the four major planning process factors were identified based on a literature review. After a pilot study, a set of eighteen items were selected and used in the questionnaire.

During the pilot study, it was found that most managers have an idealized set of planning objectives they wish to achieve, based on their perception of good planning practice. However, various organizational constraints force them to settle for a planning process that is different from the ideal.

It was decided to design the study to capture both the desired and actual state of planning. The respondents were asked to indicate the ideal level of importance for each of the eighteen planning items. They were also asked to indicate the actual level of importance attached to each of the planning item in their organization's current planning process. Both the measurements were made on a five-point Likert-type scale. The difference between the two states, was operationalized as a measure of the “planning gap” for that item. The gap could occur due to various factors such as organizational policy, lack of communication, clarity of the role of telecommunications in the organization, lack of organizational planning, inappropriate organization structure, and resource constraints.

The respondent also evaluated the quality of the output document – the telecommunications plan. The major features of the output plan were identified from the literature and the respondents were asked to indicate the degree of importance they attached to each of its features. They were also asked the actual level of detail of each feature existing in their organization's plan.

The survey was administered to 225 practicing telecommunication managers and 63 valid responses were obtained for a response rate of about 35%.

## Analysis of Input Gap

The “input gap” is defined as the difference between the perceived importance and actual usage of information inputs (organizational goals and objectives) in the planning process. This link has traditionally been weak, in spite of the emphasis given to it by researchers $[13,33]$ . In IS planning, it has been found to be one of the major problems $[18]$ . The new role of telecommunications makes it essential to minimize the input gap. A null hypothesis was used to test its existence.

Hypothesis 1. There is no difference between the perceived degree of importance and actual use of information inputs (organizational goals and objectives) in the planning process.

Paired t-tests were carried out to test the hypothesis. The hypothesis was rejected at a significance level of less than 0.005. This indicates that there may be problems in linking the organizational goals with the planning process. Various mechanisms, such as, participation in strategic planning [19], use of steering committee [25], and top management involvement [31] that have been used in IS area to improve this linkage may be used in telecommunications also.

## Analysis of Planning Gap

The “Planning gap” is defined as the difference between the perceived importance and actual usage of the planning factors in the planning process. A null hypothesis was formulated to test its existence.

Hypothesis 2. There is no difference between the perceived degree of importance and the actual usage of the planning items in the planning process.

Paired t-tests were conducted on all the planning items to test this hypothesis. For all the items, the null hypothesis was rejected at a significance level of less than 0.005. This signifies that a planning gap actually exists and needs to be bridged through better methodology.

The two issues that have the most significant planning gap are top management involvement in planning and exploring the use of telecommunications for strategic business advantage. Considering that strategic telecommunication planning is a relatively new phenomenon, in most organizations it can be expected that the involvement will be initially low, but the potential benefits that accrue from detailed planning will initiate greater involvement of top management in the planning process.

The second item, the use of telecommunication systems for strategic advantage, is a relatively new concept that is yet to be explored fully in organizations. The planning gap seems to indicate that telecommunication managers understand the potential of using telecommunications for strategic advantage but that organizational inhibitors prevent them from attaining the ideal state $[16]$ . Most organizations are only now realizing the potential of telecommunications and it may take some time before it percolates down to the planning process. Unfortunately, lack of planning methodologies to identify such potential applications is a serious handicap for planners.

## Analysis of Output Gap

The “output gap” is defined as the difference between the perceived importance of a particular feature and the extent of detail of that feature present in the plan. A null hypothesis was formulated to test its existence.

Hypothesis 3. There is no difference between the perceived degree of importance and actual extent of detail of that feature in the output plan document.

Paired t-tests were conducted to test this hypothesis. It was found that for all the items the null hypothesis had to be rejected at a significance level of less than 0.001.

## Analysis of Planning Across Organizations

Comparative analysis of organizations who have conducted long-term telecommunications planning with those who have not conducted it reveal several interesting facts. We divided the sample into two groups: long-term and short-term telecommunication planners. 55% of the sample had carried out long-term telecommunications planning exercises. Grouped t-tests were carried out to test the following hypothesis;

Hypothesis 4. The input gap between the two groups are equal.

Hypothesis 5. The planning gap between the two groups are equal.

Hypothesis 6. The output gap between the two groups are equal.

Hypothesis 4 was rejected at a significance level of 0.001, indicating that the planners had a better understanding of organizational goals and objectives and integrated them in the planning process.

## Analysis of Planning Factors & Output Factors

We also carried out an aggregate analysis of the planning and output factors. Based on the initial categorization, the eighteen planning items were categorized into four major factors – business (4 items), technology (4 items), organization (7 items), and environment (3 items). The output items were categorized into three major levels – long-term, medium-term, and short-term. Null hypotheses were formulated to test for the difference in the planning gap and output gap between long-term and short-term planners.

Hypothesis 7. The planning gap for the four planning factors is equal for the two groups.

Hypothesis 8. The output gap for the three output factors is equal for the two groups.

The planning gap between the two groups was found to be different for all the four planning factors at a significance level less than 0.005, thereby rejecting Hypothesis 7. The output gap was found to be different in the long-term plan for the two groups at a significance level of less than 0.001 and insignificant for the other two levels. This confirms the fact that short term planners lack a long-term focus in their plans.

## Conclusion

We present a conceptual model for telecommunications planning. The variables in the model were then analyzed in detail and various planning issues were highlighted. Based on this analysis, a set of items measuring the variables of the model were identified and formulated into a survey. The survey was administered to practicing telecommunication managers to determine the items that are perceived to be important in planning and their actual usage in planning: the plan features perceived to be important and the actual level of detail of each of these features in their current plan document.

Input, planning, and output gaps were defined as the difference between the perceived importance and actual usage of various planning inputs, planning factors, and plan features respectively. The input, planning and output gap were found to be significant for most of the items, indicating the necessity to refine the planning process to bridge these gaps.

The relative rankings of the planning factors according to their degree of importance and actual usage revealed some interesting patterns. While factors that were perceived to be important included the integration of IS planning with telecommunications planning and top management involvement, actual factors that were considered were mainly cost or technology oriented. Little business orientation was found in the planning process, which is a cause for concern, especially considering its new role in the organization.

It was found that for all major planning factors (i.e., Business, Technology, Organization, and Environment), there was significant difference in the planning gap between organizations which have carried out long-term telecommunications planning and those that have not carried it out. It was also found that there was significant difference in the output gap for the long-term plan between the two groups.

This study has identified some interesting points that are of relevance to the organizational manager and telecommunications practitioner.

(a) The planning issues provide a overview of the state of art in telecommunication planning. The notion of a “planning gap” may be used to rank the planning items and could serve as a basis for developing a priority list of issues that need to be addressed by the planning manager.

(b) An important management issue, both from telecommunication and IS perspective, is the integration of IS and telecommunications planning. Top management should initiate action to integrate the two planning process by removing organizational constraints that may inhibit the integration process.

(c) The existing telecommunications planning process seems to be highly oriented toward cost and technology, whereas a greater business orientation is required if telecommunications is to serve a new strategic role in the organization.

(d) There is serious concern over the lack of suitable planning methodologies that are specially tailored for telecommunications. The new role of telecommunications in the organization makes it more important to develop a comprehensive methodology, taking into account the factors identified in the model presented here.

(e) Telecommunications planning has graduated from a simple departmental exercise to an organization-wide planning exercise having strategic implications. Suitable planning and control systems, such as planning committee, project evaluation and review committee, involvement of top management etc., need to be initiated to provide structure, as well as get all concerned functions involved in the planning process.
