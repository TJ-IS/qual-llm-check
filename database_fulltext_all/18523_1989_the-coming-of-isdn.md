# The Coming of ISDN

Andrew J. Kennedy

Blackbaud MicroSystems, Worthington, OH 43085, USA

David (Chi-Chung) YEN

Department of Decision Sciences, School of Business Administration, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA

Integrated Services Digital Network, or ISDN, is the latest catch phrase in the field of telecommunications. Much effort has been put into establishing a standard for this new technology, but still more remains before the dream of a seamless communications network will be realized. This paper discusses the process taking place and the problems that this new technology faces. First, an overview of ISDN technology is given: its development, concepts, and capabilities. Then, we examine the major obstacles facing ISDN implementation, with a comparison of the pros and cons of some features. Finally, The technology is considered from an information manager's point of view: how can a manager prepare for the arrival of a mature technology?

Keywords: Telecommunications, Integrated Services Digital Network, Local area network, Digital communications.

## 1. Introduction

In the field of computers and telecommunications, many buzzwords come and go; the “computer crowd” seems to have developed a language all of their own – almost incomprehensible to the rest of the world. The latest of these is the Integrated Services Digital Network, better known by its acronym, ISDN. This technology is generally viewed by the business world as a service of the future, and not important today. While this may, in part be true, information managers should start to learn more of it, since, as with most new developments in high technology, changes are occurring at an accelerated pace that may take many by surprise.

This paper looks at ISDN technology and asks where it is going, and what factors should be considered when looking at the effect of ISDN on future business information structure; thus the goal is to prepare management for the new state of the art in data communications.

## 2. Development, Concepts, and Capabilities

The ultimate goal of ISDN technology is to create an all digital, global network that combines voice, data, and video communications over one transmission medium. Thus, ISDN is a service (not a product) that should provide a communications network to eliminate the need for separate voice and data networks. In addition, it is meant to bring these services to the customer in a simpler, more efficient fashion, and at reduced costs.

Because ISDN deals with telecommunications and will, at least initially, be transmitted over existing telephone lines, it is logical to assume that it will evolve from the existing telephone network. A problem then exists because ISDN is an all digital network, while the current telecommunication services has been an analog system. This is changing, however, as telephone companies are incorporating a step by step replacement of their analog switching and transmission systems with digital technology $[13]$ .

ISDN is really a set of standards meant to guide future development. In fact, these standards are being developed by the Consultive Committee for Internal Telephone and Telegraph (CCITT). These standards were initially define by the CCITT in October of 1984, and early product development should follow their guidelines. These standards are similar to, and compatible with, the seven layers of protocol of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) established by ISO (the International Standards Organization). The reasoning behind this use of OSI is to permit the universal connection of all data terminals and mainframes of any type that follow the framework [2]. This is extremely important if ISDN is to research its ultimate goal, where any type of equipment produced by any vendor can communicate over the same network with any other. The CCITT's recommendations are based on the assumption that ISDN will develop in three stages. The first involves the digitization of the existing analog telephone network (see the Glossary in Appendix for definition of special terms). In the second stage, this digital network will evolve into a narrow band ISDN that integrates all communication services at speeds up to 64 Kbps. The third stage will involve the expansion of ISDN into a broadband network that will incorporate greater data speeds and more services [5].

Within the first phase, the change of emphasis to digital communications is based on the fact that it can move data more rapidly, more reliably, and more economically than traditional analog-based communications. This modernization of service is now under way, having started in the early seventies. A major step in this process involved the introduction by AT&T of their T1 channel, a digital network capable of transmission speeds up to 1.544 million bps. In this way, in 1976 digital switching equipment began to be introduced in the USA. The purpose of ISDN next is to integrate voice transmissions with data communications over the same media, bringing the advantages of digital transmissions to data communications. This takes on increased importance: surveys show that digital communication is growing at an annual rate of 25%–30%, whereas voice seems to be growing at around 4%–10%.

In stage two, CCITT has two types of access services using existing telephone lines, these are: the basic and the primary rates. The main purpose of the basic rate is to provide access to Centrex users, residential users, and small businesses. The primary rate will provide integrated access to PBX users. The basic rate is now being used most in field trails; it is termed 2B + D. This rate offers two 64 Kbps B channels for transmitting voice and data and one 16 Kbps D channel which are used primarily for signalling and supervision, but also for some low level transmissions. Primary rate, on the other hand, is termed 23B + D; and it provides 23 64 Kbps and one 64 Kbps D channel. The primary rate is comparable to the T1 channel and it offers the same throughput rates.

The expansion of ISDN into broadband capabilities is seen as the ultimate step in the technology. It will incorporate speeds above the primary rate, transmit over fiber optics cables, microwave, and satellites, reach worldwide, and offer the capability of more simultaneous services.

But there would be little interest is ISDN if its only purpose was to combine voice and data over the same line. It also offers the promise of many new services, most of which are currently available through some other existing technology. The advantage of ISDN though, is that these services will be offered over one network; it will be much simpler and will be available to many more customers. Also, given economies of scale, the increased usage will help further to reduce costs.

ISDN also promises the elimination of a need for modems. Since ISDN is a digital network, there will be no need to convert the message from the digital to analog format. Another change involves the ability to send data on one channel while talking on another. Since multiple channels are available, it will be possible to talk on the phone while sending information from your terminal to the same person at the same time. Fax equipment will also be a part of the service, allowing users to send 8.5 by 11 inch hard copies between buildings or between floors. Over these channels, access to databases and video conferencing capabilities will also be available [29]. Depending on the existing communications structure, ISDN will be able either to interconnect existing LANs or to provide LAN-like services.

ISDN also offers the promise of complete portability, the ability to move machines to a different wall jack. ISDN accomplishes the reduction of multiple wiring schemes that exist when there are different networks for each feature. Other features that are just beginning to be accepted include automatic call back, caller or number identification, and directory services. This list is by no means exhaustive and new services are being added.

## 3. Obstacles

ISDN faces many obstacles in its path to total development. These involve ideas that are both conceptual and technical in nature, though the two are often closely related. Probably the major obstacle against acceptance of ISDN is in assembling a set of globally accepted standards. These are obviously extremely important to the very concept of ISDN: universal interconnectability. Any standards must first set guidelines but be flexible enough to allow for future innovation.

Major advancements have already been successfully set forth by CCITT, but many problems still exist. CCITT has set specific standards for interconnectability through the use of the OSI model, but intentionally left other areas open for product and network development. Key standards that still need to be resolved relate to the U-interface, access and signaling, data rate adaption, and interworking with other existing networks [12]. The U-interface refers to the two-wire loop configuration that connects the four-wire user to network interface with the local exchange. When the CCITT ratified the first set of ISDN standards it deliberately left the U-interface unspecified, in the hope of encouraging the development of loop interfaces that would work independent from the terminal equipment. Another area that CCITT specifically left alone was the establishment of standards for some of specific services to be offered: they left this area to be determined by the needs of the market.

It is hoped that the model and accompanying standards will provide a solid ground on which ISDN can build, while at the same time allowing future developments. The goals are to: reduce the need for proprietary interfaces; assure terminal compatibility; allow communication among a variety of terminals; be media-independent; and simplify maintenance and trouble shooting $[24]$ .

But many people are already bending the CCITT rules to fit their own needs. One occurs because some vendors that are trying to get a head start on the market by developing products according to their own standards. Also, within Europe, different countries are developing systems along slightly different lines. This is, in part, because some development started before the CCITT set official guidelines. The biggest offender has been the Japanese telephone company – Nippon Telegraph and Telephone (NTT). Its experimental system went into operation in September 1984 and ran until March of 1987. Their system interface differed from CCITT recommendations in several ways, though they claim that when their product is commercially implemented, it will be essentially compatible [25]. Hopefully the influence of CCITT will be enough that most system offerers will conform, for fear of being incompatible with an accepted network.

Related to this, the second conceptual problem facing ISDN deals with the international nature of the technology; the ultimate goal of ISDN is a global network, but can this be achieved given the national and regional developments that are taking place? Different countries tend to develop different systems, because they first look at their internal needs. For example, France made significant nation wide improvements to their telecommunications structure in the 1970's and tends to be less concerned with the development of ISDN. Given the rapid increase of international communications traffic, this issue will become more and more important.

Another problem involving this international issue is satellite technology [19]. According to some authors, the current concept of ISDN is mostly terrestrially-based, reflecting the influence of the national telephone companies. Of course, many PTTs are national monopolies in the communications field, and thus their strategy makers may not be taking advantage of existing technology. If ISDN is to be truly global, it must incorporate satellite technology, especially if it is to reach some of the underdeveloped countries with a lack of long distance wire capabilities. Gillis [5] states that two-thirds of the world's telecommunications spending in 1986, roughly \$15.3 billion, was made by 12 countries, none of them were located in Central or South America or Africa, and only one of them, Korea, was in Asia. The conclusion is that although ISDN may come, the rift that exists between countries will delay its ultimate goal of total international operations.

Another major issue that must be resolved is: Who will control the operation of the ISDN service? This brings up questions of network management, tariff management, usage management, maintenance, and diagnostics. It is obvious that the concept of “who is in control of all these features?” is extremely important, and that it will invariably differ from country to country. In the highly regulated or government owned industries of Europe, this is not as complicated an issue as it is in the United States, where several operating countries compete to offer networks and services. In the USA, the recent divestiture of AT&T has limited the USA’s ability to control ISDN development, and this has had the effect of slowing its introduction of ISDN into the USA relative to most European countries. A real fear exists among people in the US – Federal Communications Commission (FCC) that ISDN could return the USA telecommunications industry back into a regulated monopolistic operation similar to that which existed pre-divestiture. The official position of the FCC is that: “while the commission has no intention of unnecessarily regulating the development or implementation of US ISDN's, we believe it essential that we should monitor the progress of various specification efforts which are under way to ensure that our pro-competitive policies are furthered and not inadvertently frustrated. To that end, we are of the tentative view that we must monitor and participate in the international specification efforts which are now under way." European nations tend to frown on this approach, as they see it creating more problems in their dealings with the US; they would rather deal with one monolithic national organization. In general, the USA and Europe seem to be somewhat out of synch on ISDNs.

Another question is whether large corporations with established systems will allow a “phone” company to retake control of their voice and data networks now running over private networks [26]. To overcome this, the systems that evolve will have to be very attractive indeed.

Directly related to the idea of public versus private control is an issue that tends to be both conceptual and technical in nature: the issue of cost. How much will it cost to run both data and voice over these networks? While it is really too early to tell, the numerous trial applications that are currently under way should help to provide a good estimate. The big question is whether the new tariff rates will compete effectively with current analog price structures. Different sources have expressed different opinions as to what will happen in an ISDN environment. One suggests that tariff rates for this enhanced service could be twice that of existing phone services. If this is so, there will inevitably be resistance from roughly 80% of the population who have no need for these features or advanced technology. They will not be willing to pay to subsidize large organizations who are taking full advantage of all the technology.

On the other hand, there is evidence that these costs will fall. First, many believe that political and competitive pressure will help to depress prices. Secondly, the phone companies realize that huge increases in traffic throughput will have to take place immediately to benefit from economies of scale. Knowing this, most will probably accept lower profits with increased usage of the system. Thirdly, most people presume that natural efficiency will tend to drive the cost down. The cost savings of corporate users will result in an increase in traffic, adding users so that the cost savings will penetrate into the smaller consumer rates. Also, if the people offering this service are to truly achieve the benefits of large volume usage, they must consider satellite technology now. It will make ISDN available in all corners of the globe, and this will be very useful, if not invaluable, to the large multi-national corporations.

With respect to tariff versus cost, there are large difference of opinion between the USA and European service providers. The FCC hopes to allow the pricing structure to evolve through a market driven economy, while the government dominated PTTs feel that prices controls are important for a successful introduction of ISDN.

A fourth consideration also implies lower costs to the user. From the management point of view, ISDN offers vastly improved network control, and that leads to better cost control and therefore an ability to lower cost.

The final conceptual obstacle to a smooth movement to ISDN deals with the human aspects of any new technology. There is generally resistance to change to any new technology, especially those with major change to current methods and equipments, and this is irrespective of the evidence supporting its advantages. This fear of change to the workplace and the environment is presumably because people are not sure where they fit in the new structure. Thus, people continue to support the old, even though a better system exists. However, the company's competitive advantages may soon rest on its adopting ISDN, and profits from its advantages will force the issue. None-the-less phone companies must prove that ISDN will be better, faster, and cheaper. The investment will be high when changing a large corporation's entire network, and organizations will not enjoy trashing their existing equipment.

There are also some technical obstacles facing ISDN. Probably the most important, is in linking ISDN to existing structures, specifically LANs and IBM's SNA. Use of LANs has grown rapidly in the past few years and they are performing their duties well. How, then, can these technologies coexist in the future? Whereas ISDN is basically considered a wide area network (WAN), it also inherently offers the capabilities of LAN-like services, via a Centrex system, which offers a centralized hub of advanced services that radiate from the central office to various subscribers. ISDN will be able to provide connectivity between on-premises devices by routing signals through the central office. One disadvantage to this solution will be the reluctance of many companies allowing their sensitive data to leave the premises, possibly in order to connect with another PC on another floor. This is the serious problem of data security plus an increased transmission time, though it seems likely that broadband ISDNs using high speed fiber optic cables may solve the latter. At this time, ISDN may not be the best mechanism for sensitive data transmissions, but companies like AT&T and Northern Telecom are working to provide better data encryption methods to aid in solving this [30].

Currently, gateways between LANs and ISDN are difficult and expensive $[18]$ , but this will probably change as the technology advances. ISDNs will develop as backbone systems to existing LANs, while offering LAN features to customers just entering the market with simpler needs.

Interconnection to SNA is also an important topic, since IBM's system concepts have become a standard in much of mainframe networking. The two technologies seem incompatible at certain levels of the OSI protocol, specifically the bottom three [28]. IBM has been understandably slow to embrace the ISDN technology, waiting until more is known about how it will affect their products. They have, though, recently states their intentions to support the ISDN position and participated in field trials in UK and Belgium [20].

There also seems to be technical concerns over ISDN transmissions speeds, especially from people that develop, or have just installed, sophisticated LANs. They feel that narrowband ISDNs will not be able to cope with the “speed inflation” currently taking place in the corporate world. Sophisticated LANs are now offering data speeds far in excess of the primary rate of 1.5 Mbps. Ethernet, for example, offers transfer speeds of 10 Mbps and fiber LANs to 100 Mbps. However, ISDN will offer acceptable rates to a very large portion of the user public, and the eventual development of broadband networks will make the speed less of an issue.

## 4. Pros and Cons

The pros and cons of various aspects of ISDN are now reviewed.

## The need for ISDN

The first issue is very broad and addresses the question: Why ISDN?

Although ISDN has advantages, it also has many opponents. Some of their arguments are:

\- ISDN is simply a means for phone companies to increase their revenues by recapturing a market they have been losing to private networks [6, 14]. They argue that most users do not require the enhancements offered by ISDN.

\- It will result in higher costs to the small user [8].
- The USA could potentially return to a pre-divestiture regulated monopolistic situation.

\- Most ISDN services (LANs, FAX capabilities, and videoconferencing) are available through other means that are in operation and working [27].

## Arguments offered by supporters include:

\- Initial cost benefits to the large users will ultimately result in cost benefits to everyone; the overall efficiency of the system will reduce costs in the long run.

\- The FCC has announced its commitment to maintaining competition in the USA [10].

\- Although ISDN services are available through other means, the new system will combine all these systems onto one network [16]. This will reduce the cost and complexity of multiple schemes. ISDN will therefore be easier to use and support.

\- ISDN offers the potential of even more services.
- The digital format will tend to be more reliable than analog transmission.

\- There already is a climate of frustration and confusion among users; this is caused by the large number of vendors and possible system architectures to be examined when making a decision about communications needs.

\- A less tangible benefit will be the strategic possibility that ISDN will provide to developing companies or countries.

## Public control

The second issue to be clarified is that of private versus public control. Countries that operate under a regulated system, where the phone system is under government control, argue for public control for the following reasons:

\- Leaving ISDN advancements and introduction in the hands of private companies will increase risk of failure, because of higher tariff rates and confusion.

\- It is harder to deal with a fragmented industry.

\- Private control is resulting in a slower introduction process.

Supporters of private control by competing companies claim:

\- ISDN will allow more user input resulting in an ultimate product that better conforms to their actual needs. One argument against the CCITT has been that user groups are traditionally underrepresented at their meetings and don't have enough influence to make sure their needs are met.

\- A competitive environment will be more service oriented.

\- Will result in more innovation.

## Development of standards

The third issue concerns the development of standards. There are many arguments against the development of standards and who should set them:

\- They have the effect of limiting innovation: people are unnecessarily restricted by them.

\- CCITT does not have enough control to see that others adhere to their standards.

\- Opinions may be too restricted at standards meetings.

Some of the reasons that standards are important include:

\- The development of a universal standard is inherent to the whole idea of connectivity.

\- Standards offer direction for unsophisticated users and developing countries.

\- The CCITT has tried to create standards without stagnating innovation, though this is difficult.

## ISDN versus LAN

The last argument involves the concept of ISDN versus LAN. ISDN is capable of performing LAN-like services, but many feel that the LANs of today are far superior to the ISDNs of tomorrow. The arguments here against ISDN are:

\- LANs are, and will remain, capable of much greater speeds than ISDN.

\- LANs offer greater data security since the data does not have to leave the premises.

\- LAN technology is in place and works.

Supporters of ISDN in this area state:

\- ISDN is capable of performing LAN services and may be a better alternative for less sophisticated users.

\- Although ISDN is currently significantly slower than state-of-the-art LANs, it is 50 times faster than 1200 baud modems, which are extensively used today.

\- The eventual introduction of broadband ISDN will eliminate questions of transmission speed [21].

\- It eliminates the problems of multiple networks [1].

\- ISDN can interconnect LANs to form a WAN.

## 5. Questions for the Information Managers

Given this wealth of information, what does this say to a corporate information manager trying to make decisions regarding the future of the company's information systems? Probably, the most important aspect is that ISDN is not yet here, but it is coming; managers should start planning now to be prepared when it arrives. What, then, should be done to meet increasing transmission needs today? The obvious answer is that the organization should be sure that any equipment purchased will be adaptable to future ISDN when it is available. Ultimately, any purchase decision must involve a vendor which is committed to evolutionary networking and will allow access to ISDN services [11].

In general, upgradeability is always important. One of the promises of ISDN is that it will incorporate this concept better than current multiple architecture systems.

As always, the current setup must be examined. If a LAN is being used, how long has it been in operation and is it meeting current and projected needs? If so, ISDN may still be available as a backbone system to the LAN or to allow connection into a WAN. If no LAN is currently installed, ISDN features will soon be available with these services.

If the organization is currently using a Private Branch Exchange (PBX) system, can it evolve into an ISDN network? Early implementations for ISDN tends to be more geared towards use of a CENTREX system.

What size is the organization? ISDN in its early stages will tend to be more attractive to large corporations taking advantage of all features and cost savings.

Is the organization, and therefore its information needs, growing rapidly? If so, ISDN offers the promise of greater flexibility in terms of control and upgradeability. In a rapidly changing environment, it is important to ask if problems exist with the current system. Is there incompatible hardware with multiple network and wiring schemes? Is there poor communication between branches? If this is the case, the ISDN will provide positive answers. If no real problems exist, it is probably best to wait and see how the technology develops.

Another question is: What are the competition doing? If they are preparing for or begin using an ISDN, it may prove to their competitive advantage.

Finally it is also important to keep in mind the long range needs of the organization. In discussing their recent trial implementation of ISDN, a representative of McDonald's stated that they could satisfactorily meet current needs with current technology but were worried about the future.

## 6. Conclusion

The promise of ISDN is a universal telecommunications service that will handle all types of communications needs. Since all information is transmitted in digital form, the potential services for ISDN are very wide.

Consider some of the more optimistic views of the future. After the introduction of broadband ISDN, the new technology will help transmit full-color broadcast TV and hologram messages, such as three dimensional advertising at home, though the high bit rates would imply the exclusive use of optical fiber cables. ISDN also offers the potential of efficient security systems with direct connections to the local police department.

However, major policy issues need to be resolved before ISDN can become a reality. These involve questions of its development, the making of policy decisions, and the changing focus of control once it is established. ISDN must also today assume manager resistance due to the high dollar figures involved and the general confusion in the market place. Competitive pricing policies are important to speed up the introduction of ISDN and to insure that it reaches the high volume it so desperately needs.

While ISDN is really not here today, and though opinions vary on when it will be, managers must be prepared to deal with it when it arrives. Purchase of new equipment should be made with ISDN compatibility in mind. The future is not yet, but it is coming fast.

## Appendix

Glossary

Bandwidth - The difference between the highest and lowest frequencies that can be transmitted over a band.

Basic rate – Initial ISDN access service that offers two 64 kbps and one 16 kbps channels for signaling and supervision purposes. Known as 2B + D. Broadband – A technique whereby a number of different signals are transmitted over the same medium using such techniques as frequency division multiplexing.

CCITT – Consultive Committee for International Telephone and Telegraph. An organization responsible for the development of prototype standards for ISDN

ISDN - Integrated Services Digital Network.

LAN - Local Area Network. Communication system designed for intrabuilding data communications.

Narrowband - As opposed to broadband, this transmission technique carries only one signal at a time.

Optical fibers - Very thin strands of glass that can be used to carry data encoded signals.

Primary rate - Referred to as $23\mathrm{B} + \mathrm{D}$ , this access service offers twenty three 64 kbps B channels and one 64 kbps D channel for a total data speed of 1.5 Mbps.

SNA - Systems Network Architecture, an intelligent communications network developed by the IBM Corp.

T1 channels-AT&T corporation's name for a digital transmission system that carries 1.544 million bps. T1 channels were an instrumental part in the first step towards ISDN introduction, the digitizing of an existing network.
