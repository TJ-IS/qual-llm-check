# Corporate Productivity and Shared Information Technology

Christine T. Kydd and Louise H. Jones
College of Business & Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716, USA

Corporations have made large investments in information technology over the past thirty years. The current trend is to continue this investment in shared information technology (SIT), including such tools as electronic mail, distributed databases, teleconferencing, and group decision support systems. However, investment in SIT may not be appropriate for or beneficial to every company. This paper suggests that in order for SIT to be successfully implemented within a firm the corporate culture must be one that supports the sharing of information across traditional organizational boundaries. Further, the particular SIT tool selected must allow the same type of communication to take place as does the traditional communications mechanism that it is intended to replace. General guidelines are given for firms concerning conditions under which high return SIT can be implemented.

Keywords: Productivity, Shared information technology, Culture, Corporate culture, Communication, Information sharing, Information technology, Implementation.

## 1. Introduction

Executives have long been concerned with understanding the return on investment in information processing. Although the economic justification of transaction processing systems which support business is relatively well understood, the economic benefits of investment in information systems which support managers and other knowledge workers are difficult to quantify. The problems of measuring bottom line impact are even greater for investments in information technology (IT) which supports communication and sharing of information, or shared information technology (SIT).

Many organizations feel that they must invest in shared information technology (SIT) to increase organizational productivity even though the problems with implementation of this technology are well-known [5]. It has also been shown that to increase productivity technology-based IS must be congruent with other key organizational ISs, namely structure and culture [16]. The first condition is by far the most critical. A corporate culture, or “the way we do things around here”, embodies the shared values, meanings, and understandings about what is important for a company and how it will work to achieve its goals. Job structure often follows naturally from culture. For example, the cultural changes associated with implementation of world class manufacturing have led to a natural redefinition of the maintenance function $[19]$ . Conversely, it is likely to be very difficult to change job structures without a preceding change in culture and this is one reason that implementation of many methodologies for developing IS have failed $[9]$ .

## 2. Organizational Information Processing

Based on recent work, several conclusions can be drawn about how information is processed in effective organizations. First, in so-called “excellent” companies, there is a great deal of communication among people in different functional areas $[18]$ , and there may also be cross-functional management of cost, quality, and scheduling $[7]$ . This implies that communication occurs across traditional organizational boundaries and that information is shared. Further, the medium that is most often used for such communication is some type of personal, face-to-face meeting.

Thus, the “excellent” firms have realized that, in order to get the most value from their information, it must be shared and transmitted in a timely manner to those who need it. The implication for effective use of SIT is that the firm’s organizational norms must support information sharing.

Traditionally, American businesses have worked through a hierarchical structure in which corporate norms have dictated that communications paths follow the hierarchy [3], thereby allowing certain managers to monopolize information. SIT, on the other hand, allows individuals to work in a cooperative manner, often across traditional organizational boundaries. SIT permits communication to be faster and more direct and has led to large increases in white collar productivity in organizations where the culture encourages and supports information sharing [14].

Second, high performing managers process information differently than low performing managers. A recent study $[2]$ showed that excellent managers are more effective in choosing the most appropriate communications media to deal with uncertain versus equivocal situations. Uncertainty results from a lack of objective information to describe a particular situation. Equivocality results from ambiguity or multiple conflicting interpretations of the situation. Uncertainty can be reduced through the gathering and analysis of additional data. Equivocality generally can only be resolved through synthesis of the multiple perspectives and enactment of a common frame.

In addition, rich communications media, such as group meetings, one on one face-to-face meetings, and telephone contact, have been found to be more effective as a means for dealing with equivocal situations than are less rich mechanisms, such as formal rules, policies, and standard data summaries. However, the less rich structures are sufficient for reducing uncertainty, and in fact are the most cost effective way of dealing with uncertain situations.

The implication for effective use of SIT is that this technology can limit personal interactions. For example, travel expense may be reduced through use of teleconferencing but at the cost of failure to allow for equivocality resolution. That is, if teleconferencing is used in a setting where the participants need to meet face-to-face to address existing ambiguity, then the result may be dissatisfied users as well as an unresolved situation. SIT may be appropriate for use only in certain organizational settings, i.e., those where uncertainty is high but equivocality is low. Thus, organizations need to be aware of the relationship between communications structures and business situations and to implement SIT selectively, based on a match between these factors.

In addition, implementation of any new technology which impacts organizational communication and decision-making is a change which must be managed. Numerous cases have been reported in which new information systems, new systems methodologies, and new technologies have been rejected based on perceived effects on current jobs [15]. SIT may be resisted more strongly than other technologies, because it implies that individuals can no longer control information that was previously private or was used for personal gain. Therefore, SIT implementation may be resisted due to perceived shifts in power which accompany implementation of the technology [11,13].

Recently, one of the authors has proposed that this resistance may also result from uncertainty and equivocality introduced by the nature of the technology as well as the implementation process $[12]$ . Uncertainty results from new technology through an increased variety of methods for task completion. Equivocality results from complex technology which is not easily understood. Uncertainty is introduced by the implementation process when there is poor or inadequate preparation of the IS users. Equivocality occurs when the support of upper management is not clear.

These same principles can be applied to shared information technology. SIT implementation is likely to fail when workers do not understand how the new IS works, why it should replace other ways of doing things, and whether or not top management is really committed to its implementation and continued use.

## 3. Organizational Culture and SIT

## Culture and Corporate Strategy

For a company to realize a positive return on its investment in SIT, the organizational culture must be supportive of information sharing. A culture is a collective and shared value system, which directs individual behavior, particularly in ambiguous settings. It represents that collection of perspectives, values, assumptions, and expectations that allows individuals to work toward the achievement of a predetermined strategy for the firm. In successful organizations, the culture is usually quite strong in determining how equivocal situations are resolved, how communications patterns are set up, and how work gets done. A key objective is to direct and support behavior towards work that allows the company to be successful.

For example, if a firm's strategy is based on innovation and market leadership within its industry, then its culture must support and encourage risk-taking behavior. Only then are individuals likely to develop truly innovative ideas. In addition, the firm must respond appropriately to individual risk-taking, by offering rewards for the behavior itself, whether or not it leads to a fruitful outcome [17].

The same principles can be applied to an organization that is introducing SIT. The culture must provide incentives for individuals to share information. These must include evidence that when an individual's job is changed by SIT, there will be a way to restructure the job to make it easier for the worker to support the firm's strategy. Any new technology that works at odds with corporate strategy cannot succeed.

## Culture and Information Sharing

A critical aspect of a company's culture which impacts the economic value of SIT is its historical sharing of information. Kilman [10] has identified four cultural dimensions: task support, task innovation, social relationships, and personal innovation based on the organization's time horizon (short vs. long term) and emphasis (people vs. task). The task support dimension (short term/task emphasis) is characterized by high information sharing, cooperation with other groups, and concern with efficiency. In order for implementation of SIT in an organization to yield a high return on investment, the task support dimension must be strong. The other three dimensions are not as critical.

One characterization of corporate culture is based on four cultural types: integrative, exacting, caring, and apathetic based on the dimensions of concern for people (high versus low) and concern for performance (high versus low) (Figure 1) [20]. "Exacting" organizations (high concern for performance, low concern for people) and "apathetic" organizations (low performance, low people concerns) generally reward short term individual success while "integrative" organizations (high performance, high people concerns) generally reward longer term group success. Clearly, there is much more cultural support for information sharing in “integrative” organizations than in “exacting” ones.

Analysis of an organization's reward system can be used to determine the potential value of SIT. Preliminary results of content analysis of the electronic mail messages of a limited number of individuals in several different organizations have shown that “integrative” organizations use SIT to share information required to further business objectives far more than “demanding” or “apathetic” organizations. For example, 95% of the electronic mail messages received by a marketing representative of a high tech firm with an “integrative” culture dealt with “real work”, including decision and commitment-making. Similarly, nearly all of the electronic mail messages in a financial services organization, also with a strong “integrative” culture consisted of information needed to make business decisions. Interestingly, the electronic transmissions of business information were usually preceded by a personal phone call. Both of these organizations have compensation schemes that reward long term, group success.

In contrast, most of the electronic mail messages received by Research & Development employees in an organization that rewards short term individual results dealt with issues peripheral to business decisions and commitments. In one case, 95% of the messages dealt with issues such as the United Way, safety, organization notices, general and corporate news, and seminar announcements. In this company, electronic mail has replaced public bulletin boards.

Thus, culture sets the stage. When a firm's culture dictates strong internal competition for resources and rewards for short term individual results, sharing of significant business information does not occur. In these firms, the return on SIT investment is likely to be disappointing. On the other hand, if the firm's culture emphasizes cooperation and teamwork, information sharing will occur and implementation of SIT is likely to add significant bottom line value.

## Cultural and Organizational Evolution

Most organizations grow through a series of well-defined stages with different management focus, organization structure, top management style, and control and reward systems [4]. The stages appear to be caused by an alternating organization focus on external issues, or effectiveness, and on internal issues, or efficiency. The predictable crisis at the end of each stage can usually be resolved by implementing new systems. For example, rapidly growing startup companies tend to focus on market results and neglect internal efficiency. Turnaround of these firms requires implementation of IS which reduce the uncertainty associated with basic business parameters, such as the revenue contribution and costs for each product. This is the information needed to improve internal efficiency.

Similarly, mature companies may focus on internal efficiency, develop line–staff organizational structures, and lose touch with the marketplace. What is needed here is implementation of IS which support the collaboration and team effort needed for complex problem solving and innovation. This is especially important in a slow growth, supply-rich, highly competitive business environment.

The transition from a culture that is primarily concerned with efficiency, or “doing things right”, to one that is primarily concerned with effectiveness, or “doing the right things”, is necessary in a slow growth business environment. New and innovative products are valued in a fast-growth economy, while quality products are valued more in a slow-growth economy. Production capacity is usually limited in a fast-growth economy, while customer demand may be limited in a slow-growth economy.

A quality improvement program can be viewed not only as a program for improving product quality but also as a program for changing organizational culture. Successful quality improvement programs shift organizational values so that effectiveness (customer values) becomes more important than efficiency, products and processes become viewed as measurable and improvable, and people become viewed as the source for valuable ideas. “Meets requirements” has little meaning without objective measures of requirements, which means that objective information is key to the success of a quality improvement program. Organizations with successful quality improvement programs have generally also established effective communications among the people who know the customers’ requirements (sales and marketing) and those who know the technology of design and manufacturing (engineering and production). Thus quality improvement programs and the companion just-in-time manufacturing programs can be used to implement the changes in corporate culture that are necessary for business success in a highly competitive, slow-growth economy.

## 4. Guidelines for Implementing High Return SIT

Guidelines for SIT implementation usually stress planning for technology acquisition and user training, measurement, and communications. We suggest the following guidelines:

1. Assess the organizational environment to determine whether shared information will further the strategic objectives of the organization and whether the organizational culture encourages information sharing.

2. If shared information is not strategically important, defer investment in SIT until it is.

3. If it is strategically important and the culture does not encourage information sharing, plan and implement an improvement program (such as a quality improvement program) that focuses on a single, measurable objective of strategic importance to the organization and that requires involvement by everyone in the organization. The objective of this program is to develop a culture in which everyone is concerned with continuous improvement, measurable results, and shared information [8]. Make sure that management behavior is consistent with the objectives of the program [6], and that the organization's reward system encourages information sharing.

4. If the improvement program is successful, develop a plan for implementing SIT. This plan should include plans for developing an information infrastructure with standardized definitions of key data elements, an information technology infrastructure which provides access to both corporate and external data bases, and a uniform set of user-friendly tools. This set should include tools which establish communication protocols between individuals [21] and reinforce the new collaborative cultural norms.

If the improvement program has not been successful, it should be revised and continued until information sharing becomes an organizational norm.

## 5. Concluding Remarks

The importance of culture as a determinant of the success or failure of information technology investments cannot be underestimated. We know of one case where implementation of a shared information system in a collaborative organization with a strong information sharing norm produced very significant bottom line improvement. However, in another case, implementation of an electronic mail system in an organization without a strong cultural value of meeting commitments failed because it simply provided a new mechanism for “passing the buck”. In a third case the implementation of an uncertainty-reducing system (MRP-II) in an organization with a strong cultural value of collaboration and a tradition of face-to-face communication failed because it was viewed as a threat to established communication patterns.

Shared information technology can be a very valuable and useful tool for organizations and can have a favorable impact on the bottom line. However, SIT tools are not for every company. Firms interested in implementing SIT must carefully consider whether or not their business results will benefit from increased teamwork and information sharing. Traditional hierarchical organizations are often more effective in stable environments while collaborative organizations are generally more effective in rapidly changing environments. In addition, these firms must also consider whether their culture is appropriate for cooperative teamwork and information sharing across traditional organizational boundaries. If the culture and reward system do not support teamwork, they must be changed and the commitment to change must be evident at all levels of management. Finally, specific SIT tools must be selectively matched to organizational situations so that the communication tools are appropriate for identifying and dealing with the organization's situations of uncertainty reduction and equivocality resolution.
