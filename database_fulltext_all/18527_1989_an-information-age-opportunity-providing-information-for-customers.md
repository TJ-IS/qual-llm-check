# An Information Age Opportunity – Providing Information for Customers

Jae Hyon Song and Crumpton Farrell
College of Business, St. Cloud State University, St. Cloud,
MN 56301, USA

Evolutionary growth of the use of information technology in business operations now provides a new information-age opportunity for managers, that of providing information for customers (PIC). In the PIC applications, information is a product not a resource and the product is information, not data. Pertinent examples of firms using PIC and steps to be taken by any firm that desires to implement this concept are explained in some detail. This application of computer technology usually can be implemented at minimal costs. PIC provide competitive advantages to the firm by tangibilizing various information services for customers.

Keywords: Providing information for customers, Customized information, electronic data processing, Management information systems. Decision support systems, Interorganizational systems, Transaction reports, Expert-information, Customers' value chain, Privatization of information, Privacy.

## Introduction

Viewing information and information technology as resources of a corporation implies that they are inputs that can be converted into products for customers. Currently, within most firms, principal users of information are corporation employees. Historically, given evolving technology, information systems have been used for various groups of employees: replacing manual clerical transactions and report generation and supporting decision making, communicating, monitoring progress and other internal management tasks.

However, recent usage of information technology (IT) in corporate strategy (Benjamin et al., 1985; Business Week, 1985; Farrell and Song, 1987; Parsons, 1983; Porter and Miller, 1985) clearly indicates that IT has room for expansion This paper explores systems which provide Information for Customers, PIC for brevity.

The next section covers background and concepts, followed by a discussion of the types of PIC. Then, several examples of a PIC system and its applications are cited. Following are suggestions for developing a PIC system in any firm, with concluding remarks ending the paper.

## Background and Concept

When a customer buys a product or a service to meet a need, the customer is buying physical aspects of the product as well as information and data attached to the product (place, time and need utility); when buying a service, the customer is usually buying convenience, saving time, and filling a need. In many cases, product and service are complementary.

Examples are product information from sales and customer service personnel and a users' manual. Furthermore, customers need additional information in their consumption processes; some firms have recently started to use their information systems for processing data for customers and providing customized information (processed data) to their customers as a part of the product or as an independent product. For these firms, information is a product (output), not a resource, and the product is information, not data. For this new service we label it PIC – Providing Information for Customers – in this paper.

After Johann Gutenberg, about 1450, invented the movable-type printing press, the publishing industry gradually evolved, providing information as a product and a service. A private library has the product books providing a private service; whereas public library books provide a public service. The postal services provided ready distribution of printed or written matter, using improving transportation methods over decades, The nineteenth-century invention of the telegraph, telephone, and wireless permitted rapid, almost instantaneous distribution of information as a product, as a service, or both. The telephone system required use of directories, then yellow-pages, then specialized mailing lists – service requiring a product – a product from one perspective and service from the user's point of view. In the mid-twentieth century, the exploding availability of electronic computers and systems has allowed new methods of simultaneous (system-dependent) distribution of information as either a product, as a service, or both.

Consider, now, the evolution of data processing and concept of PIC therein. Traditional electronic data processing (EDP) or management information systems (MIS)/decision support systems (DSS) (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Davis and Olson, 1985) provide data processing services or information for corporate employees. However, PIC provides information for customers, not transaction processing services as in the case of interorganizational systems (IOS) (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Clemons and McFarlan, 1986; Lu and Song, 1987) which tie organizations, typically buyers and sellers, to process various interorganizational transactions.

Figure 1 shows the PIC characteristic matrix.

Providing information customized to meet an individual's needs as an independent product or as an integral part of a product is a concept which has recently evolved from transaction-data-processing services for customers. Most transactions between individuals and organizations are recorded and processed manually or by computers. The resulting records, now transaction databases, include customer information of various types – information on general market as well as individual customer needs.

This demand side of information concerning products and services has prime importance for the strategic as well as operational management of firms. Lack of information on the nature of the demand, or customers' tastes and shifts in demand over time makes new product introduction especially risky. To minimize such risks, market and customer surveys are widely used. Also, to cope with demand risks, firms traditionally had built-in measures to provide flexibility, such as holding inventory of standard products, or planning for managerial or production slacks to permit meeting unexpected demand shifts, product and service diversification, subcontracting of production, and reliance on part-time help. However, such practices can needlessly increase operating costs.

But the importance of transaction information is not exclusively within the firm; such data are equally, perhaps more, important to customers. Individual customers need the transaction information for planning and control of their future transactions as well as records for legal, tax, and personal uses. Also to search, to use, to repair, and to replace products, customers need market and product information. In the past years, not knowing exact information needs of their individual customers, it was difficult for managers to provide specific information (processed data); today transaction records from IOS can help firms to identify and specify information services needed for individual customers.

Processing, storing and analyzing these large volumes of often scattered records are difficult for individual customers. But since most business transactions are processed by various computer information systems, providing various data processing services or information for individual customers is neither difficult nor expensive. Thus, providing information from their transaction databases or from other databases for customers is one of the new services made possible by information technology, with a wide application potential in product differentiation and diversification. Due to their availability, ease of processing by information technology, and closer relatedness to suppliers' products, transaction-data-based PIC are most popular.

Next, types of PIC services are addressed, followed by a section citing several examples, with their PIC implications, from various industries: financial, consumer credit, health care and wholesale distribution.

## PIC Services

The PIC concept permits ready delivery of three broad types of services: (1) summary and activity reports aggregated for an account, a customer, or a household, (2) product and market information judged to be important for the customers, and (3) related products and services information. PIC information are different from product promotion; PIC information are customized and targeted to a specific customer while promotion is aimed to general customers. Thus, for a PIC system, a firm must determine information needs of the customers and have databases as well as information processing skills and delivery media. Figure 2 which expands on Figure 1 visualizes PIC systems in a schematic.

In Figure 2, the databases include transaction records, market data, and product data. The PIC system processes the transaction database to generate summary and activity reports for a customer; further, needed information for individual customers is predicted and specified from the customers' buying records. In production and marketing operations, a firm generates and collects a vast amount of data on products as well as markets, processing and delivery of customized information for customers are not that difficult; the difficult part is to specify the needed information for customers; tying electronically customers to firms has substantially reduced the difficulty.

Applications in the following section are examples of information services provided; it is useful to classify PIC systems in terms of transaction PIC systems, expert-information PIC systems, and comprehensive PIC systems. Transaction PIC systems provide summary reports for a customer's record keeping; expert-information PIC systems provide information services for products, markets, and related services and products for customers; comprehensive PIC systems provide both summary reports as well as expert-information.

## Pertinent Business Applications

Case 1. Fidelity Investments, the world's largest mutual fund company, now offers individual investors more than 85 open-ended funds, grouped into 6 different families of funds to meet diverse purposes of investors and to diversify their risk. The funds include growth funds, growth and income funds, bond funds, money market funds, and select (sector) funds.

Fidelity provided records for individual transactions. Since a typical investor tends to invest in more than a few funds for diversification, record keeping was not a simple chore. To make it easy, Fidelity encouraged customers to consolidate all their separate-fund-based accounts, thereby creating a single account for each customer or a household. For each single consolidated account, Fidelity now provides a monthly report showing all the transactions of the various funds in one consolidated customer account for each month including a year-to-date activity summary. Thus, customer preferences and marketing analyses are easily ascertained.

Case 2. Gold MasterCard holders receive monthly statements that show all the transactions sorted out by their type of expenses and also summaries. This consolidated and organized report of various card uses simplifies the record keeping chore for customers. This increases the usage of MasterCard significantly for those who want to organize their record keeping for tax and financial planning purposes. Of course the MasterCard company obtains potentially valuable data on their customer's card uses and a marketing edge.

Case 3. In late summer 1987, 3M Corporation's Medical Products Group and Abbott Laboratories (drug industry) announced the Quick Link system, the joint formation of an order-entry computerized system, whereby hospitals in the USA could order medical supplies from 3M and Abbott Laboratories, saving order-entry costs and providing improved services. This is an application of computer systems with the full PIC potential yet to be realized.

Case 4. Within industrialized nations, food distribution firms – food wholesalers and distributors – have been undergoing consolidation and mergers in order to minimize costs and provide better services to retail food outlets. In 1970–80, there were over 800 food wholesalers in the USA; in 1986 there were only 319, a sixty-percent reduction. By 1992, estimates suggest there will be only 150 USA food wholesalers. Retail store chains are letting large food wholesalers distribute foods from manufacturers to each retail store; retail stores are doing what they know best – selling at retail. Food wholesalers are merging and consolidating, becoming more capital intensive and relying upon computer networks to optimize distribution and ordering costs as well as improve services (Wall Street Transcript, 23 November, 1987 pp. 87524–525). For food manufacturing and processing firms, for advertisers and trade associations, PIC becomes an important competitive advantage. Combining PIC with point-of-sales (POS) data from retailers is also valuable.

Case 5. Comp-U-Card International, Inc., members call 800 phone numbers (a PIC concept) and tell the operator the product they desire. A computer scans lists containing the latest bids from 500 independent distributors, wholesalers and retailers and ascertains the best deal. Comp-U-Card places an order to the lowest bidder, and the merchandise is delivered to the customer's home. The company collects membership fees and commissions (Business Week, May 18, 1987, pp. 73–74).

The first two examples, Fidelity and Gold MasterCard, are PIC applications wherein companies provide consolidated summary and activity reports. Changing their reporting systems from traditional transaction based to customer accounts simplifies record keeping for Fidelity and Gold MasterCard customers. Other benefits include cutting down mailing and paper processing costs for Fidelity. In addition to these immediate benefits, there exist long-term benefits from the data that Fidelity and Gold MasterCard now collect on their customers. Since the data are aggregated at the decision-making unit level, individual or household, the data are a rich source of information for analyzing financial or spending decision-making behavior. Fidelity and Gold MasterCard provide better information to their customers; long-term bonds exist between customers and their firms. Customer turnover is low. They achieved their strategic advantages by adding a customer information processing function to their routine mutual fund processing and reporting systems. Fidelity identified a stream of needed services and introduced them, such as the Fidelity CD Service and Mutual Fund Guide.

Computer systems have been usually considered as supporting equipment for the activities which are internal to the firm. Fidelity and MasterCard are examples of firms using their resources to benefit their customers, a significant departure from conventional thinking. This concept for using information technology is based on the ability to identify information needs, economies of scale for information systems, and institutionalization of information services for the customers. Computerization of transaction processing helps to identify target groups of customers for information products and services. Economies of scale imply that the extra services can be provided with small marginal costs or in some cases it actually reduces processing costs for the firm by industrialization (Levitt, 1986) and computerization. Institutionalization of information services for customers means that information services become a part of their customers' routine life through learning and specialization, which will build up a long-term exit barrier, precluding their customers switching to other firms.

Cases 3 and 4 illustrate how some medical and food supply systems process interorganizational transactions, which have a considerable PIC potential by providing summary and activity reports as well as expert-information on markets and products for purchasing decisions. Some newspaper and magazine distributors help their retailers by providing market information gained from their distribution databases. Also, some medical and dental clinics send reminders to their patients for certain regular checkups. These are examples for the comprehensive PIC systems.

The final case, Comp-U-Card, is an expert-information PIC application whereby a firm obtained a new business by providing information support for their customers' product search. Unlike previous examples, the database for Comp-U-Card is not a by-product of transaction record processing but a created one, which meant a higher initial start-up cost for the system.

From these applications, it is clear that providing information for customers can be beneficial. Some PIC systems were originated as transaction PIC systems and some as expert-information PIC, both types are moving toward comprehensive PIC systems by providing transaction reports as well as expert-information. PIC systems are currently at the beginning stage or yet to be implemented in many firms.

## Developing PIC

PIC provides additional value to a firm's customers by improving activities required by customers. In the past, in most firms, information systems have been developed to process and support activities within each firm, but not really considered as a benefit or a supporting tool to their customers. Implementation of PIC requires a major change, shifting management focus from production processes to consumption processes in generating value and by letting customers use some corporate resources directly.

Developing a PIC service is a combined process of new-product development and information systems development which requires:

(a) Developing PIC service concepts,

(b) Feasibility of PIC in terms of economics, information technology, operation, and acceptability,

(c) A prototype development and testing, and

(d) Commercialization and further improvement.

PIC opportunities exist in areas where customers need information services as a part of the supplying firms' primary products or independent products. Examples include banking, insurance, stock brokerage, home appliances, wholesale supply activities, retail store supply, supply services for building contractors, dental and medical services, inventory control services, and distribution centers.

Systematic identification of customer information needs is possible by considering the chain of activities in consumption – the customers' value chain. A customers' value chain represents a sequence of activities performed by customers to meet their needs; the similar corresponding concept for producers – the producers' value chain – consists of a series of activities performed by the producing firm including purchasing, production and marketing (Porter, 1985). A customers' value chain typically includes realizing a need, searching products or services to meet the need, purchasing, delivering, learning to use, using, and repairing or replacing, plus record managing. PIC opportunities exist for one or some of these activities for which companies can identify target customers and provide customized information.

Next, each firm needs to explore the question: what data or implicit information do we have that could be provided as a service to or for each of our customers?

After the identification of potential needs, target customers, and data, ways to organize data and delivery of the information for the customers should be determined by investigating conceivable uses of data by the possible decision makers and users. By way of examples, for financial-data for tax reporting purposes, transactions should be aggregated by the tax reporting unit; automobile repair records should be organized for each case. Recent trend of changing dumb-products to intelligent-products by using chips is an example of ways to provide routine operational information for users.

Important questions in this product concept development stage are (1) what needs and wants are to be met, (2) how to identify information needs, (3) how to acquire needed data, develop and deliver information to customers, (4) what are the primary benefits, (5) how is the product or service different from other competing products, and (6) what is the marketing strategy in terms of target markets, price and goals. For the information needs identified, it is useful to separate information expected by the customers from information judged to be needed by the customer; the former would provoke customers and the latter would be a source of competitive advantage. PIC ideas and concepts that do not mesh well with corporate objectives, strategy, and capabilities should be screened and dropped from further consideration.

Assessing economic feasibility requires an estimate of costs and benefits. Relevant costs in providing PIC include computer and system programming costs, costs for data acquisition, added software and hardware costs, report generation and distribution costs.

Direct benefits are measured by the increment of present sales and revenue. PIC as a part of the firms' primary products can increase their sales by charging a premium price for their products and increasing their sales volume, since customers may be less willing to switch to other firms which might complicate information processing such as transaction record keeping. Primary products of the company will be differentiated from competing products by providing additional information of value to customers.

Indirect benefits of PIC for a firm have many facets. As noted before, the systems establish a bond with customers; the managers of the firm are required to consider all aspects of their clients needs, and a competitive edge is also established. Additionally, data aggregated and organized into transaction records for a PIC system at individual or household levels, i.e., at the level where the transaction decisions tend to be made, can be very useful in analyzing micro-level behavior leading to new products and services, in determining unfilled related consumer needs, and in permitting understanding overall market behavior by using cross-section as well as time-series data. Transaction level data may not provide adequate insights on buying behavior of individuals, households, and organizations. Such analysis would require consolidation of transaction data at a proper level. Examples include medical services at individual or community levels, life insurance at individual and family level, automobile usage at the household level, and usage of personal computers for individuals and organizations.

In general, the transaction relationship between two parties changes as one party collects more information on the other party. Thus, the transaction becomes more differentiated and the involved parties' roles become more specialized. For example, when a firm obtains detailed information on a customer, that firm may be able to devise an improved way to meet the customer's needs. Customers do not need an extensive search to find the right product; the firm takes over some functions in the customer's buying decision process. Again, specialization could mean long-lasting buyer-seller relationships than otherwise. One needs only reflect on consumer-credit-card database services, or business-credit data services. Witness the burgeoning cooperation between the Baby Bells and data service organizations.

All these potential benefits – collecting high quality data on individual customers, building up exit barriers which discourage customers from switching to other firms, and potential sources of extra revenues from the services derived from increased sales or from marketing the data to third parties – can be done with slight additional costs in most cases and with savings in many applications.

PIC systems may require further privatization and commercialization of what has been, so far, public or free information (Schiller, 1981). As the demands of firms to identify their current and potential customers' information needs grow, issues related to privacy, accuracy, accessibility and property (ownership of information) may become problematic (Mason, 1986). To understand market behavior, it is necessary to analyze the market at various levels including transaction, individual customer, family and organization. Transaction level data are probably sufficient for transaction analysis, but they may not provide insights on the buying behavior of individuals, families, and organizations. This process requires consolidation of transaction data at a proper level. Examples are medical service at individual level, life insurance and family car at family level, and personal computer at individual as well as the organizational level. Consolidation of related data files enhances the information contained in the data, especially relationship types of information. Data consolidation can be perceived as a threat to privacy. Additional data that the company wants to collect through IOS should be clearly justified by the needs and the direct benefits for the customers. Organizational and social acceptability regarding the PIC services should be critically assessed.

After a prototype PIC is developed, a market test is advisable to learn how well the PIC service will do before making a final decision for its full scale implementation.

To recap, in the past, mass production and mass marketing of modern industrial products and services have met customers needs and wants only approximately, which has been a source of inefficiency. Two basic ways to enhance overall efficiency are differentiation by customizing products and enhancing your customers' information processing capabilities. Information technology has been a primary tool to improve internal management operations using techniques such as JIT (just-in-time) inventory systems and computer aided manufacturing (CAM); PIC systems are designed to enhance customers' information processing capabilities.

In our information age, managers must explore every possibility to define and discover information, usable to someone, and then to capitalize upon opportunities.

## Concluding Remarks

Information is a resource for organizations as well as an output for organizations. Companies can create new information products or strengthen existing products/services. Transaction records have value to companies as well as individuals or households. Most firms today process transaction records by computers. PIC systems based on transaction databases are easy to implement. Examples given were of what some innovative firms are doing to provide PIC, giving these firms a competitive edge.

This trend has a vital place in corporate strategy. We conclude that almost every firm has an opportunity to apply this PIC concept to enhance their performance and productivity, current and future. We are moving from the position of using telecommunication and computer technologies in the production processes and internal operations to using these technologies for providing information for customers.
