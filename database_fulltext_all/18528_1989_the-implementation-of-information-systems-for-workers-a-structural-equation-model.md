# The Implementation of Information Systems for Workers: A Structural Equation Model

Evan E. Anderson

The University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75083, USA

Previous studies of information systems implementation and utilization have been concerned with the behaviors and attitudes of managers or their surrogates. This paper employs a system of five structural equations to represent the causal relationships between the characteristics of workers, jobs, and systems that may account for the satisfaction and utilization of information technologies by nonsupervisory workers. Results indicate that the character of the job and the individual worker have little impact on the ultimate satisfaction and usage of CBIS by workers, while education and training in the system and opportunities for enhanced work productivity are important.

Keywords: Implementation, Decision support systems, Nonsupervisory workers, Structural equations, Organizations, End users, Productivity.

1. Introduction

Computer based information systems (CBIS) are being used as aids to decision making at every level of organizations, including that of nonsupervisory workers. This is made possible by the decreasing costs of computers and their peripherals, the increased possibilities of workplace networks and shared databases, the greater ease of using these technologies, and a recognition by management that workers can, through technology, become more productive. While the scope of decision making is narrower for workers than managers, they nevertheless have a substantial need for information and decision support services. Indeed, a 1973 study conducted by the University of Michigan's Survey Research Center found that one of the most important factors contributing to white-collar job satisfaction was having enough information to do their work [61].

Most previous research regarding the acceptance, satisfaction and/or usage of information technologies has involved managers or their surrogates, e.g. [3,4,17,25,29,30,43,51]. Additionally, there have been investigations of systems implementation involving nonsupervisory professionals such as teachers [52], policemen [77], and sales representatives [50]. Few studies, however, have focused on the variables and issues affecting the acceptance and integration of CBIS in the decision processes of workers. Exceptions have included the staff of a union organization [49], clerks of financial institutions [55,74], and claims representatives of the Social Security Administration [75].

This study investigates the factors and conditions that affect the implementation and utilization of information systems by workers. It proposes a system of structural equations to describe the relationships between job characteristics, affective and behavioral responses of workers, organizational facilitators in the design and implementation of systems that may affect their acceptance, and the use-satisfying attributes of the CBIS.

## 2. A Macro Perspective of Technology in the Workplace

Within the context of an organization specified by resources, goals, constraints, and opportunities, managers define operations and activities. In particular, they define and create the work environment, design jobs, hire workers, acquire technology, and assign tasks to workers. Their decisions involving the role of technology are not made without constraints. In particular, the conditions for implementation are affected by the characteristics of the technology, by personal systems that determine, through job descriptions and agreements, the organization and assignment of work, and by control systems that influence employee behavior [59]. Except for the influence that workers have through their participation in decision making, they have little opportunity to affect the implementation of technology. Hence, their action set consists primarily of reactions to technology, which in some cases may adversely affect productivity and/or job satisfaction. Mumford [57] has argued that many of these impediments can be overcome, in the case of CBIS, if the processes of design and implementation take into account the social environment of systems.

Figure 1 shows the interaction of three basic inputs to work within organizations: the workers, their jobs, and the available technology, where technology is defined as a tool [28]. These three components each have their own unique characteristics and degrees of compatibility with one another. The output or productivity of an organization depends substantially on the mutual reinforcement between them. The more compatible and cooperative they are, the greater the productivity of workers, as measured by the quantity and/or quality of tasks accomplished. In Figure 1 the output of their union is defined by the area of the triangle. Productivity may also be affected by the socio-environmental qualities of work life such as organizational politics, personal relationships, and job growth opportunities [15].

The characteristics of workers, jobs, and technologies are not static. Indeed, through change, it may be possible to improve their compatibilities [44]. Change is very substantially conditioned, however, by the flexibility of these three work elements. Management generally has considerable latitude to initiate change in job design and technology, but is limited in their ability to insure worker acceptance. In Figure 1 improvements in the compatibility of jobs and technology are represented by shifts of the intercepts $J_{i}$ and $T_{i}$ to the right and/or left of the origin (o). The new boundaries for productivity are indicated by the dashed lines. It should be noted that productivity need not derive symmetrically from the compatibilities of jobs and technologies with workers. That is, one may be more compatible with the worker than the other and, therefore, contribute more positively to output.

## 3. Model and Structural Equations

Much of the empirical research using statistical models in the management sciences is characterized by some form of specification error; one of the most frequent is equation omission. The variables and factors that theoretically and logically account for the explanation of effects are often numerous and complex in their interdependencies. Single equation models, by definition, hold that there is a single endogenous variable and that all other variables are exogenous or lagged versions of the endogenous variable. Despite the relative ease of estimating and testing single equation models, the world is frequently not that simple. Indeed, the multiplicity of interdependent variables argues that there is probably more than one endogenous variable [32]. If all relationships between variables are defined by a single equation, additional endogenous variables, those actually determined within the system, are treated as though they are exogenous. This creates some important estimation problems because these “hidden” endogenous variables generally will be correlated with the error term and the use of ordinary least squares (OLS) will produce biased

To increase the likelihood of correctly specifying the important causal relationships in the implementation of CBIS for workers, this paper employs structural equations. The implementation model for workers involves five endogenous variables, eight exogenous variables and five equations. The variables used in this study, and their sources, are defined in Table 1. Exogenous variables relate only to endogenous variables and correspondingly have only unidirectional arcs. Nevertheless, many exogenous variables have complex indirect relationships with some endogenous variables through one or more other endogenous variables. Each endogenous variable is defined as a function of one or more exogenous and/or endogenous variables. ## 3.1. Job Growth Opportunities and Ease of Learning CBIS

Technology has the opportunity to enhance productivity only if it is accepted and used. Two of the exogenous variables that are conjectured to affect the integration of information technologies into the job setting are: design participation, and user education and training in the system.

Employee participation in decision making did not originate with systems design, nor is that its sole application. It has been widely used in such areas as: job redesign, goal setting in production and quality control environments, and operations improvements, where it has been found to improve employee attitudes, motivation, and productivity $[19,45,65]$ . There are primarily two arguments for user participation in the design and implementation of CBIS. First, it may be used as an intervention strategy to facilitate acceptance and change $[15]$ , or what Mumford et al. refer to as a “lever of manipulation” $[56]$ . Second, user participation creates a forum for the exchange of information and experience between users and designers [14].

In the early years of MIS research there were diverse views on the value of user involvement. Ackoff [1], and Dickson and Simmons [23], for example, argued that the lack of user participation in systems design contributed to MIS failures, while Rappoport [62] argued that, “...To insist upon detailed systems design knowledge by managers as a prerequisite for new management information systems is tantamount to calling for an information systems moratorium...”. Elsewhere the author has argued that user participation should be regarded as a decision variable and determined after giving consideration to various contextual aspects of the problem, technology, user(s), job responsibilities, and organizational design, rather than as an axiom of implementation [6].

Studying managers in their organizations or their surrogates in laboratory experiments, numerous researchers have found a positive relationship between user involvement in systems design and satisfaction or attitudes toward the system $[39,71,78]$ . Such evidence, however, is far from conclusive. Summarizing the results of twenty-two studies Ives and Olson $[40]$ concluded that “eight claim to demonstrate a positive relationship between user involvement and various measures of system success, seven others present mixed results, and the results from the final seven are negative or nonsignificant”. It is also important to note that previous and very extensive behavioral research into the effects of employee participation has found that it has a more profound impact on attitudes than on behaviors $[20,47]$ . Hence, while there is considerable evidence that user involvement in design leads to greater satisfaction with the system, there is much less evidence that it increases system use $[29,43,51]$ .

Job related training and education has long been recognized as one of the most important potential job enriching, productivity enhancing activities that an organization can provide for its employees $[9,33]$ . Additionally, there is a special need for user training and education in MIS $[21,26,76]$ because of its technical nature, and because of the rapid technological developments that create obsolescence and change. Fuerst and Cheney, in a study of lower level managers in the oil industry, found user training to be one of the most significant factors accounting for general and specific use of a DSS [29].

Design participation and user training are regarded as exogenous variables that have direct effects on two endogenous variables, job growth opportunities, and ease of learning CBIS. Job growth opportunities are defined to be the potential for personal growth through one's job. It is conjectured that user training and design participation allow the worker to become more knowledgeable and experienced in CBIS and, therefore, they will find systems easier to understand and use. Second, it seems reasonable, apart from the functional contributions of design participation and user training, that they may cause the worker to experience a specific job satisfaction associated with the opportunity for personal growth and development. Workers may interpret training and design participation as a source of enrichment and opportunity that extends beyond their ability to complete tasks. Mumford et al. [56] have argued that there is a “knowledge contract” between the employer and employee and that employees expect, in return for use of their skills and knowledge by the employer, that the employer will reward them by assisting them to increase their skills and knowledge.

It is further hypothesized that the length of time an employee has been using CBIS in their present job relates directly and positively to the ease of learning to use systems. The longer workers are in their jobs, the more familiar they will be with formal and informal information flows within the organization, and the more insights they will have into the applications of CBIS [29]. Additionally, the more experienced they are with computer systems, the fewer fears and anxieties they should have in specific applications of CBIS.

## 3.2. Job Satisfaction

The acceptance, satisfaction, and usage of information technologies by workers must be considered in the context of their jobs and their affective responses to them, that is, their job satisfaction. Apart from the person holding a particular job, what is the potential for job fulfillment and satisfaction, as derived from the basic character, scope, and design of the job [35]? Hackman and Oldham [34] have argued that the more motivating the job, the more the employee will be satisfied with it. In addition to job satisfaction, we believe that a more highly motivating job will encourage the worker to seek improved job performance and, therefore, they will be more inclined to use productivity improving tools such as CBIS.

Furthermore, we believe that there is a causal relationship between the specific satisfaction associated with opportunities for personal growth and development on the job and job satisfaction. After analyzing nearly 1,700 responses of people to questions about their work, Herzberg [38] created a dichotomy of factors leading to extreme dissatisfaction and extreme satisfaction with their jobs. Satisfier factors included growth opportunities, recognition, advancement, and achievement; all are indirectly related to design participation and job related education. In a summary of the dimensions of jobs that consistently have been found to contribute significantly to employees' job satisfaction, Locke [46] cites intrinsic interest of the work, and opportunity for learning, along with others. Mumford et al. [56] have argued that the "psychological contract" between employees and employers causes employees to seek employers that will fulfill their needs for recognition, achievement, responsibility, advancement, and work interest.

Hence, our third structural equation specifies the endogenous variable, job satisfaction, as a function of an exogenous variable, the motivating potential of the job, and another endogenous variable, job growth opportunities.

## 3.3. Decision Time Using CBIS

The fourth structural equation establishes worker productivity as an important endogenous variable capable of explaining user satisfaction. Employees recognize the relationship between productivity and compensation, promotion, recognition, and other rewards. To the extent that employees are motivated to be more efficient, they are more likely to regard CBIS as a work aid and judge it by improvements that it can make in their work performance. Therefore, usage and satisfaction with CBIS will tend to be higher when workers can accomplish the same outcomes with less time and effort, and/or increase the total amount of work accomplished [22]. The potential of CBIS to improve worker performance establishes what has been described as a “personal stake” in the system. Schultz et al. [68] have argued that the higher the personal stake, the greater the user’s incentives to become involved in the development of the system, learn the system, and to use it.

Some empirical support exists for these conjectures. Bjorn–Anderson [11], for example, has reported that clerks experienced an increase in job satisfaction when manual methods were replaced by batch processing. Similarly, Turner [73] found a significant improvement in productivity between work groups (performing the same clerical job) that had substantially different levels of computer systems usage.

In this study, we have used satisfaction with the amount of time required to make a decision using CBIS as an indicator of productivity. Regardless of the source(s) of time lost under CBIS, a critical question is, “How are decision times affected?” Longer decision times generally have the effect of reducing the volume and/or quality of work accomplished. They may increase uncertainty, the likelihood of errors, and/or reduce the payoffs from decisions $[27]$ . The impact of decision time on the quality of decisions varies substantially. At the worker level, decisions are generally structured, narrowly defined, and do not involve important resource commitments. Responsiveness is frequently a criterion for job performance and, in that context, decision time is important. Barber and Lucas $[8]$ , in a study involving CRT operators, found that job satisfaction and performance decreased with greater response times. Streeter $[70]$ has noted, however, that the frustrations of time delays can be eased when work can be rearranged or when several tasks can be performed concurrently or interchangeably. Nevertheless, such frustrations tend to increase, as the number of alternatives to be considered increases $[53]$ .

The fourth equation proposes a causal relationship between the endogenous variable decision time and exogenous variables: internal work motivation, time consciousness, perceived time pressure of workers, the amount of manual work required to use CBIS, and the endogenous variable: ease of learning to use CBIS. Decision times are expected to vary inversely with the environmental and self induced stresses of workers to act quickly. Increases in work motivation, time pressure, and awareness of time utilization (time consciousness) are argued to create an urgency in task fulfillment that will tend to reduce decision times [48]. Manual work, however, is expected to vary inversely with decision time. It raises the personal costs of systems usage to the worker, reduces their productivity, and lowers their satisfaction with CBIS. A favorable effect is created, however, by systems that are easy to learn to use. Such systems will encourage users to develop their knowledge and understanding and, thereby, create levels of proficiency and expertise that would allow them to make more timely decisions.

## 3.4. User Satisfaction

The last structural equation argues that the endogenous variable, user satisfaction with CBIS, is caused by the direct effects of the endogenous variables: ease of learning CBIS, job satisfaction, and decision time using CBIS, the exogenous variable: manual work to use CBIS, and indirectly by all other endogenous and exogenous variables. Others [17] have argued, though without empirical support, that job satisfaction is a dependent variable that may be increased through successful CBIS design. In this study, we have argued that workers who enjoy high job satisfaction are more likely to accept and use new technologies in their jobs. Furthermore, the easier those technologies are to understand and use, the more users will perceive that their productivity may be improved by them, and the greater the likelihood that workers will become satisfied users.

Satisfaction and utilization of information technologies, even in a very positive social-technical environment, will not tend to occur unless the perceived value of their contributions exceeds their costs and inconvenience. Design participation and training that facilitate learning may reduce decision times, enhance worker productivity and contribute to system satisfaction. However, the amount of manual work required to use CBIS directly and indirectly reduces user satisfaction by making CBIS more onerous and costly to use. As Keen [42] has noted, the decision to use a DSS over alternative facilities occurs because the DSS provides adequate benefits for low incremental effort. Lucas [50] studied the reactions of sales representatives to a newly implemented OCR input system and found a relationship between satisfaction and the cost/benefit tradeoffs of using the system.

## 4. Estimation Methods

Using the notation of Table 1, a structural equation is developed for each of the endogenous variables. See Table 2. The $\beta_{ij}$ are structural parameters for the causal relationships between $Y_{i}$ and the predetermined variables $X_{j}$ . Equations (1) through (4) are recursive, while equation (5) is simultaneous. Each variable is measured in terms of deviations about its mean; therefore, the constant terms can be omitted without loss of generality [60]. In addition to simplifying the expressions of our structural equations, standardized coefficients also remove the effects of different measurement scales. Therefore, it is possible, within the same system or population, to compare the relative strengths and importance of various variables [36]. The desirability of using standardized coefficients, however, is not unanimously accepted. Blalock [12], Schoenberg [67], and Duncan [24] have argued that the unstandardized coefficients are preferred because comparative studies and longitudinal studies generally involve different populations and, hence, different distributions of system variables.

Though we are unable to establish that the estimators for equations (3), (4), and (5) are unbiased, we can conclude, under asymptotic properties, that they are consistent. For large samples, $Y_{1}$ and $U_{3}$ in equation (3), for example, will be uncorrelated and $Y_{3}$ will be normally distributed. A biased, yet consistent, estimator may not equal the true parameter on average, but $|\hat{\beta}_{ij}-\beta_{ij}|$ approaches zero with unit probability as the sample size approaches infinity.

In structural equation models, we must consider whether the data are capable of estimating the conjectured relation, rather than inadvertently estimating another relation of the same form within the same causal structure; that is, “Are the equations identified [54,64]?” Identifiability is concerned with the extent to which we can find unique solutions for $\hat{\beta}_{ij}$ from the reduced form equations. If we estimate the parameters $\Pi_{ij}$ of the reduced form equations, does the number of structural equation parameters equal the number of reduced form parameters? If so, we can solve uniquely for the values of $\hat{\beta}_{ij}$ , that is, the equations and system is just-identified [18,64]. Interested readers should see appendix 1 for an additional discussion of identification. Table 3 presents a detailed report of the conditions for identifiability and an evaluation of each equation.

For reasons discussed earlier, equations (1) and (2) can be estimated with OLS and their estimators are unbiased and consistent. Several methods exist for the estimation of parameters in equations (3), (4), and (5). One of the most accepted approaches is two-stage least squares (2SLS). 2SLS divides parameter estimation into two separate phases. Consider equation (3). In the first stage equation (1) would be estimated. In the second stage $\hat{Y}_1$ would be used as an “instrumental variable” in place of the sample data for $Y_1$ to estimate

$B_{31}$ . That is, the parameters of equation (3), $B_{31}$ and $B_{34}$ , would be estimated in the second stage using $\hat{Y}_{1}$ and the sample values of $X_{4}$ . The same sequence of steps are involved in the estimation of parameters in equations (4) and (5). However, if the error terms of two or more of these equations are correlated, as is later reported, three stage least squares (3SLS) is the appropriate estimation method.

## 5. Data and Measures

Data were collected from approximately 90 nonsupervisory workers of a high technology firm located in the southwestern United States who were users of batch and/or interactive systems. They all performed job tasks that were directly related to unit organizational processes in such area as: accounting: personnel, purchasing, inspection, technical support, and production control. Over a two day period, groups were assembled consisting of eight to thirty-five persons to answer a battery of questions presented on a single questionnaire. There were a total of eighty-three questions used to measure the variables in this model. If one or more questions were unanswered, the respondent was disqualified and removed from the sample, so that the final sample consisted of sixty-nine workers.

As can be seen from Table 1 many scales were taken from previously published research, and in some cases, such as Hackman and Oldham's (MPS: motivating potential of the job), they represent classic approaches to measurement. Questions developed by the author and colleagues used a seven point semantic differential scale. The adjectives used for extreme points of the scales for the following exogenous and endogenous variables were: none and extremely frequent for design participation; extremely dissatisfied and extremely satisfied with training in the system, ease of learning to use CBIS, amount of manual work required to use CBIS, an decision time under the system; 'not at all' and 'over five years' were the extreme points for the question concerned with the length of time an employee used computer based systems.

Researchers have cited many different factors and conditions that affect the satisfaction of users with CBIS. Bailey and Pearson [7], for example, identified a total of thirty-nine such factors from the literature and their own research. Users in this study were asked to evaluate four of the most important: accuracy, reliability, timeliness, and relevancy. They rated their satisfaction with these four attributes for each of the system types (batch and interactive) on a seven point semantic differential scale. The adjectives used for the extreme points of these scales were extremely dissatisfied and extremely satisfied. The variable $Y_{5}$ (User Satisfaction: CBIS) was computed as the average of their responses for these attributes. See footnotes b. and c. to Table 1. Methodologically, satisfaction may be a more appropriate measure of system success for workers than usage since many of them are compelled to use CBIS in order to perform their job tasks, that is, usage may not be voluntary. Lucas [50] makes the point that “...Where use is inappropriate (for example, where all respondents have to use the system or model), researchers have employed measures of satisfaction and/or favorable user attitudes as indicators of successful implementation.”

Indices used to measure all other variables: job growth opportunities, the motivating potential of the job, job satisfaction, time consciousness, and internal work motivation were constructed from the exact questions and computed according to procedures employed by their original authors.

It is assumed that respondents interpret and treat the various semantic differential scales used in this study as equal interval scales. Boyle $[16]$ concludes that violations of this assumption are not likely to lead to distortions in regression coefficients since that depends on the coincidence rather than the magnitude of errors. Furthermore, Bohrnstedt and Carter $[13]$ argue that even when the assumption of equal intervals is violated, parameters should be estimated by regression methods.

## 6. Analyses

The model proposed in this paper is based on a causal ordering of relations, with each directed graph representing a hypothesized cause and effect relationship. In full recursive models with just-identified equations, one can decompose the total covariance between any two variables into direct and indirect effects through path analysis [5,31]. However, when the model is overidentified, the component effects in the model will not generally sum to the observed correlation coefficient. Thus, a biased estimate of the true correlation coefficient, as well as an ambiguous interpretation of effects, results from a decomposition of the total covariance. Our analyses in this study will occur at three levels: the variable within an equation, the equation within the model, and the full model compared with a “trimmed” version of the full model [72]. The trimmed model is derived from the full model based on tests of statistical significance of variables and equations.

The concept of theory trimming argues that, according to some criteria of statistical significance or meaningfulness, some arcs or causal effects can be deleted to yield a more parsimonious model. Heise [37] argues that a relationship may be eliminated from a model if the magnitude of the effect is so small in relation to other effects that it has no practical or theoretical interest. We have employed that $t$ and $F$ distributions, respectively, to evaluate the statistical significance of variables and equations. Our approach for evaluating the full model against the trimmed version is based on Akaike's [2] information model selection. See appendix 2.

Table 4 presents the standardized parameter estimates, standard error of estimates, and associated t statistics. Using $\alpha = 0.10$ , the t statistics indicate that several variables, or causal relationships, are insignificant. They are design participation in equations (1) and (2), length of time using CBIS in equation (2), job growth opportunities in Equation (3), work motivation, time consciousness, and perceived time pressure in equation (4), and job satisfaction and decision time using CBIS in equation (5). Two of these variables, however, length of time using CBIS, and decision time using CBIS are significant for somewhat larger $\alpha$ 's.

Table 4 presents an $R^{2}$ and F statistics for each equation. Again, for $\alpha = 0.10$ , there is one insignificant equation, equation (1). However, two of the endogenous variables, job growth opportunities and job satisfaction, are insignificant as predetermined variables in subsequent equations. $Y_{4}$ (Decision Time) is not quite significant at $\alpha = 0.10$ , but would seem to be close enough to be retained in trimmed versions of the full model.

By virtue of insignificant t and F statistics, it seems reasonable to eliminate most of the causal relationship found on the left side of Figure 2. In particular, these results suggest that the character of the job and the individual employee contributes very little to the ultimate satisfaction of workers with CBIS. Despite the theoretical appeal of variables such as job growth opportunities, job satisfaction, motivating potential of the job, and others, it appears that worker satisfaction with CBIS depends more on their training and experience, the functionality of the system, and the implications of CBIS for user productivity. For example, it is interesting to note that the manual work required to use CBIS varies inversely with decision time and user satisfaction, and is highly significant in both cases.

## 7. Conclusions and Managerial Implications

Given the potential increases in productivity due to applications of information technologies and resulting reductions in organizational costs. It is understandable that managers are keenly interested in better understanding the workplace factors and conditions that affect the acceptance and use of CBIS. It is interesting to note that the factors having the greatest impact on the system satisfaction of workers are determined, to a large extent, by design, development, and implementation considerations, and therefore, manageable. These results should provide considerable optimism that successful systems can be created without measuring, understanding, and changing a set of complex organizational, job, and human factors.

From our analyses, it appears that variables related to the individual, as an employee, have very little effect on their satisfaction with CBIS. These variables surprisingly include work motivation, satisfaction with opportunities for growth and development on the job, time related pressures, and overall job satisfaction. Furthermore, it appears that the design of the job, through its affects on the motivating potential of the job and job satisfaction, is unimportant. By comparison, some of the most important variables, such as education and training, and length of time employees have been using systems in their jobs are capable of managerial planning and control.

A surprising result, though, concerns the insignificant impact of design participation. It has virtually become a principle of systems design and implementation, and has gained wide acceptance because of the evidence relating it to user satisfaction. However, much of the empirical evidence fails to support a relationship between design participation and usage, apart from acceptance and satisfaction. Furthermore, these results are entirely consistent with the general conclusions of thirty years of behavioral research that has found participation to have more impact on attitudes than on behaviors $[53]$ .

A possible explanation for the poor performance of design participation concerns the organizational level of workers and their prior experience and expectations with regard to consultation about matters that affect them. Most previous research that has found a relationship between participation and satisfaction has involved middle and upper level managers. Managers are accustomed to participating in decisions and have an expectation that their inputs to decisions will be sought and used [69]. This is probably not true of most employees at the worker level of organizations. Hence, they may not regard participation as a condition for acceptance and a source of motivation.

Even when there is participation, employees at the worker level may expect their views and opinions to have less impact on final outcomes. While a strong advocate of human considerations in systems implementation, Mumford states, “...resistance has not shown up so far in relation to EDP, this is because largely female clerks have been on the receiving end of new computer systems. This is a group with little power, influence or job investment…” [58].

Swanson [71] has cited the possible importance of employees' conceptions of their organizational role for bringing about change, as it relates to the process of making inquiry possible through an MIS. Few workers probably see themselves in this role. Additionally, if they are accustomed to little influence over decisions and developments in the work place, they will be more detached from the meaning and importance of participation in systems design. Robey and Farrow, for example, found that “…participation is only effective if it allows users to exert influence toward both conflict generation and its constructive resolution” [63]. It is most likely that workers, even when participating in systems design, expect to have little influence and, hence, they may find participation less important.

An approach frequently used to enhance and upgrade worker performance is job related training and education. Organizations can improve the acceptance and utilization of information technologies by assisting workers to become more knowledgeable and experienced in CBIS, i.e. through education. This study has found that training makes an important contribution to ease of learning and, in turn, to decision time and user productivity. The potential contribution of education and training to successful systems implementation appear to concur with those of previous research e.g., [29,66].

Our research provides support for the contention that the productivity enhancing aspects of technology will tend to promote user satisfaction. Conversely, impediments, delays, inconvenience, difficulty, and lost time that arise from users trying to understand and utilize CBIS, jeopardize successful implementation. Managers should take steps to insure that the time and effort required to use technology is less than the individual savings gained. Demonstrable improvements in productivity, enabling the worker to accomplish more tasks and/or complete tasks more easily, will encourage greater use and satisfaction with CBIS.

## Appendix 1

The equations of our model are not just-identified. If equations are not just-identified, they may be either overidentified or unidentified, which is determined by evaluating their order and rank conditions [41]. Table 3 presents the results of those evaluations, and its footnotes define the requirements of both rank and order conditions. The order condition for identifiability is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition. When the number of restrictions placed on the coefficients of the structural equations are solely exclusion restrictions, the order condition requires that the number of omitted exogenous variables be equal to or greater than the number of included endogenous variables minus 1. If they are equal, the equation is just-identified, and if they are greater than that quantity, the equation is overidentified.

The rank condition, which is sufficient for identifiability, was found to be satisfied for each equation. Through the order condition, each equation was determined to be over-identified.

## Appendix 2

To implement Akaike's method, let the true model be defined by

$$
Y B _ {0} ^ {\prime} + X A _ {0} ^ {\prime} = U, \quad \text { with }\tag{6}
$$

$$
E \left[ U ^ {\prime} U \right] = n \Sigma_ {0} = n R _ {0} ^ {- 1},\tag{7}
$$

where Y is an $n \times G$ matrix of endogenous variables, X is an $n \times k$ matrix of exogenous variables, and $B_{0}^{\prime}$ and $A_{0}^{\prime}$ are parameter vectors. Now, suppose equation (6) can be approximated by

$$
Y B ^ {* \prime} + X ^ {*} A ^ {* \prime} = U ^ {*}, \quad \text { with }\tag{8}
$$

$$
E \left[ U ^ {* \prime} U ^ {*} \right] = n \Sigma^ {*} = n R ^ {* - 1}.\tag{9}
$$

The criterion under Akaike's method is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} I I = \log L (Y; \hat {\theta} ^ {*}) & \\ - \operatorname{trace} \left[ J (\theta^ {*}, \theta_ {0}) E (\hat {\theta} ^ {*} - \theta^ {*}) \right. & \\ \cdot (\hat {\theta} ^ {*} - \theta^ {*}) ^ {\prime} ], \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

where $\theta$ is a matrix of unknowns. $\Pi$ is computed for the full and trimmed models, and the model with the highest value is chosen. It should be noted that $J(\theta^{*}, \theta_{0})$ can be consistently estimated by

$$
- \left(\frac {\partial^ {2} \log L (Y , \hat {\theta} ^ {*})}{\partial \theta \partial \theta^ {\prime}}\right).\tag{11}
$$

Simulation experiments using the larger model, with parameter vector $\hat{\theta}$ as the true model, were used to estimate $E(\hat{\theta}^{*}-\theta^{*})\cdot(\hat{\theta}^{*}-\theta^{*})'$ . Each simulation involved one thousand trials.
