# Assessing DSS Effectiveness Using Evaluation Research Methods

Gerald E. Evans

Management Department, University of Montana, Missoula, MT 59812, USA

James R. Riha

Computing Center, The Claremont Graduate School, Claremont, CA, USA

One of the crucial issues in MIS/DSS technology is in guaranteeing their effectiveness and efficiency both, for present and future implementations. This paper describes a thoroughly researched and qualitatively rigorous methodology (known as Evaluation Research), which can be used to develop guidance for MIS personnel involved in the evaluation of DSS. Our objective is to describe a way to provide a more rigorous scientific approach for the evaluation of MIS/DSS projects. This approach is based on assessing the realistic (objective) value of information as opposed to current approaches that are based on the perceived (subjective) value of information. To facilitate understanding of Evaluation Research and its application to MIS/DSS, a case study involving a DSS for Hazardous Waste Management is used throughout this paper.

Keywords: Decision support systems, Management information systems, Evaluation research, Systems evaluation, Hazardous waste management, Quasi experimental methods.

## Introduction

In their book, Keen and Scott Morton assert: "The decision-centered approach to design and implementation [of DSS]... requires a formal evaluation methodology" [18]. Although some approaches have been suggested to evaluate MIS [10,13] the unique characteristics of DSS demand new evaluation methods that can accommodate them.

The design and implementation of a DSS is an integrated activity composed of: computer technologies, information processing procedures, organizational structure, and people. Consequently, there is a growing need to incorporate more rigorous and precise scientific methods to the evaluation of DSS as they impact the organization.

This type of evaluation is especially important, as DSS are becoming more important in meeting the overall demands of organizational strategies. DSS systems are quickly becoming important in all types of jobs (operational, managerial, and strategic planning); they incorporate the domains of information technology, office automation, and decision support and they assist in all types of business activity (such as productivity analysis, forecasting, and strategic planning).

Academic journals in the fields of Information Science and Cognitive Psychology. These publications and professional presentations encompass a variety of topics including: problem solving, expert systems, statistical analysis, evaluation methodologies, and User-Interface design. His current research interests include Information Systems Design and Evaluation, Cognitive Aspects of User-Interface Design, and applications of Information Systems in Organizations.

Up to now, most attention has centered on efficiency using an objective (quantifiable) criteria, while the effectiveness of the same systems has been based on subjective (perceived value) criteria.

This paper provides a scientific basis for the evaluation of DSS design and its implementation. The evaluation of DSS is viewed from a standpoint of the contribution the system makes to achieving organizational goals and its performance in the operational environment. Specifically, to establish one method of evaluating and improving both the efficiency and the effectiveness of DSS.

To facilitate this discussion, a case study will be used which demonstrates the evaluation of the effectiveness of an actual DSS. The DSS used for the case is a hazardous waste management and tracking system designed and implemented for the State of Nevada. Since the system was built using grant money from the State and Federal Environmental Protection Agencies, there is an ongoing concern that the DSS actually improved the decisions of State officials in their notification, inspection, and compliance activities.

## Effectiveness and Efficiency

One of the key differences between DSS and other information systems is its emphasis on system effectiveness, rather than efficiency alone. System efficiency is easily defined and consequently relatively easy to measure and evaluate; e.g. efficiency, in an operational sense, is formally defined as output divided by input. Inputs are usually money, time, and other resources, whereas outputs are normally the processed information (e.g., reports and listings). Consequently, efficiency asks whether we are processing information faster or cheaper; see Alter [4]. Efficiency for a DSS would focus on the speed of decisions or the cost of the decision-making process.

In contrast, effectiveness in DSS asks whether we are making better decisions. Thus, effectiveness is concerned with using the correct information or procedures in arriving at a decision, and whether the decisions are beneficial to the organization.

## Complicating features in DSS Evaluation

It is important to note that a more effective decision does not imply that it is more efficient. In fact, in some instances a more effective choice may be less efficient in the short or the long run, since the goal may be more quality oriented than profit oriented (i.e., higher reliability but reduced throughput). Other aspects of system effectiveness include using correct information in decision making, involving the appropriate individuals and groups, following the expedient political trajectory, and incorporating individual manager's decision making style. Each of these involve variables that are difficult to identify and quantify. Consequently, an integrated and comprehensive DSS evaluation methodology must address both effectiveness and efficiency.

Another complicating factor is the emphasis on judgmental or nonprogrammed tasks [24]. These tasks lack structure and are difficult to automate using conventional Operations Research/

Management Science methods. Managing investment portfolios, long range decisions on product development, and marketing strategies are examples of “semi-structured” tasks. With these, it is difficult to verify success because the goal is unknown or immeasurable. If the focus of DSS are better decisions, a criteria must be established: only then can an evaluation of effectiveness be accomplished. Clearly a formal methodology must be capable of defining and then evaluating these decisions [2].

A third complicating feature is that these systems evolve and change over time. The systems change and evolve because the organization changes. Sometimes, the organization changes because of by the implementation of the DSS (or a modification of it). As a result, the new system exerts force on the organization. Clearly, evaluation methods for these systems must be able to function in a changing environment. Another implication is that the only way to determine whether goals are being met, decision quality is improving, or appropriate procedures are being reinforced is to examine the system in its environment.

## Recent Developments in DSS

Two recent developments make the evaluation of effectiveness even more difficult $[14]$ . Firstly, group decision support systems necessitate consideration of issues like quality interaction, stimulation and suppression of individual creativity in a group context, and transferal of information from one individual to another in a group. These are really social variables that bear directly on the quality of the decisions and thus on overall system effectiveness. Here, no distinction is being made between individual and group DSS.

Secondly, there is a growing trend toward adapting the system to the manager's thinking style [8]. Evaluating how well a system supports individual decision making style involves identifying and measuring cognitive processing skills. This involves: problem solving style, information needs and preferences, subjective impressions of system support, and the quality of the final decision. Consequently, we examine a social science methodology (Evaluation Research) which was specifically developed to address such problems.

## Evaluation Research (ER)

This is a methodology designed specifically to evaluate the effectiveness of government, social, and education programs, [12,23]. These have been used to evaluate the effectiveness of health care delivery systems [1,9], educational programs [6], information systems in human service organizations [26] and managerial effectiveness [25]. The following is a brief introduction to demonstrate the applicability of ER in evaluating DSS.

## Applicability of Evaluation Research to DSS

There are several features that make ER useful for DSS [21]. First, most of the methods of evaluation are designed to assess some aspect of program effectiveness [27]. An emphasis on effectiveness makes ER a potentially valuable tool for evaluation of DSS. Second, most programs evaluated using ER methods deal with fairly unstructured problems. As such, many of the decision criteria (e.g., better training and better health care) are difficult to identify and quantify. Likewise, the DSS components (e.g., flexibility of interface, user friendliness, and modeling format) are difficult if not impossible to manipulate so that causal inferences may be made. Again, these difficulties are similar to those encountered in evaluating DSS.

Third, like the evaluation of DSS, ER takes place in the same operational environment as that in which the DSS are operating and being administered. Arriving at meaningful conclusions in such environments is difficult with traditional experimental methods that are used primarily in laboratory settings. ER methods have been specifically developed to make causal inferences possible and conclusions meaningful in such operational environments.

Finally, the programs evaluated by ER methods are, by their very nature, growing and evolving entities, making it necessary to evaluate effectiveness under conditions of change and growth $[20,19]$ . This implies that evaluation had to occur over a span of time; that requires methods capable of addressing both formative (in process) and summative (concluding) concerns. As a result of a legislative mandate to evaluate the effectiveness of social programs and the unique nature of the problems addressed, rigorous evaluation methods have been development.

## Evaluation Research Orientation

Perhaps the key feature of ER is the orientation it brings to the operational environment. ER views social programs as “social experiments” [22]. Using this approach implies that the DSS and surrounding environment are considered an ongoing experiment. The ER methodology defines not only the methods and procedures used to administer a program (DSS), but also extends to the evaluation of the stated goals of the program itself. As such, ER techniques provide a means of transforming the evaluation process of DSS effectiveness from a subjective, judged (perceived) basis to a more scientific rigorous (quantifiable) based discipline. This transformation further allows effectiveness criteria to be viewed in terms of the realistic value they provide to the information organization or system.

From the perspective that programs are social experiments, several important methodological considerations emerge; most important is formative and summative evaluation [15,7]. Formative evaluation is essentially feedback at specified intervals during a program's (DSS) implementation; it is used to guide development of the life cycle. Formative evaluation uses data on past effectiveness to indicate necessary changes to improve future effectiveness. In any given program there may be several formative evaluation cycles.

Summative evaluation normally occurs at the end of funding cycles and determines whether the goals and mission of the program have been achieved. Summative evaluation asks whether the entire approach of a particular program had any influence on the dependent variables of interest (i.e., were better services provided?).

Evaluation research methods are applied research projects in process [11]. This means that there is an emphasis and commitment to the collection of valid and reliable historical data. The data must be collected in such a way that meaningful causal inferences can be made. To accomplish this, both experimental and quasi-experimental methods are used. Other methods, such as goal attainment scaling and management by objectives, are incorporated into the evaluation research approach [13].

## ER Evaluation Types

There are common types of evaluations which use evaluation research methods $[23]$ . First is the evaluation of need. This type of evaluation seeks to determine the needs of a community or organization. Once the needs are determined, an intervention program can be designed. It is interesting that a needs assessment in evaluation research and in information systems proceeds in a similar manner.

A second type is the evaluation of process. Process evaluations seek to determine who is using a program (DSS), whether the target population is being reached, and how well the designed program has been implemented. The application of this type of evaluation to DSS is obvious. Determining whether or not a system has been implemented in accordance with its design is an important aspect of systems implementation.

A third type is evaluation of outcome. This is perhaps most important for a social or educational program, since it addresses effective intervention in an area of concern. Again, its application to DSS is clear. How well a system assists managers in making decisions is key to evaluating system effectiveness.

The final type deals with efficiency. Does the program achieve results at reasonable cost? Is there another way of achieving results that is more cost effective? This is important: an evaluation approach which is suitable for evaluating DSS effectiveness can also address issues of efficiency.

In summary, the ER methodology can address the needs, process, outcomes, and fiscal efficiency of DSS.

## Conducting an Evaluation of a DSS

To illustrate the usefulness of Evaluation Research methods in DSS, it is useful to examine how a typical evaluation is conducted. An outcome evaluation is examined because it tends to be a primary concern for DSS and it was the type of evaluation conducted for the Neveda hazardous waste monitoring system. It should be noted, however, that the methodology would apply equally to other types of evaluation. There are nine steps or phases in such an evaluation:

1. Identify What to Evaluate

2. Establish Evaluation Criteria

3. Organize for Evaluation

4. Collect Performance Data

5. Maintain Historical Data

6. Analyze Performance Data

7. Formulate Conclusions and Recommendations

8. Take Corrective Action

9. Evaluate Evaluation Process

Each is now discussed.

## Identify What to Evaluate

The areas covered in this activity include: financial, operational, and human factors considerations.

Financial Evaluation. This is a classic part, and probably the most often used method for evaluating a information system. It is usually called a “cost/benefit” analysis, an attempt to determine the savings and other benefits that results from the implementation of the information system. For the Nevada hazardous waste system, the benefits were easily documented: if a state fails to meet Federal guidelines for notification, inspection, and compliance monitoring, Federal funds are terminated. Recently, one state lost \$50,000 for failing to meet Federal EPA standards.

Operational Evaluation asks whether the information system is meeting the needs of the business or solving business problems. The main question is whether the information system will work from a people processing-rather than a computer processing point of view. In hazardous waste management, new Federal legislation (the Small Quantity Generator Act) have increased the number of firms regulated by a factor of 10 to 50. In Nevada, the increase from 48 to over 2500 regulated firms and businesses demanded a computer based system for processing notification, inspection, and compliance monitoring.

Human Factor Evaluation. Computer information systems are by their very nature, vehicles of change. People are resistant to change. Thus, there is always the potential for conflict during the implementation of a computer information system. For this reason, an assessment should be made to determine the reactions of people to the new system to find if it is enhancing, impeding, or obstructing its intended purpose. This was accomplished for the hazardous waste system by interviewing the personnel. The results of the study indicated that both management and field staff saw a need for change from a manual to a computer based system.

## Establishing Evaluation Criteria

Since ER involves rigorous methods, it proceeds much like any other empirical investigation. The first step is to identify the important criteria (the independent and dependent variables). The primary research question is whether the new system actually produces better decisions (e.g., quality, accuracy, and reliability of decisions.) Thus, the independent variable is the implemented of a DSS.

The dependent variable(s) in ER would include measures of outcome goals, since the primary interest is the quality of decisions being made. Consequently, the only way to determine whether a DSS is producing the desired outcomes is to identify explicitly the outcome goals of the system. The dependent variables used in the evaluation of the Nevada hazardous waste system included: user perceptions, accuracy of records, quality of inspection program, and success of compliance schedules. The operational definitions of each variable produced dozens of quantified variables for data analysis.

There are two desirable results derived from setting goals, apart from their value as dependent variables in the evaluation. Setting goals forces the users and system builders to be realistic about their goals and expectations. It also forces them to think through them as part of the DSS design process. The goal setting phase would include:

1. Identifying the decisions to be supported.

2. Identifying the decision makers to be supported.

3. Outlining the deficiencies in the present decision making.

4. Stating the desired improvements in decision outcomes.

5. Devising a scaling system for the outcome goals.

For example, one decision support goal of the Nevada hazardous waste system was determining which firms to inspect. The decision model used includes such factors as past compliance record, type of waste, type of notifier (e.g., generator, transporter, or disposer), and location. The decision makers supported were field inspectors and office staff. The primary deficiency in the present system was the absence of an on-line database.

More accurate and complete records, better inspection schedules, most effective compliance monitoring, and long range planning capabilities were the decision support goals. The scaling system emerged from the operational definitions of these concerns and is too extensive and technical to address here.

After identifying the relevant problem criteria, the appropriate ER method must also be selected. There are three different research methods that can be used in ER: non-experimental, quasi-experimental, and experimental. There are advantages and disadvantages to each. The obvious disadvantage of non-experimental methods is the difficulty in reaching any causal conclusions. Consequently, non-experimental methods are essentially descriptive. However, descriptive data are not useless. Potentially valuable information can be derived from descriptive data, such as measures of change. However, attributing an observed change to a particular intervention variable (such as a DSS) would be inappropriate using only non-experimental methods.

The most common non-experimental design is a single group, pretest/post-test design. An example of this design would occur when a department within an organization is functioning as the group in which the DSS is implemented. Measurement of specific criteria (ratings of decision quality, measures of user satisfaction, and qualification of decision outcomes) are taken before and after the installation of a DSS. A simple paired t-test can then be used to determine whether a reliable change in these variables occurred after implementation of the system. (Hotelling's $T^{2}$ would be used if a multivariate design were used.) Although this is useful information, it is difficult to determine whether the change was due solely to the DSS or merely as a product of natural change over time, since no control group was used.

One way of isolating changes in the dependent variables due directly to the intervention of the DSS is to obtain measures of change in these variables independent of the system. This approach is, typically, not feasible due to the iterative nature of the development process, but it could be of use in a situation where there is a master-plan for DSS development, though this currently is quite rare. Given this constraint, however, one approach would to be observe and measure the change in these variables over time, prior to the installation of the DSS. Such a test would require planning far in advance of the actual of the DSS, but if this could be accomplished a single group design would allow some causal inferences to be made about the effectiveness of the DSS.

Because of the difficulty in making such causal inferences, more experimentally rigorous methods should also be used whenever possible. True experimental methods, in which subjects are randomly assigned to experimental and control groups, are almost impossible to use in operational environments, since user groups are fixed and in place. Attempting random assignment to groups would be very disruptive and unacceptable in most organizations.

Quasi-experimental methods $[11]$ provide a useful alternative to experimental methods, since they can be used in operational environments to make causal inferences. There are two primary quasi-experimental designs useful in ER: time series and non-equivalent control group designs. A time series design would require an outcome variable to be measured at intervals before and after implementation of the DSS. The criteria could be ratings of decision quality, attitudes of decision makers, amount of information used in the decision, etc. These variables would be measured using the same scales or techniques at each interval. The data would be analyzed using interrupted time series methods.

Essentially, if the DSS is affecting the outcome variables, reliable changes in the time series would be observed between the pre- and post-implementation data. A beneficial feature of this design is that cyclic or other effects (such as seasonal variations) can be removed from the time series using conventional techniques.

Time series techniques are being used in the evaluation of the Nevada hazardous waste system. Since statistics on past notification accuracy, inspection quality, and compliance effectiveness are on record, the impact of the DSS could be determined as statistics are collected after implementation. At present, too little time as passed since system implementation to arrive at specific conclusions from the time-series data.

Non-equivalent control group designs are based on the use of a second decision making group as a control. For example, an experimental group of managers would use the DSS in making decisions while the control group would not. The difficulty with this design is an inability to determine whether differences between the groups are due to the independent variable (implementation of a DSS) or inherent differences between the groups. Fortunately there are relatively simple techniques for dealing with nonequivalent groups; specifically, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).

ANCOVA proceeds by identifying and measuring important variables (covariates) in which the two groups may differ. Usually the co-variate used are ones that may have an impact on the dependent variables (i.e., decision outcomes) apart from the independent variable (the DSS). Possible covariates associated with a DSS include: experience of the managers, education level, or years with the firm. Ideally, the two groups should be as nearly identical on these variables as possible. However, since we cannot generally manipulate the individual characteristics of a group, we must control these differences statistically. Once the co-variates are identified and measured, their effect on the decision outcomes is removed, using a regression technique. Then, the differences between the group using the DSS and the control group will reflect the true effective impact of the DSS.

## Organizing for Evaluation

An effective evaluation mechanism is usually applied on a project basis. The projects tend to be ad hoc in nature; each study has a single purpose or goal. As such, it is essential to consider the following aspects in coordinating an evaluation:

(i) An overall plan needs to be developed before starting the evaluation. This should identify all the resources required. These include: people, facilities, and equipment. In addition, a schedule for the project should be prepared, including specification of the deliverables.

(ii) All the required resources need to be made available and structured so that the project can be directed according to the prepared schedule.

(iii) Coordination of the project ensures that all involved parties are aware of, and receptive to, the project's efforts, goals, and recommendations. Control of the project is maintained by ensuring that the measurements collected match those of the plan.

As outside contractors, it was difficult to maintain organizational control for the evaluation of the Nevada hazardous waste system. Ideally, the primary concern for evaluation should come from within the organization. This would insure that data are collected and analyzed.

## Collecting Performance Data

The key to data collection in ER is that it is build into the operation of the system. For some dependent variables, such as the amount of system use, and specific DSS tools used by particular managers; data collection can be handled by the system itself. For other variables, like manager's attitudes, goal achievement or decision process the data collection must be handled by rigorous procedures. As a consequences of dealing with a computer system, these procedures can be built into a DSS so that an end user will have to fill out a form on the screen before beginning or ending a session. These data can then be collected by the DSS for later analysis.

With the Nevada hazardous waste system, several forms were designed which appeared on the computer screen. Many of these forms are mandated by the Federal EPA as part of the process of hazardous waste management. However, since many of the dependent variables for evaluating system effectiveness are entries in these forms, data collection was automated considerably.

An important precaution in data collection is to apply the process consistency. Each end user must respond to the dependent variables at the specified time. Since the actual operational system is being used in the experiment, data must be collected in the process of using the system. Fortunately, with a computer based system, adequate safeguards can be built into the system to insure the users provide the appropriate information at the specified times.

## Maintaining Historical Data

It is necessary to establish and maintain the appropriate reference groups and population distributions, as well as maintain required sample distributions in order to make reliable and accurate comparisons. This stage requires personnel who have the following qualifications: training in the appropriate statistical and methodological techniques; having a knowledge of computer technology and human factors; and being knowledgeable of overall organizational goals.

## Analyze Performance Data

Once adequate data are collected (normally after several months), the data can be analyzed. The methods used are straightforward statistical analyses, such as time series analysis, regression discontinuity, analysis of variance, analysis of covariance, multivariate analysis of variance and covariance.

The one caution is in the quality of the data. Poor data cannot be overcome by good data analysis. Even statistical control procedures, like analysis of covariance, cannot succeed if variables are poorly defined and measured or the research design is ill-conceived. Therefore, the data analysis stage cannot be viewed as an arena where all the previous mistakes can be remedied.

## Developing Conclusions and Recommendations

Once the data have been analyzed, conclusions on the effectiveness of the system can be made. With multiple dependent variables, the specific areas of system effectiveness can be identified, as well as the effectiveness of the system as a whole. Areas of effectiveness are indicated when the group of decision makers using the DSS score higher on the outcome goals than the control group making similar decisions without it or the time-series shows a significant change in slope or intercept after implementation of the DSS. Naturally, there may be more detailed conclusions that involve level of system usage, attitudes of the managers, or changes in the decision process due to the DSS. In general, the conclusions are directly related to the system goals specified in the first stage.

There may be several cycles of data collection, analysis, and conclusions depending whether formative or summative evaluation is the goal. Usually several cycles of formative evaluation will be used followed by a summative evaluation which is used for a major systems modification.

Several cycles of formative evaluation have occurred for the Nevada hazardous waste system. These evaluations have resulted in modifications of the user interface, adjustments in the database design, addition of a data screening sub-system, and alternation of the menu structure.

The timing of the evaluation should be well planned and based on the specified goals. It should not occur too early, e.g., when users and developers are still pondering the prototype, but it should not be too late after a large number of DSS modules have been developed. Ahituv and Getz [2], suggest that the optimal timing for formative evaluation is when a specified module reaches a “steady state” in terms of its acceptance by the user and a subsequent problem is under investigation. At this stage, the portions of the system that resulted in more effective decision making will be incorporated into a modified system while the ineffective parts of the old system will be discarded or modified. The formative evaluation of a DSS module should become an integral component in the development of subsequent modules. Formative evaluation would primarily include steps 3 thru 8 in fig. 1.

The optimal timing for a summative evaluation is after a large number of DSS modules have been in use or collected into an overall DSS tool. At this time, the impact of the DSS on the organization and its goal may be assessed. This form of evaluation is normally an attempt to assess the systems benefits in monetary terms (e.g., saving costs, increase in profits). Summative evaluation would correspond to steps 1 thru 9. Summative evaluation of the Nevada hazardous waste system has not yet been completed.

## Taking Corrective Action

Once conclusions have been reached, they are used to either modify the DSS or make a decision about its overall effectiveness. In ER, an evaluation report is prepared from the data, analysis, and conclusions of the evaluation. It is important to make sure the report reaches the individuals within the organization who have the authority to approve system modifications.

## Evaluating the Evaluation Process

Finally, the evaluation process should itself be reviewed. The review should address the quality issues of: identification of omissions and necessary improvements; judging the correctness, readability, and understanding of the process and the product; and adherence to standards and methodologies. This suggests that a constructive critique of the process and product will be the output of the review. The critique should also focus on the validation of the process and product as it pertains to current or future business operations.

The most important conclusion of the review of the evaluation of the Nevada hazardous waste system was that collection of data must be the responsibility of individuals who are committed to the evaluation process. Otherwise, the best research strategy and data analysis is of little use.

## Conclusions

Evaluation research is a collection of research methods bound within a philosophy of applied experimentation. As applied to DSS, ER provides an integrated and rigorous means of evaluating both system effectiveness and efficiency. The benefits are clear. First, the extensive ER literature provides the necessary theoretical and practical guidelines for applying these methods to a variety of systems and settings. Second, this approach is uniquely suited to evaluating operational environments and is thus a valuable tool in the refinement of a DSS. Third, this methodology provides reliable and valid data. Consequently, ER offers a means of moving from a perceived (subjective) based approach to more realistic (objective) based approach to evaluation of DSS.

To conclude, the purpose of this paper was the shed some light on the recent developments causing problems for traditional DSS evaluation procedures and to suggest a social science approach that will fit into the general concepts of future DSS design and evaluation. This approach is not proposed as a total replacement for current practices, but as a guide for the further elaboration necessary to develop the practical aspects of DSS evaluation.
