# Automatic Information Processing Activities and Operational Decision Making: A Case Study of Consequence

Rene van der Vlist

State University of Leiden, 2312 KM Leiden, The Netherlands

The introduction of scanning facilities in the supermarkets of a dutch supermarket chain had effect on the quality of the jobs as experienced by supermarket employees. Job satisfaction decreased also. When all, or a sufficient number of supermarkets of the chain are equipped with scanning facilities the question arises how it will affect operational decision making at the level of the single supermarket. Top executives hesitate about the amount of centralization/decentralization of operational decision making. In order to reach a sound decision some considerations should be kept in mind: (1) Scanning information is information about past behavioral acts. Extrapolations should be treated with much care. (2) Scanning information is information disconnected from the situation in which the information came into being. (3) When operational decision making would be based on scanning information, employees might lose interest.

Keywords: Operational decision making, Scanning information, Barcode.

## Introduction

Automation projects in practice often appear to be organizational change projects as well. That is to say: In order to exploit the possibilities automation offers the organisation tends to be adapted. When this is reconsidered in advance, and advantages and disadvantages are carefully weighted and especially when these have been the focus of consultation between employer and employees, one can not object to this. However in practice we see that the possible consequences of automation often are not well-considered and that workprocesses and contents of tasks and jobs can change thoroughly and unexpectedly.

Where automation and information processing activities can have direct consequences on the level of individual tasks and jobs it is also possible that the way the information is used has an indirect but powerful influence on jobcontent as experienced.

In this article a case study will be presented illustrating this double effect. First we shall report the results of research concerning the consequences of the introduction of scanning equipment in a Dutch supermarket chain on the (experienced) content of jobs. Next we shall try to picture possible long-term effects of scanning as the basis of an information system depending on the way the system will be used. It will be made plausible that a decentralised way of operational decisionmaking is superior to centralised operational decisionmaking when it comes to the effectiveness of the organisation as well as the quality of jobs and motivation employees.

## 1. Scanning and its Advantages

## 1.1. Equipment

Doing your shopping in a supermarket you will notice on most articles a so-called “barcode”. This code can be read by scanning equipment. With the aid of scanning equipment the barcode is automatically translated in a numbercode indicating the kind of product, its manufacturer and its country of origin. When the scanning equipment is linked with a computer the computer can be programmed so that each identified article is matched with its due price. Supermarket computers may be linked with the headquarters of the supermarket chain they belong to.

When the supermarket computer is linked with the pay-cash-deck (in which case the printer is attached to the cash-register) the cashier is able to hand out a cash-slip indicating each individual product bought as well as its price.

## 1.2. Hard and soft savings

Scanning has its advantages. It enables the supermarket to keep precise track of its stock for each sort of article. It enables the supermarket to get a clear picture of the number of customers during the day and the week and the amount of money spent per customer. It enables the supermarket to estimate the need for cashiers during the day and the week. It makes it much earlier to make up cash-accounts and so on.

This implies that scanning can lead to savings. Savings are of two kinds: ‘hard’ and ‘soft’ savings. Hard savings are savings that follow directly from the investment. Soft savings do not follow directly or automatically from the investment but can be realised by making use of the information the equipment provides.

Hard savings of scanning equipment are for instance:

An increase of productivity of cashiers (each customer needing less time than in the traditional system) to be estimated at 10 to 30%

\- It is easier and faster to make recapitulations of cash receipts.

\- There is no further need to price each individual article, instead a whole shelf can be priced.
- It leads to less “leakage” at cash points as a consequence of “misrings”, “underrings” and “sweethearting”. This leakage can normally be estimated as about one percent of total sales but will diminish drastically when scanning is introduced.

## Soft savings are:

\- Less training time is needed for new cashiers.

\- As it is very easy to keep track of the stock of each article there is no need to have large bufferamounts. This implies that it must be possible to use the available space more economically and to display more articles.

\- This may lead to an increase in total sales.

\- It is easier to analyse the effects of commercial campaigns.

\- The number of personnel needed during the day and the week can be estimated more correctly.

\- Articles that tend to deteriorate when time passes can be kept under control.

\- It must be possible to improve management procedures and the quality of decisions.

Now that most articles do have barcodes it becomes attractive (economically) to introduce scanning on a large scale. This is more so as – based on hard savings only – it is estimated that the investment needed to equip supermarkets with scanning technology will be regained within two to three years.

## 2. Scanning and its experienced effects on the quality of work

## 2.1. The design of the study

During the year 1983/1984 one of the biggest supermarket chains in the Netherlands decided to introduce scanning in its supermarkets. To begin with 25 supermarkets were chosen in order to gain some experience and to learn how to deal with the introduction and the disturbances it may cause.

For the department of social and organisational psychology of Leiden University this indeed was a unique opportunity to study the effects of the introduction of scanning on the quality of work as experienced by supermarket personnel (see Adema c.s. (1984), Van der Vlist (1986)) By that time ten supermarkets used scanning.

Preliminary research was done in one supermarket that had already some years of experience with scanning and five supermarkets operating in a traditional way. This preliminary phase was needed in order to test research instruments and to do thorough observations of all the activities of supermarket personnel, their contacts with customers etc.

Five out of the other nine supermarkets equipped with scanning were equipped with this new technology less than two months ago. Due to too many disturbances during the introduction period of this new technology (with presumed effects on satisfaction of customers as well as employees) these five supermarkets were left out of the research. The four remaining scanning supermarkets were matched with four supermarkets operating on a traditional basis.

So the research-design was of a comparative nature in which the quality of work as experienced by supermarket personnel was compared between scanning- and non-scanning supermarkets.

Comparisons were made for job-content, work-relations, working-conditions and job-satisfaction. Terms of employment were not compared. In the long run the introduction of scanning may have effects on terms of employment. These, however, were not foreseen within the short time period that had elapsed in the case studied.

Altogether 103 interviews were held with supermarket employees, 51 in scanning supermarkets and 52 in traditional supermarkets. In fact all the employees were interviewed, including the supermarket managers.

In order to study possible effects on (experienced) job-content we used an instrument designed by Algeria (1980). This instrument is designed to measure 24 job characteristics as experienced by the employees. The instrument is based on Hackman and Oldham's "Job Characteristics Model" (1976). In Fig. 1 and example is given showing how the first characteristic is measured.

Each job characteristic contains anchors as in the example given in Fig. 1. The anchors are added in order to reach intersubjective consensus with respect to the meaning of the concept measured.

Possible changes in experienced work-relations were measured with a structured interview with questions on formal and informal consultation, involvement in activities of importance to the operational management of the supermarket, the way the execution of tasks is controlled, the number of contacts with colleagues, type of leadership etc.

## 2.2. Results

Job-content. Head-cashiers $(n=7)$ were the only group experiencing extra difficulties in their jobs that tended to be more complex. Cashiers $(n=33)$ experienced statistically significant changes in seven out of 24 job-characteristics:

\- less changes in work-pace,

\- less discretion with respect the sequence of actions that have to be performed,

\- more tied to the work-place (pay-cash-deck),

\- less people (colleagues) to get in touch with during the performance of the job,

\- a more important task in the whole of the operations of the supermarket,

\- it is more distinct how the job has to be done.

From this overview it is evident that cashiers of scanning supermarkets experience more routine, that there is less variation in work-content, less discretion in the way tasks are executed etc.

This tendency was found in all the other groups of employees distinguished with the exception of head-cashiers (as already mentioned) and supermarket managers. Supermarket managers did not experience statistically significant changes in jobcontent.

Work-relations. In Point of Sale supermarkets (supermarkets equipped with scanning) less exchange of information takes place between higher-status employees and lower-status employees. Also, in P.O.S. supermarkets the employees are less satisfied with formal and informal consultation.

As far as leadership is concerned: In P.O.S. supermarkets leadership tends to be less task-oriented as well as social-oriented than in traditional supermarkets. It is as if the situation needs less leadership. This holds on each hierarchical level. Though significant, none of these changes were dramatic.

Working-conditions. Comparative differences in working conditions could not be found with the exception of cashiers. The cashiers in P.O.S.-supermarkets complained of the ergonomic characteristics of the pay-cash-deck, the perceived social control due to the high-pitched tone each time an article is scanned, being tied to the work-place etc. These complaints appeared to be connected with a substantial drop in job-satisfaction.

Job-satisfaction. Job-satisfaction is significantly lower for the whole group working in scanning supermarkets (n = 51) compared to the group working in traditional supermarkets. It is also significantly lower for certain subgroups as first grade salesmen and cashiers. Supermarket managers are the only group experiencing no drop in job-satisfaction.

## 3. Effects on Operational Management

Though the introduction of scanning appears to have some effects on the experienced quality of work, these effects are as yet not very dramatic.

As the supermarkets changed to scanning only recently, some of these effects may be due to resistance to change in general that may gradually level off. In any case the economic advantages of scanning are so dominant that we may expect that scanning will be introduced within a few years in all the supermarkets (some 500) of the chain. (In fact, writing this article now, we already know that it has only been because of technical set-backs that this has not been realised by now. At the moment some 60 supermarkets of the chain apply scanning.)

This development gives rise to the question what will happen when in the future all, or a sufficient number of supermarkets apply scanning.

We tried to get information with respect to the way scanning will be used in the future by interviewing top-executives of the chain (some 10 open interviews). What we were especially interested in is what policy the chain will follow with respect to information analysis and operational decision-making on supermarket-level. To be precise, one may consider the possibility that major decision making on the operational level of each supermarket could best be taken over by headquarters. What we have in mind is decisions with respect to provisioning (when, what, what amount), advertising campaigns, pricing of articles, but also number of employees needed (also: when, with what kind of tasks etc.).

The other extreme would be to leave all decision making to the supermarket itself (which would imply to make less use of the possibilities of aggregated information). Between both extremes many possibilities exist and are feasible.

It become clear that top-management had not reached a decision yet. Some top-executives were convinced that centralized decision making would be superior to decision making on a local level. Their main point being that the analysis of large quantities of data is complicated and that supermarket managers are not able to handle this amount of information efficiently and correctly. At the same time they doubted that these managers would be able to learn this. Other top-executives were less outspoken and preferred to postpone the decision. As far as we know a final decision has not been reached yet.

In order to reach a sound final decision we think that some considerations should be kept in mind:

1. Scanning information is information about past behavioral acts. The information of data gathered with the aid of scanning devices concerns only the past. Analysis of this information will show regularities as these developed in the past. Extrapolations should be treated with very much care (as the emperor Frederic II noticed already in 1245: the straight line is as an opiate for thought). Extrapolations can not deal with changes in the environment which have effects on buying behavior. These environmental influences may affect buying behavior in individual supermarkets to a large degree without having such an effect on the overall figures based on all the supermarkets taken together. Environmental changes which will probably have effects on buying behavior in an individual supermarket may be known in the supermarket itself as a consequence of the fact that supermarket personnel is itself part of this environment and because the employees do have frequent contacts with the customers and the environment of the supermarket. When this knowledge can not have any influence on the operational decisions made for the supermarket this will certainly lead to less effective decisions as well as to frustrated employees.

2. Scanning information is information disconnected from the situation in which the information came into being. Information bearers can be distinguished in signals and symbols. Symbols are used by people and function in normal communication processes. Signals however do function in (goal-oriented) processes in biochemical organisms and in “machines”. Artificial information processing is limited to the processing of signals [Galjaard (1979), Nauta (1972)] which implies:

\- The use of “language” of which information bearers have a fixed and unvariable “meaning”, while transformational and operational rules also are fixed. Communication between people uses human language of which the information bearers are symbols (words and sentences).

\- The meaning of words is not tied up once and for all, but goes through a certain development, not to be arbitrarily determined beforehand. The rules of language (use) are de facto rules in stead of de jure rules [Nauta (1972)].

Artificial information processing of sales data (= scanning information) in supermarkets implies the transformation of symbol-loaded communication and behavior into (fixed) signals: Customers either buy a product or they do not buy it. Only the endresult counts and the choice behavior of customers, preceding the choice, is left out: the endresult is disconnected from the situation and the deliberations in which the behavior took place. No computer system can absorb the positive or negative attitude with which a customer decides to buy. People (employees), however, may be sensitive to these extra-behavioral aspects.

The information that becomes available in headquarters is extensive and valuable. It concerns information with respect to sales of each kind of article in each individual supermarket per time unit. It concerns amounts of money spent per customer. There is information with respect to amounts of money spent during the day and the week etc. There is information with respect to the effects of sales-campaigns, the effects of price-increases and reductions etc. But the information is disconnected from the situation in which it materialised, which makes it difficult to develop “good theories” when unexpected changes occur:

When the sale of a particular product drops more or less suddenly then it is important to understand why. When the drop in sales happens in all the supermarkets theories are as a rule not too difficult to formulate. When this happens only in one supermarket it is almost impossible to develop a “good theory” at the level of headquarters. “Good theories” can only be developed at the local supermarket itself. They are then based on communication and observation of the behavior of customers. And that is exactly what happens in each local supermarket. Communication may be based on complaints, customer-panels, observation of customers as well as casual communication and short interviews. In fact all supermarkets we observed used some form of communication with clients and the immediate surroundings of the supermarket in order to increase the quality of operational decisions.

3. When operational decision making at the level of the individual supermarket is based on scanning information employees may lose interest. Super-markets have certain characteristics of “front-line”-organizations [Smith (1979)]. Front-lineorganizations according to Smith, are typified by certain characteristics:

\- The implementation of the policy of the organization is to some extent carried out by workers in direct contact with clients. This certainly is the case in supermarkets where employees have frequent contact with customers.

\- These employees are relatively highly skilled or do have specialised knowledge and skills. This is also the case where it concerns employees in supermarkets.

\- Employees have ‘discretionary’ power. They have to some extent in supermarkets.

\- Employees are in the possession of accurate information which is or can be very relevant to the organisation. This is also the case in supermarkets where employees do have knowledge about reactions of customers.

Front-line-organizations do need the motivation of employees which implies that employees understand and accept concern-policies. When there is no direct relationship between the communication/observed behavior of clients and the 'behavior' of the supermarket (operations) – which is obviously the case when operational management is transferred to headquarters – then highly motivated employees gradually tend to get frustrated as their suggestions how to deal with clients cannot be followed through. As a consequence employees tend to lose interest in the operations of the supermarket as well as in clients/customers. The organization as a whole then gradually develops 'machine'-like characteristics and becomes a centralized bureaucracy.

At the same time we may expect that the type of leadership of local supermarket managers will change. What we have seen is that local managers not only use task-leadership as well as social-emotional leadership but that in every local supermarket there is some evidence that supermarket managers are very much interested in the attitudes of customers. They are as if it were “future-oriented”, trying to find out what takes place in the surroundings of the supermarket and possible future client-behavior. They do instruct their personnel likewise. We might call this kind of leader behavior future-effectiveness leadership [van der Vlist (1970)]. It encompasses the planning and other managerial activities of leadership. When operational decision making is completely transferred to headquarters the basis of this kind of leadership behavior vanishes almost completely.

From these considerations it follows that a complete centralization of operational decision-making has its disadvantages: it treats the future as if it were a simple extrapolation of the past (and by doing so it denies the possible influence of outside factors), part of a closed system; it denies the multiple faceted background of overt behavior of customers by only taking into account the end result of deliberations; and it denies the fact that only well-motivated employees are inclined to deliver service. A complete centralization of operational decision making will lead to sub-optimal functioning.

Does this imply that all operational decision making should be left to the local supermarket itself? That would be jumping to conclusions. It is for instance quite obvious that advertising campaigns can best be analyzed on the basis of large sets of data; the same holds for price-elasticity research of certain articles etc. In other words the best solution will be a mix of centralization and decentralization and needs probably some experimentation.

## 4. Discussion

In chapter three we reasoned that a complete centralization of operational decision making is probably not a very good strategy and that the best solution probably is some mixture of centralization and decentralization. Does this imply that such a mixture has to be chosen? That would be too extreme a point of view. Certainly a completely centralized, ‘bureaucratized’ chain of supermarkets can function. However, it will be less effective, less service-oriented and an awkward place to work in. In due time it will attract employees that fit into the system as well as customers. It will have a completely different culture.
