# Effect of End-User Computing on Job Satisfaction: An Exploratory Study

Jawaid Abdul Ghani \*

Department of Business Information Systems, Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, MI 49008, USA

Abdul Rahim Al-Meer \*

College of Industrial Management, Dhahran 31261, Saudi Arabia

The effect of the use of computers on job satisfaction was examined. In results from a questionnaire returned by 85 professionals, it was found that the use of personal computers had a positive effect on job satisfaction, particularly when the individual was working on high scope tasks, i.e. tasks with high variety, identity, autonomy, and feedback. Use of mainframe computers had no significant effect on job satisfaction.

Keywords: Job satisfaction, Task characteristics, End-user computing, Computer impacts.

## 1. Introduction

End-user computing (EUC) is the direct, hand-on use and development of Information Systems by the principal users of the systems' output or by their immediate support staff [26]. EUC is rapidly surpassing traditional indirect uses of the computer through the data processing staff [3]. In 1986 there were 28 million workstations (terminals and desk top computers) in the U.S.A., resulting in a ratio of one workstation for every two white collar workers [9]. It is estimated that by 1990 this ratio will have narrowed to 3:4, and that American businesses will have spent \$250 billion on the necessary hardware and software for these workstations [25]. Business periodicals predict that these developments “herald a huge surge in productivity for the 10 million managers in the U.S.” [6], and that Information Technology has “in one way or another, changed the jobs of … almost half of the U.S. workforce” [5].

Researchers have found, however, that the effects of computing are difficult to measure since they “typically emerge in a varied, subtle, and evolutionary manner” [8]. The effects of traditional transaction processing systems have been mixed. In a review of the literature, Attewell and Rule [2] found that in some situations automation has had a “deskilling” effect by “stripping relatively skilled jobs of their conceptual content”, while in others it has had an “upgrading” effect by “absorbing many of the manual aspects of information processing, and allowing humans to concentrate on conceptual and decision making tasks”. They also noted that “while the effects of new technology were not dramatic, where change was reported, computers were most often said to be enhancing job satisfaction”.

Research on EUC has focused on developing taxonomies describing different levels of user technical sophistication and on deriving implications for the management of EUC (see for example [21]). Increasingly, researchers are stressing the importance of studying the impact of EUC on individual job related behavior [17]. One effect is on efficiency, in that the computer allows the user to automate the intrinsically clerical tasks of the user's job [1,20]. Gerrity and Rockart, however, observe that the long-term value of EUC lies in the “accelerated learning on the part of the user about his or her job, and the discovery of innovative new approaches to tasks that actually can transform the nature of the job” [10]. Indeed, it is argued that EUC acts as a catalyst, encouraging fundamental changes in the way people view and understand their work (see for example [15,7]). In a study of 2500 end users, Danziger and Kraemer found that the context of use, the computer package used, and the personal characteristics of the user can significantly influence the kind of impact that computing has on a given person, but that the organizational environment has virtually no effect.

The advent of EUC can be considered as an incidence of work redesign, caused by the advent of new technology. The job characteristics model of work motivation $[13]$ suggests that work redesign affects the satisfaction, motivation, and productivity of individuals at work. The objective of this exploratory study is to measure the effect of end user computing on job satisfaction of the user, taking into consideration the task scope and demographics of the individual.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Setting and Subjects

A self-administered questionnaire was distributed to 200 professionals attending continuing education short courses at the King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals in Dhahran, Saudi Arabia during Spring 1987. Another 200 questionnaires were distributed to professionals in various organizations located in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia on a convenience sampling basis.

A total of 85 usable questionnaires were returned, giving a response rate of 21%. The final sample consisted of 35% expatriates (Western 7%, Asian 28%), and 65% Saudi nationals. Seventy nine percent of the respondents had undergraduate and 21% had graduate degrees; 53% had technical degrees. All respondents were male. The average age was 31.9 years (std dev = 6.8 years) and average work experience was 8 years (std dev = 6.8 years).

## 2.2. Measures

Job Satisfaction was measured by a sub-scale of the Job Descriptive Index (JDI) developed by Smith et. al. [23]. O'Connor, Peters and Gordon [18] found that the JDI was the most frequently employed measure of job satisfaction observed. Further, Johnson, Smith and Tucker [14] reviewed the related literature and concluded that the JDI is the most carefully constructed measure of job satisfaction.

Task Scope is defined as the extent to which a job has the characteristics of task variety, identity, autonomy, and feedback $[11]$ . The task characteristics were measured by the Job Characteristics Inventory (JCI) developed by Sims, Szilagyi, and Keller $[22]$ . Their initial validation work and findings by Pierce and Dunham $[19]$ suggest that the JCI is comparable to other perceptual measures of job characteristics in terms of validity and reliability $[12]$ . Task scope was calculated as the sum of the scores on the four task characteristics. The appendix contains examples of the actual items used to measure task scope and job satisfaction.

Demographic Variables included the age, number of years of work experience, level of education, and type of degree.

Computer Use was measured by asking respondents whether they directly used the computer in their professional work, and, if so, whether they used a personal (desktop) computer and/or a mainframe terminal. Responses were validated by asking the respondent to specify the name(s) of the software package(s) used, and also the number of hours he used the computer per week. The

## 2.3. Statistical Analysis

The correlation between the variables was measured by the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients. Stepwise regression was used to select the independent variables which significantly explained the variance in job satisfaction. Similar analyses were done on subgroups in order to identify any moderating effects of task scope.

## 3. Results

The means and standard deviations for job satisfaction were 36.45 and 8.61, and for task scope were 13.97 and 1.81, respectively. Table 1 shows the Pearson product-moment correlations among the variables for the total sample. The table shows that there is a significant correlation between job satisfaction and the use of a PC (p < .1), and with task scope (p < .001). Note that there is no significant correlation between job satisfaction and the use of a mainframe terminal (MF).

The results of a stepwise regression analysis, using job satisfaction as the dependent variable and all the others as independent variables confirmed the preceding results. Only two variables, namely task scope (F=14.24, p<.001) and PC (F=4.18, p<.05) entered the model at p<.15.

From Table 1 we can see that there is no correlation between the use of a PC and task scope. A t-test indicated that there was no significant difference in task scope between PC users and non-users (t value = 0.33, p = 0.74). In order to further investigate the effect of task scope, a sub-group analysis was conducted to determine any moderating effect of task scope on the relationship between job satisfaction and the use of a PC. The median task scope of the total sample (13.92) was used to classify the respondents into two groups according to their task scope score. A significant correlation was found between job satisfaction and the use of a PC for the high task scope individuals (p < .1), but not for individuals with a low task scope. A stepwise regression on each subgroup confirmed the preceding results.

## 4. Discussion

The objective of this study was to explore the effect of computer usage, together with task scope and user demographics, on individual job satisfaction. Table 1 shows that satisfaction is positively related with task scope, as predicted by the job characteristics model of work motivation, and confirmed by other researchers. The results indicate that there is a significant positive effect of using a PC on job satisfaction. This is consistent with the (largely anecdotal) findings of researchers in EUC. Direct end users report that the computer allows them to get more work accomplished, to enhance their understanding of their work, and that it provides them with a greater sense of accomplishment. It is interesting to note that while the PC had a significant positive effect on job satisfaction, using a mainframe (MF) had a slightly negative effect on satisfaction. This can be explained by the findings of other researchers. Lee found that users preferred using the PC over the MF due to greater accessibility, independence, self-development, ease of use, and a lower “intimidation factor”. Danziger and Kraemer found that software tailored to user needs (as is usually the case for PCs) was the key to higher satisfaction. Sprague and McNurlin argue that while a mainframe user is always “awarec of being connected to and under the control of a large computer” the PC user has “full control and authority over the machine”. Maybe most important is that, as noted by Quillard et al., PC users compared to MF users are typically using the technology by choice and are performing “pc-assisted” work rather than “computer-mediated” work.

It is particularly interesting to note that while PC use is not related to task scope, yet the latter does play an important role as a moderating variable in the effect of PC use on job satisfaction. This seems to indicate that the use of a PC does not directly affect the characteristics of the job itself. However, if an individual is already performing a job which is high in scope, then the use of a PC will significantly increase his satisfaction with the job. Such a high-scope job involves the use of a greater variety of skills, requires the completion of a whole and identifiable piece of work, provides the individual with direct feedback on his performance, and allows the individual greater autonomy in selecting the specific ways of carrying out his work. The introduction of a PC in such an environment allows the individual the opportunity of exploring new approaches to existing tasks, and of personal development, resulting in a feeling of greater self-accomplishment.

These results are consistent with those found by other researchers. Based on a study of end-users in eight organizations in five different countries, Bjorn-Andersen and his colleagues [3] found that the effects of computer use on task characteristics were mixed, and that task uncertainty was a key intervening variable. The study concluded that the computer has greater impact on individuals performing high uncertainty tasks. In high uncertainty tasks (which are similar in characteristics with high scope tasks), the computer was found to both enrich the job by allowing user to gain insights into the nature and complexity of tasks, and also to reduce stress by structuring parts of the job.

This research has explored one aspect of a much broader issue, the behavioral and organizational impacts of information technology. Needless to say, this is an important subject of study, due to the increasing pervasiveness of information technology and due to the breadth and variety of its effects. At the same time it is difficult to study, since as Mason comments “the mediating factor is the mind … in that people associated with the new technology change their mental sets, their cognitive behavior, their patterns of attention, learning and mental engagement”, and hence the impacts are “situational, not inherent in the technology”. The range of research opportunities in this area is vast, and one hopes that researchers, both in information systems and in organization behavior, will give it the attention it deserves.

## Appendix

The Job Characteristics Inventory measures task variety, identity, autonomy and feedback using 5, 4, 6, and 6 questionnaire items respectively. Each item is rated on a 5 point Likert scale from very little to very much. Example items are listed below. The complete instrument is contained in [22].

## Task Variety

How much variety is there in your job?

How similar are the tasks you perform in a typical work day? (reversed scored)

## Task Identity

How often do you see projects of job through to completion?

The opportunity to complete work I start.

## Task Autonomy

To what extent are you able to act independently of your supervisor in performing your job function?

The freedom to do pretty much what I want on my job.

## Task Feedback

To what extent do you find out how well you are doing on the job as you are working? The opportunity to find out how well I am doing in my job.

Job satisfaction was measured by 18 items. Respondents were to place a Y or N beside each item to indicate whether or not it described their job, or a ? if they could not decide. Examples of the items are listed below. The complete instrument is contained in [23].

Fascinating, Useful, Routine, Tiresome, Boring, Challenging, Creative, Frustrating.
