# Globalization of the Software Industry: Trends and Strategies \*

Chai Kim, Stu Westin and Nikhilesh Dholakia
College of Business Administration, The University of Rhode Island, Kingston, RI 02881, USA

The major trends and strategies associated with the incipient globalization process in the software industry are explored. Particular attention is given to the newly industrializing countries (NICs) vis-à-vis advanced countries (ACs). First, some trends that are especially significant from a global perspective are examined. Next, a preliminary analytical framework is presented to organize the rather limited existing knowledge about globalization of the software business. Following this, global software strategies from NIC and AC perspectives are discussed. Finally, some tentative conclusions and future research needs are identified.

Keywords: Globalization, Global networks, Newly industrializing countries, Software development, Software exports, Software imports, Software industry, Software maintenance, Software productivity, Software trade, Strategies for software productivity, Technology transfer.

## 1. Introduction

Although the United States continues to be the world's premier market and production center for computer software, several incipient and powerful developments are likely to globalize the software industry in the near future. Consider the following:

\- Singapore has launched a concerted national drive to become Asia's leading software production center and has managed to persuade many multinationals to locate software development centers within its borders [4,6,21].

\- Major American users, such as American Express, Citibank, and Texas Instruments, are contracting out software development jobs to Indian firms or affiliates [9,17].

\- Brazil has decreed that software cannot be imported into that country if functionally equivalent local software exists, thereby giving a boost to indigenous software entrepreneurs and touching off trade disputes with U.S. software firms [3].

\- An American accountant has set up a software cooperative in Shanghai to undertake software development project for multinational customers [7,15].

In some ways, these developments are reminiscent of the global shifts in production that have occurred in industries such as textile garments, consumer electronics, watches, and toys. While there are similarities to these shifts of labor-intensive manufacturing to developing-country locations, the software situation is unique in many ways. Software is a quintessentially intellectual product, requiring small capital investment but rather large investment in people with high-level technical skills. Standardization is slow in coming in software and production methods. The software business is rife with innovative upheavals which result in notoriously short product life cycles. A large part of the software business is custom and maintenance work. Because of these special characteristics, globalization of the software industry deserves careful study by the advanced countries (ACs) as well as by the newly industrializing countries (NICs).

In this paper, we explore the major trends and strategies associated with the incipient globalization process in the software industry.

## 2. Software and the Software Industry

Software is an intellectual creation which comprises the programs, procedures, rules and any associated documentation pertaining to the operation of a data processing system. Software is independent of its carrier media.

As an intellectual creation, software production requires minimal investment in physical capital but substantial investment in highly-trained human capital. The human capital for software production resides in those professionals trained in computer science, engineering disciplines, business specializations, and information systems. A growing number of such professionals are from NICs. The wages of these professionals in NICs are substantially below comparable wages in ACs. The existence and growth of such indigenous, highly skilled, and relatively low-cost human capital in NICs has set up the basic conditions for the globalization of the software industry.

It should be emphasized at the outset that, although the potential for increasing software activity in NICs exists, the realization of this potential is not automatic and is constrained in many ways. While the core aspect of software production – writing computer code from detailed program specifications – is shiftable to selected NICs, other aspects of the industry are not as shiftable. These include conceptualization, documentation, installation, and support. Cultural differences, limited local markets, insufficient R&D, inadequate telecommunications facilities, and delayed access to new technology create barriers for NICs trying to increase their share of the global software industry.

Given the strong preconditions for globalization, as well as the barriers just discussed, the pace of globalization in the software industry will be determined by two sets of factors. The first pertains to trends in the software industry. The second set of factors pertains to the policies and strategies of NICs and ACs.

## 2.1. Resource Demand and Supply

The growth rate for the computer industry in the world market was almost 50 percent in 1984. According to one estimate, the U.S. computing revenues will be about 12.5 percent of the GNP by 1990 [11]. These are impressive figures when one considers that the U.S. automobile industry contributed only 2.3 percent to the GNP in 1980. The 1987 revenue for software alone has been estimated at \$40 billion [33].

Current demand for software professionals greatly exceeds the available supply. It is estimated that demand could exceed supply by as many as two million people by 1990. Increasingly, software people are being forced to direct their efforts toward software maintenance activities instead of development of new needed software. A result of this is that the demand for new software systems is growing much faster than our ability to produce and maintain them. This unmet demand for systems and systems professionals creates a situation which is favorable for NICs that wish to increase their involvement in the supply side of the economic equation.

## 2.2. Software Cost Structure

The relative cost components of information systems are changing in significant and often dramatic ways. The ratio of hardware to software costs has altered drastically and is still changing. In the early 1960s, the ratio of hardware costs to software costs was 80:20. By late 1980s, it had reversed to 20:80 and the projections are that it will be 10:90 in early 1990s.

Within software costs, the share of maintenance costs has been surprisingly high and is likely to increase. Most firms today direct 60 to 80% of their software dollars toward software maintenance [20]. The backlog of software upgrading and maintenance jobs is already high and increasing. Despite the availability of sophisticated software engineering tools, a majority of software practitioners are pessimistic about significant breakthroughs in the productivity of software maintenance tasks [16]. We expect the software industry to continue to be a service-oriented industry – predominantly involving maintenance of existing software.

These cost components have three implications for globalization, two favorable to NICs. First, the falling hardware/software cost ratio implies a decline in capital-intensity and an increase in knowledge (or human capital) intensity. NICs with large number of computer professionals will benefit from this. Second, the high proportion of maintenance implies more intense intellectual “drudge work.” NIC professionals, who have a narrower set of employment alternatives than AC professionals, are more likely to accept such work. Finally, the iterative nature of maintenance work demands close and continuous customer contact. The NICs are at a disadvantage here, although improved telecommunication and software engineering techniques will partially overcome this.

## 2.3. New Productivity Tools and Efforts

Concerns about the relatively low productivity and escalating costs of software production activities are fueling efforts to standardize and streamline software development and maintenance. One key development is the increasing availability of software engineering tools that enable the systemization of many laborious software tasks. According to the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (formerly National Bureau of Standards), there are now more than 200 software tools which can be used in systems analysis, systems design, software construction and maintenance [27]. Examples of such tools include online symbolic debugger, screen painter, and documentation generator.

Perhaps more significant than the availability of individual software engineering tools is the recent advent of Integrated Project Support Environment (IPSE) systems. These provide a coordinated set of software tools which help in all phases of a project life cycle. Help in project management is also provided.

With IPSE systems all information concerning the project is maintained in a common, shared database. Because of this feature, these systems are particularly well suited to situations which involve geographically dispersed project teams $[23]$ . The disadvantage of IPSEs is that their use generally entails a significant initial investment in high-powered workstation hardware $[32]$ . This could become a barrier for smaller NIC software firms lacking the needed capital resources.

IPSE developers have recognized the need for public tools interface which would allow independent or foreign software engineering tools to be used in conjunction with an IPSE project. Such an interface, when developed, will enable programmers to move from project to project more easily without incurring the usual startup costs associated with learning a new environment.

Another trend toward productivity enhancement is the emergence of fourth-generation languages. Using such languages rather than traditional third generation languages, elaborate software packages can be built, modified, and tested with fewer statements in a syntax that more closely approximates natural language.

## 2.4. Standardization of Programming Languages and Environments

The popularity of the Unix operating system and the IEEE “trial use” standard for Posix (Portable Operating System) [12] serve as examples of the trend toward operating system standardization. This trend tends to increase the portability and reusability of software by reducing the dependence on the host computer system. In addition, programmer portability is greatly increased by reducing the need to learn new systems and development environments.

The trend toward language standardization is illustrated by the advent of standard languages, where subsets, supersets, and dialects are forbidden, and compiler verification is compulsory. The Ada programming language exemplifies this trend $[29]$ . At present, Ada is a standard of the U.S. military, NATO, the American National Standards Institute (ANSI), a U.S. Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS), and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). These features provide a level of software transportability which was not possible in the past $[31]$ .

There are at least two implications of these trends which favor the globalization of the software industry. First, the increased similarity of development environments will shorten the learning curve when new, heterogeneous projects are undertaken. Second, the greater degree of hardware independence reduces the need for commonality of hardware between user's and developer's site.

## 2.5. Software Modularization and Reusable Code

The use of software modules, a practice which is stressed in software education and fostered by the design of modern programming languages increases the practicality of reusing code. With increasing standardization of languages and operating systems, the reusability of software is extended to heterogeneous computer environments.

The Ada language goes well beyond previous programming languages in supporting the reuse of code. Ada modules, called packages, are compiled separately. This allows the individual pieces of a software system to be developed, tested, and maintained independently.

Since 1983, U.S. defense contractors have been mandated to use Ada in writing mission-critical software, and as much as 65% of new software developed by devfense contractors employs reused modules [26]. Ada is gaining popularity in many types of applications worldwide.

As the degree of software modularization (and their independence) increases, so does the viability of farming out portions of projects to foreign producers. Specifications for software “subassemblies” can be divulged without breaching the security of the major software system.

For NICs, new productivity tools, standardization efforts, and other industry trends hold a mixed message. To the extent the NICs can acquire and internalize the new tools and skills of software development, they will benefit. In those aspects of software work that call for specialized hardware or training, NICs may be at a relative disadvantage, at least initially.

## 2.6. Easier Transportability of Software

The rise of global courier services makes it possible to transport software media, such as disks and tapes, across distance in short periods of time. Even more promising are the electronic communications networks, both private and public, that enable rapid transmission of voice, data, and images. Intense efforts are under way to put into place global standards and protocols to permit almost any communication or information devices in the world to interact $[24]$ . But much before the dream of a universal and integrated telecommunications network becomes reality, progress is already being made in specialized global networks $[22]$ . Examples include the BITNET system that links universities across the globe, dedicated satellite transmission facilities of companies, and the growing array of datacom equipment available off-the-shelf.

NICs will generally benefit from developments that make it easy to transport software and related information (e.g., IPSE databases) back and forth. Those NICs will benefit that have highly reliable dedicated or universal data communication links. As integrated networks capable of carrying voice, data, and image over the same transmission medium emerge, NICs that install such networks will be in a better position to participate in those aspects of software work that require interactive and iterative procedures.

## 3. Towards an Analytical Framework

Like any other type of international trade, trade in software arises because of differential endowments and costs of factors of production. The comparative advantage of NICs arises from two basic conditions: the relatively abundant endowment (relative to internal needs) of skilled software professionals, and their relatively lower wage. These conditions occur in NICs where higher education in technical areas has surged ahead of other technological development. In other words, for some technological assets, there are more technical professionals in NICs than in ACs. This tends to keep the wages of technical professionals (such as software developers) lower in NICs vis-à-vis ACs.

The endowment and cost-competitiveness ratings indicate the overall competitiveness. The following conclusions can be drawn:

1. NICs are especially competitive in programming – which is mainly needed at the system development stage.

2. NICs excel in the cultural aspects of group orientation and attention to detail; these occur in detailed, team-programming tasks, e.g., in large, complex software systems.

3. NICs are at a disadvantage in the areas of innovativeness and performance-based incentives. This will hamper their effectiveness in system design and in developing novel methods of improving productivity. This is exacerbated by poor communications with the end user.

4. NICs hold a potential advantage in project management due to lower cost of management. But the culture of managing sophisticated software projects calls for well-specified milestones and deliverables. This is not yet ingrained into the NICs. The advantage in project management will be realized only when cultural barriers are overcome.

5. NICs also hold a potential advantage in hardware engineering due to low costs of technical employees. The realization of this advantage is contingent on the transfer of large hardware installations to NICs.

An immediate need is to assess the effectiveness of strategies that NICs and ACs have adopted or are contemplating for development of the software industry.

## 4. Global Software Strategies: NIC Perspective

The immediate advantage that NICs hold is in the area of programming tasks entailing coding and testing from detailed software design specifications. There are indications that NIC software firms and governments are already acting to the shift in software coding and testing tasks from ACs to NICs. Countries as diverse as China, South Korea, Singapore, and India have started software development work for AC firms on a contract basis or through software centers.

Some of the NIC government policies regarding software, however, are still based on an industrial and manufacturing model that has little relevance to an intellectual production process.

## 4.1. Industrial Age Policies

Three of the industrial age policies which are being applied to the software industry are:

1. Reserving the domestic market for local software producers.

2. Compulsory registration or licensing of new software production projects.

3. Preference to local producers for government software procurement.

The policy of market reservation can quickly become counterproductive in a world of global software standards and worldwide information networks. A country relying solely on local software resources to satisfy internal demand will lag behind in a matter of months; software advances build rapidly on one another. A country with an inward-looking software development strategy will simply not incorporate the latest techniques and advances.

Compulsory registration of software projects and products may have limited utility in NICs, especially if mainly used by planners to develop a database of software resources. But if it restricts software product types to a few producers, as with industrial products, then the policy will prove disastrous. The traditional advantages of economies of scale do not exist in software production. In fact, the advance of software occurs when many competing approaches flourish; creative and innovative designs are the lifeblood of this industry.

Preferential government procurement of software from local producers has limited value in fostering local production. Since the government is usually the largest software customer in NICs, obtaining government contrasts could mean the difference between survival and collapse. But once again, relying only on locally produced software could put an NIC behind. The best policy would offer a moderate price advantage to local firms and qualify joint ventures with AC firms for purposes of bidding on government work. In this way AC firms could be induced to shift some work to NIC affiliates.

## 4.2. Information Age Policies

A proactive and globally-oriented approach is likely to have a much greater long-run payoff for an NIC aspiring to become a key player. In the short run, a system of incentives (tax rebates and export incentives) can be built to aid the software industry develop and grow. Given the global and fast-changing nature of the industry, the incentives should be offered to all software firms meeting the specified production or export conditions. Specifically, affiliates and joint ventures of AC-based software houses should be included in the program. This will induce AC-based firms to locate software development operations in the NIC and increase technology transfer. Other incentives can promote joint ventures between AC and local firms. Loans at reduced interest rates, land and equipment grants, and training subsidies are some incentives for this.

Other NIC actions with direct payoff are improvements in telecommunications networks and provision of access to state-of-the-art hardware and software tools. For expensive equipment and tools, resource centers can be created for local software producers.

In the longer run, NIC strategies should be geared to providing a technological and human resource base through curricular changes, scholarships for software education, R&D grants, and creation of resource centers. In areas where NICs have distinct disadvantages – e.g., software engineering, creativity, or systems analysis skills – specific investments should be made to upgrade the local endowment.

A final area is the protection of intellectual rights. Many NICs are notorious for violating copyrights and patents, and AC-based firms will be reluctant to shift software work to NICs if they are not satisfied that their rights are fully protected.

## 5. Global Software Strategies: AC Perspective

In the advanced countries (ACs), software policies and strategies are mostly a private sector concern. This is not to say that the government in an AC has little interest in the software industry. The promotion of standards is an example of an AC government strategy to influence the course of its industry. By and large, however, the course of the software industry in an AC is shaped by the rapid strategic moves of their software firms. They face a global environment in which the competitive advantage and ability of some NICs is rising. While this competitive edge is limited to highly structured tasks (coding, maintenance, and testing from detailed specifications), there are other areas where NICs may soon become competitive. ACs retain an overwhelming advantage (and this will continue) in the software design phase. Access to users, widely diffused analysis skills, and cultural facility in one-on-one user interaction continue to give ACs an upper hand.

AC software firms can choose to treat the growing programming capability of NICs as an opportunity or a threat. If they treat this as an opportunity, then they should help the NIC institutions promote local software industries. If the AC firms regard NIC software capability as a threat, then efforts should be directed to software engineering tools that automate large parts of software development and thereby provide dramatic increases in productivity. Unless such tools are proprietary, however, NICs may have as much access to these tools and no lasting advantage may result. Under this scenario, software engineering tools are best viewed as productivity enhancing rather than as competitive weapons.

A review of the strategies in the AC software industry indicates that both directions are being followed. There is some shifting of programming tasks to NICs. There are also rapid advances in computer-aided software engineering. In the long run, these two directions may be complementary rather than competing. Software activities in each firm may be bifurcated into those that are best shifted to NICs and those that are best handled in ACs.

## 6. Conclusions

The software industry is globalizing just as the automobile or the consumer electronics industry did. Lower wage rates in NICs are a driving factor behind globalization of the software industry. There are unique features, however, that need to be studied. Software is an intellectual product. The globalization pattern may prove to be a prototype for many intellectual products and service-oriented industries.

The emerging pattern is one of shifting some programming tasks to NICs and the enhancing of software engineering capabilities in ACs. As the industry evolves, it is likely that a truly global production system (linked with multi-media telecommunications) will emerge. Since the processes are intellectual, the software industry could overcome geographic barriers in production. NIC and AC strategies will influence the way the production pattern develops.

We must examine the whole range of productive and facilitative resources that affect software development. The endowment, cost, mutual substitutability, and interaction of such resources should be studied across a range of ACs and NICs. Based on this, better strategic and policy guidance can be provided to the software business in ACs and NICs.
