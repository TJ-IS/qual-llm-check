# The Evaluation of Decision-Aiding Systems: Guidelines and Methods

Robert M. O'Keefe \*

Department of Decision Sciences and Engineering Systems, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, New York 12180-3590, USA

Few decision-aiding systems, such as Expert or Decision Support Systems, are formally evaluated. Yet when used to aid a decision making process, there is an assumption that an organization receives a return on the investment inherent in developing (or purchasing) and implementing the system. This return is generated by more effective, more efficient, or delegated (and hence cheaper) decision making. This paper considers the conceptual problems in evaluating decision-aiding systems. It proposes nine guidelines to which an evaluation method should adhere. Three general methods for evaluation are then discussed: control group experiments, attitude scales, and multiple-criteria models. Each is reviewed in the light of the conceptual requirements for evaluation, as represented by the nine guidelines. A general multiple-criteria method that fits the nine guidelines is then presented.

Keywords: Expert systems, Decision support systems, Evaluation, Multiple-criteria modeling.

## 1. Introduction

Decision-aiding systems are computer systems that are used to aid the process of decision making. Approaches to decision-aiding include Decision Support Systems [22], Visual Interactive Modeling [6], consultative Expert Systems [19], and some Management Information Systems [11].

Decision Support Systems (DSS) facilitate the retrieval of pertinent data and its use in pre-defined or user generated models. Management Information Systems (MIS) that are built to support decision making are similar, but typically do not contain a model component. Visual Interactive Modeling (VIM) systems allow the user to view a simulation of the effect of a decision or the control of a system under an operating algorithm. Consultative Expert Systems (ES) suggest one or more ‘solutions’ or courses of action following a consultation with the user. Increasingly, however, as larger and more powerful systems are being built, the distinction between these types of decision-aiding system has borne blurred.

All developed decision-aiding systems are evaluated, if only when management or system developers subjectively declare the resulting system a ‘success’ or a ‘fairlure’. Formal evaluation of systems, however, is rare and, where carried out, sometimes adhoc and poorly done, providing little information that can be used to determine the contribution of the implemented system to organization performance. Lack of proper evaluation can have considerable adverse effects: biased subjective judgement may result in an inadequate system being forced upon users or, at the other extreme, a useful and effective system being underused.

Traditionally, electronic data processing systems performing transaction processing can be evaluated by considering the return on investment, using measures such as the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) or Cost-Benefit Analysis. However, systems where the major return on investment is partially unquantifiable or intangible have proved difficult to evaluate [23], and the move towards decision-aiding systems has made this situation even worse. Often the entire perceived benefit of a decision-aiding system is intangible; for example, increased user effectiveness, a better decision making process, a greater consensus for the chosen decision, etc.

## 2. Previous Approaches

The major ‘communities’ that have looked at the evaluation problem from their particular standpoint are the MIS, DSS, ES and Management Science/Operations Research (MS/OR) groups. There has also been some work under the heading of Systems Engineering.

## 2.1. Management Information Systems

Faced with the inadequacy of traditional data processing evaluation methods, the MIS community has produced a considerable amount of work on evaluation. Approaches that have been proposed are centered on either system usage, user satisfaction or information economics.

If system usage is high, it is tempting to conclude that the system is successful. However, this may certainly not be true where system usage is mandatory, for example, due to the removal of a previous manual system, or there is management pressure to use the new system. Further, limited use of a system may not indicate failure, since

(a) the nature of the system may be such that use of the system is only expected in rare instances; e.g., crisis management systems [5],

(b) occasional use of the system in complex situations may produce a high payoff, or

(c) initial use of the system may produce a worthwhile shift in management perceptions and decision making, so that the system is effective but then falls into disuse.

Given the problems with system usage, user satisfaction is generally regarded as a better indicator of system success. There have been attempts both to determine the important elements of user satisfaction [4], and to measure it (measurement methods are reviewed by Ives, Olson and Baroudi [20]). However, user satisfaction is also a problematical evaluation indicator, since

(a) there is some evidence that user satisfaction is linked with user expectations [14]; high expectations may result in poor user satisfaction irrespective of the value of the system, and

(b) achievement of important organizational goals, such as better information and decision making, may be adequately achieved despite user dissatisfaction with the user interface, response time, etc.

A number of researchers, for instance Ahituv [2] and Shalchi [28], have proposed the application of information economics to MIS evaluation. They argue that as an MIS increases information flow, the payoff from an MIS can be evaluated in terms of the value of the information it provides.

Information can be measured from the point of the view of the organization or the individual. Increased information flow to the individual should have the effect of providing a sounder basis for decision making. Assessing the marginal value of information is at best difficult and at worst impossible. As shown in many experiments (for example, see [12]), the level of presentation and the degree of summarization are important contributing factors. As recognized by Ackoff [1], uncontrolled production of large amounts of information can be detrimental.

## 2.2. Decision Support Systems

As a DSS is directly used to support decision making, the DSS community has frequently emphasized increased decision maker effectiveness as an evaluation criteria, suggesting that the evaluation of a DSS can be equated with the measurement of effectiveness. Unfortunately, the measurement of effectiveness can be difficult $[17]$ , perhaps even more so than user satisfaction. Sharda et. al. $[30]$ discuss a number of laboratory experiments that measure the pertinence of a DSS to a specific task by measuring effectiveness. However, in all cases effectiveness is measured against a known optimum or a base case: in effect, effectiveness is equated with performance. Whilst such studies provide evidence that a DSS can increase performance, they do not provide a basis for measuring effectiveness of real DSS.

One valiant attempt to provide a semi-formal method that evaluates the benefit of a DSS prior to development is value analysis $[21]$ , which seeks to identify the expected benefits of a DSS and to produce a cost threshold below which development of the system is worthwhile. The process is iterated over a prototype and subsequent versions, so that the benefit and cost of the next iteration is assessed at each stage.

## 2.3. Expert Systems

The ES community has viewed evaluation as a mixture of validation and implementation efficiency. An ES performs an expert task and thus validation is crucial: it is necessary to ascertain that the ES performs at an acceptable level close to human performance $[27,34]$ . Subsequent to validation, researchers have focused on evaluating implementation efficiency, i.e., factors such as the user interface, ease of use, etc. $[13]$ .

This approach belies the grounding of ES workers in Computer Science and is similar to the evaluation of non-decision-aiding software, for example a language compiler. Once validated, the software is assessed in terms of the efficiency with which it performs the task for which it was designed. There have been little or no attempts to evaluate an ES in the context of the decision making environment in which it is used, a notable exception being Liebowitz [24], who used a multiple-criteria model to compare an ES with a presently used manual method.

## 2.4. Management Science / Operations Research

Traditionally, within MS/OR a model has been judged as successful if it has been installed and operated with real world data $[3]$ . This implementation criteria is similar to the system usage criteria of MIS, where it is assumed that usage of the system implies a subsequent benefit.

There has been a realization, however, that the payoff of much MS/OR work is not the implementation of a model, but less tangible benefits, such as increased management understanding of the problem. A number of authors have argued for a view of MS/OR work that takes account of this, for instance, Ginzberg [14] and Dando and Bennett [10]. Further, MS/OR workers increasingly build computer-based systems that are directly used by clients. Hence, as with DSS, there is a need to evaluate through measuring the effectiveness of decision making following the intervention of OR/MS workers.

## 2.5. Systems Engineering

The specification, design and implementation of a decision-aiding system can be viewed as an engineering design problem. As with other engineering tasks, evaluation is then the process of comparing the finished product against the previously established specification. Variations on this theme, such as that developed by Sage [29], provide an interesting alternative to those discussed above.

## 3. Conceptual Problems

Reviewing the attitudes of the various communities, and focusing on decision aiding-systems, helps to identify specific conceptual problems. They lead to nine guidelines for the construction of a general evaluation method for decision-aiding systems.

## 3.1. Measuring Effectiveness

As the rationale of a decision aiding system is the support of a decision making process, an appropriate evaluation method must address the effectiveness of decision making with the system. While measures of usage, satisfaction, efficiency, and implementation are appropriate in some circumstances, they do not necessarily measure the benefits to an organization of a decision-aiding system. Hence the first guideline is

Guideline 1: The evaluation of decision-aiding systems should attempt to measure effectiveness.

Hamilton and Chervany [17] suggest that effectiveness can be perceived as goal-centered or system-resource centered. The first involves the determination of objectives for the system; it can then be evaluated against these objectives. With the second, a normative state is proposed for the system, which can than be evaluated against it.

For decision-aiding systems, the definition of a normative or ‘good’ state is difficult: the evolutionary nature of decision-aiding systems development means that the appearance and performance of any ‘final’ implementation is difficult to specify.

Typically, however, it is possible to set objectives for system effectiveness, even if their statement may be somewhat loose e.g., 'I want a system that helps me make this decision better'. Thus the second guideline is

Guideline 2: The evaluation of decision-aiding systems requires a goal-centered approach.

The problem with using objectives is that it is difficult to measure the attainment of an objective directly: there may be no way to assess a ‘better’ decision, since the link between the decision and organizational outcomes may be tenuous, or only perceivable over a considerable elapsed period of time. Thus, it is necessary to use indicators [4] or performance measures that contribute to the achievement of objectives, and can be measured. For instance, a decision may be ‘better’ if the user’s confidence in the decision is increased. This leads to

Guideline 3: It is necessary to use indicators to assess indirectly the achievement of each objective.

A further problem with objectives is that they will probably change over the course of development and implementation. Thus it may be necessary to amend objectives, and identify new indicators, as required.

## 3.2. Level of Effectiveness

Any increase in effectiveness must be observed at some organizational level. As discussed by Hamilton and Chervany [17], effectiveness can be considered at three different levels

(a) user effectiveness; i.e., an increase in the effective performance of the user of the system,

(b) organizational effectiveness; i.e., how well the goals of the organization are met, or

(c) developer effectiveness; i.e., the success of the developer in providing support and systems.

Ideally, decision-aiding systems should be measured in terms of organizational effectiveness, since use of a system should enhance the attainment of organizational goals. Unfortunately, the link between use of the system and organization goals is often very weak, so it is necessary to focus on the user, and what Hamilton and Chervany [17] call the ‘user processes and performance’. It is assumed that the organization benefits if user performance is increased, along with the processes whereby a higher performance level is obtained. Given this

Guideline 4: For decision-aiding systems, user effectiveness appears to be the most relevant level for measurement and evaluation.

## 3.3. Ex-ante vs. Ex-post Evaluation

Evaluation can proceed prior to development and/or implementation (ex-ante) or following development and/or implementation (ex-post). Ex-ante is useful for deciding between different designs or proposals, but rarely provides any information for subsequent assessment of the implemented choice. As an example, see Belton [7] on using a multiple-criteria model for selecting from different system proposals.

Ex-post, such as the expert system evaluation by Liebowitz [24], is far more common. There are many reasons for this; it seems natural to evaluate a system after development, and it may only be possible to evaluate it after implementation. Whereas a one-off evaluation ex-post may be easy to arrange and perform, it has three major drawbacks

(a) The system may be ‘judged in the light of expectations and objectives that go beyond those that might reasonably have been established prior to … development’ [23].

(b) Evaluation occurs after the great majority of the development effort has been expended.

(c) Since many decision-aiding systems are evolutionary, it may be difficult to identify an appropriate point in time to evaluate ex-post.

Ex-post can be useful prior to greater distribution of the implemented system or the preparation of future development. What developers really need, however, is a method that allows for evaluation at any stage in the development process, and particularly one that allows for evaluation against the original objectives specified prior to development. Thus

Guideline 5: For decision-aiding systems, a method is required that allows for evaluation prior to development with multiple evaluations before, during, and after implementation.

## 3.4. Expectations

As discussed by Ginzberg [15], user satisfaction is to some extent dependent upon expectations held about the system. When expectations are not met, users are dissatisfied. Ginzberg concludes that ‘users must be involved so that they can form realistic expectations about the system’. It is thus important that an evaluation procedure realizes this problem. This can be achieved by making sure that both the objectives and level of effectiveness adequately reflect expectations, that indicators allow the measurement of expectations, and that expectations are assessed ex-ante. Thus

Guideline 6: An evaluation procedure for decision-aiding systems should explicitly address user expectations.

Further, an evaluation procedure can be an important tool in controlling unreasonable expectations, since it can provide a method for establishing expectations prior to development.

## 3.5. Evaluator Viewpoints

An evaluation is generally performed from a particular viewpoint. With decision-aiding systems, three viewpoints appear possible: the developer, the user, or the manager responsible for initiating the system (who may also be the user in many instances) [18].

The previously discussed positions of the communities concerned with evaluation reflect different viewpoints. Most MIS and DSS views have focused on the user, whilst the ES and MS/OR view is that of the developer. Combining viewpoints can make evaluation misleading. For example, a poor quality user interface may result in a negative assessment from the user. For the manager, however, if the objectives of the system are still met, then the annoyance of a poor quality interface may be relatively unimportant. Similarly, poor quality documentation may be unimportant to the developers and users but considered crucial to the manager who must pay for maintenance. Further, how a system is perceived and how it is used may not coincide [32]. Users may perform a task adequately using a system even if they detest it.

Although instances of evaluation from the developer and user viewpoint may be appropriate in some instances, the majority of decision-aiding systems require a manager viewpoint. This is because

(a) with many DSS and VIM systems, the manager will also be the user, and

(b) with many ES and MIS systems, the users are often lower level staff that do not relate their perceptions of the system to its managerial objectives.

## Hence

Guideline 7: The evaluation of decision-aiding systems requires a management oriented viewpoint.

## 3.6. Prescriptive vs. Descriptive Evaluation

Most evaluations are descriptive: a system is evaluated against objectives or a normative state, resulting in some measure of its appropriateness. For evolutionary systems, it would be far better to have a prescriptive evaluation method, such that it provides a measure of ‘where to go next’ in development. Unfortunately, a prescriptive method can be difficult to design and use. One good example is Chandler [9], who developed a method that uses multi-objective programming to assess the best way of using development effort to increase user performance. Thus any method that is easily comprehensible by management is likely to be descriptive: prescriptive actions will have to be decided subsequently. Hence

Guideline 8: Although a prescriptive method would be useful for decision-aiding systems, it may be necessary to accept and use a descriptive method.

## 3.7. Validity

Validation is the process of checking that the model component of the system behaves like the 'real world'. Validity is a major problem for builders of model-based decision-aiding systems: systems with built in models, be they analytical or knowledge-based, need the model validated to an acceptable level. Evaluation may be meaningless if the model is actually invalid; then use of the results may be damaging. This problem is particularly acute with ES, where the system is supposed to perform at a level close to human performance [27]. With systems that allow the user to build a model or alter existing models, the problem is even greater. Such models are rarely validated, and there is therefore a danger that decisions will be based on invalid models [8].

Hence, for many systems, proper validation, or facilities that reduce the possibilities of misuse through invalid model development, is a sine qua non. This needs to be separately addressed, prior to evaluation. To have ‘validity’ as one of a number of criteria in the evaluation process is misleading and dangerous. Thus an important guideline is

Guideline 9: Validation is the cornerstone of evaluation for many model-based decision-aiding systems and needs separate attention.

## 4. General Methods

Out of the various evaluation procedures for decision-aiding systems previously proposed, most fall into two categories: control group experiments and multiple-criteria methods. Attitude scales have been used to evaluate human-computer interfaces [26], and appear to be another appropriate method.

## 4.1. Control Group Experiments

In some instances, it is possible to measure effectiveness via a control group. The system is implemented for one user or set of users and not for another, then the effectiveness of the two groups is compared. Examples include King and Rodriguez [23], who discuss the evaluation of a strategic and policy planing MIS, and Grabowski and Wallace [16], who present the results of evaluating an ES for training marine pilots.

Typically, in a control group experiment the effectiveness of users is assessed by comparing the performance of users of the system against the performance of those without the system. One or more tasks are given to all users, and the measurement of user effectiveness is done by either relating user performance to a known optimum performance level, or, where no ‘optimum’ exists (as is normally the case) by peer review. Associated indicators can be measured by either monitoring users, for example identifying the amount of data they use to perform each task, or questioning them using an attitude scale.

As an evaluation tool, control group experiments fit a number of the guidelines. They can concentrate on effectiveness (guideline 1), include the use of indicators (3), are naturally user oriented (4), and allow a management viewpoint (7). Expectations are also addressed (6), to some extent, by comparison with the current method.

The guidelines also indicate two major problems with control group experiments. First, the method is not goal-centered (guideline 2): comparison against present methods means that the evaluated system may still fall short of the original objectives. Second, it is not possible to evaluate prior to development, or perhaps even prior to implementation (5), and the practicality of the situation may mean that only a single evaluation experiment is feasible.

Other problems exist when using this approach. To negate the effect of natural performance variance between users, both groups should be large, but this is frequently impossible to achieve. A system built for a few specific individuals or a single user, for example, will not be amenable to the control group approach. Further, it will almost certainly be necessary to use a simulated environment and maybe even synthetic tasks to conduct the experiment; thus the validity of the evaluation may be questionable.

## 4.2. Attitude Scales

A simpler evaluation method is attitude scales. Whilst these can take a number of forms, a format that seems appropriate for decision-aiding systems is the measurement of conformity with statements, as shown in Figure 1. Here users are asked to number their level of agreement on a five point Likert scale using several ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ questions. The number of questions of each type should be identical, and the questions themselves should be intermingled and not identified (unlike those illustrated). A simple total measure can be obtained by adding the scores for the positive questions and then subtracting the scores for the negative questions. Thus for n questions of each type, and a scale with s levels, the total will range from $-n(s-1)$ to $n(s-1)$ .

Attitude scales do not fit two of the more important guidelines: there is no direct attempt to measure effectiveness (guideline 1) and the approach is not goal-centered (2). However, careful selection of the questions can make good use of indicators (3), and the approach is implicitly user oriented (4). Further, it may be easy to perform a number of evaluations at different stages of development (5). Using the scale, an evaluation of the present method can be made prior to any development; this provides a measurement against which all subsequent evaluations can be judged.

Although simple, attitude scales are easy to use, and they provide limited prescriptive information for future development (8). They are most appropriate for evaluating a system with many users, as the mean measurement provides some robustness.

## 4.3. Multiple-Criteria Methods

A number of previous evaluation procedures have used various multiple-criteria methods. Belton [7] and Shoval and Lugasi [31] use them to select amongst a number of computer systems, Liebowitz [24] uses one to compare an ES with a present manual method, and Lucas and Moore [25] apply a simple scoring model to the selection of a system project.

A model is constructed by defining the relevant measurable criteria for the system being evaluated. The strength of multiple-criteria methods is that the criteria measures can be weighted, allowing the development of a model that takes into account the relative importance of different criteria. A simple weighted additive model can be used to combine criteria; where the number of criteria is few it may be possible to measure each one relative to every other, as done by Liebowitz [24], and use Saaty's Analytic Hierarchy Process to produce a measure. Such overspecification allows for the monitoring of the consistency of the judgments.

Multiple-criteria methods have a considerable advantage over other methods: they are applicable when evaluation is being performed by a single manager, whether the manager is a user or otherwise. The weights can be assessed for the manager in question, and performance against the criteria measured independently of the weightings. However, there is a problem where a large number of users or managers need to evaluate the system separately, since the weightings of each user may differ.

In relation to the guidelines, the advantage of multiple-criteria methods for decision-aiding system evaluation is that the criteria are the indicators that directly contribute to the specified objectives: hence the approach explicitly fits guideline 3. How well the other guidelines are followed depends upon the way in which the model is constructed and used. If the indicators address user effectiveness and are management oriented, then guidelines 1, 4 and 7 are satisfied. Criteria can be structured, with lower level criteria contributing to criteria at a higher level: if the top level criteria are the objectives for the system, with the lower levels modeling the indicators, then the model is explicitly goal-centered (guideline 2). A multiple-criteria approach can be designed that fits the other guidelines. The model can be used to assess expectations prior to development (6), and then used to produce comparative measures at subsequent stages of development and implementation (5).

## 5. A Generalized Multiple-Criteria Method

The basis of the approach is to obtain an ex-ante management assessment of the present state of decision making and the expectancy of the situation following the introduction of the decision-aiding system. Let the evaluations of each of these be called $E_{PS}$ and $E_{E}$ respectively. After development, the process yields an evaluation of how the system performs, called $E_{S}$ . This allows comparison with both the present state and the expectancy. $E_{S}$ can be updated as further development and refinement proceeds.

Prior to development, the basic model is constructed, and $E_{PS}$ and $E_{E}$ are produced. This is done is seven stages, as follows

Stage 1: Define the objectives for the system.

The objectives for the developed system are specified: some possible objectives are shown in Figure 2 (adapted and extended from Hamilton and Chervany [17]). For example, key objectives for most DSS and VIM are ‘improved decision making’ and ‘improved decision making process’. For many ES, ‘delegated decision making’, such that the task can be performed by lower level staff, is a key objective.

Stage 2: Identify the relevant indicators for each objective.

For each objective, measurable (in the sense that relative assessments can be observed) indicators must be identified. For example, ‘improved decision making’ may be indicated by an increase in the ‘understanding of the problem’, the ‘degree of co-operation and consensus’ amongst managers, and the ‘confidence in the decision’. The same indicator may feed different objectives. A specific application may include very domain specific indicators.

Stage 3: Develop a Likert-type scale for each indicator.

A simple way to provide a scale for all indicators is to use Likert-type scales. Figure 3 shows these for the indicators ‘consideration of alternatives’ and ‘thoroughness of analysis’. Whilst the number of points on the scale can be varied between applications of the method, it is useful if they are constant between the chosen indicators for each application.

Stage 4: Ask management to indicate both the present state and the expectancy for each indicator.

This stage is the key to measuring managerial expectations. For every indicator, the scale value for both the present state of decision making and expectancy of the implemented system is assessed. For example, with ‘consideration of alternatives’, only a few alternatives may be identified at present (scale value 2), but the expectancy may be that a decision-aiding system will allow most of the alternatives to be identified (scale value 4).

After this stage, there exists a qualitative assessment of the present state and expectancy. The differences in scale scores can be presented and compared, which may be sufficient for a particular evaluation. If a quantitative assessment is necessary, the scores for each indicator can be combined using a multiple-criteria model. This requires that the indicators and objectives be weighted.

Stage 5: Weight all indicators.

Weights need to be elicited from management, using one of a number of well known methods, for instance pair wise comparison, where the importance of each criteria is weighted relative to every other. (This and other methods are discussed in Watson and Buede [33].) It is usual to normalize the weights to sum to 1.

## Stage 6: Weight all objectives.

Similarly, the relative importance of each objective can be assessed by determining a weight for each objective. These must also be elicited and are often normalized also.

Stage 7: Produce a total measure for both present state and expectancy.

A total measure can be produced for both the present state and the expectancy, using the weights for indicators and objectives and the scale values obtained in stage 4. If there are n objectives, each with $I_{j}$ indicators where $j=1\ldots n$ , the chosen scale ranges from 1 to s, and each indicator has a weight $V_{i}$ and scale measure $U_{i}$ where $i=1\ldots j$ , then the weighted measure for each objective is

$$
\mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{j}} = \frac {\sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{I} _ {\mathrm{j}}} \mathrm{U} _ {\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{V} _ {\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{sI} _ {\mathrm{j}}}.
$$

If the weight for each objective is $W_{j}$ , then the overall measure is

$$
\mathrm{E} = \sum_ {\mathrm{j} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{O} _ {\mathrm{j}} \mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{j}}.
$$

Using the two sets of scale measures will provide a measure for the present state, $E_{PS}$ , and for the expected state, $E_{E}$ . The gap between $E_{PS}$ and $E_{E}$ represents the ‘expectancy gap’ that has to be bridged by the developed and implemented system. Following development, managers can be asked to measure the state of the system using the scales developed in stage 3. This provides $E_{S}$ , an evaluation measure of the working system, where in normal circumstances $E_{PS} < E_{S} < E_{E}$ . As the system evolves, $E_{S}$ can be updated by reassessing the system using the stage 3 scales.

## 6. Conclusions

As the number, size, and cost of implemented decision-aiding systems grows, their evaluation will become increasingly important. Yet there are few guidelines or methods for evaluation.

This paper has attempted to develop some guidelines by reviewing work in the communities that build decision-aiding systems. Nine guidelines were presented, of which four are probably of paramount importance: attempt to measure decision-making effectiveness, use a goal-centered approach, do multiple evaluations before, during, and after implementation, and explicitly address user expectations.

Control group experimentation, attitude scales, and multiple-criteria models are three proposed evaluation methods. Control group experimentation allows a direct comparison of the system against other methods, but is not goal-centered, and may prove difficult to instigate. Attitude scales are generally too simple, but may be useful where there are a large number of users, all of which have to provide input to the evaluation process. Multiple-criteria models can be built that explicitly address objectives by way of indicators, and a generalized method has been presented that builds expectancy and multiple evaluations into the process.

Whatever guidelines are followed or measurement method is used the crucial thing is to consider evaluation from the start. There is too much temptation to postpone evaluation effort until the very end of the development process, when all too often the development budget is already overspent, and the project is running late. All systems are in some sense subjectively evaluated: it is of benefit to everyone to make the evaluation process more meaningful and informative.
