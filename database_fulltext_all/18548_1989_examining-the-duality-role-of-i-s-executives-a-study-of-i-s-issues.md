# Examining the Duality Role of I.S. Executives: A Study of I.S. Issues

Donald L. Amoroso

College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado, Colorado Springs, CO 80907, USA

Ron L. Thompson

School of Business Administration, University of Western Ontario, London, Ont., Canada N6A 3K7

Paul H. Cheney

College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409, USA

This article addresses the duality of the IS executive as “manager” or “technician.” IS issue surveys were empirically examined and compared to identify trends. To add to the base and currency of issue studies, the authors conducted a survey of 50 IS executives. The results for this and three previous studies were standardized to allow for more meaningful comparisons. Significant trends were observed between issues across studies indicating certain trends in many of the IS issues. Factor analysis was used to categorize the issues into managerial and technical ones. The Walsh Test, a nonparametric statistical technique, was used to test general managerial and technical trends among the four studies. Managerial issues were not found to increase significantly in importance while technical issues declined in importance to the IS executive.

Keywords: I.S. executives, I.S. management, I.S. issues, I.S. organization.

It is true that the IS executive is becoming a strategist who plays a major role using information technology as a tool to shape the future. In 1981, Ives and Olson [8] asked whether the typical IS manager was a “manager” or a “technician.” This question has been prevalent in much of the recent practitioner literature but it had not been addressed empirically. Can this duality be more clearly defined? Evidence in favor of one position or the other can be derived from several existing opinion surveys of critical IS issues; these provide needed information on the perspective of IS managers. Synnott pointed out the importance of issue surveys in that they point out a path to effective IT management [12]. Information systems professionals will have to develop a set of skills in order to solve future IT problems and take strategic advantage of IT within their organizations. Empirical testing of the IS issues provides a much stronger evidence of positive or negative trends than does “eyeballing” the data. Several questions will be addressed by this research:

1. How have the most critical issues in information systems changed over time?

2. Are there distinct categories of issues as suggested by Hartog and Herbert [6] and Brancheau and Wetherbe [2]?

3. To focus on the duality problem of IS managers: are managerial issues increasing in importance and, conversely, are technical issues declining over time?

Previous opinion surveys have therefore been examined and the results of our current study are presented and discussed in detail. The problems of comparing ranks is illustrated, and standardized scores are calculated to provide a more rigorous basis for comparison, T-tests are employed for each issue and between pairing of studies. Issues are then grouped, using factor analysis, in order to identify at the margin those issues which are either not categorized or load into two factors.

The factor analysis provided useful information when grouping the managerial and technical issues. The Walsh Test, a nonparametric statistical procedure, was employed to test whether the observed differences is managerial or technical factor composites were significant [1].

## IS Issue Studies

Since 1980, a series of widely published opinion surveys have attempted to keep abreast of trends in the relative importance of IS issues. The primary objective of the surveys has been to give an indication of those issues which are perceived by practicing IS managers as requiring the most attention and subsequent resource support.

In 1980, Ball and Harris administered a survey to 325 Society of Management Information Systems (SMIS) members [1]. Questions that were asked included a pre-defined set of needs and issues of SMIS members and the role of SMIS. Each of the issues was rated on a Likert-type scale from 1 (not important) to 6 (very important). The authors reported the means and standard deviation of responses for the eighteen issues. Although this was the first major study to address the topic, the issues for subsequent studies changed substantially, and hence it was not included in our analysis.

The second survey was conducted in 1983 by a group of four researchers associated with the University of Minnesota [5]. The method used in developing the list of issues for the 1983 study was systematic and comprehensive. The Delphi approach was used to identify and rank the top issues from members of the Society for Information Management (SIM), which asked them to identify and describe briefly 5 to 10 key issues. A list of commonly chosen items was compiled and sent out for ranking and discussion. Over 100 responses were returned and their rankings compiled to generate a new list. This process was repeated twice, until the final list of 15 issues was compiled.

The Center for the Study of Data Processing at Washington University sponsored a third issues survey in 1985 [6]. This study involved a similar list of topics to the 1983 study but the focus was somewhat different as the researchers also wished to include professional development and research issues. The researchers of this study used a two-stage process with a small number of reviewers to develop a list of key issues. A survey was then conducted using the developed list. The respondents were asked to rate each issue on a Likert-type scale of 1 (not important) to 4 (very important) rather than ranking the issues, as did the 1983 study. Eight issues were added to the 1983 list, and five previous issues were dropped.

The final IS issues survey, included for comparison, was conducted in 1986 under the guidance of one of the investigators involved with the 1983 study., Revisions were made to the list of issues in an effort to keep the topics current. This time a five-stage Delphi approach was used and a number of issue changes were observed from the 1983 survey. To be more specific, five of the top twenty issues in 1986 were not ranked as key issues in 1983. This may have reflected either the rapidly changing nature of the IS field or the bias of the sample which changed over time.

## The 1988 IS Issue Survey

In order to add the base of recent issue studies and provide historical data for trend analysis, a current survey of 50 IS executives attending a week long executive seminar was conducted at a major North American university. Two of the authors, among others, participated in delivering the seminar materials as well as using the opportunity to gather information for this study. Most of the organizations represented by the parparticipants are considered to be among North America's largest and most respected corporations. Fifteen common issues from the last three studies were selected for use in our survey for subsequent comparison and trend analysis.

The participants were asked to complete the instrument in Appendix A. The results were subsequently analyzed and the top-ranked issues served as a basis for subsequent investigation. Geographically, all major regions of the country were represented. Almost half (41%) of the respondents were from the service sector, about a third (36%) from resource or manufacturing based industries, and the remainder (23%) from education or government. This contrasts with the 1986 study, where 34% were in service industries, 43% of the respondents were in manufacturing, 6% in education or government, and the remainder (17%) did not specify.

The position held by the respondents within the organization is also of interest. For the current study, 52% held the title of Director or Vice President and were the chief executive responsible for the Information Systems function, directly reporting to the firm's president. Another 41% were called Manager of IS reporting two levels removed from the President, while the remaining 14% were either IS staff members or IS consultants.

After analysis of the instrument, the respondents were asked a number of informal follow-up questions. For example, the participants were asked to describe their MIS environments, operational problems, and corporate successes. In selecting interview data, we looked for particularly intuitive or pertinent comments. The findings will be presented in light of the insight gathered.

## Perspectives on the Key Issues

The responses most frequently cited as important focus more on managerial than on technological issues. By far the greatest emphasis appears to be on integrating IS more closely into the organization's goals and structure. It should be noted that the issue of strategic planning, where consistently ranked as the number one issue for previous surveys, was omitted from our list of issues. The primary reason for this was that the phrase is a 'catch-all', with potentially different meanings for different respondents. This issue showed up as the number one issue all previously published and unpublished issue surveys. The respondents were asked to rate each issue on a scale of Very Unimportant (1) to Very Important (5).

The Top five issues were: Aligning IS Goals with Business Goals, Matching the IS Organization with That of the Enterprise, Effective Use of Data Resources, the Role and Contribution of IS, and IS Effectiveness and Productivity. These were investigated in detail; by questioning the respondents via informal interviews on the top key issues, the researchers were able to gather evidence as to why they were rated as most important.

Focusing on the top five issues is obviously arbitrary. Other issues, such as the facilitation and management of end-user computing (which ranked sixth), are also important and require the attention of IS executives. Also, the rankings reflect aggregate responses from individuals representing vastly different organizations. Specific enterprises are acting within different frames of reference and hence may need to focus on a different set of issues, depending on their specific situations. Nevertheless, the rankings give a good indication of the critical issues facing most IS managers today. As the concerns of IS executives are explored more deeply through interviews, a clearer view of their wide range of concerns was acquired.

## Issue: Aligning IS Goals with Business Goals

This issue was first by a fairly wide margin. This reflects a general change in focus, recognizing the need for IS to support the organization in a changing competitive environment. It also reflects the more generalist orientation of today's IS executives, who are able to view technological and related issues within the broader perspective of the corporations' well-being. Respondents felt that this issue also encompassed the more specific topic of using information systems for competitive advantage, which has gained increasing visibility recently. Competitive advantage results from the recognition of opportunities through creativity and innovation, followed by rapid and effective implementation of information technologies to take advantage of these opportunities.

## Issue: Matching the IS Organization with That of the Enterprise

The effectiveness with which IS can support the enterprise's information needs is dependent on its position within the enterprise. In discussions with IS managers, there appears to be two primary considerations when attempting to establish appropriate reporting relationships. One involves whether to have IS report to a traditional functional area with a potential bias in systems development and maintenance priorities, reporting in a staff relationship which may isolate IS from the mainstream of the enterprise versus establishing a separate functional area for IS with a path to the president. The second consideration is whether to centralize the important IS functions and to what extent this should reflect the remaining organization of the firm.

## Issue: Effective Use of Data Resources

With the trend toward the use of database management systems having easier retrieval languages plus the end-user computing resolution in general, the need for effective data management and administration becomes more apparent. Most IS managers in our study reported being charged with the responsibility of balancing priorities between ready access to data and an increasing need for data security and integrity. This situation is aggravated by a failure of top-level managers to view data as an important corporate asset.

## Issue: The Role and Contribution of IS Within the Firm

This issue has increased substantially in importance over the past few years and is consistent with the perceived importance of the first two issues. Despite its potential contribution, many respondents stated that IS is still viewed as a necessary but not too desirable expense in many organizations. The relatively high ranking of this issue reflects the desire of senior IS managers to gain a better understanding of the role of IS. They also perceive a need for an increased understanding of the current and potential role of IS by other senior executives within the firm.

## Issue: Measuring IS Effectiveness and Productivity

This issue has been important to IS executives for many years and there is no indication it will decrease in importance in the near future. Measuring performance is critical for sound management, but measuring the benefit side of the cost/benefit equation continues to be a major problem. In many cases the senior managers of an enterprise have been forced to accept perceptual measures as surrogates for quantifiable benefits. The performance of many of the respondents was evaluated upon very nebulous criteria. This reflects the underlying difficulty IS practitioners (possibly aided by academics) have experienced in their attempt to quantify the value of information. Nevertheless, measurements will continue to be a critical issue as organizations invest increasing resources in IS.

## Comparison with Other Recent Surveys

We now examine the trends in the importance of key issues across all surveys to provide some insight into change and its potential impact. In addition to gathering data on current trends, it is important to predict where IS researchers and practitioners should focus their energies in the future. An examination of the rankings in Table 1 for the most recent comparable survey (1986) shows a great deal of similarity for five of the top six issues. Specifically, the rankings were almost identical for Matching the IS Organization with That of the Enterprise, the Role and Contribution of IS, Effective use of the Data Resource, IS Effectiveness and Productivity, and Managing End-User Computing. For most of the remaining issues, however, differences of varying magnitudes were observed.

It should be noted that the rankings displayed for the three previous studies do not represent the actual ranks obtained. Rather the ranks were adjusted to provide a common base for comparison. This required removal of the issues not included in the 1988 study and collapsing the ranks of the remaining common issues. For example, Information Architecture was not included in the 1988 study but ranked eight in the 1986 study. It was, therefore, removed from the comparative analysis and for each subsequent issue in the 1986 study, the rank was adjusted upward by one. This method of rank adjustment does not violate the underlying assumptions of ordinal data where attribute magnitude is not considered [9].

One approach for comparing the results of previous studies would be to examine the changes in ranks for each issue over time. This was the approach taken in previous studies. For example, one would observe that Facilitating and Managing End-User Computing was ranked first in 1983, fourth in 1985 and 1986, and sixth in 1988. From this we might conclude that EUC has decreased in importance over the five-year period, and perhaps offer suggestions for why this might have occurred.

There are, however, several potential problems with this approach. First, in two of the studies (1983 and 1986) the respondents were asked to rank the identified issues in order of importance, thereby producing ordinal data. Assigning ranks to a small group of issues can be challenging. But as the list grows to 20 or more items, it becomes extremely difficult at best. Second, the assignment of ranks does not allow for recognition of the magnitude of differences involved. To illustrate, in the 1988 study, the mean responses for Software

To repeat, one of the major advantages of recurring opinion surveys is in comparing previous research studies to determine trends. There were differences in the type of data reported from the studies, specifically “mean ranks” versus “ranked means.” In order to compare relative changes in the issues from one study to another, we standardized the data for each study based upon reported means and standard deviations to a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Table 2 illustrates the standardized scores for each of the surveys included in our comparison. Issues with missing values in the table were not identified in those studies.

Subsequent comparisons were made between each individual issue across studies. The t-test was most appropriate in determining statistical significance of each issue. Table 3 shows the results of the tests. We have no reason to believe that response bias nor the variability of responses has changed much over the past five years. Therefore, the standard deviations observed for the 1988 study were employed where necessary to conduct the tests. All of the standard deviations were tightly ranged from 0.74 to 0.99.

By standardizing the scores (responses) for each of the studies, it is possible to include some consideration of differences between studies. The use of t-tests gives an indication of whether or not the observed differences between studies are significant. A quick examination of Table 3 shows that many significant differences at the 0.01 level have been observed. Since we do not hypothesize a direction of change for any specific issue, the more stringent two-tailed test was employed. Of the 77 potential differences tested, 37 were not significant, 8 were statistically significant at the 0.05 level, and 32 were significant at the more conservative 0.01 level. But, these observations need to be treated with caution, since (1) the standard deviations employed were assumed to be relatively similar to the 1988 study, and (2) the differences in measures, instruments, and samples make interpretation somewhat tenuous at this point. Nevertheless, we feel reasonably confident in stating that a number of significant differences have occurred. There are a number of ways of examining and interpreting the results displayed in Table 3. Specifically, the analysis could be directed to differences between studies and/or by individual issue across all studies. First, one could examine the overall differences between pairs of studies. For example, the middle column displays the results of the comparison between the 1986 and 1988 studies. One would expect that, if the samples are similar, the general perceptions would not change substantially over the period of one year and hence there should be few statistically significant differences. In fact, only 2 of 14 potential differences were significant at the 0.01 level. Also, the pattern of differences, magnitude and direction, are roughly similar between 1983 and 1986, and 1983 and 1988. The only major exceptions are with Organizational Learning and Telecommunications, which showed significant differences between 1986 and 1988.

Interpretation of the above observations might proceed along the following lines. There is a general similarity in patterns observed between 1983 and 1986, and between 1983 and 1988. The samples used for the 1983 and 1986 studies were roughly the same. Also, there was a general lack of difference between the 1986 and 1988 results. This implies that the sample used for the 1988 study was also similar, since one would anticipate few significant changes in perception over the course of roughly one year. Our instrument in the 1988 was developed primarily from the 1983 and 1986 questionnaires.

Considering the 1985 study, however, 4 of the 11 potential differences between 1985 and 1986 were significant at the 0.01 level. Also, for 4 of the 11 issues compared between 1985 and 1986, the direction of change was different than that observed between 1983 and 1986. This could imply that, for a number of reasons (such as differences in the sample of wording and interpretation), the observations from the 1985 study might not be directly comparable with those from the other three.

A second approach to interpreting observed results would be to examine them issue by issue. For example, End-User Computing, Software Development, and IT Integration all show fairly strong trends of decreasing in importance. The differences, especially for the longer time periods, are statistically significant and the t-statistic values are relatively large. One would, therefore, feel somewhat confident in concluding that these issues have decreased in importance over time. In contrast, IS Role and Use of Data appear to have increased in importance.

Difficulties arise, however, when attempting to interpret results on an individual issue basis. For example, with Organizational Learning, the observed difference was strong and positive between 1983 and 1986 and strong and negative between 1983 and 1988. How should this finding be interpreted? Do the results from one or more of the studies represent an anomaly? Could it be that the issue was interpreted differently by the respondents in one of the studies? Following our examination of individual issues across studies, we identified groups of issues in order to develop managerial and technical composites.

## Grouping Issues

Rather than mapping relative rankings for individual issues into composites as did the previous surveys, we attempted to categorize the issues into four of five groups to identify issues at the margin. Categorizing the issues allows us to visualize the movements in clusters, even though individual issues may show positive or negative trends within that cluster, as we demonstrated earlier. As a starting point, we used the Brancheau and Wetherbe study, which divided the issues into the two general categories of managerial and technical groups. This initial categorization provided an intuitive breakdown of the issues while also yielding a more manageable number of variables in each category. Following the approach used by Hartog and Herbert, factor analysis, a multivariate statistical technique, was employed to produce logical groupings of the IS issues. Table 4 depicts the final results of a factor analysis.

Five factors were extracted from the analysis. The Scree Test, a plot of factors against eigenvalues, was used to eliminate factors which did not significantly contribute to explaining the variance. The cumulative percent of variance explained includes 69.8% and 61.1% for each of the factor runs. No negative loadings or unexplainable factors resulted from this analysis. Factors were retained with loadings greater than 0.40. Although 0.30 is often used, Nunnally suggests more stringent factor retention guidelines with smaller sample sizes [10].

The results of the factor analysis indicated at least one difference from the general managerial-technical categorization used by Brancheau and Wetherbe. Specifically, Managing and Developing Decision Support Systems did not appear to be aligned either with technical nor managerial issues. In general, however, the factors verified the authors' independent groupings. For example, the issue of Planning and Implementing Telecommunications and the Integration of Technologies intuitively are closely related. Managing End-User Computing was placed into two categories. Coincidentally, this same variable was placed in two factors in the Hartog and Herbert study. One variable, Managing Office Automation, was not classified in our factor analysis. We felt that this question cut across all categories, also our guidelines eliminated items with a loading less than 0.4.

Factor analysis is often used for construct confirmation, yet it is most appropriate as an exploratory tool. This analysis proved to be worthwhile at the margin, identifying issues that logically fit into neither category or need to be loaded into two categories. The factor analysis, then, allowed us to group related issues into the broad managerial and technical categories to further test trends between studies. We found two factors of managerial variables: Planning/Management and IS Organization, and two factors of technical variables: Development and Integration. Decision Support Systems was placed in a unique factor and we could not find evidence that it was solely managerial nor technical in nature.

## Uncovering Managerial and Technical Trends

In order to uncover significant movements in each of the trend categories, the ranked data were tested for differences. Initially, the Wilcoxin Signed Rank Test, a nonparametric statistical procedure, was suggested to detect differences with ordinal data between each of the ranked samples. However, Conover recommends using the more powerful Walsh test, identical to the Wilcoxin Test for sample sizes less than seven [4]. The Walsh Test, similar to the Wilcoxin Signed Rank Test, utilizes information about the direction and the relative magnitude of the differences considered. Siegel reported the power-efficiency of the Walsh Test as high as 99 percent and nowhere lower than 87.5 percent for most values of N and alpha [11]. He also suggested using significance levels as high as 0.20 for exploratory research. However, we used the more stringent alpha value of 0.10 as a cutoff point in order to detect significant differences consistent with our individual issues analysis.

Due to different sets of issues among each of the previous studies, it was necessary to adjust the rankings to a common set. This was accomplished by removing those issues which were not included in the current study and adjusting the ranks accordingly, as described earlier. The 1980 study was not included in our analysis, primarily because a significant number of its issues were not present in the later surveys.

We tested our hypotheses that the differences in ranks are decreasing over time, meaning that (1) the ranks in managerial issues are getting larger, while (2) the ranks in technical issues are declining in importance. The Wilcoxin Signed Rank Test (not shown here) was also conducted for each of the samples of differences and verified the results of the Walsh test.

Surprisingly, managerial issues have not increased in importance over the years, but remained relatively stable. No significant differences were found with either of the managerial factor composites. Therefore, our hypothesis that managerial issues are increasing in importance was not supported. This finding differs from the 1986 study where they stated that “management/enterprise issues have increased in importance”. It is clear that certain individual issues have increased while others have decreased since 1983. Taken as a group, the managerial issues have not significantly increased in importance, although they may be exhibiting an upward trend visually.

As expected, technical issues exhibited a significant decline in importance since 1983. Differences were detected between the 1988 study and both the 1983 and 1985 studies. Significant differences were also found between the 1986 and 1985 studies; this supports our premise that the 1988 study was not an anomaly. Our second test hypothesis was supported. This is not a contradiction when compared with the results of the first test, the stability of managerial issues. Although there has been an increase in the importance of managerial issues in three of the survey comparisons, the stringency of the Walsh test failed to show statistical significance.

Finally though, the overall stability in the importance of managerial issues has naturally resulted in a decline in the technical issues taken as a group. This finding supports the 1986 findings where a steady decline was observed [2]. One should not interpret the decline of technical issues as unimportant for the 1990s; rather, they are no longer critical to IS executives.

## Conclusions

One should use caution in interpreting the key issues and study results presented here. Information systems and related management issues are dependent on the mix between business and new technologies. One example of this is the duality of the End-User Computing issue and our failure to categorize Office Automation as either managerial or technical. Differences in the sample of respondents across the studies suggests caution in interpreting the findings.

Comparing issues across the four studies also presented some problems to the researchers. For one thing, different methods of rating responses was used. Brancheau and Wetherbe reported ranks, while Hartog and Herbert presented their results as rated means on a four-point scale.

The authors argue that to facilitate the monitoring of trends or to aid in forecasting the importance of issues, it is necessary to adopt standard instruments employing more discriminant interval-scale measures. Although individual issues may be added or dropped over time as the need dictates, those which remain should use constant descriptions to ensure reliable interpretation by respondents. Unfortunately, we see the opposite happening. Numerous IS issue surveys are appearing which do not exhibit appropriate psychometric properties. This results in a proliferation of IS research which is not generalizable, comparable, nor useful.

Factor analysis, used here to categorize the issues, proved useful in distinguishing between managerial and technical responses. The breakdown of issues is relatively consistent with the Brancheau and Wetherbe logical categorization of management-enterprise and technical-application issues.

The Walsh test, a nonparametric statistical technique, was useful in identify general trends empirically among the four studies. Managerial issues were not found to increase significantly in importance, while technical issues therefore declined since 1985.
