# Utilizing High Technology: Computer-Aided-Design and User Performance

Hal G. Gueutal

State University of New York at Albany, Albany, NY 12222, USA

A recently developed work-sample test was employed to assess individual performance utilizing computer-aided design technology. Overall task performance, as well as perceived difficulty, confidence, and latency, were found to be predicted by a hypothesized set of individual difference variables. Task performance, was found to be associated with perceptual skill and degree of experience. Confidence in judgments was predicted by perceptual skill, attitudes, and sex of the operator. Perceived difficulty was predicted by perceptual skill and attitudes. The final criterion variable, latency, was found to be predicted by attitudes and sex of the operator. Some of the most interesting findings relate to sex differences. Overall, the task performance of males and female subjects did not differ. However, while female subjects accomplished the task in less time, they were less confident in their judgments and found the task to be more difficult. Findings are discussed as they pertain to previous research and employee selection.

Keywords: Computer-aided-design, User performance, Selecting employees, Individual differences, Job performance.

Introduction

The impact of new technologies on design and manufacturing has been the subject of much discussion. New technologies are often cast as critical to enhancing productivity and competitiveness. In the design area, Computer-Aided-Design (CAD) systems are now the technology of choice in the development of products ranging from integrated circuits to medical vaccines. CAD systems also assist the management of manufacturing operations, allowing the configuration of processes and products to be rapidly changed to meet market needs. As CAD applications become widespread, an increasing percentage of the work force will find that computer graphics applications have become an integral part of their job. It is critical, therefore, to understand those variables which underlie effective job performance when utilizing CAD technology. Such understanding will help ensure that organizations realize possible productivity benefits. The search presented here is among the first investigations to evaluate the correlates of effective CAD performance.

CAD utilizes interactive computer graphics technology to replace the laborious and time consuming process of manual drafting. By using CAD systems, changes, revisions, and redesigns can be made much more quickly than is possible through manual methods. In addition, more advanced systems allow changes made in one portion of the design to be automatically reflected in other related parts.

This research presents a new work-sample CAD test and investigates the role of several factors hypothesized to be predictive of task performance and affective outcomes.

## Literature Review

The need for a growing pool of educated and competent CAD users is well documented $[6]$ . However, a poll of CAD vendors $[3]$ suggested that only 40% of draftspersons converting to CAD systems eventually become expert operators. Foundylier attributes this lack of success to the “aptitude” of the users, but does not discuss the specific aptitude variables that may explain this performance difference. Unfortunately, virtually no published research specifically addresses individual differences or user characteristics as they impact effective CAD utilization. In the absence of reliable empirical information, industry has had to rely on anecdotal stories, untested interviews, observation, and surveys to identify applicants who hopefully will be better performers $[10]$ .

The lack of empirical literature describing the determinants of effective CAD performance stems primarily from the fact that there are no standardized measures of task performance. While objective system attributes (e.g., calculations per second, resolution of the screen, error checking) may be assessed, there are no published measures of user skill or performance with CAD systems. Therefore, in order to conduct research in this area, a measure of CAD performance had to be developed. This measure, the Computer Graphics Performance Test (CGPT), is based on job analysis interviews with practicing CAD engineers and discussions with academics involved in CAD systems development. The CGPT may be viewed as a work-sample test, since it incorporates critical elements of the engineer/designer's job in a scoreable testing format.

Having developed a criterion measure, the next step was to determine, based on relevant literature, a preliminary set of variables that may predict performance on the task. A body of literature exists pertaining to user skills and characteristics as they relate to the use of other types of computer systems. For example, general cognitive ability, experience, and decision making behavior have been studied in the context of Management Information Systems (MIS) [18]. Given the difficulty inherent in evaluating a complex technology such as MIS [4,9], the criterion measures typically used have been attitudinal (e.g., user satisfaction), rather than actual job performance. Similarly, the small amount of research that has focused on users and computer graphics has been restricted to attitudinal surveys of job incumbents and their managers.

Human factors researchers have contributed to understanding user–system interactions [12]. However, much of this research has been restricted to questions of system physical characteristics rather than characteristics of the user: the focus has been on making systems easier to use, rather than studying how user characteristics affect job performance; the typical dependent variables were attitudinal or fatigue-related.

Given the relative lack of extant literature pertaining specifically to CAD performance, it is necessary to draw from related areas of study. These include: industrial psychology, interactive graphics systems, and MIS. Based upon this literature, the potential predictors of CAD performance can be grouped into six classes.

The classes of predictor variables are: cognitive, attitudinal, experience, demographic, system design characteristics, and work setting. These are hypothesized to be predictors of three types of outcomes: objective task performance, latency, and affective reactions. Fig. 1 summarizes these relationships. Operationally the cognitive variables measured were general cognitive ability and field independence/dependence. The attitudinal measure consisted of a scale defining “attitudes toward computers.” The experience variable was the user’s amount of computer experience, and the demographic variable was the sex of the operator. System design characteristics and work setting were not varied in this research; no specific hypotheses were therefore tested with regard to them. Objective task performance was defined as the number of correct responses on the CGPT. Latency referred to the amount of time required for the user to reach a decision. The affective outcomes measured were user confidence and perceived difficulty.

The first set of predictor variables related to cognitive skills. These are thought to impact reasoning and perceptual processes. In the present investigation, general cognitive ability and field independence were chosen to represent two components of cognitive skills.

General cognitive ability may be viewed as a measure of reasoning ability or, by some definitions, general intelligence. Past research suggests that cognitive skills such as general intelligence have a direct effect on decision making performance when using a MIS. A recent review of the relationship between cognitive ability and job performance [7] concluded that cognitive ability is among the best overall predictors of job performance. Hunter states, “General cognitive ability predicts performance ratings in all lines of work, though validity is much higher for complex jobs than for simple jobs.” Other research [8] reviewed 287 studies of cognitive ability and performance on general technical jobs and found an average validity coefficient of 0.62. Therefore, it seems reasonable to suggest that general cognitive ability will impact performance on a complex task, such as CAD work, where users must associate several items of visual information and, through a process of analysis and elimination, determine the correct response. Individuals with higher levels of general cognitive ability should do better on the task, find the task less difficult, and have greater confidence in their judgment. The measure of general cognitive ability used in this study, the Wonderlic Personnel Test [17], has been shown to have predictive validity for various engineering jobs.

The second cognitive variable, field independence/dependence, is a perceptual skill which describes the ability of individuals to extract information from a complex visual field. Field independent individuals are more easily able to disembed a simple shape from a complex pattern. That is, they tend to perceive stimuli as discrete from the background in which the stimuli is presented. MIS research has reported that field independent users performed better than field dependent users in an active problem-solving-task utilizing visually portrayed information [1]. Given the highly visual nature of CAD work, involving sophisticated line drawings, it seems reasonable to hypothesize that users who can more-easily disembed specific portions of a drawing from the visual field will be more effective performers. Field independent users should be able to separate out the lines on a given geometric plane from a three dimensional drawing more easily than field dependent users; given their perceptual skill, field independent users are hypothesized to find CAD tasks less difficult and have greater confidence in their judgment.

The attitudinal variable, attitudes toward computers, was included based on a relatively large body of theory and previous research which suggests that attitudes impact behavior [cf., 5,14]. It is hypothesized that users who have negative attitudes toward computers will view the task as more difficult, take longer to reach a decision, and perform more poorly than users with more positive attitudes.

Users with greater amounts of experience (average hours per week spent working with computers) should have less difficulty in making judgments and exhibit greater confidence in their decisions [2]. Experience with computers was therefore expected to be positively related to task performance and confidence, and negatively related to perceived difficulty and latency.

The fourth set of potential predictors was demographic variables. In this investigation, the only demographic variable was sex of the user. Other demographic variables, such as user age, were not included as there was very little variation in age among the sample studied. With regard to user sex, discussions with faculty and engineers suggested that female students and engineers seemed to report difficulty with the CAD systems more often than male users. This may be due to a bias on the part of the mostly male interviewees or the result of women being less confident in their performance on a job that has been traditionally dominated by male engineers. Since no prior data existed to verify an actual difference, the inclusion of user sex as a variable in the analysis must be viewed as exploratory. It is hypothesized that while male and female users will perform equally well of the task, female users will report less confidence in judgments, experience more difficulty, and take longer to reach a decision.

## Hypotheses

$H_{1}$ : Performance on the CGPT will be positively related to field independence, general cognitive ability, degree of computer experience, and attitudes toward computers.

$H_{2}$ : Perceived task difficult will be negatively related to field independence, degree of computer experience, general cognitive ability, and attitudes toward computers. Female users will rate the task as more difficult than male operators.

H3: Confidence in the correctness of the user's decision will be positively related to attitudes toward computers, degree of computer experience, general cognitive ability, and field independence. Rated confidence in decisions will be lower for female operators than for male operators.

$H_{4}$ : The amount of time required to make the decision will be negatively related to attitudes toward computers, degree of computer experience, general cognitive ability, and field independence. Female operators will take more time to reach a decision than will male operators.

## Method

## Subjects

The subjects were drawn from graduate and undergraduate classes at a technically-oriented university in the Northeastern USA. Eighty-eight individuals, 58 male and 30 female, participated. The mean age was 24 years. Participants were equally divided between graduates and undergraduates. Technically trained individuals constituted 68% (n = 60) of the sample and nontechnically trained 32% (n = 28). There was no difference between male and female in the proportion of subjects who were technically trained. Virtually all subjects had used mainframe computing systems in their course work and/or job.

## Measures

Independent variables. The independent (predictor) variables were assessed via three measures. These were: the Wonderlic Personnel Test, the Group Embedded Figures Test, and a questionnaire tapping demographic and computer experience.

The Wonderlic Personnel Test is a commonly used test of general cognitive skill or ability. The version used in this study was Form I, 1981 Revision. Subjects had 12 minutes to complete as many of 50 items as possible. The total number correct was used as an index of general cognitive ability. Scores ranged from 18 to 41 with a mean of 29.74 (sd = 4.9). This mean compares favorably with those reported for samples of engineers and college graduates (reported mean = 29). The Wonderlic has been used in a variety of employment settings and has been shown to be related to job performance across a range of jobs [15].

The Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) [16] is a paper and pencil test used to assess the degree of field independence/dependence among subjects. The test requires subjects to identify target outlines embedded in complex geometric patterns. Individuals who can accomplish this task successfully are considered field independent. Scores on the GEFT can range from 0 to 18. Past research indicates split-half reliability coefficients of approximately $r_{xx} = 0.80$ . The mean number correct in the present study was 15.6 (sd = 2.69, range 7 to 18) compared with a national mean of approximately 11.3. This level of performance could serve to attenuate the relationship of GEFT scores and the criterion variables.

The demographics and computer experience questionnaire was adapted from prior research $[13]$ and collected background data and specific information regarding experience with computers. Items pertained to computer related educational and work experience, leisure computer use, experience with drafting and graphics, and attitudes toward computers. Degree of computer experience was defined as the average number of hours of computer usage per week. This included all types of computer usage including employment, course-related, and personal. The attitudes toward computers scale was made up of five Likert-type items. The reliability (internal consistency) of the scale was $\alpha = 0.77$ .

Dependent Variables. The Computer Graphics Performance Test is a recently developed CAD task. It assesses the subject's ability to perform a critical component of engineering jobs and may be viewed as a work-sample test. Specifically the CGPT assesses the subject's ability to integrate computer generated two-dimensional graphic information and correctly identify a three-dimensional (isometric) CAD engineering drawing. These two dimensional perspectives, known as “wireframes”, are typical of engineering drawings.

Some systems allow the engineer to create 3-D representations from these 2-D drawings. It should be noted however, that many of these systems present a solid image, thereby obscuring the structural detail of the object, or present a 3-D wire frame image without automatically identifying any errors in the resulting view. Thus, it is possible to draw an object based upon 2-D views which is impossible to actually construct; e.g., a 2-D design of a plane wing could have two or more support struts pass through the same physical point. Consequently, the ability to design in 2-D space and visualize the result in 3-D is a critical job skill. Indeed, based upon discussions with engineers using and academics researching CAD technology, it was felt that the CGPT evaluates critical job skills required in CAD engineering jobs. Further, the ability to utilize 2-D engineering drawings and visualize a 3-D composite was considered a prerequisite of adequate job performance.

Specifically, the CGPT presents subjects with 2-D top, side, and front views of an object (the standard engineering views) and asks them to identify which of three 3-D views (also presented on the CRT screen) is correct, based upon the two-dimensional information. Version 2.0 of the test, used in the present study, contained 20 stimulus screens. Fig. 2 presents a sample screen.

The drawings included in the CGPT were based upon problems drawn from engineering and drafting texts, as well as actual mechanical devices designed with the aid of CAD technology. The stimulus screens in the test were divided into five sections of four items each. The equipment used to design and administer the CGPT was a commercial CAD system, specifically a Prime 750 mainframe computer and software and IMLAC high resolution raster graphics terminals.

Perceived task difficulty and confidence in the correctness of the solution chosen were measured following each stimulus screen. Subjects rated how difficult they felt the design problem had been and how confident they were in their solution. The difficulty scale ranged from “not at all difficult” to “extremely difficult.” Likewise, the confidence scale ranged from “not at all confident” to “extremely confident.”

The last criterion variable, latency, was automatically measured by the system. A timer was started as soon as the subject requested the stimulus screen and was stopped when the subject stated and confirmed their solution.

## Procedure

The study was conducted in two sessions approximately one week apart. During session 1, the informed consent agreement, demographic and computer experience questionnaire, the GEFT, and the Wonderlic Personnel Test were completed. The order in which the measures were administered was counter-balanced across individual groups. Session 1 lasted approximately 75 minutes.

Session 2 involved collection of the CGPT data. A maximum of three users were run simultaneously on the CGPT using individual work stations. Starting times were staggered so that each participant could be given individual instruction, and to ensure that all subjects understood the procedures. Written instructions (read aloud) were used to standardize the administration of the task and the collection of the data. Participants were informed that they were being timed on the task, but that there was no time limit. Subjects used a light pen to indicate which of three images they believed was the correct solution. The system then indicated their answer and asked for a confirmation. The response and amount of time required to make the decision (latency) were then automatically recorded. Immediately after completing each item in the CGPT, subjects rated the perceived

## Results

The data were analyzed utilizing correlation and stepwise multiple regression techniques. Table 1 presents the zero-order correlations and table 2 presents the regression analyses.

The third hypothesis was that user confidence would be positively related to attitudes toward computers, degree of computer experience, general cognitive ability, and field independence. Males were expected to report greater confidence in decisions. The multiple correlation coefficient for this analysis was $R_{m}=0.638\ (p<0.001)$ . Examination of the beta weights shows field independence (beta = 0.247, p<0.01), attitudes toward computers (beta = 0.251, p<0.01), and sex of the operator (beta = -0.472, p<0.001) to be significant. Neither degree of experience or general cognitive ability was found to be related to perceived confidence. Hypothesis 3 was supported, with three of the five predictor variables entering the equation.

The fourth hypothesis dealt with the relationship of latency to the previously noted independent variables. The $R_{m}$ for this analysis was 0.36 (p < 0.01). Variables entering the equation were attitudes toward computers (beta = 0.294, p < 0.01) and sex of the operator (beta = 0.278, p < 0.01). While sex of the operator was significantly related to latency, the direction of the relationship was opposite that expected. That is, while females were hypothesized to spend more time in decision making than males, the data show that the opposite was true. Interestingly, latency was the only criterion variable which was not related to field independence.

## Discussion

The results of this study suggest that a variety of factors may be important in explaining task performance in the use of CAD technology. In addition, significant portions of the variance in user confidence, perceived difficulty, and latency can be explained based on some of the same predictors.

Task performance on the CGPT was most clearly related to perceptual skill, specifically field independence. Users who were field independent did substantially better in arriving at correct decisions. Fourteen percent of the variance in task performance could be accounted by field independence alone. This result compares favorably with our previous research, though the magnitude of the relationship was somewhat lower; this may be have occurred because the range of GEFT scores was restricted and resulted in an underestimate of the true correlation. These findings may therefore be viewed as conservative estimates of the relationship between this perceptual skill and CGPT performance. Degree of computer experience was also found to be related to CGPT performance. Inspection of the zero-order correlations for attitudes toward computers and user sex shows that both variables approached significance. Consequently, it would be unwise to exclude these factors from future consideration.

Most surprising was that general cognitive ability did not relate to CGPT performance. Both previous research with the CGPT and reviews of the predictive validity of cognitive factors suggested that these skills would relate to task performance. Since the findings are therefore somewhat inconsistent, further investigation is needed. For example, alternative measures of general cognitive ability should be studied as they relate to CAD job performance. Further work, in which a greater portion of the domain of general cognitive ability is sampled, may better test the link between it and performance utilizing CAD systems.

The results with regard to perceived difficulty and confidence provide some of the most interesting findings. As before, field independence was associated in the hypothesized direction with both outcomes. That is, individuals who were field independent reported greater confidence and less difficulty. While not measured, it seems reasonable to suggest that users who are more confident and find the work less difficult may be more motivated and have higher overall job satisfaction. Not surprisingly, subjects with more positive attitudes found the task to be less difficult and were also more confident.

Sex of the operator was strongly related to confidence and, to a lesser extent, perceived difficulty, but not related to performance. Female subjects were significantly less confident in their judgments and found the task more difficult even though they were as accurate as their male counterparts. If this finding generalizes to the workplace, employing organizations may need to give special attention to female employees in order to remedy this “deficit”.

The final criterion variable was latency. This was the only dependent variable with which field independence was not found to be associated. This suggests that while field dependent users do less well, have less confidence in their judgments, and have more difficulty making them, they do not tend to take any longer to make them. This is somewhat surprising, as it would seem reasonable that these individuals might try to compensate for their difficulty by spending more time in the judgment process. One potential explanation for this may be that field dependent users realize their difficulty, become frustrated, and move through the test as quickly as possible to “escape” the unpleasant task.

With regard to attitudes and sex of the user, both were found to predict latency. Specifically, females (as well as those with more positive attitudes) took less time arriving at a decision. This is somewhat surprising, since female operators were less confident and found the task more difficult. The “escape” response may also operate here.

The limitations of the current research should be noted. First, the Computer Graphics Performance Test is a recently developed work-sample criterion measure that has not been linked to actual job performance. As a result, the generalizability of these findings to the work place is untested. Clearly, the CGPT does not assess all the performance factors included in the work of engineers and designers utilizing CAD technology, rather it is designed to assess performance on a critical skill dimension. Future research will seek to link performance on the CGPT with measures of actual CAD engineering performance. Establishing such a linkage will support the generalizability of these results and enhance the utility of the test to researchers and practitioners.

The second limitation concerns the representativeness of the sample. There is often a question about the generalizability of the results based on a “student” sample. In this study, subjects were drawn primarily from engineering classes; they either had experience with the technology or would utilize CAD systems in the future. They represent the next generation of CAD users. For this reason, these result (especially those pertaining to enduring traits such as general cognitive ability and field independence) may well generalize to the workplace. However, this remains an empirical question.

## Conclusions and Implications

Computer-Aided-Design utilizing computer graphics is rapidly becoming the technology of choice for the design of a wide range of products. Projections indicate that virtually all organizations engaged in design and manufacturing will be required to adopt CAD technology to remain competitive. A recent review of CAD software [11] noted that, in 1982, CAD systems averaged \$110,000 per workstation. Today however, users may acquire the hardware and software required to implement a similar CAD system for an investment of less than \$10,000. The critical issue then, is not in buying the technology, but in its effective utilization. This research provides some of the first empirical findings related to factors which impact individual performance in using this emerging technology.

Four sets of predictor variables (perceptual, cognitive, attitudinal, experience, and demographic) were investigated as they relate to four outcome variables (task performance, user confidence, perceived difficulty, and latency). The results indicate a consistent relationship between perceptual skill and task performance, confidence, and difficulty. In addition, experience was important in predicting task performance. These results suggest that the job performance of CAD users may be maximized by selecting those who are field independent and have had substantial experience with computer systems. In addition, training costs and time may be lower for individuals with the perceptual skills which facilitate job performance.

Employers filling positions involving the use of CAD equipment may find perceptual skill measures of value in identifying effective job performers. Some individuals, due to their perceptual skills, may be less likely to succeed, report less confidence, and find CAD work more difficult. Consequently, employers may wish to include such measures in the assessment of the potential job success of applicants for positions requiring CAD equipment use. This can lead to higher overall levels of job performance and reduced turnover. Likewise, if the CGPT is shown to be empirically related to job performance, it may be utilized as a new selection technology.

One potentially troubling finding was the relationship of user sex to confidence and difficulty. That is, female employees may be less confident in the results of their work when utilizing CAD systems and perceive the work to be more difficult. This is troublesome for several reasons. First, the confidence deficit is not based on a performance differential. In fact, it may be argued that female employees will be superior performers when compared with males since they accomplished the task as accurately, but in less time. However, if this lower confidence levels translates to turnover, or fewer women entering jobs requiring system use, a broad group of the work force may not be adequately represented in jobs utilizing this critical technology. Organizations may consequently need to give special attention to female users to remedy confidence problems that could develop and adversely impact productivity, morale, and retention.

Clearly, a great deal of research remains to be conducted before a more complete understanding of the factors which underlie job performance in the utilization of CAD technology is realized. Generalizations from these data must be made very cautiously. Other cognitive/perceptual variables such as cognitive complexity and decision making style should be included in future research. Likewise, additional experience and background factors (e.g., specific course work, years of work experience, experience with a variety of computer systems) should be studied. Research should also continue on the development of the CGPT and seek to assess its predictive validity with respect to actual CAD job performance.
