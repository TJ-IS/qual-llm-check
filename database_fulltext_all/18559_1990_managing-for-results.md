# Managing for results

# An examination of professional group perceptions of organizational practices

Charles K. Woodruff

Winthrop College, Rock Hill, SC 29733, USA

The primary purpose of the study was to ascertain if organizational practices, as perceived by individuals comprising information systems (IS) professional groups (i.e., system analysts, programmer analysts, programmers, and operations personnel), are related to job satisfaction and job performance, and, if such relationships exist, to determine which perceived organizational practices (factors) are significant for each IS professional group. Job satisfaction variance is explained by people, structural, and task oriented factors. Job performance variance is explained only by people oriented factors. Significant factors vary for each IS professional group.

Keywords: Practices, Professional Group Perceptions, Job Satisfaction, Job Performance, Motivation, People Practices Perceptions, Structural Practices Perceptions, Task Practices Perceptions.

## Introduction

Human resource management (HRM) problems have become one of the most perplexing challenges facing the information systems (IS) organization. Although such problems are by no means of recent origin, their impact upon the IS organization has continued to grow in spite of concerted efforts by IS management. While much of this growth may be attributed to the expansion of the overall role of the IS organization itself, the urgency of the HRM problems has been exacerbated by the long term trend of decreasing costs of hardware compared to personnel.

Efforts of IS management to control hardware costs have benefited from economies derived from hardware technology development. Unfortunately, IS management has frequently been frustrated in their efforts to control costs ascribable to personnel. Many IS managers find themselves unprepared by training, experience, or personal attributes to deal with the seeming myriad of problems concerning IS personnel; e.g., productivity, work quality, motivation, creativity, turnover, absenteeism, and commitment.

Research on IS personnel has contributed to a better understanding of these problems. For example, one area focuses on the planning, organizing, and coordinating considerations within IS organizations $[29,32,38]$ while another on job design-motivation research $[9,10,11,14]$ has typically built upon the Job Characteristics Model of Hackman and Oldham $[15,16]$ . Productivity issues have been addressed from a variety of perspectives $[6,25,35]$ with project teams receiving the attention of various group dynamics researchers $[20,23,37]$ . Commitment and turnover problems have been the focus of several research efforts $[2,3,4,34]$ . Occupational stress and related health problems $[19]$ also have received the attention of IS researchers. Bartol and Martin [5] have produced a review of some of the IS personnel research.

If the underlying behavioral factors in the work setting can be identified and assessed, value may accrue from identifying the viable options, determining tradeoffs among these options, formulating a well defined direction focus, establishing appropriate priorities, allocating resources, and better managing time and energies.

A construct that offers significant potential is the culture of the work place. Schein defines culture as “... the pattern of basic assumptions that a given group has invented, discovered or developed in learning to cope with its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, which have worked well enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel in relation to those problems.” [31] A more succinct definition is offered by Gibson, Ivancevich, and Donnelly; culture is “... a system of shared values and beliefs that produce norms of behavior. Values (what is important) and beliefs (how things work) interact to cause norms (how we should do things).” [13]

Culture exists at all levels. For example, Reich [30] posits that Americans have been required to choose between a culture typified by the realm of government and politics versus the realm of business and economics; a choice that often is posed inappropriately. At the corporate level, Peters and Waterman, in In Search of Excellence, state: "Without exception, the dominance and coherence of culture proved to be an essential quality of the excellent companies." [28] Ouchi [27] points out that Japanese companies utilize a basic mechanism of control which is embodied in a philosophy of management reinforced by the culture of the organization. Deal and Kennedy [12] emphasize the importance of the underlying subcultures: it may be positive or negative in support of the larger, encompassing culture. A culture is positive if it contributes to the effective performance and efficiency of the organization. A negative culture typically produces turmoil and resistance as reflected by lowered performance and efficiency.

While much has been written about organization cultures and subcultures, little empirical research is reported. However, the complementary concept of organizational climate enjoys extensive research support. Although it evolved independently of “culture,” its roots in psychological theory have fostered the development of valid and reliable measurement instruments from its inception. Tagiuri and Litwin [33] offer the most frequently quoted definition of organizational climate “… a relatively enduring quality of the internal environment of an organization that is experienced by its members, influences their behavior, and can be described in terms of the values of a particular set of characteristics of the organization.”

In noting a kind of merging of organizational culture and climate, Ashforth states:

“Given the view of culture as shared assumptions and values, it should not be surprising that the concept may have come to encompass that of climate. It is not a large conceptual step from shared assumptions (culture) to shared perceptions (climate). To be sure, the concepts do slide greyly into one another. ... Culture ... informs climate in two ways. First, it does so directly by helping individuals to define what is important and to make sense of their experiences. ... Second, culture informs climate indirectly via its very impact on the objective work environment – the raw material of climate perceptions.” [1]

## Miner adds,

“...climates would fall somewhere between values and the artifacts and creations that are the visible manifestations of culture. Because of the nature of climates and the ways in which they are measured, people are fully aware of organizational climates. Climates are perceived and thought about. From this standpoint, climates represent a particular level of culture – one that is important but has not been given the attention it deserves by culture theorists and researchers. ... Given that organizational culture apparently affects performance and productivity, and that culture and climate are related, organizational climate should have implications for performance as well.” [26]

In addition, he argues that organizational climate arises from and is sustained by those organizational practices and customary activities valued by the organizational members and the organization itself. Practices simply are those actions taken within the organization in order to accomplish objectives. Some practices are formally defined by written policies, procedures, rules, etc.; others may be defined informally by structures, roles, norms, traditions, etc. For the individual organizational member, the challenge is to rationalize the proper meaning of the formal and informal organizational practices and their interactions within the climate of the organizational setting.

In order to focus on the organizational member, an assessment of practices must be determined for each individual. Measurement at this level means that perception of organizational practices held by each individual must be evaluated. One consequence is that one person's perception of a situation may vary from another's. However, as Hackman and Lawler state, “... it is the redefined task rather than the objective task which the individual tries to perform, and thus only those aspects of tasks or jobs which are actually perceived or experienced by a performer can have an impact on his performance and attitudes." [17] Therefore, it is the perception of an organizational practice that reflects the redefinition of the assigned tasks, rather than underlying objective organizational practice.

Figure 1 conveys the fundamental relationships between job satisfaction and job performance of the individual and the encompassing culture of the organization. While culture is characterized by shared assumptions held by organization members, culture informs climate which is perceived (shared) by organization members. In turn, formal and informal organizational practices interact with climate. From this interaction, each organization member through individual perceptions of the practices of the organization strives to give meaning to the practices. The process of perception involves receiving and selecting environmental stimuli, organizing and storing them, and interpreting them to form affective responses (e.g., job satisfaction) and influence behavior (e.g., job performance).

## The Study

Participants in the study were 202 employees in 12 IS organizations in a metropolitan district located in the southeastern United States. The professional groups consist of system analysts (n = 16), programmer analysts (n = 52), programmers (n = 63), and operations personnel (n = 71), which include supervisors, console operators, tape/disk and forms handlers, data control coordinators, etc. Data entry operators, library employees, employees with dedicated hardware or software expertise, and administrators were excluded from the study. All centers are business data processing centers for firms engaged in insurance, banking, utilities, manufacturing, and transportation.

Males constitute 75 percent of the study participants. A profile of the typical participant reveals the persons to be between 26 and 35 years of age, married, and at least one but not more than five years of information systems experience with their first, current IS employer.

## Study Constructs

## Perceived organizational practices

House and Rizzo [18] developed an Organizational Practices Questionnaire from an earlier instrument, the Organizational Description Questionnaire [22]. This instrument consists of 100 items that are based on interview findings and concepts whose origins are the organic-mechanistic and differentiation-integration organization characteristics of Burns and Stalker [7], Lawrence and Lorsch [21], and McGregor's Theory X-Theory Y [24] leadership patterns.

Responses of the participants are measured on a seven point Likert scale; they were subjected to factor analyses which produced ten factors or scales of perceived organizational practices after elimination of certain items due to low reliability or weighting. The resulting scales have split half reliabilities that range from 0.67 to 0.93. See Appendix 1 for the definition of each scale.

## Job satisfaction

The long form of the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSQ) was utilized to measure a general satisfaction scale (computed by summation of one item from each of the twenty a priori scales). Reliability and validity data for the MSQ have been reported in Weiss, Dawis, England, and Lofquist [36]. Satisfaction in this instrument is defined as the degree of correspondence between the needs of the individual and the reinforcement of these needs within the work environment.

## Job performance

Each participant's manager or supervisor assessed performance by the multitrait measure approach. See Appendix 2 for the definition of each trait. A summatted measure was computed for this study.

## Perceived Organizational Practices

The factors as measured by the questionnaire present an interesting overview of organizational practice dimensions that elucidate and describe the environments of the IS organizations as perceived by the participants. These factors can be categorized into three orientations: (1) people, (2) structural, and (3) task.

## People orientations

These mirror perceptions held of the IS organization's managers as well as those of pressure and stress induced upon the individual. Perceptions of management held by organizational members include the ability of their manager to make appropriate decisions, identify and select valued personnel, listen to subordinates and their ideas, initiate and facilitate cooperative work groups, and prioritize the development of personnel. Views of stress experienced while performing tasks are perceptions held by the individual organizational member. Thus, people orientations include: (1) Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel, (2) Job Pressure, (3) Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation, and (4) Emphasis on Personnel Development.

## Structural orientations

These are perceptions of the form of the organization itself. The organizational form includes the allocation of tasks and corresponding authority-responsibility relationships, the formal reporting relationships (e.g., number of hierarchy levels, span of control), and the grouping into organization departments and sub-units to facilitate effective communication, coordination, adaptability, and integration of effort. Structural orientations thus include: (1) Adherence to Chain of Command and Avoidance of Conflict in Direction, (2) Formalization and Tolerance of Error, (3) Adequacy of Communication, and (4) Adequacy of Authority and Group Adaptability.

## Task orientations

These describe perceptions of the tasks or activities of the organization. They include the utilization of the selected technology to define the appropriate tasks and sub-tasks and their sequence of execution and the avoidance of dysfunctional conflict arising from issues of control, power, goal priorities, and benefit recipients. Perception of the planning process and its execution are viewed as the means whereby the strategic management of the system or project has been institutionalized. Task orientations include: (1) Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Planning and (2) Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Implementation.

## Interpretation and Evaluation

## Descriptive and correlation relationships

Table 1 presents the arithmetic mean, standard deviation (SD), and correlation coefficient relationships of each of the study variables. The arithmetic mean and standard deviation specify the level and dispersion of the values. Correlation coefficients describe the strength and direction or relationship between each of the study variables.

Several of them reflect noteworthy relationships. Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel, an overall assessment of management credibility by participants, correlates significantly in a positive direction with all other study variables except Job Pressure. In turn, Job Pressure correlates with fewer other study variables and is the only study variable with a negative correlation. Job Pressure perceptions appear to have inverse relationships with perceptions of (1) Adherence to Chain of Command and Avoidance of Conflict in Direction, (2) Adequacy of System/Project Planning, and (3) Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Implementation; interestingly, there is a positive relationship with Job Performance.

The outcome variable Job Satisfaction has significant positive relationships with all other study variables except Job Pressure. The outcome variable Job Performance has significant positive relationships with all other study variables except (1) Adherence to Chain of Command and Avoidance of Conflict in Direction, (2) Adequacy of System/Project Planning, and (3) Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Implementation.

## Stepwise regression procedure

Tables 2 through 6 present the regression of all IS personnel and each professional group (i.e., system analysts, programmer analysts, programmers, and operations personnel) on organizational outcomes (i.e., Job Satisfaction and Job Performance). All study data were converted into standardized Z-score values (i.e., population mean equals zero and a standard deviation equals one) prior to statistical analysis. The use of Z-score values permits direct associations to be made between comparable unit values of each study variable. Further, a stepwise regression procedure has been utilized in which the available independent variable (perceptions of organizational practices) that will make the largest contribution to the explained variance ( $R^{2}$ ) of the dependent variable (Job Satisfaction or Job Performance) is selected for the next entry into the regression equation. This establishes an ordering of entry that determines the most significant independent variable first and proceeds stepwise until the remaining independent variables fail to produce a statistically significant contribution.

The incremental increase of the shrunken explained variance ( $\Delta R^{2}$ ) reflects the contribution of the particular independent variable last entered into the regression equation.

The beta regression coefficient provides the means whereby the influence of each independent variable on the dependent variable may be assessed. For example, the top portion of Table 2 displays the results of regressing in a stepwise procedure the perceptions of organizational practices on the dependent variable Job Satisfaction.

The perception of Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel makes the greatest contribution to the explained variance of Job Satisfaction. If this were the only independent variable to consider, the beta regression coefficient of 0.652 means that when Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel changes by one SD unit the predicted Job Satisfaction score increases by 0.652 SD unit. (Remember that all SD units have a value of one.) As additional significant perceptions of organizational practices are added, the beta regression coefficients of previously entered perceptions of organizational practices decrease. For Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel, the beta regression coefficient decreases from 0.652 to 0.534 to 0.373 to 0.300 to 0.289 when the perception of Job Pressure is considered.

## Analysis of Results

## Job satisfaction

A review of Tables 2 through 6 reveals that people, structural, and task oriented factors to varying degrees regress on Job Satisfaction. Three of the perceived organizational practices failed to regress on Job Satisfaction: (1) Emphasis on Personnel Development (a people oriented factor), (2) Formalization and Tolerance of Error (a structural oriented factor), and (3) Adequacy of System/Project Planning (a task oriented factor). Two of the perceived organizational practices influence Job Satisfaction significantly: (1) Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel and (2) Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation (both people oriented factors).

## Job performance

Only three perceived organizational practices regressed on Job Performance: (1) Job Pressure, (2) Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation, and (3) Emphasis on Personnel Development (all people oriented factors). Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel, which influenced Job Satisfaction significantly, failed to regress on Job Performance. Interestingly, neither structural nor task oriented factors regressed on Job Performance. Given the extensive commitments made by most organizations to support both structural and task orientations in order to enhance Job Performance, this seems to be a paradox.

Conclusions

For the IS manager, what are the implications? Certainly the organizational outcomes, Job Satisfaction and Job Performance, are valued in IS organizations. The extent to which each is pursued varies among organizations. Data in this study indicate a statistically significant relationship between these outcomes, although the relationship is relatively weak (0.29 correlation coefficient in Table 1). While clarification of this relationship is beyond the scope of the study, much may be stated for a positive and significant correlation; e.g., reward satisfaction follows valued performance.

Job satisfaction variance is explained by people, structural, and task oriented factors, although the single incidence of a task oriented factor (Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Implementation for programmers) is a rather weak contribution. The two perceived organizational practices that influence Job Satisfaction most significantly, (1) Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel and (2) Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation, clearly deserve greater management attention. Emphasis on Personnel Development (a people oriented factor) failed to regress on Job Satisfaction, but had a significant influence on Job Performance.

Job performance variance is explained by only three of the people oriented factors: (1) Job Pressure, (2) Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation, and (3) Emphasis on Personnel Development. This finding might question the common emphasis on the structural and task oriented factors to enhance IS organization effectiveness. Certainly much effort in most IS organizations is devoted toward improved planning, communication, and avoidance of conflict. However, the relationship here is one of explained variance in the dependent variables as predictors. Nevertheless, IS managers might find greater payoffs should they pay greater attention to personnel development, receptiveness, and cooperation to enhance organization effectiveness.

As one views Tables 2 through 6 it becomes apparent that perceptions of organizational practices are related to both job satisfaction and job performance of the individual. The strength of the relationship and the significant perceived organizational practices mix varies, depending upon the professional group. Managers initiate and execute organizational practices at the organization level, not the level of each individual, and only limited at the level of the various professional groups. Nevertheless, one of the primary purposes of all managers is to influence, in favorable and valued directions, the behaviors of organization members. Therefore, the IS manager must temper the conceiving, development, and execution of practices at the organization level with the anticipation that such practices will be perceived differently within each professional group and, consequently, will influence varying individual attitudes and behaviors of the IS organization members.

## Appendix 1

Definition of Perceived Organizational Practices Factors

1. Management Ability to Make Decisions and Select Personnel (14 items; reliability: 0.93): Management is perceived as being able to make decisions that are accurate, clearly understood, and made in a timely manner. Management understands quantitative methods and analytic approaches to decision making, and utilizes these techniques when they are appropriate for dealing with complex decision problems. Personnel are selected by management on the basis of proven competence, with primary consideration given to members from within, rather than outside, the organization.

Sample Item: "It is difficult to get problems resolved because those in authority do not respond to or make prompt decisions on recommendations."

2. Job Pressure (4 items; reliability: 0.67): The degree to which manpower and time are inadequate to accomplish the tasks assigned for the schedule committed.

Sample Item: “There is always too much work and too little time or manpower to accomplish it.”

3. Management Receptiveness and Intergroup Cooperation (11 items; reliability: 0.81): Management is interested in and encourages suggestions from organization members at all levels. When a work group or individual needs help with a task, assistance is readily available from other organization members and work groups.

Sample Item: “My work group receives a good deal of cooperation from other groups.”

4. Emphasis on Personnel Development (4 items; reliability: 0.73): Management encourages and rewards the development of organization members through training, coaching, and counseling.

Sample Item: “Top management expects supervisors to develop the capabilities of their subordinates and they are rewarded for it.”

5. Adherence to Chain of Command and Avoidance of Conflict in Direction (8 items; reliability: 0.67): Members of the organization at all levels understand and observe the chain of command with a minimum of conflict in direction within the organization.

Sample Item: “There is some question about who is really running my group.”

6. Formalization and Tolerance of Error (7 items; reliability: 0.72): Duties, authority/accountability, and performance standards/appraisals have been established in writing, and are available to all organization members. Individual errors do not necessarily jeopardize the careers of members.

Sample Item: “My duties, authority and accountability are documented in policies, procedures or descriptions.”

7. Adequacy of Communication (5 items; reliability: 0.77): Capability of the organization communication channels, formal and informal, to provide information that is accurate, timely, and adequate to accomplish assigned tasks.

Sample Item: “Information is available as needed.”

8. Adequacy of Authority and Group Adaptability (6 items; reliability: 0.72): The degree to which authority is delegated consistent with the demands of the task and the organization members assigned. Work groups are capable of adapting quickly to changing directions when conditions necessitate such changes.

Sample Item: “Time is lost in getting a group to work effectively after a change in plans or a project change occurs.”

9. Adequacy of System / Project Planning (15 items; reliability: 0.91): Planning of systems/projects is considered adequate, so that the mission of each work group is communicated and understood by organization members, required specifications and material are available before work begins, and work progress is freely communicated to all affected groups and individuals.

Sample Item: “Written documents (such as budgets, schedules, project specifications, procedures, or program plans, job descriptions, etc.) are used as an integral part of the job.”

10. Avoidance of Conflict in System/Project Implementation (9 items; reliability: 0.85): The degree to which policies, procedures, guidelines, and standards of performance are followed with consistency to avoid conflict in the implementation of systems/projects.

Sample Item: “Work is completed only to find that it does not fit with the requirements of the overall task and therefore must be redone.”

## Appendix 2

Definition of Job Performance Characteristic Traits

Quantity of Work: The volume of useful output associated with the ratee's job assignment. Output is of sufficient quality to satisfy requirements of the job assignment over which the ratee has direct control.

Quality of Work: Those characteristics of the output which enhance the usefulness to the recipient. The ratee has direct control over the output quality and there is a minimum of subsequent rework of the output.

Job Knowledge and Skills: Those characteristics of the ratee which enable the ratee to sufficiently solve problems, technical, conceptual, structurally oriented, etc., that are normally encountered in the job assignment.

Judgment Ability: The ability of the ratee to exercise discretionary behavior to arrive at a wise decision when confronted with problem situations, often unstructured and at short notice.

Job Initiative: Those characteristics which allow the ratee to undertake, without specific instructions, actions and activities deemed to be desirable in the performance of the job assignment.

Adaptability: The ability of the ratee to adjust properly and expeditiously to changing and unstructured situations, as well as problems encountered in the job environment.

Cooperation: Those displayed characteristics of the ratee to act or operate jointly with facility users and fellow workers.

Innovativeness: The demonstrated ability of the ratee to introduce something new or novel to effect a desired change to alleviate or solve problems that are generally characterized by their uniqueness or complexity.
