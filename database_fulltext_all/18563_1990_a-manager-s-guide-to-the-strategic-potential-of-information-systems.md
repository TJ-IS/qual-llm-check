# A manager's guide to the strategic potential of information systems

Mark C.S. Lee and Dennis A. Adams

College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, Texas 77204-6282, USA

Much has been publicized about the strategic possibilities of information systems (e.g. Foremost McKesson's Economost, American Hospital Supply Corporation's ASAP and American Airlines' SABRE). CEOs now demand that their IS executives achieve similar successes. However, many executives have neither the opportunity nor the time to evaluate and to understand the many different proposed methodologies or frameworks that have been constructed to help identify strategic opportunities for information systems. This paper seeks to select the major frameworks available for determining the strategic opportunities of information systems, and then to evaluate them. Finally, we offer our perspective on these different models as representative of current management thought on the strategic nature of information systems.

Keywords: Competitive Advantage, Strategic Information Systems, Information Systems, Information Systems Planning.

## Introduction

A current concern in the information systems world is the strategic use of information systems. Virtually all top-level information systems (IS) executives have heard of the strategic successes of American Airlines' SABRE, American Hospital Supply Corporation's Analytic Systems Automated Purchasing (ASAP), and Foremost McKesson's Economost. Through the publicity generated by the business press about the strategic importance of information systems, many CEOs have also begun to recognize the strategic significance that information systems can have for their companies. When American Airlines derives 54% of its income from SABRE, senior executives can appreciate the effect upon the bottom line [2].

There have been techniques and theories designed to exploit a company's distinctive qualities for strategic advantage. However, IS executives are burdened with traditional IS management problems such as ensuring their systems' uptime, project management and program maintenance. They have little or no time to spend on reading the various management literature on strategic opportunities possible through information systems.

This article will provide capsule descriptions of the existing strategic information systems tools currently available, together with a framework whereby IS executives can identify those tool(s) most appropriate for their particular needs. The descriptions will cover IBM's Business Systems Planning Methodology, McFarlan's framework, Wiseman and MacMillan's Strategic Thrust-Strategic Target Matrix, Ives and Learmonth's Customer Resource Life Cycle, and Porter and Millar's model, and will be preceded by a review of the key competitive strategy concepts that are the foundation to most of these tools. This review will help senior IS executives understand how these tools can be best used for their particular competitive environments. These tools will be discussed as to purpose, focus and scope of the model, key concepts, resource requirements, strengths, and limitations.

Purpose. Each of these models are designed to help information systems executives and even senior executives determine the strategic possibilities available through the innovative use of information systems.

Focus and scope. Each may have a different focus or scope for determining the means for obtaining competitive advantage. The focus would be on the particular aspect of a firm's operations that would be targeted as its competitive edge. The scope would then be the corresponding impact on either the firm's internal operations or its external marketplace.

Key concepts. Some of these models might involve concepts that are either unfamiliar or are not common in the business world. Then the concepts relevant to the particular construct would be discussed and explained.

Resources requirements. More often that not, these models do not describe the resources required to identify the strategic opportunities. However, whenever possible, we would list the resources that are needed as indicated by the models. The resources to be considered include land, labor, capital and information. These do not include the resources required to actually implement the identified opportunity.

Strengths. Each of these models have their own particular strengths depending upon its individual mixture of focus, resource requirements, theoretical validity, and time-tested validity.

Limitations. Similarly, each will have its limitations. We will examine whether a construct's strengths outweigh its limitations to the extent that it will actually be useful to MIS managers.

## Background

IS managers are often thought of as being technical types who have neither the need nor the ability to understand strategic business concepts. An IS manager once inquired of his senior top management about the firm's business goals and strategies. The reply was, "You're a computer guy, what's it got to do with you?" [20].

Before IS managers can aspire to convince senior management of the strategic potential of information systems, it would be to their advantage to he able to talk the same language (i.e. essential strategic concepts) as their superiors.

The key strategic concepts that have been widely discussed during the past decade are based upon the ideas of Michael Porter of the Harvard Business School [27]. Professor Porter discussed five forces affecting an industry's profitability and growth, three generic strategies for obtaining a competitive edge, and the value-added chain [27,28]. We will discuss each of these three concepts, together with some relevant examples, as to how they are being applied to identify possible strategic uses of information systems.

## Five Forces of an Industry's Structure

Prior to Porter's work in competitive strategy, companies had often believed that the only entities that affected their growth and profitability were their immediate competitors in the existing marketplace. In other words, Bank A's rivals were other banks offering the same services and financial products.

However, Porter asserted that these intra-industry rivals were not the only determinants of an industry's and similarly a firm's growth and profitability. He proposed that there were five basic forces (Table 1) determining an industry's competitive structure: bargaining power of the industry's suppliers, bargaining power of the industry's buyers, threat of new entrants into the industry, threat of new or substitute products, and intra-industry rivals.

The concept of these five determinants of an industry's competitiveness have been widely accepted by top management in companies all over the world and IS executives must understand these five industry determinants.

Bargaining power of the suppliers. Suppliers determine an industry's competitive structure because they provide the resources by which that industry produces its outputs. Thus they can influence an industry's profitability if they affect the prices or the quality of the needed resources. An example of how a supplier has been able to influence the retail drug stores' competitive structure is Foremost McKesson's use of a sophisticated order-entry information system. Via this system, Foremost McKesson has been able to ensure that its customers are able to maintain just-in-time inventories that help them reduce inventory costs. Furthermore McKesson's information system stores information on client drug stores' on-shelf stock shelving arrangement. This information allowed the system to instruct the shipping department as to the packing order of requested items so that the drug stores can easily unpack and arrange the ordered items. The system also provides the drug store with the necessary price stickers based upon the stores' requirement [8]. McKesson's information system has allowed retail drug stores to improve their profit margin, and at the same time, ties them to Foremost McKesson as their principal supplier, thus reducing their bargaining power.

Bargaining power of the industry's buyers. Buyers exert pressure upon an industry by seeking the best possible terms for themselves whether in the form of lower prices, more value-added services or higher quality products. They can play off one competitor against another, all of who affects an industry's overall competitive structure and profitability.

The automotive parts suppliers were severely impacted by General Motors' implementation of an electronic data interchange system (EDI) which allowed GM's buyers to locate the cheapest available parts for automobile production. Without the installation of these systems, the parts suppliers were not eligible to be GM's selected suppliers list. Each supplier will have to spend at least hundreds of thousands of dollars to install an EDI system, and profit margins would be substantially reduced. However, automotive parts suppliers have no alternative because GM represents a major buyer of their outputs [33]. GM estimated that EDI could save up to \$1 billion annually in information communication costs [6].

Threat of new entrants into the industry. Managers often do not realize the threat that new entrants to a market can pose to its competitive structure. For example, financial services are no longer provided only by banks but also by non-banking entities such as insurance companies, brokerage firms, and retailers. Banks no longer are the only source of funds for borrowers. Even General Motors is in the financial services industry through its General Motors Acceptance Corporation.

Threat of new or substitute products. Consumers are only interested about the utility or satisfaction that they receive from a product or service. Whether the derived satisfaction arises from one product category or another is unimportant. This means that consumers can switch their buying preferences to new or substitute products if they believe that these products offer them greater utility/satisfaction, or the same utility/satisfaction at a lower cost. An example of a new and innovative product is the introduction by Merrill Lynch of the Cash Management Account (CMA) which combined the features of a checking, savings and stock brokerage accounts into one account. Consumers were very receptive to this new product, which escalated from 180,000 accounts in 1980 to 1,000,000 accounts in 1983 [18]. Merrill Lynch makes more than \$60 million per year through the management of \$20 billion in client funds deposited in its CMA accounts. Merrill Lynch's competitors had no such product on the market, and were forced to play catch-up.

Intra-industry rivals. These are the competitors that most managers consider as being the immediate dangers to their firms' profitability. These are the competitors that are considered when a firm undertake a marketing campaign, a shift in distribution patterns and other similar competitive moves. How then can a firm seek to avail itself of initiatives that would allow it to gain a competitive edge over its intraindustry competitors? Porter discussed the three generic strategies that a firm may choose in order to gain an edge.

## Three Generic Competitive Strategies

Porter evaluated the different marketing, production, and delivery means whereby a firm may choose as its means of sustaining growth and profitability. He determined that these different strategies can be reduced to three generic strategies of cost leadership, differentiation and focus. Each strategy has its own characteristics, and a firm will choose a generic strategy that matches its own strengths and weaknesses. Though a firm may want to choose more than one strategy, this move may be unwise because the firm might then be spreading its resources too thinly, and thus be unsuccessful.

Cost Leadership. This generic strategy emphasizes the competitive advantage of being the cost leader in an industry. This means that a firm, the cost leader, incurs the lowest production costs of all the firms within that industry, perhaps through scale economies. Thus the cost leader can price its product offerings low enough so that it can still make a profit while other rival firms are sustaining losses. This strategy should be evaluated continually by the firm that uses it. In the world of information systems, technological innovations can easily allow a competitor to leapfrog the cost leader in being the lowest cost producer. The cost leader must ensure that it maintains its technological lead, and be ready to implement responsive changes whenever necessary.

Differentiation. This generic strategy requires that the firm offers a product or service that is perceived as being a unique offering. Differentiation allows a firm to earn above-average returns because the firm can establish a defensible position against the five determinants of industry structure. An example of a firm using differentiation to establish a defensible market position is Federal Express. When Fred Smith first started the firm, his strategy involved the integration of information and communication technologies to ensure reliable overnight delivery of packages. Up till then, airfreight packages generally took a few days at best. By offering a highly differentiated service, Federal Express was able to capitalize on an unmet market demand for overnight delivery service. It took the firm only ten years (1973–1983) to achieve \$1 billion in revenues [10]. Even now, Federal Express seeks to differentiate itself. Over the past few years, it has been developing the

COSMOS IIB tracking system, which allows it to provide instantaneous packagestatus information to itself, and its customers [11].

Market focus. By this strategy, a firm seeks to focus upon a particular buyer group, product line or geographic market, and to serve that market better than its competitors. A market focus firm should be able to serve its market more effectively than its competitors who might be serving a much broader market, and thus be spreading its resources too thinly.

An example of how a firm had been able to use its information systems to focus upon its particular market niche, and thus establish a dominant position is Federal Express. Right from the very beginning, Federal Express had invested heavily into its information systems and automated facilities so as to create the overnight delivery package business $[10]$ . It has been able to maintain its leadership position by its continued investment in information technology so as to provide real-time information on packages to customers whenever needed.

This strategy is similar to the differentiation strategy in that it is possible for the firm to establish a defensible position against the five forces assailing it. It is possible for the firm to earn above-average returns for the market niche that it serves.

## Value-Added Chain

A company's value chain is made of the “technologically and economically distinct activities that it performs to do business” [28]. These activities (Table 2) consist of primary activities (i.e. inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) together with support activities (i.e. corporate infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, procurement), and are the means whereby a firm can seek to implement its cost leadership or differentiation strategies [28].

An example of how IT has affected inbound logistics is the earlier example of GM's electronic data interchange (EDI) system by which GM has been able to leverage its buying power to reduce its resource costs, and also to improve the delivery performance of its suppliers. An example of how IT has affected the marketing and sales activities of a firm's value chain is a magazine distribution firm's information system. The firm used its information system to analyze the sales patterns of the magazines sold by its customers, and thus used its knowledge to inform its customers of their best-selling magazines. This helped them to improve their product-mix and consequently their profit margins [22].

## The Selected Models

The models chosen for this study are IBM's Business Systems Planning methodology, Ives and Learmonth's Customer Resource Life Cycle, Porter and Millar's model, Critical Success Factors methodology, Wiseman and MacMillan's Strategic Thrust-Strategic Target Matrix, and McFarlan's framework. These tools were chosen because each, by its own means and to some extent, sought to address the issue of how information systems can be used to identify and exploit competitive opportunities. We will discuss the extent to which we believe that each fulfills its stated purpose.

Business Systems Planning. Perhaps the most well-known strategic systems planning tool known to the IS world is IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP) methodology. This methodology was developed in response to the large-scale systems development projects that IBM undertook for its clients. Though this methodology is a proprietary product, it is well-known and documented. Thus it is easily accessible to many companies. Furthermore other structured methodologies that have been developed (e.g. Andersen Consulting's Foundation) are similar to BSP [19].

IBM has defined the purpose of BSP as “a structured approach to assist an organization in establishing an information system plan to satisfy its short-term and long-term information requirements” [19]. These can include “assigning systems resources to support high-return projects and managing system resources to support business goals” [19]. Its focus is upon planning an enterprise-wide information system from the top down, and implementing this system piece by piece from the bottom up.

The scope of this methodology is primarily internal operations based upon the notion that the information systems structure exists to support the organizational functions. The basis of BSP is the interviews with senior executives that determine their perspective about the organization and its information needs. Thus the primary resources would be the interviewers as well as management's time and energy spent determining the firm's information needs. An interview typically takes two to four hours [30].

Its primary strength is that it involves the firm's top management which helps them to communicate better with the information systems departments, and consequently obtain a better understanding of the capabilities and costs of information systems. Furthermore, BSP is highly structured and well-documented.

However, the quality of the methodology depends on the quality of the task force, and the results of the study can be difficult to implement [9]. Furthermore, BSP was designed for a centralized environment because it was developed at a time when IBM's principal revenue products were mainframe computers. This might cause strategic problems when a proposed competitive strategy is based upon a decentralized architecture.

An aircraft parts manufacturer once used BSP in an effort to better identify and understand its competitive environment. Though this was an opportunity for the company to identify the competitive forces that affected its industry, it was unable to identify and develop opportunities for leveraging its information systems to compete more successfully in its industry. The IS department's improved understanding of the corporate data flow could not conceal the fact that BSP was more suited as a IS planning tool than as a strategic systems planning tool [31].

Ives and Learmonth. Ives and Learmonth's model is intended as a guide in finding new opportunities for the successful application of information system technologies (IST). By using this model, new applications can be identified throughout a resource's life cycle, or existing applications can be used in new settings. Thus by determining the point at which it wishes to focus its competitive energies, a firm can use information technology strategically to gain competitive advantage.

The focus here is upon the firm's relationships with its customers, and how these relationships can be improved through the use of information technologies to differentiate its product. The model postulates the existence of a life cycle of thirteen steps, as viewed by the customer. The thirteen steps are: establish requirements, specify, select source, order, authorize and pay for, acquire, test and accept, integrate, monitor, upgrade, maintain, transfer and dispose, and monitor orders [14]. The supplier can assist the customer in managing one or more steps of this life cycle with the help of IST.

The primary resources needed to implement this model were not explicitly stated. However, given the thrust of the model, it can be surmised that no additional resources will be needed except for the creativity of top management in applying the CRLC to determine the specific opportunities.

The primary strength of this model is that it allows management to view each distinct step of the CRLC as opportunities of applying IST for service differentiation and thus be able to establish a competitive advantage. However this model cannot be used as a prescriptive model in determining opportunities for the strategic use of information systems. Thus the CRLC model is a post-facto model helpful in determining how information systems have been used strategically, and not a normative model in exploiting the strategic possibilities of information systems.

A particularly notable example of how information systems technology has been used to improve a firm's relationship with its customers is Toys "R" Us. By implementing an innovative point-of-sale system, Toys "R" Us has been able to ensure that customers who shop at its stores are served more expeditiously and efficiently. No longer will cashiers have to laboriously key in product codes and prices onto the cash register; the codes are entered via handheld laser gun scanners. The information system then automatically looks up the correct and up-to-date prices. Checkout times during the busy Christmas season had dropped between 40% to 60%. Shoppers no longer left the Toys "R" Us in frustration because the checkout lines were too long. Toys "R" Us has already attributed part of the 30% increase in 1986 revenues of \$1.05 billion to \$1.36 billion in 1987 to the improved customer service made possible by its information system [12].

Porter and Millar. Porter and Millar's model is intended to help general managers respond to the challenges and opportunities of the information revolution [28]. It offers a framework whereby managers can assess the role of IT in their businesses and thus set their priorities to turn IT to their competitive advantage. Its focus is upon the value chain that is present in the delivery of services or products. Since IT permeates the entire value chain, some and perhaps even all the links in the value chain can be enhanced or transformed to provide a means of competitive advantage. The scope of this model depends upon the portion of the value chain that will be affected by IT. Thus it can either be internal operations or external marketplace.

An information intensity matrix (Table 3) relates the information intensity for a product's value chain to the information content contained within that product. The information intensity of a product's value chain can be understood as the amount of information processing that is required to acquire, process and then deliver the product in its final form to the users. A product's information content refers to the amount of useful information contained within that product that is actually received and understood by its users. Though products may have a low information content rating, the increasing utilization of information technology to manufacture and deliver products result in the corresponding increase in the information intensity of the value chain. Porter and Millar use this matrix to identify the role that information plays in the product offering, as well as the process used to deliver the product to the customers. Thus the matrix is useful for identifying the extent to which information technology can change the forces affecting the industry's structure.

The primary strength of this model is its reliance upon ideas that have been widely accepted by top management such as Porter's five industry forces. Thus top management can relate well to the model. However there has not been much research on the model.

Benetton SpA, the highly successful fashion retailer, has effectively exploited the informationintensive nature of the retail fashion industry to its own benefit. Though its products have very low information content, its production and marketing processes that are part of its value chain are highly information-intensive.

By virtue of its innovative investments in information technology for the production and marketing processes, Benetton has been able to respond to changes in fashion trends faster than anyone else in the fashion industry. Its ability to provide overnight adjustment of production, via its highly computerized manufacturing environment, allows its licensees to receive items within two to three weeks of order.

Benetton can delay production for garments until a order has been placed by a franchisee, thereby eliminating inventory costs. The flexibility afforded by the computerized manufacturing system allows Benetton to respond very promptly to changes in fashion trends. It can delete slow-selling items from its product lines, and expand production of fast-selling clothes.

As much as 20% of new fashion ideas depend upon previous fashion ideas. Thus the process of designing new clothes is highly information intensive. Benetton has been able to leverage its expertise in information systems by recording its previous fashions on a laser disc-based computer database, which is then accessed via personal computers. Via this easily accessible database, Benetton can further reduce the time spent in designing new clothes, thus improving its response time [21].

Its success in exploiting the information-intensive nature of the fashion industry is part of Benetton's tremendous success; from a meager \$2,000 investment in 1955, Benetton has become a billion dollar company with sales of \$2.5 billion in 1987 [3,25].

Critical Success Factors. The critical success factors (CSF) methodology, as developed by Rockart, attempts to make explicit those few key areas that dictate managerial or organizational success [30]. It was designed to be used by top-level executives as a means of defining their information needs by determining those factors that help them achieve successful operations. It later was expanded by Rockart and Bullen to be an MIS planning tool [5].

Critical success factors are those key areas of activity in which favorable results are absolutely necessary for a particular manager to reach his or

her goals [30]. The primary focus of the methodology is upon the executive's own experience and intuition in determining what factors are truly critical for the success of his firm.

The scope of this tool depends upon the factors that are identified. If the CSFs include such factors as delivery of service, then the scope is external marketplace. If the CSFs identified include effective and efficient systems, then the scope is internal operations.

In the original study done by Rockart with nine companies, an average of four hours with the chief IS executive, and two hours with other IS executives were needed to complete the required interviews [30]. All these interviews can be performed by one analyst trained in the CSF methodology.

A strength of this methodology is its enthusiastic acceptance by senior management as its basic concepts and aims are easily understood. Furthermore, it supports structured, top-down analysis, which aids in identifying information services that can impact a firm's competitive position. Finally, it is flexible, and does not require a rigorous format in the interpretation of CSFs [4].

However, some lower-level managers that are more used to hands-on operations may have difficulty understanding the conceptual nature of CSFs because of “their lack of experience in conceptual thinking” [4]. Finally, it can be difficult for those farther away from the top of the organizational hierarchy to identity substantive organizational CSFs.

Rockart also points out that CSFs will differ for different organizations for the following reasons: the level of development of the firm's IS organization: recent organizational history of the IS function; the human, organizational and financial structure of a firm, and the executive's 'world-view'. A proactive executive will tend to have different types of CSFs compared with those of a reactive manager.

For consumer package-goods companies, a critical success factor would be their marketing efforts. They must quickly analyze the effectiveness of their marketing campaigns so that they can speedily react to dynamic market conditions.

In 1985, Metaphor Computer Systems introduced Metaphor, a computer-based marketing decisionsupport system, to aid marketing experts determine the efficacy of their marketing campaigns. Using such a system, a marketing manager can quickly adjust its marketing campaigns so as to augment successful strategies and to eliminate weak strategies [13].

In 1986, Proctor & Gamble invested \$10 million in Metaphor Computer Systems because it recognized the system's key contribution in helping Proctor & Gamble to fulfilling its critical success factor, its marketing campaign [17]. Despite their rivalry in the marketplace, companies such as Johnson & Johnson, Pepsico and Carnation have also invested in Metaphor systems because of its key contributions to their same critical success factors.

This methodology can be used beyond the MIS arena. Others such as Munro and Wheeler have suggested that CSFs can be used to direct an organization's efforts to develop strategic plans [3,4].

Wiseman and MacMillan. Wiseman and MacMillan's strategic thrust-strategic target model is designed to identify the opportunities available in gaining a competitive advantage via the innovative use of information technology, and thus add value to the products and services offered to clients [36]. Its focus depends the specific target, which can either be the supplier, customer or competitor.

The primary instrument for this framework is the option generator (Table 4) which contrasts the different categories of strategic targets (i.e. competitor, customer and supplier) against strategic thrusts (i.e. innovation, differentiation and cost.)

Note that this model uses three of Porter's five forces affecting an industry, and two of his three generic strategies available in obtaining competitive advantage. Less important considerations are the mode of strategic thrust (offensive vs. defensive), directions of thrust (usage vs. provision), information systems skills used (processing, storage or transmission). The scope of this framework is the external marketplace as the strategic targets are all external to the company.

McFarlan. McFarlan's framework is designed to demonstrate information technology's importance as a strategic resource via a series of questions [22]. The focus is upon assessing the impact of IT in a firm's business environment by answering the following five questions:

1. Can IS technology build barriers to entry?

2. Can IS technology build in switching costs?

3. Can IS technology change the basis of competition?

4. Can IS technology change the balance of power in supplier relationships?

5. Can IS technology generate new products?

The primary concepts that are present in this framework are based upon the five forces that Porter identified as affecting an industry's structure. The scope of this framework includes not just the external marketplace in which a firm competes but also the other complex industry forces, such as possible substitutes produced by other industries, with which a firm must contend to maintain profitability.

The primary strength of this framework is that it allows top management to consider more than just the existing competition as part of the determinant of the firm's profitability. The ranking process table (Table 5) can help indicate how a firm can obtain a competitive advantage by examining its business environment and thus establishing the primary goals of its IS expenditures. Cells are ranked in importance via the values contained in them. The lower the value, the more important that activity is for the specific industry. The rehabilitation and maintenance of an existing system has the highest priority because the daily operations of an existing system must be executed reliably and effectively before any further initiatives can be undertaken. Only then can a firm seek to undertake activities that would help it to either attain competitive advantage, or to maintain competitive parity.

Nevertheless, McFarlan does not actually address the issue as to what steps can a firm can take in actually applying his framework to identify the strategic information systems opportunities. However the factors comprising McFarlan's framework have been used by firms as the basis for determining how information systems can be enhanced and leveraged as a strategic tool.

In 1981, United Services Automobile Association (USAA), which provides insurance coverage for approximately 1.7 million current and former service personnel as well as their families, began a \$100 million systems overhaul which included the development and installation of an image-processing system [26]. Given the size and growth of the insurance industry, USAA initiated the move towards a paperless office in attempting to decrease the cost of doing business while gaining a competitive advantage. In doing so, USAA had focused upon the twin goals of experimenting with new technology while attempting to gain a competitive advantage in the relatively stable insurance industry. These two goals, in McFarlan's framework, are highly important areas for IS resource allocation.

The service-orientation of the insurance industry necessitated USAA's search for improving service delivery for its customers. The outmoded process of receiving, storing and processing information on paper documents proved to be an inefficient process which incurred heavy transaction costs and hindered service. The need for improved document handling prompted USAA's venture into new information technology. The image-processing concept which was first promulgated in 1982 began to be implemented in 1988.

The prototype implementation which involved about 100 employees thus far had saved \$2.5 million, which is an average of \$2,500 per employee [23]. By the end of 1989, all new documents received by USAA will be scanned and stored into an image-processing system. Gradually all terminals will be hooked up to the image-processing system. This system combined with other information systems innovations such as expert systems and networks have lowered USAA's policy underwriting expense to 9%, in comparison to the industry's average of 13% [34].

In addition to the reduction in paper-handling costs, USAA has focused upon gaining a competitive advantage in service delivery. The image-processing system has allowed USAA service agents to access recent documents within 10–20 seconds while talking with customers over the phone. Thus existing customers would be inclined to remain with USAA while new customers would be attracted to an insurance firm that delivered service as promptly as it collected premiums. Thus, USAA's use of IT innovations such as the image-processing system and critical intelligence have helped to reduce costs and to obtain a competitive advantage in service delivery.

## Discussion

In an attempt to better understand the relationships between these methodologies, a framework is presented. Three dimensions comprise this framework: the role of IT, the focus of the model, and the desired organizational impact. The role of IT pertains to the sponsor or the user of the competitive model. Some of the models generalize to broader organizational issues from the goals and opportunities of information technology, while the reverse is true of other models. The focus of the model can be internal or external to the organization or a combination of the two. Finally, the desired organizational impact pertains to the goals or support offered. As such, a model could be used to improve (cost) efficiencies or make the organization more effective either in the marketplace or internally.

These dimensions were suggested by the models themselves. For example, Critical Success Factors are concerned with those controllable elements that are critical to the ongoing function of the organization, while the Customer Resource Life Cycle is focused on relationships that are external to the organization. BSP originates within the IT area, while Porter and Millar begin with organizational concerns that incorporate information throughout the value chain. Finally, several of the models are aimed at improving efficiencies by better deploying organizational resources. A majority of the models also are positioned to improve effectiveness in the marketplace. As will be seen, however, this effectiveness is tempered by the sustainability of the innovation.

The framework is depicted in Table 6. Using this framework, BSP, for example, would be classified as an internally focused, information technology-oriented model aimed at improving organizational efficiency. The classifications in the framework are not mutually exclusive, but can be used to compare the various models and aid in matching model to task. It would appear that many of these works are not specifically oriented to improving internal efficiencies, but are more focused on competition in the marketplace. This could be a function of the models selected for inclusion in this study and/or current trends in the use of information technology for competitive advantage.

Efficiency can be defined as the use of minimal resources to obtain an objective. Effectiveness is attained when maximum results are obtained from a fixed amount of resources. Efficiency and effectiveness are often associated with, respectively, the internal and external focus of the organization. These, however, are not exclusive arrangements. EDI is a good example of a strategically employed technology that can be classified as externally efficient. By strengthening the ties between two organizations, the market interactions are more efficient. As these ties are used to compete, they can also improve external (market) effectiveness. Of course, EDI as implemented by GM is also considered to be externally effective as well because of the close ties created between GM and its suppliers. Both parties are more efficient in the manner in which business is transacted and their interactions are also more effective.

The American Hospital Supply Company example is characterised as a system that was initially designed to improve internal efficiencies (i.e., to cut order entry costs.) However, as the system became more sophisticated, it began tying customers more closely to AHSC which was externally effective while still providing the internal efficiencies it originally garnered. Consequently, it is possible to design a system that is efficient or effective and then later refocus that system.

The sponsorship of the system relates to what functional part of the organization initiates the project. By beginning in the information systems organization, managers are asking, “What problems can IT solve?” Conversely, by starting at the organizational or functional level, managers pose “What can IT do to solve these problems?” EDI is an example of the former as a technology goes looking for a problem, while AHSC’s system exemplifies the latter by employing IT to provide a solution to a data entry problem.

Some IT solutions are better suited to internal functions of the organization (e.g., common implementations of electronic mail systems.) On the other hand, some technologies attempt to affect the organization's external relationships. For example, American Airlines SABRE and its Yield Management/Scheduling systems are systems that affect internal and external operations.

In summary, by looking at information technology itself, we are able to create a classification scheme, a framework, that can be used to organize the competitive models discussed here. Because it is independent of the model and was derived from the changes brought about by IT, this framework can also be used to classify other models.

Most of these models are descriptive in nature and do not actually prescribe any major strategic actions. There are hardly any initiatives that management can undertake with the assurance that those actions would lead to competitive advantage. This, of course, is not surprising. Nevertheless they do offer insights that are useful in identifying strategic opportunities. Perhaps the primary weakness of a model that would lead to the successful strategic use of information systems is the logical fallacy of such a model.

It would be logical to assume that there is no easy and predefined path to using information systems for competitive advantage. Competitive advantage can be defined as the result of a firm's successful exploitation of its distinctive competence. If all firms could use a cookbook approach to identify strategic uses of information technology, then none of these firms would hold an edge over other firms. Clemons has pointed out that more efficient operations through the use of strategic use of information systems is insufficient; if the information technology is easily imitated, then other firms can achieve the same result easily. Thus competition will revert to its original state and the pioneering firm will have attained a competitive edge only for a short period of time [7]. Citibank's early introduction of automated teller machines (ATMs) helped it to achieve substantial growth in its consumer banking business because the ease and convenience of ATM banking were highly desired by consumers. However, in 1985, other New York banks collaborated in developing a cooperative system called New York Cash Exchange (NYCE) which is now more widely used than Citibank's system [1,32]. Though Citibank had achieved an early lead in the innovative use of information technology, its technological and market lead are threatened by NYCE [1]. As of mid-1987, NYCE operated $300\%$ more ATMs than Citibank [1,32]. At the same time, the cost of ATM banking has not outweighed the benefits. Though ATM technology was an early competitive edge for Citibank in vying for market share in New York's retail banking industry, the level of competition has now returned to parity, albeit at a higher cost caused by the introduction and expansion of ATMs [24].

The sustainability of the competitive advantage is an important issue. Most researchers agree that a competitive edge is sustainable only in the short run. This is depicted in Table 7. Table 7a shows the relative competitive uses of information technology within an industry. The midline, or status quo, represents the average use of this technology. To the right of the midline is relative competitive advantage, to the left is disadvantage. Firm A in Table 7a has a slight advantage over the average firm in the industry and a substantial advantage over firm B. To overcome the relative disadvantage, firm B would have to commit more resources than would firm C.

After implementing a new technology, firm A moves further away from the industry status quo as seen in Table 7b. If firm A's advantage is substantial, a mobility barrier is erected. As time passes, the majority of firms in the industry adopt the new technology themselves and the status quo shifts, cutting firm A's advantage. However, firm B, for some reason, was unwilling or unable to adopt the new technology. A mobility barrier is erected and firm B is no longer an effective member of the industry. The status quo can also represent a competitive necessity. Once a majority of the market adopts and successfully employs a technology, it is no longer an option. ATMs are a good example of this phenomena. It is generally infeasible for a bank to not offer ATM service to its customers. In this case, the ATM system is not a competitive advantage, but a necessity or competitive liability.

The advantage gained, however, can create problems for the innovator. For example, the home banking and ATM innovations were notable information technology failures. Table 7d depicts two similar industries with similar IT requirements. Firm A implements an advanced technology. However, firm A by implementing this technology, has actually altered its intra-industry relationships and erected a mobility barrier which isolates it from the original industry, and in fact places it at a disadvantage in the similar industry 2.

The question then is what factors affect sustainability? Keen [16] identified adaptability (the degree of change required to offer new services), the capabilities of existing internal and external systems, flexibility of organizational environments and existing systems, and the quality of the IT implemented. However, as we have seen, these factors must be viewed relative to the industry and potential market entrants. For an advantage to be sustained, it must be defended by improving the quality of the technological innovation and keeping the organizational environment and resources flexible enough to adapt to the industry's response.

In spite of the publicity about the strategic use of information systems, little has been said about the concomitant risk of failure. As Vitale points out, strategic success comes only with the undertaking of a venture with a high probability of failure [35]. Furthermore given the inability of IS to deliver reliable and effective routine, operational information systems on time and within budget, it would seem much harder to design, develop and implement strategic information systems in light of their complexity and importance. Strategic failures of information systems are rarely publicized. A firm's failure to exploit the strategic potential of information systems may often lead to its reduced profitability. An example is a food products firm which was considered a leading and innovative IT user. This firm failed to see the impact that IT played in allowing retail chains to increase its buyer power through the use of point-of-sale terminals. Thus the firm failed to leverage its existing information systems capability to protect itself against the retail chains' buying power [15].

Furthermore the highly touted successes of strategic uses of information systems such American Hospital Supply Company, American Airlines and Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account (CMA) relied on end-users' acceptance of the innovations provided by information systems. Merrill Lynch's CMA initially was not successful until Merrill Lynch lured its brokers to market more effectively [37]. Johnston and Carrico noted that "the success of any strategic systems depend upon its voluntary use" [15]. Merrill-Lynch was fortunate that it realized CMA's success depended upon incentive rather than mere persuasion. Thus, the human element is an important key in determining the success of any strategic use of information systems.

Another important element is top management's support of strategic information systems initiatives. Lederer and Mendelow's study revealed that 80% of IS managers surveyed had "experienced difficulty convincing top management of the potential strategic impact of information systems" [20]. Thus IS managers must strive to overcome top management's reluctance or inability to recognize the strategic potential of information systems by means such as education of top management, marketing of IS accomplishments, and promotion of a business image for the IS department [20].

An important point to consider is that the operations of the information system is typically not the Holy Grail for a firm in its effort to maintain and increase its profitability. Even the successes that were achieved through the use of information systems were possible only at already profitable firms. Even before the success that American Airlines had with SABRE, it already was a company that was a major competitor in its primary market of airline transportation. Foremost McKesson was already an important player in the retail drug distribution industry even before it introduced Economost as a means of tracking inventory and customers' orders [8]. Thus IS executives must keep in mind that information systems can help leverage a firm onto greater profitability, and not necessarily be the panacea to a firm's problems.

Johnston and Carrico have pointed out that there are three different levels by which firms can be categorized in their use of information systems $[15]$ . The first level is where firms use information systems primarily to improve administrative and managerial functions such as accounting and decision support systems. These applications are prevalent in all companies. The second level is where information systems exist to support strategy. Such a situation exists where top managers “define and develop corporate strategies without explicitly considering the strategic potential of IS” $[15]$ . The third level exists where firms hold IS to be integral to their corporate strategy. These firms use IT to affect not only their internal operations but also the external marketplace. These firms are able to integrate their IS with their corporate strategy because they are in industries that “involve significant information content” $[15]$ .

IS executives should realize that not all firms can use information systems to the same extent and with the same success that firms such as American Airlines and Foremost McKesson have achieved. Nevertheless, there is still room for IS executives to increase the impact that IS can have upon their firms' operations. Every edge that a firm can develop can only help in attaining increased profitability.
