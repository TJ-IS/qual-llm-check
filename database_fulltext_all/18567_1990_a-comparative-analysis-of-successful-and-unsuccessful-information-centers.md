# A comparative analysis of successful and unsuccessful information centers

Bindiganavale S. Vijayaraman

Department of Management, The University of Akron, Akron, OH 44325, USA

Hindupur V. Ramakrishna

MIS/DS Department, Old Dominion University, Norfolk, VA 23529, USA

Findings of a field study that examined some differences between successful and unsuccessful Information Centers (ICs) from the end-users' point of view are presented here. The study involved ten large companies representing a diverse range of industries. The research methodology consisted of Selection Phase, where ten large organizations with ICs were selected, Interview Phase, where IC managers were interviewed and detailed background information of ICs was collected, and Survey Phase, where the responses of 238 end-users were collected. Results of the interviews and the data analysis of the survey are presented and discussed. The ICs were grouped as successful and unsuccessful based upon the end-user's response to the End-User Computing satisfaction instrument. End-users from successful ICs perceived higher satisfaction and higher benefit for services under all categories of support compared with end-users from unsuccessful ICs. As the levels of end-users advanced, the satisfaction and benefit for services tend to decline for both successful and unsuccessful ICs. Support from successful ICs was perceived significantly better than from unsuccessful ICs on all six dimensions: adaptability, timeliness, availability, dependability, accessibility, and adequacy. Many other differences between the two groups have also been explored.

Keywords: Information Center (IC), End-User Computing (EUC), End-User Information Satisfaction.

## Introduction

Information Centers (ICs) are organizational entities that are created to facilitate and coordinate End-User Computing (EUC). The Information Center concept was first introduced by IBM in Canada in 1973. Since then, ICs are gaining popularity in the business world. Since ICs are important in effectively managing End-User Computing, there is a need for better understanding of what makes them successful or unsuccessful.

Although the Information Center concept is accepted widely in organizations, very few firms understand the need for differentiated education, support, training, and control for the different classes of end-users [13]. The support activities of ICs are offered in a passive mode, and end-users have to actively seek assistance to obtain the services they need [6,13]. Although this approach to support delivery is necessary, it lacks proper focus and balance to meet all of the support needs of end-users [4]. This kind of support is “technology focused” rather than “problem focused.”

Given the growing importance of End-User Computing, organizations should expend more effort to determine what services to provide and how they should be delivered $[9]$ . One of the most important steps to ensure the successful implementation of ICs is to develop an understanding of the needs and expectations of the clients of the service. As a result, it will be easy to provide them with the products and services that satisfy their needs. Once management understands what their clients want and expect, the IC can determine how to deliver their needs. This approach is more effective than trying to determine how to make end-users want what the IC has to offer. So the success of ICs depends on the availability, accessibility, and quality of support services provided to the end-users.

Research, both conceptual and empirical, in identifying factors affecting EUC success in organizations has been very limited so far. Brancheau and Wetherbe [2] examined the differences between higher rated and lower rated ICs. They note that higher rated ICs focused on a narrow range of services, communicated their intent to users, developed a competent and friendly staff, and steered a moderate course between control and slack. Though this research identified some useful factors, the results are not generalizable due to a heavy reliance of perceptual measures in the study and also due to small sample size. Rivard and Huff [12] proposed and tested a model of the factors of success for User Developed Applications (UDA). The user information satisfaction with UDA, a single item variable, was used to measure the success of user developed applications. User satisfaction with the support provided by the DP department, user attitude, and perception of user friendliness of software tools were found to be important factors toward overall user satisfaction. However, there were weaknesses pertaining to the reliability and validity of the measures. The authors suggested for further research the development of an EUC satisfaction instrument based on a User Information Satisfaction instrument to measure the success of EUC.

With the continued increase in knowledge work in organizations, it is critical that organizations develop and nourish EUC environments. With many diverse sets of factors contributing to the success (or failure) of EUC, an empirically validated model linking the relevant factors (or variables) to EUC success would provide some guidelines to organizations developing and managing EUC environments. As the body of research available in this field is still limited, it is probably premature to develop a model of EUC success.

The research reported here is an attempt to further our knowledge of factors affecting EUC success/failure. Specifically, we examined the differences between successful and unsuccessful ICs. Organizational environment, IC operations/policies, and nature of applications are some factors we examined that explain the differences between successful and unsuccessful ICs. As most of these differentiating factors are controllable, organizations can work towards developing successful EUC environments.

## End-user computing success

Though the importance of EUC in organizations has been recognized by researchers and practitioners alike, instruments to measure EUC success are not available. Hence, we could either use measures that are available for measuring Information Systems (IS) success or we could develop some modified measures that are more suitable for measuring EUC success. We have elaborated on the two options below.

As IS success is a multi-faceted (and not completely well understood) construct, researchers have used many surrogate measures. These include user information satisfaction, system utilization, decision effectiveness, and organizational performance [3]. According to Zmud [15], success ultimately depends on how well the Management Information Systems (MIS) has supported decision making. Thus, user satisfaction with a MIS and user performance with a MIS become important evaluation measures. The use of user satisfaction as a measure of information systems success is very common in MIS and Decision Support Systems (DSS) research [8,10]. User information satisfaction is a perceptual or subjective measure of system success and serves as a substitute for objective determinants of information system effectiveness [7]. Powers and Dickson [11] concluded that user satisfaction is the most critical criterion in determining the success or failure of a computer system.

Bailey and Pearson [1] developed an instrument to measure user information satisfaction (UIS). The instrument included a series of semantic differential scales for each of 39 dimensions. Their instrument includes measures of both the system and support quality perceived by the user. The UIS instrument was refined and validated by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [7] in an exploratory study. Twenty-one of the original 39 constructs were included in the shortened instrument. The shortened instrument was found to be reliable and valid in measuring user information satisfaction.

Cheney, Mann, and Amoroso [3] suggest that end-user information satisfaction provide a readily available surrogate measure of End-User Computing facilities success. Since EUC is different from the traditional information systems development, the instrument developed by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [7] is not appropriate for measuring EUC success. Vijayaraman [14] developed an EUC satisfaction instrument by appropriately modifying the instrument developed by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi to suit the EUC environment. He also demonstrated the instrument to be reliable and valid. This instrument consists of 21 items (see Appendix A). We have used this instrument for our research. As EUC is not a well understood concept (construct), and as this is an exploratory study, we used two additional instruments to measure EUC success. The intent was to develop a better understanding of the construct.

The first instrument was developed and validated by Harris and Brightman [5]. This measures satisfaction with overall support (from ICs) on six dimensions – adaptability, timeliness, availability, dependability, accessibility, and adequacy. These attributes of information services were found to be important to users in enhancing their performance [5]. The evaluation of these measures were subjectively measured on a scale of 1 to 5, 1 being poor and 5 being excellent (see Appendix B).

To identify whether the support services provided by ICs fit the needs of end-users, a list of 49 support services were compiled and grouped into five major categories: Hardware, Software, Functional, Training and Education, and Miscellaneous support (see Appendix C). The end-users were asked to rate the 49 services in terms of perceived satisfaction, perceived benefit, and perceived level of cognitive task on a five point likert scale, 5 being high and 1 being low. Perceived satisfaction was defined as the extent of the end-user's perception of satisfaction towards the IC service they have used. Perceived benefit was defined as the extent of the end-user's perception of benefit of the service. Benefit includes importance, timeliness, and usefulness. Perceived cognitive task was used to measure the effectiveness of support services provided by ICs. The effectiveness of IC depends on the ability of a service that meet the end-user's cognitive need. Low level cognitive tasks are tasks which require the ability to recall or recognize ideas and facts in a situation. High level cognitive tasks are tasks which require the ability to use abstraction, rules, methods, and principles in concrete problems and situations. End-users were asked to rate the IC services in terms of their level of cognitive tasks it supports.

## Research method

The methodology consisted of three phases: (1) Selection Phase, (2) Interview Phase, and (3) Survey Phase. The Selection Phase involved the identification of companies with established ICs. Company participation in the study was based on size, geographic location, and whether the company had an IC or not. Size was considered important because the study was intended to consider only those organizations with mature ICs. Geographic location was important because of the need to conduct personal interviews with the IC managers. After initially scrutinizing 25 companies, ten companies with established ICs were selected for further study. Nine different industry types are represented in this sample of ten companies. The number of end-users served by ICs in these organizations ranged from 75 to 2000 (with an average of 600).

During the Interview Phase, structured and unstructured interviews with IC managers were conducted. In this phase, detailed background information on the management and organization of the ICs such as hardware and software evaluation criteria, IC budget, number of end-users, number of IC staff and their background, training methods, level of personnel using IC, and the type of applications developed by end-users were collected.

In the Survey Phase, information about the support needs and expectations of end-users who are managers, professionals, and staff personnel was gathered. Questionnaire sets were sent to IC managers who were asked to distribute them randomly to end-users. The number of questionnaires sent was proportional to number of end-users in each of the companies. The following survey instruments were sent to each subject: (1) EUC satisfaction instrument (Appendix A); (2) Satisfaction With Overall Support instrument (Appendix B); and (3) a list of 49 support services grouped into five major categories: Hardware, Software, Functional, Training and Education, and Miscellaneous support (Appendix C). As 30 percent response rate is normal for these kind of questionnaire studies, we sent 720 sets of questionnaires with an expectation of about 200 responses. Even though we received 272 (38%) sets of responses, only 238 (33%) were usable. The others lacked complete information.

## Results and discussion

Information Centers were divided into two groups: successful and unsuccessful groups based on the overall scores on the end-user computing satisfaction instrument. The total score of the 21 items on the EUC satisfaction instrument was averaged for each company and rank ordered. Based on the ranking, the ten companies were divided into three groups – most successful (3 companies), average (4 companies), and least successful (3 companies). In order to be able to highlight the differences between the successful and unsuccessful groups, data from four average companies was dropped from the analysis. A 't' test, to identify whether the two groups are statistically different with respect to the average scores on the instrument, yielded a significant result (p value of 0.001). This provides a valid basis for our classification of companies into successful and unsuccessful groups.

## Summary and conclusions

This study examined the differences between successful and unsuccessful Information Centers. The End-User Computing satisfaction instrument was used to measure the success of ICs in organizations. ICs with high scores on EUC satisfaction instrument were categorized as successful and ones with low scores were classified into unsuccessful. In this empirical research, we have identified significant differences along many dimensions between successful and unsuccessful ICs. They are summarized below.

Successful ICs have a higher budget per end-user, lower end-users per IC staff ratio, and higher budget per IC staff compared with unsuccessful ICs. The ICs among the successful group were located in corporate offices, compared with the unsuccessful ICs which were located in subsidiaries. This indicates that the corporate ICs are providing better services than ICs in subsidiaries. This could be due to the fact that corporate ICs have more budget per end-user and probably have better management support.

Successful ICs considered range of applications as an important hardware evaluation criteria, whereas unsuccessful ICs considered on-line help, cost, compatibility, and ease of use as important criteria for hardware evaluations. As far as the software evaluations were concerned, range of applications and ease of use were important to successful ICs, whereas on-line help, compatibility, vendor support, and cost were important for unsuccessful ICs. This could be due to the fact that unsuccessful ICs have low budget per end-user and a higher ratio of end-users per IC staff.

Satisfaction with overall support on all six dimensions – support adaptability, timeliness, availability, dependability, accessibility, and adequacy – was significantly higher for successful ICs compared with unsuccessful ICs. This indicates that ICs should provide better support on all six support dimensions to be considered successful by end-users.

End-users supported by successful ICs perceived higher satisfaction and benefit on all five support categories compared with end-users supported by unsuccessful ICs. End-users from successful ICs perceived higher satisfaction than benefit for services on all support categories, whereas end-users from unsuccessful ICs perceived higher benefit than satisfaction for services on all support categories. This is another indication why end-users are unhappy with their IC service.

The task ratings are significantly higher for successful ICs as compared to unsuccessful ICs for all support categories. This indicates that successful IC services support higher level of cognitive tasks compared with unsuccessful ICs.

For the first three types of end-users along the Rockart and Flannery's [13] end-user taxonomy, the perceived satisfaction and perceived benefit declined as the level of end-users advanced for both successful and unsuccessful ICs.

The majority of applications supported by successful ICs were departmental or corporate applications, whereas the majority of applications supported by unsuccessful ICs were personal applications. Among unsuccessful ICs, a higher percentage of end-users who received services from ICs were first line supervisors, whereas a higher percentage of users who received IC services were middle and upper level management among successful ICs. Successful ICs used a wide range of training methods to train end-users, whereas unsuccessful ICs used fewer methods.

Our aim in this research was to further our knowledge of factors contributing to EUC success. We have identified some factors that have not been identified in prior research. However, due to the very nature of our exploratory research, some of our findings are not statistically as rigorous as would be desired. Many of the factors we have identified are controllable by organizations and by ICs. This gives them a mechanism to develop and nurture successful EUC environments.

With the continued increase in the share of knowledge work in organizations, productivity increases in knowledge work are becoming more and more critical for survival. One way to achieve productivity increases is by developing and nourishing successful EUC environments. As the research in this area is still in its infancy, we do not have a clear understanding yet as to what makes an EUC environment successful (or unsuccessful). In present research we have probably not identified all the factors that contribute to (or affect) success. Research should be continued to identify further factors and to validate prior findings about factors already identified as important. Research should also be directed toward developing empirical relationships, that are statistically rigorous between the factors and EUC success. We believe that research is also necessary in developing better measures of EUC success.

In summary, we believe that organizational success (and probably survival) will depend on productivity gains in knowledge sector work. These gains in productivity can be enhanced by developing and nourishing successful EUC environments. A good understanding of the factors that contribute to the success of EUC environments and the development of a good, empirically validated model of EUC success are crucial to organizations. A trial and error approach for developing EUC environments, taken by many organizations so far, has proved to be costly. A more systematic approach toward this end is very much necessary.
