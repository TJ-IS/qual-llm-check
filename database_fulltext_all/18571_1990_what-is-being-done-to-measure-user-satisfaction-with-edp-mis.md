# What is being done to measure user satisfaction with EDP/MIS

David W. Conrath and Olivier P. Mignen
Dept. of Management Sciences, University of Waterloo, Waterloo,
Ont. N2L 5C5, Canada

Although there is a side literature on the measurement of user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services, virtually all of it is normative. It indicates what one should do and what the benefits ought to be. We were unable to find any studies that indicate whether or not organizations are measuring user satisfaction and, if they are, how they are doing it. We set out to do this with a large mail survey of the largest corporations in Canada. Only one quarter of the respondents stated that they have a formal procedure to measure user satisfaction with the services and products provided by the EDP/MIS departments. However, those that do seem to be following the principles set out in the literature and appear to be reaping the projected benefits. The products and services provided by EDP/MIS are perceived to have improved, as are the relations between these departments and the users of their services, as a result of measuring user satisfaction.

Keywords: User satisfaction, EDP/MIS evaluation, Measuring satisfaction with EDP/MIS, User satisfaction factors, Why measure use satisfaction, Impact of satisfaction measurement.

## Introduction

The intention of this paper is straightforward. There is a substantial literature that suggests it is important to measure user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services. Furthermore, the literature indicates how this should be done. What has not been investigated, however, is whether this advice and these tools have been put to use. To put it quite simply: Are organizations measuring user satisfaction? If they are, how are they doing it and what purposes are perceived as being served?

The issue of determining the level of user satisfaction with the products and services of EDP/MIS departments is not new $[27,34,40]$ . For more than ten years, researchers and practitioners have recognized that computer service productivity entails both efficiently supplied and effectively utilized EDP/MIS outputs $[19,30]$ , and successful usage is correlated with the user community's sense of satisfaction with those services $[2]$ . They argue that no matter how effective a system might really be, if it is not perceived to be satisfactory, it will be underutilized. Thus, its potential benefits cannot be fully realized. If you don't know how a user perceives computer-based services and their delivery, and why, you don't have a basis for changing this perception or improving the system.

The need to understand the user's point of view is even greater today. It is increasingly difficult to remain current with the pace of technological change as the array of hardware and software offerings continues to grow. But with the advent of personal computing, users are no longer captive. Furthermore, they are becoming far more knowledgeable. Thus, users are less and less tolerant of complex and time-consuming allocation and budgeting procedures, escalating costs, incompatible standards, duplication, delays resulting from backlogs and shifting priorities, ill-defined policies and procedures, inadequate information on available resources, and benefits that are not managed in an organized or systematic fashion [1,6,7,8,24,36,41]. The solution is not just to focus on the remedies per se, but to develop the means to ensure that the problems are recognized before they become major. This is virtually impossible without some means of gauging user perceptions.

Other evidence suggesting the importance of measuring the level of satisfaction with information system products and services is the number of measures that have been developed and studies that have been undertaken $[2,4,16,17,21,22,31,32,33,37,38,42,43]$ . Of particular interest is the work that has focused on the measurement instruments. The key contribution has been that of Bailey and Pearson $[2]$ . They reviewed 22 studies of the computer/user interface to generate a list of factors that might affect user satisfaction. After adding two factors based on reviews by data processing professionals, they validated the list by comparing it with critical incidents mentioned by 32 manager users. The final result was a list containing 39 distinct elements. This was later reduced to 21 factors by Ives et al $[22]$ .

A number of the researchers, working essentially in the same area, have considered user satisfaction or its surrogate in a much narrower context. Barki and Huff [3], for example, examined attitudes toward decision support systems. Good et al [16] studied user satisfaction as it pertained to a particular installation in a university environment. Irving et al [20] looked at users' perception of computerized performance monitoring and control systems. Lehman et al [25] were concerned with satisfaction toward graphics systems. Rivard and Huff [35] took user developed applications as the independent variable. In all of these cases, the measures of user satisfaction were designed to be context specific.

Other researchers have been more general, and Bailey and Pearson [2] reviewed some of them. In addition, Gallagher [15] developed an approach to measure the perceptions of the value of a management information system. The focus was on the product and not the quality of the service provided, a shortcoming shared by a more elaborate scheme proposed by Jenkins and Ricketts [23]. They used 20 items which were presented as attributes of five factors: input procedures, systems processing, report content, report form and report value. Rushinek and Rushinek [37], noticing that previous studies ignored the impact of service quality on user satisfaction, added an aspect which they called “technical support” (trouble-shooting, user expectations, documentation). And recently, Guimaraes and Gupta [17] adapted previous work [2,22] to focus on the satisfaction of top management with the MIS department.

To come back to the questions posed at the beginning of this paper, is anyone doing anything about measuring user satisfaction? And if so, what? In our review of the literature we could find no studies that addressed these questions. Thus, these two questions became the raison d'être of this paper, which is based on a study of large Canadian companies. Before describing the methodology used to collect the data, the results obtained and their implications, we review the dimensions of user satisfaction that others have suggested ought to be investigated.

## Factors Related to User Satisfaction

Virtually all the studies found in the literature might be called academic in nature. They are concerned with what does and what does not affect the level of user satisfaction with computer-based products and services. Presumably if one could determine such a meaningful cause and effect model $[16]$ , one could develop policies and procedures that would lead to more satisfied users $[38]$ . Thus one would want to be sure to collect data on factors that affect user satisfaction, as well as the level of satisfaction itself, to determine what is unsatisfactory. Now let us consider the factors for which empirical evidence exists in the literature.

## User Expectations

Gutek [18] notes that users are likely to hold preexisting attitudes about most common services. When actual service is positive in comparison to initial expectations, users report high levels of satisfaction. On the other hand, when perceived service is below that expected, users are very likely to be dissatisfied. Rushinek and Rushinek [38], who found that fulfilled user expectations have a strong positive effect on overall satisfaction, support this notion. So do Comtois and Conrath [9] and Doll and Ahmed [11].

## System Usage

System usage is an unusual factor as it has been proposed as both a cause of user satisfaction and as a consequence $[4]$ . The causal argument is based on dissonance theory $[14]$ . Significant use of a service and a negative attitude toward it create cognitive dissonance, and the typical reaction is to remove the dissonance by reducing use to only that level which is mandatory or by adopting a positive attitude. The consequence argument is that attitudes toward an object influence intentions and ultimately influence behavior $[29]$ . Positive attitudes lead to increased usage. The evidence is not clear-cut, though it appears that usage has more impact on satisfaction than vice versa $[4]$ . In any case, the two are positively related.

## Technical Support

Rushinek and Rushinek [37] analyzed the influence of “technical support” – in terms of troubleshooting, training and documentation – on user satisfaction. While the results regarding training were inconclusive, they indicated that troubleshooting and the quality of documentation had a strong positive effect. The implicit argument is that the more supportive the EDP/MIS activity appears to be, the more the users will be satisfied with its products and services.

## Type of Computer

Several (e.g. [13]) have suggested that users favor micros over mini- and mainframe computers because of their simplicity, portability, privacy and the perception of greater user control. Current services, with common interfaces to all three levels of computing, would appear to make the type distinction less important. Nonetheless, Rushinek and Rushinek [38] tested the impact of this variable on user satisfaction. Their findings appear to confirm the suspicions of Farmer [13] and others, as users indicated a higher level of satisfaction with micro as compared to mainframe systems.

## Power and Control

Going back to the observations of Crozier [10], researchers have noted that when the users of a service lack power and control over the providers of that service, conflict and dissatisfaction with the provider are likely to prevail. This may be one reason why users seem more satisfied with micro than mainframe systems. Using the notion of interpersonal power, Butler [5] formulated and tested the following hypothesis: if a user of a service is dependent upon a provider of that service, the user's functional satisfaction with the service is positively correlated with the user's overall power over the provider. The results of his empirical work were inconclusive.

## User Involvement

The presumption is that user involvement during the system design phase will lead to a better understanding of the system, and one better tailored to specific needs. Therefore, users should be more satisfied with the system than if they had not been involved, a conclusion that is consistent with the theories of participative decision making $[26]$ . Baroudi et al. $[4]$ tested this relationship in the context of user information satisfaction, and found support at a statistically significant level.

## Communication

The problem of ensuring effective communication and understanding between users and system designers and programmers is not an easy one to solve. In particular, the lack of familiarity of most users with the specialized vocabulary of computers and information systems often makes communication difficult $[12]$ . Although no study was found assessing the impact of effective communication on user satisfaction, common sense would suggest a positive relationship between the two. Furthermore, it is hard to imagine meaningful user involvement without the means for effective communication.

## Research Questions

Although the purpose of this study is more descriptive than analytical, we felt it appropriate to postulate several research questions to provide a focus.

## Is Anyone Measuring User Satisfaction?

The first issue concerns whether or not organizations that one might expect to be measuring user satisfaction are doing so. In particular, we felt that any organization with more than 100 employees and over \$10,000,000 in gross revenue would be a candidate. Such organizations would be large enough to be likely to be using computer-based products and services. Furthermore, these services are probably of sufficient importance that one ought to be concerned whether or not they are satisfactory. In general one would expect that the larger an organization is, the more likely it is to have a formal mechanism to deal with that concern. This leads to two specific questions:

(1) What proportion of the larger (>100 employees and >\$10,000,000 in revenue) organizations have a formal procedure for measuring user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services?

(2) Are larger organizations more likely to have a formal procedure for measuring user satisfaction than smaller ones?

Another parameter that one could argue would have an influence on an organization's dependency on computers, and thus be concerned about whether these products and services are perceived as satisfactory, is industrial sector. The tertiary sector, being essentially white collar in nature, is very dependent upon computer based data processing and information systems. Banking and insurance are excellent examples. Many of the things they do today could not be done without computers. The secondary sector, manufacturing, also makes heavy use of computing, but the administration of these businesses is less dependent upon the computer. This is even more the case for the primary sector (e.g. mining). The resulting question is:

(3) Does the proportion of organizations that have formal procedures for measuring user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services vary according to industrial sector? The presumption is that the proportion is highest for the tertiary sector and lowest for the primary sector.

What Do Those That Measure User Satisfaction Do?

Taking the subset of organizations that have a formal means for determining user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services, a number of questions arise. Since most of these would seem to be obvious, they will be stated with minimal elaboration.

(4) What do organizations consider as important determinants of user satisfaction?

(5) What instruments and procedures are used to measure user satisfaction? In particular, do they measure user satisfaction as such, or do they use operational surrogates such as system usage?

(6) Who is responsible for measuring user satisfaction? Is it essentially an EDP/MIS activity or are outside experts and/or users involved to a significant extent?

(7) Who among the user population is being measured? Are users themselves being asked, or are representatives or managers of users being polled?

(8) When did the organization initiate the measuring of user satisfaction? How frequently is the exercise undertaken?

(9) Why is the organization measuring user satisfaction? What is the rationale used to justify the exercise?

(10) What is done with the results obtained from measuring user satisfaction? Who gets them and how are they used?

(11) What effects have been brought about by measuring user satisfaction? Has it improved the relations between users and EDP/MIS personnel?

## Methodology

The study was divided into two phases. The first involved sampling a broad population with a simple questionnaire to determine who does and who does not have a formal procedure to measure user satisfaction. The second phase covered only those organizations that indicated they had a formal procedure.

## Phase One

Subjects: The sample consisted of the EDP/MIS managers from 425 Canadian corporations, taken from the Canadian Key Business Directory (Dun & Bradstreet, 1986), subject to the following constraints. The organization had to have more than 100 employees and over \$10,000,000 in annual revenue.

Instruments and Procedures: Each manager received a personally addressed letter explaining the nature of our study, a simple one page questionnaire, and an addressed and franked return envelope. The questionnaire contained two questions. The first asked: "Does the EDP/MIS department have a formal procedure to assess the level of client/user satisfaction with the provision of EDP/MIS products and services?" The second was: If yes, who is responsible for (informed about) this activity?" We asked for name and title. This was the person to whom we would address the phase two questionnaire.

## Phase Two

Subjects: The subject population contained all persons (one per organisation) who were identified as being responsible for the organization's formal procedure to assess the level of client/user satisfaction with the provision of EDP/MIS products and services.

Instruments and Procedures: Each subject received a personally addressed letter explaining the nature of the study, a questionnaire to be completed, and an addressed and franked return envelope. The respondents were assured anonymity, although they did have to identify themselves if they wished to receive a copy of a summary report of the study.

The questionnaire was lengthy; it covered 13 pages. Unfortunately this was necessary if we were to cover all of the listed research questions. On the positive side, we felt that people who were involved in the targeted evaluation activities would tell us about them, since we indicated in the cover letter that they appeared to be on the forefront of such work, and they should be interested in finding out what others were doing as well.

The specifics of the questionnaire (a copy of which can be obtained from the senior author) were based on the literature (especially [2]) and a series of interviews we conducted with local EDP/M1S managers and evaluators. The subjects covered included: the approach(es) used to collect user satisfaction data, who designed it (them) and collected the data, the factors covered, the users targeted, the reasons for the exercise, how the results are used, and the consequences of measuring user satisfaction. Questions were generally of three types: those which had yes/no answers, those which involved checking items on a list (usually associated with a number or percentage), and those which used Likert-type scales (e.g.: not considered – very important, not relevant – principal objective, and deterioration to a very great extent – improvement to a very great extent).

As a pretest, the questionnaire was submitted to 10 graduate students who were studying information systems and research methodology. Problems of semantics, sequencing and form were brought to light and resolved, based on their feedback.

Telephone follow-up contacts were made one month after the first mailing to those persons who had not yet responded to encourage them to do so. A second copy of the questionnaire was mailed to subjects who indicated that they had not received the first.

## Results

## Phase One

Response Rate: We received 163 answers to the first questionnaire. Since the sample size was 425, the response rate is 38 percent. While most would consider this acceptable for a mailed questionnaire, we feel that the response rate would have been higher had our questionnaire not been associated with another, more lengthy one (138 responded to it).

Question 1: 42 organizations indicated that they had a formal procedure to assess user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services, 26 percent of those responding. Since there is no empirical literature on the subject, we cannot put this figure in the context of expectations.

Question 2: Size was evaluated in terms of both number of employees and annual revenue for those organizations for which we had such data. Groupings for each of the two dimensions were established based on distributing the organizations roughly equally across five categories (with approximately 30 in each one). The results can be seen in Tables 1 and 2.

In neither case is there any evidence to suggest that there is a relationship between organizational size and whether or not there is a formal procedure for determining user satisfaction with computer-based products and services. The respective chi-squares are: 3.485, $p \leq 0.480$ , for number of employees; and 0.451, $p \leq 0.978$ , for annual revenue. This we find very surprising, as we assumed that the larger an organization is, the greater would be its investment in computer-based white collar systems, and thus the greater would be the concern for determining whether or not these systems were perceived to be providing satisfactory services.

## Phase Two

Response Rate: We received 23 useable questionnaires, although in certain instances some of the data are incomplete, from the 42 organizations that indicated they had formal procedures to assess the level of user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services. This gives a response rate of 55 percent. One reason that it was not higher is that some of the organizations that said they had a formal system may have found that did not when placed in the context of our questionnaire (we received one reply which stated this explicitly).

Question 4: We asked questions about 33 factors which might be considered to be relevant when measuring user satisfaction. A five point scale was used for each: not considered, and four points from not important to very important. Table 4 presents these factors ordered by their perceived importance. The first thing to note is that the majority of our respondents (22, one set was discarded because the subject did not follow the instructions) considered all but the last seven factors as very important or next to it.

Let us now examine each of the seven factors discussed in the second section to determine how important our respondents think they are in regard to measuring user satisfaction.

User Expectations: The matching of expectations appears to be of considerable importance. It ranked second among all factors, as 16 checked very important and five checked the column next to it. The strong concern for the match between the help needed by users and that actually provided (9th on the list) gives further support to the perceived relevance of this factor.

System Usage: The responses to the statement on system usage (see 21) placed it almost two-thirds of the way down on the list. Only four persons deemed it to be very important, and looking at the collective responses it is obvious that many other factors were felt to be more important. Apparently our respondents did not consider it to be a major independent or dependent variable.

Technical Support (trouble shooting, training, documentation): Statements on these factors were just above the midpoint on the ranked list. Documentation (11th) and training (16th) are seen as important issues by a substantial majority of the respondents, but they don't make the top ten.

Type of Computer: We did not make any statements comparing micros with mainframes, but we did ask about the satisfaction with and the appropriateness of the choices regarding EDP/MIS supplied hardware and software. The responses to these statements (19th, 26th) indicate that our EDP/MIS respondents were more concerned about other issues of user satisfaction.

Power and Control: The four items at the bottom of the list (30 through 33) cover, either directly or indirectly, the relative power and control between the users and the service providers. Perhaps these low rankings can be related to the findings of Lucas [28] and Saunder and Scamell [39] who discovered that users do not generally perceive EDP/MIS specialists to be powerful. If the latter are aware of this, then power and control would not be perceived by them to be a source of user dissatisfaction.

User Involvement: Participation (8) was seen as an important factor (all but two persons checked very important or the column next to it). Furthermore, the statement on cooperation between users and the EDP/MIS staff was third on the list. This suggests a concern for user involvement, as well as for the following factor.

Communication: Five different aspects of EDP/MIS – user communication were listed. The two that dealt with the issue directly (accessibility of the EDP/MIS staff and problems encountered by users when communicating with them) were ranked 13th and 15th, showing strong but not overwhelming interest. The other three focused on information, and they were slightly further down the list (17, 18 and 22).

It is quite clear that most organizations use a mixture of formal and informal methods. While interviews and questionnaires are used in 15 of the 23 companies, even greater use is made of feedback from the managers of user departments. Not only does this head the list, but when respondents indicated who the members of the steering committee were, virtually all were senior managers of user groups.

While 11 organizations do measure system use, none of them do it in isolation; all 11 rely on other measures of user satisfaction as well. Thus, there is little evidence that EDP/MIS staff rely on surrogates rather than measuring user satisfaction directly.

Question 6: When it comes to the design and execution of the measurement of user satisfaction, EDP/MIS personnel are the ones most heavily involved. In only one organization of the 23 that responded are EDP/MIS personnel not involved in the design of the instruments and procedures. In this case it is the “corporate services productivity group.” In three instances use is made of evaluation specialists within the EDP/MIS department; and five organizations use specialists from other departments to help. But in only seven cases are system users involved, and only one firm indicated that they made use of external consultants.

By and large, EDP/MIS staff design the procedures, collect the data and analyze them, with little support from others, particularly users. This is despite the fact that EDP/MIS personnel appear to be very concerned about user cooperation, user participation and good user communication. There appears to be an inconsistency between their concerns and their actions.

Question 7: Users are subjects of the evaluation exercises, as one would hope, but in only 13 of the 19 organizations for which we have a response to this question. More often it is the managers of user groups who provide the data (in 16 of the 19 cases). Representatives of user groups are involved in 9 organizations.

EDP/MIS activities, and we may not have found the same results if we had sampled users. Still, despite the tendency toward justifying a behavior (measuring user satisfaction) by indicating that it is beneficial, the consistent positive responses suggest that things have improved, and that knowing the whys and wherefores of user satisfaction, as many authors have suggested, is worthwhile.

## Summary and Conclusions

Despite a literature which extolls the measurement of user satisfaction with EDP/MIS products and services, and has been doing so for more than ten years, not many organizations appear to do it. Only 26 percent of those who responded to our simple questionnaire indicated that they had any formal mechanism in place to gauge user satisfaction, and this sample was taken from the largest corporations in Canada. Furthermore, the vast majority of those who do, put the procedure in place within the last five years. Given that those who do measure user satisfaction perceive substantial value in doing so, the question is: why haven't more organizations decided to do it as well? While this is a research question which requires an independent investigation, we can make certain conjectures based on the evidence at hand.

As the literature suggests, and as we found is the practice, user satisfaction should be measured directly, rather than indirectly by means of “objective” surrogates, such as system use and the like. Such an approach, since it is “soft”, would be quite foreign to most EDP/MIS departments, and yet with but one exception these were the departments responsible for undertaking the activity. Furthermore, there was no evidence of external pressure to measure user satisfaction, and thus the initiation appears to have come from within the EDP/MIS departments in response to a perceived need. In fact in only 30 percent of the cases were the users involved in the development of the procedure. It is not surprising, then, to find that not too many have embarked on a task which is quite outside their areas of expertise. Why user groups and/or top management have not been more insistent in undertaking such an exercise, especially considering the importance of computer-based activities to most organizations, is another question worth asking.

For those who do measure user satisfaction, it was encouraging to note that their prime concerns were to improve the services they provide and to establish better relations with the users of these services. This was reflected in answers to two separate sets of questions: “what factors do you look at in your measure of user satisfaction?” and (five pages later) “why do you measure user satisfaction?” Our respondents also indicated that internal politics and objective factors, such as system usage and type of computer, were not particularly relevant to the measurement exercise. Of at least equal importance, the EDP/MIS respondents felt that the measurement of user satisfaction actually led to the improvements they sought.

We close by noting a significant shortcoming of the study. Our sample was comprised solely of those associated with EDP/MIS departments. While this is appropriate for determining what they are doing and why, the raison d'être of our study, it cannot provide us with the users' points of view. Issues, such as the value of such an exercise, cannot be fully resolved without knowing how users perceive them Thus our findings represent only one part of the picture. The rest will not be complete until the formidable task of sampling users in both organizations that do have a formal procedure in place and those that don't has been accomplished.
