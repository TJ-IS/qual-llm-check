# Effects of computer aided quality circles on organizational productivity and satisfaction

Dean Elmuti and Yunus Kathawala
Lumpkin College of Business, Eastern Illinois University,
Charleston, IL 61920, USA

The research presented here is a longitudinal field study that compares changes in perceptions of productivity and job satisfaction for participants and nonparticipants in a computer aided quality circles program in a multinational firm in Saudi Arabia. Employee efficiency, productivity and absenteeism changes related to the implementation of the computer aided quality circles program were measured by collecting and analyzing actual organizational data. Data for each of the measures were collected for an 24-month period, ranging from 6-months before to 18-months after adoption of the program. The results of this study indicated that participation in computer aided quality circles program had a positive and significant impact on productivity and job satisfaction. In addition, the research findings suggest that using computer aided quality circles tends to make clerical jobs more demanding at the same time challenging.

Keywords: Productivity, Efficiency, Satisfaction, Quality circles, Computer information systems, Real time worked, Absenteeism rates and participation.

## Introduction

The introduction of computer information systems into offices and plants has offered great promise for increased productivity and efficiency. At the same time, the use of this technology has potentially adverse effects on office employees such as alienation, anxiety, and discontent. While there is little consensus about what changes occur, there is general agreement that an understanding of the consequences that computer systems have on employees' jobs is a significant and unresolved topic of investigation and research. Early studies on the impact of computer information systems on office employees were characterized by speculation of one sort or another. Bradley (1977) noted that the use of computer information systems changed the nature of the employee's job. Their jobs provided less satisfaction, were more demanding, productive, and caused more anxiety than prior to computerization. Turner (1980), also found that changes do indeed take place in the task environment and that, from the employee's standpoint, these changes tend to make the job

Using computer based systems in a routine job also increased productivity. Massey (1986) conducted a survey to examine the impact of computerization on business performance, the results indicated that 54% of the respondents felt they had “lost” or only “broken even” with their decision to computerize.

In another study Rossman (1986) offered several suggestions to avoid “Pitfalls” of computerization, and one of the suggestions was to gain employees support by soliciting input from others regarding their area of expertise. Greenwood and Gupta (1983) state that improving business productivity can only be achieved through users and management participation and involvement in computerization process.

The central theme that runs through most of the studies on organizational productivity and satisfaction reflects a common belief that the use of participatory programs similar to quality circles (QC's) generally improves organizational productivity and satisfaction. Rosenberg and Rosenstein (1981) indicate that an increase in the level of participatory activity through QC's is associated with an increase in productivity and satisfaction. Johnson (1981) believes that QC's provide employees with the training and opportunity to analyze problems and the tools to develop solutions. Richards (1984) argued that white-collar workers are ripe for quality circles. He believed they offer greater opportunity for performance and productivity improvement than their blue-collar counterparts. Further, Marks, Mirvis, Hackett and Grady (1986) showed that QC participation led to improvement in employee productivity and a reduced absenteeism rate.

In his research, Griffin (1988) indicated that attitudes, behaviors, and effectiveness all improved initially as a result of quality circles, but then dropped back to previous levels, and Ruffner and Ettkin (1987) suggested that quality circles offer one alternative for dealing with the growing frustration, anxiety, and discontent of today's workers.

In allowing employees to participate in problem-solving, organizations develop a renewed interest in their organization's performance, and therefore in improving their own performance. The quality circle concept is a philosophy of management. It is not an end in itself, but the means of achieving greater productivity and job satisfaction. This paper discusses longitudinal and experimental field study that examines the effects of computer aided quality circles on organizational productivity and job satisfaction in a selected multinational organization in Saudi Arabia. It also attempts to provide insights into the understanding of using computer based systems aided by participatory programs (quality circles) in a business environment.

## Research Methodology

## Research Site and Participants

This research was conducted in the spare parts vehicle division of a large sized diversified multinational company in Saudi Arabia. The company presently is engaged in marketing, sales, and distribution of vehicles and spare parts throughout Saudi Arabia. During the time of this study, the company was not doing well financially, due to several problems including high operation costs, low productivity, high absenteeism rates, and intensive competition in the auto industry.

In response to these problems, the spare parts division management decided to computerize operations, aided by a quality circles program. This was done on an experimental basis before its implementation in the entire company. The division general manager invited several experienced Japanese managers to design such a program and to participate in its implementation.

The primary responsibility of the Japanese managers was to select a suitable information system, to train employees, and to manage the system. The spare parts division had a work-force of 318 employees at the time the study was planned. Volunteers were solicited through several information sessions. Employees eligible for the program were engaged in division operations, such as clerical, accounting and sales functions, warehouse activities, and newly hired data processing personnel. Forty-two of the 102 eligible employees chose to participate in the program. These volunteers were trained by the Japanese managers for two-weeks and placed into six quality circles on the basis of work location and area of responsibility.

The QC's training program in Problem solving skills included methods to identify system user problems, to set priorities, to discover causes, and to propose solutions. Meetings were usually held weekly for one to two hours on company time and in company facilities. A first line supervisor or a system analyst usually served as circle leader. Each circle had its own leader to coordinate its internal workings and to represent the circle to outside entities. The Japanese managers made it very clear from the beginning that the success of the computer based system depended, to a large degree, on the cooperation and involvement of all job holders and support from top management.

In order to have an accurate assessment of the consequences of the computer aided quality circles program, it was necessary to have a control or a comparison group. This control group was to consist of 42 of the 102 employees who chose not to volunteer for the project. The participant group was 100% male, with an average age of 28 years, average experience of 4 years, and had two to four years of college education. The control group was also 100% male, with an average age of 29 years, average of 5 years of experience, and had two to four years of college education.

## Measures

Three hypotheses have been developed to test the data from this study. They are as follows:

Hypothesis I. Participants in computer aided quality circles will report higher overall productivity than will non-participants.

Hypothesis II. Participants in computer aided quality circles will show higher levels of job satisfaction than will non-participants.

Hypothesis III. Participants in computer aided quality will report lower absenteeism rates than will non-participants.

The hypotheses are all stated to reflect the claims made by quality circles proponents and also the findings from the literature on organizational behavior.

A questionnaire was designed to include a wide range of job satisfaction and productivity indicators with measures derived from Likert's (1967) "Profile of Organizational Characteristics." The Likert instrument has been shown to have accept table levels of reliability and validity across a variety of settings (Taylor and Bowers, 1972). Responses are on 5-point scales ranging from very little extent to a very great. In addition, the Likert instrument was translated into Arabic, and, along with the English version, given to participants and non-participants. This was done to ensure better understanding of the subject matter.

A first survey was conducted before the implementation of the computer aided quality circles program and a second survey was conducted 18 months after initiation. The purpose of such a design was to compare changes in perception of job satisfaction and productivity for participants and non-participants in the computer aided quality circles program. The definitions of the variables used in this study are explained in Appendix A.

Actual Organizational Data. The method to assess the impact of computer aided quality circles on employee efficiency, productivity, and absenteeism rates was to use actual organizational data from the surveyed firm.

Data for efficiency, productivity, absenteeism, and realtime-worked measures were collected for a 24-months period, ranging from 6 months prior to the implementation of the computer aided quality circles program to 18-months after the project began. Average rates then were computed for one month intervals and compared between participants (N = 42) and non-participants (N = 42), the control group in this study.

Analysis of Perceptual Data. The statistical test employed to evaluate the hypotheses was the Univariate F-tests across time periods for each variable. As Table 1 shows, there were significant differences over time for each of the variables for the participant group before and after program implementation. There were significant differences from program initiation between the two groups with respect to productivity and job satisfaction.

Further analysis of the influence of participation in the computer aided quality circles program on productivity and job satisfaction was done with the use of multiple regression analysis. Table 2 presents the results of this analysis, which indicates a positive relationship between measures of productivity, job satisfaction, and computer aided quality circles as reflected in the multiple regression ratio. Results show that 58 percent of the variation in productivity and 47 percent of the variation in job satisfaction is explained by linear regression on the computer aided quality circles program dimensions. The F-ratios indicate that linear associations are statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Further analysis of the influence of participation in computer based systems without any aid from quality circles as perceived by participants in the program was also done with the aid of multiple regression analysis. This determined the proportion of variance in productivity and job satisfaction scores as explained by computer based system scores. An attempt was made, through an interview with participants in the project, to assess the impact of participation in computer based systems on productivity and job satisfaction without considering the influence of participation in a quality circle program. The approach taken to measure computer impact was from self-report measures. Respondents were asked to what extent the productivity and job satisfaction gains are due to the computer based systems, whether manually implemented quality circles would achieve the same results; and whether it was possible that computer based systems could achieve the same results without quality circles.

Table 3 presents the results, which indicate a positive relationship between measures of computer based systems and productivity and job satisfaction, as reflected in the multiple regression ratios. The results show 38 percent of the variation in productivity, and 22 percent of the variation in job satisfaction was explained by linear regression on the computer based systems dimensions. The F-ratio indicates that linear associations are statistically significant at P < 0.05. The finding also indicates that it was not possible that computer based systems could achieve the results without the aid of quality circles, as reflected in a significant decrease in resulting R squares for the productivity and satisfaction measures.

## Summary and Conclusion

Several significant findings emerged as a result of this study. First, the effect of participation in a computer aided quality circle program on employee perception of job satisfaction and productivity were tested statistically for magnitude and direction as well as for dependency. Statistical analyses were found to substantiate Hypotheses I and II, which predicted that participants in a computer aided quality circles program would report higher levels of productivity and job satisfaction than would non-participants.

Second, the productivity measures clearly indicate a positive impact of computer aided quality circle participation on employee efficiency and overall productivity claimed by QC proponents due to participation in the program. Although the overall productivity rate increased substantially for the project participants, no increase was found in the non-participants group.

Third, the results of this study suggest that significant differences were found between computer aided quality circle participants and nonparticipants in decreased absenteeism rates. Absenteeism rate dropped consistently for the project participants, while the absenteeism rates prior to the implementation of the project for the participant and nonparticipant groups had been the same. Quality circles provided employees with the training and opportunity to analyze problems and (with the tools) to develop solutions. Some employees learned how to correct and solve problems on their own, how to implement changes, and how to get involved in the decision-making process; all these responsibilities make clerical jobs more demanding, yet at the same time challenging.

A practical conclusion drawn from this study indicates that the introduction of a computer aided quality circle program into an organization further requires the introduction of multifaceted changes in person-job relationships. These changes include training in a variety of new skills, a greater degree of goal setting and feedback for specific tasks, group approaches to problem solving, and the structuring of communication flow between workers and management.

The spare parts division experience ties the concept of participation through quality circles and computer based systems to real life application. Quality circles are a viable means and effective tools for improving the work environment and offer an alternative for dealing with the growing anxiety and frustration of today's employees. However, such programs are dependent on well trained managers, and require continuous training and information dissemination among management and employees. Quality circles have high transferability as a method of initiating employee participation among a variety of organizations and applications. When properly run, they will yield benefits to employer and employee alike.

Another practical conclusion of this study is that true and lasting productivity gains and high level of job satisfaction can be realized only through the effective utilization of people and the system within which they operate. Productivity improvement is more than simply a matter of capital investment and labor. It involves a host of other factors, such as better work methods and techniques, the increased use of innovation and technology, motivation, and the utilization of proven management techniques.
