# Support systems for high level policy making: What makes them special

Dov Te'eni \*

The Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH 44106, USA

Two information systems for Governmental policy making institutions, one in Egypt and the other in Israel, are compared and contrasted to understand and describe the unique character of the decision-making activities in such institutions. It is argued, that the underlying model of decision processes that is assumed in the design of conventional DSS is inadequate for very high level policy making environments. A broader view of the decision-making activities is presented and is shown to fit into a theoretical framework that combines the structured and anarchic perspectives of decision making. It is further argued, that support systems for high level policy making require an unconventional approach for their development.

Keywords: Policy making, DSS, Decision making, Government, Development.

In the information systems literature, there seems to be a consensus that (a) it is important to use a systematic methodology for developing decision support systems (DSS), and (b) a common decision process is assumed, namely Simon's [13] three stage model of intelligence, design, and choice or some variant of it. Three methodologies are widely used for DSS design: the decision research approach [15], the linguistic approach [9], and the ROMC approach [14]. Recently, Ginzberg and Ariav [7] suggested that developers take a contingent approach in selecting the most appropriate methodology, because different methodologies are more suitable for different environmental conditions. In this paper, it is argued that, as a result of distinct contextual effects, policy making at high office differs substantially from the decision making processes that have been the target of conventional DSS, and will, therefore, require unconventional support systems. Moreover, support systems for high level policy making (HLPM) require a distinctive methodology and environment for their development.

The paper concentrates on the organizational aspects of support systems for HLPM. The first section gives two case studies of support systems for cabinet level policy making. One is about support systems for the Egyptian Cabinet and is taken from the work of El Sawy and El Sherif [6]. Their work describes a large information systems unit that supports the Cabinet by building isolated support systems. Their findings on the nature of policy making activities and implications for the design of effective support are briefly summarized. The second case summarizes experience with the construction of a unified support system for an Israeli Cabinet Minister. Put together, the two cases indicate some major characteristics of the information processing activities of high officials that determine the nature of the support they should receive.

The second section proposes a general analysis and design strategy that identifies opportunities for supporting the information processing activities. The third demonstrates this approach with the system developed for the Israeli Minister. The final section discusses the advantages of combining the structured and the anarchic views of decision processes to create a theoretical basis for building support systems for HLPM.

## The Egyptian Case

El Sawy and El Sherif describe the decision-making process in the Egyptian Cabinet as that of attending to sets of issues with shifting priorities. Sets of issues circulate continuously among key participants of the Cabinet and their immediate working environment. Issues are deliberated, and they are eventually resolved by decisions, actions, or environmental changes, often reappearing under new circumstances.

To support the top policy makers in Egypt, an information and decision support center was built to design and help implement support systems for specific issues. The center employs teams of both technical and bureaucratic agents to implement systems using standard tools, such as dBase III, Lotus 1-2-3 and Focus. Examples of the systems developed are a DSS for customs tariff policy formulation and an information base on all industrial companies in Egypt.

El Sawy and El Sherif propose a procedure for developing support systems for strategic decision making that structures an issue, defines the information requirements around it, defines the information support and decision support services, and implements the system. The authors note a number of shifts in emphasis between the conventional approach to DSS development and their own. The focus shifts from a decision maker with a specific decision to groups of interacting issues, from decision making and alternatives to attention and agenda. The favored domains shift from tactical and operational to strategic, from one shot decisions to recurring decisions, and from functional applications to cross-functional applications. The design objectives shift from customization for individual decision makers to group consensus.

These shifts are of sufficient impact to warrant a change in the structure of the support systems and the way in which they are developed and managed.

## The Israeli Case

A small unit of around ten information systems professionals was involved in building and maintaining a comprehensive information system to support the information needs of a single Cabinet Minister. The Ministry's organizational chart is depicted in Figure 1. The Minister, who is the principal decision maker (DM), has a large staff which includes technical experts in relevant fields and liaison officers responsible for the day-to-day contacts with his subordinates. The large amount of incoming information has made it necessary to create an information center, which in practice operated as an efficient but passive archive, i.e., it served primarily as a storage device.

The Minister is continuously confronted with major policy issues presented to him in the form of recommendations. These are usually made by subordinates and are often supplemented by external opinions. His decision may very well be the final stage in a lengthy and iterative process that consists of a thorough analysis performed by professional units in the organization. He and his immediate staff often need to rely on expert advice to review and evaluate the analyses presented to them. The minister also deals with numerous pressing issues that are to be decided upon at very short notice. He has management functions in addition to decision making, such as projecting leadership, but here we shall concentrate only on the decision-making aspects of his work.

The Minister's major decision-making activities involve:

1. evaluating recommendations and choosing between conflicting recommendations;

2. being briefed on issues that need no immediate action;

3. considering information supplied but not used in the preceding analyses (e.g., last minute developments);

4. taking into account his own personal stands, political views and sensitivities, and including information known to himself but not to his advisers; and

5. authorizing and assuming responsibility for the final choice of action or inaction.

Any one of these decision-making activities may be applied to a diversity of topics and may require the understanding of different knowledge domains. Moreover, in most decisions, one must consider a multitude of aspects that lead to different and often conflicting value judgments. For instance, decisions concerning industrial projects, such as authorizing the building of a new factory, may involve complex technological details, internal political disputes, diplomatic implications for relations with allies, domestic and economic impact, and more.

Furthermore, attention continuously shifts back and forth from one issue to another. The DM is under constant pressure to process more information than is feasible, given cognitive and time constraints. Uncontrollable events, the Minister's and others' activities, and changes in personal perceptions or values all affect which issue will be addressed next and how. Administrative mechanisms, such as the management of a strict agenda and set forums for attending to different kinds of issues, were designed to manage the processes of attending effectively to pertinent problems. The Minister's immediate aids are responsible for filtering and organizing incoming information around issues for his consideration.

The Minister's personal impact can be noticed in all aspects of the decision making activities. He exhibits an authoritative and systematic style and has expressed a high preference for a rigid framework for the decision process. At the same time, he has insisted on open and personal communication in a stimulating environment when appropriate. For example, personnel appointments are made in a forum that is administered in a fixed and converging pattern, while other regular forums are designed only for deliberation and idea generation and are left to follow their own dynamics.

In comparing the characteristics of the decision process normally followed in the development of conventional DSS with the characteristics of the decision processes in the Minister's working environment, the following differences in orientation are noteworthy. In HLPM, there is a striking variety in the content and form of the issues addressed. They can be intensely time consuming for relatively long periods or have to be completed within hours. They all seem to be critical. The conventional DSS is geared towards assisting the DM in the analysis phase, while in HLPM, the DM usually evaluates the product of analysis performed by subordinates or experts. In HLPM the DM deliberates over value judgments and requires that they be referenced explicitly in every analysis; in conventional DSS, value judgments are considered insofar as they can be embedded in a numerical criterion function. Moving from conventional DSS to HLPM, the emphasis shifts from analysis of a specific decision to control over the entire decision life cycle of many decisions. Conventional DSS rely on data from internal transaction processing systems and both internal and external management information systems, seeking automated data links when possible. In HLPM, automated data links are usually infeasible, due to a lack of procedural and technological standards among agencies reporting to the Minister. The information sources are usually secondary and are transformed by experts before reaching the Minister's desk. Political and national security considerations in HLPM will usually turn physical isolation into a blessing rather than an inconvenience. Finally, the DM's personal decision style is crucial in determining the information services.

## The Unique Characteristics of Information Processing

Put together, the two cases present some important insights into information processing activities in HLPM. The following is a list of attributes that capture the unique character of information processing in HLPM and, at the same time, have operational implications for the design of information systems to support HLPM. Some of these attributes may be found in lower level decision making environments, but in HLPM they will usually appear in extreme form.

Variety of decision types: HLPM can be characterized by a very large variety of topics that require different kinds of skills and knowledge for their detailed analysis. Furthermore, the DM tackles different issues in many different ways. Some issues are deliberated in wide forums, some have to be decided immediately with whatever information is instantly available, some can be covered in a predefined sequence of stages at regular meetings, and so on.

Staff work: principal decision makers in HLPM work closely with a staff of professional experts and personal aids. The DM rarely performs the entire policy making life cycle and depends heavily on the products of previous information gathering and analysis, as performed by others. These products are usually organized and presented by staff members.

The first and second attributes have immediate implications for the design of information systems. The support system will have to provide means for sharing information and knowledge throughout the entire organization (principal DM and staff). It should allow for the support of partial decision cycles and be able to incorporate the products of other segments performed by other agents. If it is to support the detailed analysis of the decisions, it will have to include multiple models for analysis.

Shifting from one issue to another: Attention continuously shifts from one issue to another. These shifts are largely governed by changes in the environment (e.g., public opinion).

A support system should allow for easy transition from one issue to another and include means for temporarily freezing issues and reopening them under appropriate conditions. Thus the support system has to manage the set of concurrent decision-making processes over time and ensure effective attendance to all issues.

Urgency: Issues that are treated in HLPM are often urgent due to a real crisis (e.g., a terrorist act) or political pressure (e.g., public opinion voiced in the media).

The support system should be able to react quickly with whatever information it has available or with information that can be generated instantly.

Criticality: Matters that reach the attention of a high level policy maker are usually of national concern, they affect the nation or require national resources. The actions taken will usually be open to intensive criticism making it important that they be consistent with articulated policies and demonstrably so.

The creation or augmentation of support systems will usually be justified economically. It is, therefore, important to promote technical feasibility of new applications and spend less effort on economic feasibility. In addition, the public scrutiny given to critical decision underscores the role of support systems in promoting consistency in decision making.

Multiple functions and objectives: Issues usually span a number of functional areas with conflicting objectives. There is a high probability that relevant information will exist from previously resolved issues or with agencies outside the DM's immediate responsibility.

An integrated information or knowledge base will be needed. Easy access to other sources of information will also be needed.

Strategic: The majority of issues addressed are strategic in terms of time span and magnitude of impact. Even if they are not explicitly concerned with long range planning, they may have long term implications, such as the impact of local incidents on relationships with other countries.

Thus, the decisions will usually be ill-structured with no well-defined decision models, and so will require suitable support for human-machine collaboration.

Recurring decisions: Some of the decisions reappear under different or similar circumstances; e.g., the appointment of personnel and Governmental aid to local industries. The system should provide relevant knowledge from past experience and possibly feedback on past performance.

Personal effect in decision making: Clearly, the DM's value judgments and priorities are crucial to the choice of issues to be addressed and their outcomes. In addition, the personal style of the DM at HLPM and, to a lesser extent, that of the immediate staff, has a strong impact on how issues are addressed. In HLPM, one would expect the designer to adapt to the user's style rather than augment it (e.g., see the designer's dilemma in [5]).

One may argue that some of these attributes can be found in conventional DSS, even if they appear to a lesser degree. The contention is that each of these attributes has implications that have generally been ignored in the design of conventional DSS, and moreover, that the overall impact of these attributes is sufficient to call for a reevaluation of the appropriate support systems and the way they should be developed.

## Supporting the Decision Making Activities

## A General Approach for Developing HLPM Support Systems

The unique character of HLPM calls for unconventional development strategies. One such strategy can be demonstrated in the general approach taken by the information systems team that worked for the Israeli Minister, in which the author participated. The systems analysis and design focused on the DM and his team and the sole objective was to improve the quality of the decision-making processes. Whether the quality of decisions influences, for example, the public's welfare or the DM's popularity was outside the scope of the analysis. This assumption is a short cut to Churchman's classical systems approach [2], in which a statement of the system's overall objectives is the first step. The analysis focuses, therefore, on identifying sources of interference with the effective information processing required for high level policy making and examining how, if at all, the interference can be eliminated or corrected with a computerized support system.

This approach differs from current approaches to DSS design. Unlike the prototype approach, it requires a detailed plan of what can be done to improve the decision process and does not rely on the DM's perception of the problem. In this respect, it also differs from the ROMC approach in which designers are required to support the user's representation of the problem. Indeed, like the decision research approach, it systematically identifies the differences between normative and descriptive models of decision making. The user's perception, understanding, preferences, etc. may all be sources of defective decision making. In contrast, however, to the decision research approach, the proposed approach shifts the concern from the analysis stage of the decision-making process to the entire policy making life cycle. Moreover, the system designers are concerned with the control of a multitude of decision-making processes and not with the support of a specific decision situation.

A Support System for HLPM: The Minister's Example

The design of the Minister's support system can demonstrate the general approach. It was proposed that the organization's information be structured to be effective in the presentation and storage of data for computerized support of the entire decision life cycle, without imposing unwarranted structure on the processes involved in policy analysis. Consequently, a simplified model of the decision process was formulated and served as a point of reference for the design of the Minister's DSS.

Structure was achieved by requiring all parties bearing on the issues to summarize their positions using a fixed format that included a general description of the problem, background information, alternative solutions, recommended action, and reasoning behind the recommendation. The DM's staff could then consolidate the individual positions into a uniform decision platform.

The consolidated decision platform's format is intended to facilitate the PM's final judgment by presenting the essential information effectively, supporting the assessment of the quality of the decision-making process, and promoting further search for information. The functional design of the decision platform is based on the criteria for quality decision making as proposed by Janis and Mann [8].

They suggested the following major criteria for effective decision making:

1. thorough consideration of a wide range of alternatives;

2. thorough consideration of the full range of objectives to be fulfilled and the values implied by the choice;

The uniform decision platform, shown in Figure 2, was designed to encourage these decision-making activities. Background information should include objectives, a general set of relevant beliefs, and any evidence that bears on the issue. Alternative solutions are the union of solutions suggested by the participants. Positions of all participants are taken from their recommendations. When the issue is resolved, the consolidated platform is updated with the resolution and the reasoning behind it. It seems profitable to underscore the operational recommendations, which conceptually are a part of the alternative recommended. These include major stages, deadlines, conditions under which to review the decision, etc.

Assuming that decisions can and will be structured on a uniform decision platform, it is possible to formulate a simplified model of the decision process in HLPM. The model is depicted in Figure 3. The major stages of the decision process are initiation, evaluation and choice, and implementation. The DSS consists of an information base and programs that help control the decision process. A decision is initiated internally by the DM and his staff, or externally. External initiators may be from within the organization headed by the DM or from outside the organization. Once a decision is initiated, individual positions are solicited and a consolidated decision platform is constructed. Transforming a problem into the decision platform may be lengthy and expensive, it may require expert analysis and depend on multiple sources of information, but only its product is incorporated into the support system. The platform is studied and the recommendations are evaluated. The process is iterative. The DM may require additional information or a revised platform before making a final decision. In other cases, the DM will review the issues with no action-oriented resolution but with the understanding that the issues should be pursued or frozen until a later date or event.

Figure 3 shows the complete decision life cycle for a single issue, yet there are many issues open at various points of the life cycle. The overall organizational decision-making activity should, therefore, be pictured as a dynamic set of life cycles in which attention shifts from one to another.

It also indicates the interactions between the DSS and the decision-making process. The following short scenario illustrates how the DSS is used at various stages of the process. New information is fed into the information base and the DSS helps initiate new decision-making processes, supplement existing decision platforms, and trigger control actions on current decision processes. Once the decision has been initiated, the participants are asked to present a recommendation in a uniform decision platform. From these recommendations a consolidated platform for decision is constructed like the individual platforms and includes the positions of all the participants. If necessary, the consolidated decision platform is supplemented with additional information from the DSS before it is submitted to the DM.

The consolidated decision platform is presented to the DM as the front page of the brief along with all the original communications. The DM evaluates the alternatives, often in the presence of some or all of the parties that contributed to the process. Information that was added during the evaluation and choice stage is added to the consolidated platform. Usually, a resolution is reached and passed to the DM's subordinates to be implemented. In some cases, the issue is suspended, with instructions given – such as to obtain more information or wait for some event. At this stage, the DM's information officer enters the consolidated platform into the DSS and initiates control over implementation. The implementors are responsible to the DM's staff for reporting on the progress and quality of the decision implementation stages. Actual results, as opposed to planned results, are recorded and may later serve as a basis for decision assessment.

The DSS supports the coordination of information flow and storage. It helps to process and analyze the control information, the portfolio of decisions made by the organization, and the incoming information. Above all, it serves to integrate all types of information into a coherent picture and to manage the decision-making activities over time.

Note again that use of the DSS is dependent on the utilization of a uniform decision platform. Control over the work carried out by the DM's agents is facilitated by structuring the presentation of their results. Uniformity in presentation helps the coordinator of a particular decision to review and integrate the incoming material into one decision platform. A coherent and uniform platform eases the DM's task of processing the large amounts of relevant information. Yet, no attempt is made to impose structured processes on the analyses that are performed by the DM or his agents. Since most of the problems encountered are ill-structured, such an attempt would be futile and damaging (see also [1]). It should, however, be noted that this procedure may indirectly affect the analysis stage by the requirements it imposes.

The uniform platform enables the user to ask WHAT-IF questions; i.e., to postulate hypothetical situations with different assumptions and material constraints and then to search for decisions that would have been affected by these new situations. For example, a decision on a joint research project with another country was conditioned, for financial reasons, upon good relations with a third party. In a different context altogether, one may pose a WHAT-IF question on the bi-national relations with the same third country by positing a temporary situation of “bad relations”. This new state would, in turn, trigger a “no longer valid” situation for the joint project decision, thus enabling the user to inspect all the resulting effects of “bad relations” defined in the information base. As a result, the joint project would have to be reevaluated and, possibly, suspended.

## Technology and Procedures

The most difficult stages in building this system were the analysis and design of procedures. Once these were complete, it was decided to use a hybrid of a data base management system, a word processor, and a set of project control functions. The following short account of the technology and its use underscores the special needs for support, security, and privacy.

Decisions and other pertinent information, such as major communications, were stored as records with fixed and variable length segments. The fixed segments included the data required for the decision platform and for the project control functions. The variable segments stored additional information in free format. Indices were constructed for keywords and other selected fields, and dictionary files were built to control input and enable easier retrieval. For example, a geographical dictionary allowed the user to retrieve records according to regions, e.g., a query on North Africa would extract records about Morocco. The data base management system included standard functions, such as file maintenance, a query generator, and a report generator. Hierarchical relations among records allowed the user to specify general decisions that were comprised of retrievable subtasks.

A member of the DM's staff was appointed information officer and was assisted by a clerk. The information officer was responsible for introducing the system, monitoring data entry, organizing decision portfolios from the system's output and other sources, organizing ad hoc reports with the report generator, and building and maintain dictionary files, of which the keyword dictionary was the most time consuming. He also served as a liaison between the other users and the data processing professionals, who were responsible for operating, securing, and maintaining the system, augmenting software (primarily writing new reports), and instructing new users. For example, the previous WHAT-IF question on issues contingent upon relations with another country required a new report that was developed within twenty four hours using query functions from the data base management system. The system was implemented on a mini-computer with six work stations located in offices in the same building and additional terminals for maintenance and development.

Security and privacy were major concerns. For example, the users purchased a dedicated mini-computer in order to isolate the system even though computer services were available on a mainframe. The system was constantly monitored by the data processing team and the work stations were physically disconnected from the computer when the particular office was closed. Printed output was automatically logged and strict procedures for shredding it were enforced. Access to the system was restricted according to user identification and working station; only the information officer could access the entire data base. Despite these and other measures it was difficult to introduce the system to people who were accustomed to a very high degree of confidentiality. It took, for example, over four months to persuade the stenographers to use the word processor rather than typewriters in order to avoid retyping data.

## Discussion

There are two major perspectives in studying decision processes, the structured and the anarchic. The structured perspective [10,11] is based on Simon's three stage model of identification, design, and choice. The anarchic perspective builds on the garbage can model [3]. On the whole, the anarchic perspective is better suited for ill-structured problems in which there are conflicting value judgments [4]. In comparison to the structured perspective, the anarchic perspective does not assume a clear beginning and ending of the decision process, it recognizes that issues can be addressed without choice and explicitly treats disagreement on goals. Moreover, the anarchic perspective explicitly considers participation and contextual dependence. Participants are important as carriers of problems and solutions and the presence of particular personalities has strong implications on the decision process and outcome. Exogenous events influence the decision process, and in particular, they redefine the decision maker's priorities for attending to concurrent issues. Finally, in the anarchic perspective, passage of time plays a part in reemerging issues and in evolving evaluation criteria.

In a study of decision processes in the Canadian Government, Pinfield [12] suggested that elements from both perspectives can help in understanding the decision behaviors of top policy makers. Indeed, it would seem that the conventional approach to DSS builds on the structured perspective of decision making, while some of the findings about support systems for HLPM can be explained using the anarchic perspective. In particular, note the treatment of issues without necessarily choosing a solution and the importance of explicitly considering conflicting value judgments. Furthermore, it is important to consider the dominant role of a Minister's personal style and values. Note also the need to manage and attend to concurrent issues, the need to follow up on the earlier phases performed by others and to reactivate dormant issues with no clear beginning or end for a specific decision process, and the overall need to manage the implementation of decision making. All these findings fit in well with constructs that appear in the anarchic rather than the structured perspective. Yet the structured perspective should not be ignored. In the Israeli case, it served well in designing the support systems by identifying and organizing information processing activities according to their functional contribution to the overall process of policy making, and, thereby, determining opportunities for computerization. This is manifested in the decision process of Figure 3 and the corresponding decision platform of Figure 2.

There also seems to be a fit between some of the directions for developing support systems for HLPM and general trends in executive information systems that have recently emerged in the private sector. For example, Zmud [16] suggests that senior executives' decision behavior differs in a few respects from that traditionally supported by DSS: senior executives manage issues rather than people or resources, they create decision situations rather than react to them, they work with ill-defined, mostly qualitative, and wide-ranging data sets, they work through networks of people rather than decision models, and they implement decisions rather than make decisions.

## Conclusion

The paper began with a description of Egyptian and Israeli experiences with HLPM, from which emerges a unique character of decision-making activity. The practical solutions that were implemented on both sides of the (peaceful) border differed drastically. The Egyptians organized a large unit of professionals who help end users to implement specific DSS. The Israelis built a unified support system for a single Minister that structures the presentation of information over the decision life cycle. Yet from both solutions, we can deduce that HLPM requires unconventional development strategies. Moreover, there is an apparent need to consider alternative models of the decision-making activity as a basis for designing appropriate information systems. This need can be understood in a more general framework as a transition from a single perspective on decision making to one that builds on both the structured and anarchic perspectives. Once this transition is accepted, the designer can systematically examine the implications of the broader perspective for the design of information systems for high level policy making institutions.
