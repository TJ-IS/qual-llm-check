# Organizational learning curve in software installation: An empirical investigation

Satya Prakash Saraswat and

John T. Gorgone

Computer Information Systems, Bentley College, Waltham, MA 02154, USA

Findings of an empirical investigation conducted to determine the presence of an organizational learning curve in the implementation of software packages for business applications are presented in this paper. Time spent by teams on the implementation of forty software packages on various mainframe, mini, and turnkey systems over a period of six years is analyzed.

There is an organizational learning curve in the process of software implementation, and its magnitude varies from 6 to 36 percent. Higher rates of learning were observed for packages implemented on mainframes than those on mini-computers and turn-key systems.

The paper suggests that these findings can be utilized for measuring and improving organizational productivity in the areas of software development and implementation and cost-benefit analysis of large systems projects.

Keywords: Learning curve, Software installation, Organizational progress function, Productivity improvement, System implementation.

## Introduction

The application of quantitative techniques of operations research for effective management and control has become widespread in recent years. One of these techniques, primarily used for generating operating forecasts and controlling production costs in manufacturing and assembly tasks, is the industrial learning curve $[5,8,17,19,23]$ . It has been commonly observed in manufacturing and assembly operations that as a worker performs a task repeatedly, the direct labor requirements to complete a subsequent unit of the task decline. Its magnitude depends entirely on the cumulative number of previous repetitions of the task. This phenomenon is known as the “learning curve”. The industrial learning curve is an exponential relationship between the cumulative production and the decrease in the amount of time required to produce a subsequent unit of output. It is found to be universally present in industrial tasks $[10,11,12,13]$ .

The amount of effort required by groups of individuals to complete a subsequent unit of task declines continuously as the total number of tasks performed by the groups increases. The installation teams in large software companies represent such a system of men and machines. Since the implementation of business applications packages, as opposed to their design and development, is an activity that involves a large number of essentially repetitive steps, organizational learning is expected to be present in this process. The objectives of this study are to measure the organizational learning in the implementation of applications software, and to identify the most important characteristics on which the magnitude of organizational learning depends.

## The Industrial Learning Curve and Organizational Learning

In a comprehensive study of the production of aircraft in the 30's, T.P. Wright observed that one of the important factors affecting the cost of the aircraft was a consistent improvement in the efficiency of workmen with practice in repeatedly performing the assembly tasks required for aircraft production [22]. The relationship between the cumulative number of the aircraft produced and the number of man-hours required to produce a subsequent aircraft was remarkably precise. With each doubling of the cumulative number of aircraft produced, the direct labor cost to produce an additional unit declined by 20 percent. This uniform decline in direct labor cost requirements was attributed to worker efficiency resulting from learning due to repetitive performance of assembly tasks. This phenomenon has been known as the “industrial learning curve” in manufacturing plants [11,15].

Figure 1 exhibits a plot of the exponential relationships in a typical learning curve on an arithmetic scale. With each doubling of cumulative units of production, direct labor hours required to produce a subsequent unit decline by 20 percent. This is known as a 20 percent learning curve.

When the performance of an organization or a team of workers in an organization improves due to the repetition of a task, the phenomenon is known as the “organizational progress function”, or “learning curve”. These curves have also been termed: “experience curve”, “cost-quantity relationship”, “cost curve”, “efficiency curve”, “production acceleration curve”, “improvement curve”, and “performance curve” [14]. The industrial learning curve is different from the organizational progress function in two important aspects: the scope of the task and the structure of the learning system. The industrial learning curve centers on a limited number of physically identifiable, quantifiable, and measurable steps in the production process, and the individual worker as the task agent. The progress function, on the other hand, deals with a conglomeration of tasks as a process, and a group of individuals and organizational sub-units as the learning agent. Progress functions are found to be present in many tasks and large scale processes such as nuclear power plant design, railroad operations and computer program development [17].

## Implications of Organizational Learning Curve For Software Productivity

Control of software development costs and reliable estimation of resource requirements for software projects have been a major concern in the software industry since the sixties $[4,6]$ , and it has been widely recognized that the software industry has been slow to adopt engineering discipline in controlling costs. Commonly used quantitative techniques of management and control can be used in the software industry, as well as in manufacturing. This fact has been recognized by experts in software engineering, and it can be argued that learning-by-doing may contribute to productivity gains in the design and implementation of large software systems. Large corporations like IBM have explored this possibility extensively in the design and manufacture of computer hardware and have found it very useful as a production control tool [1]. Magnitudes of organizational learning, when accurately measured and their determinants properly identified, can be utilized in the software industry for improving organizational productivity in the areas of software development, software implementation, and cost-benefit analysis of large systems projects.

An important reason for undertaking the investigation of the learning curve phenomenon in software implementation can be found in the nature of the software installation process. While the development of applications software is primarily a non-repetitive intellectual activity, multiple implementations of the same software package involve a significant number of steps which are predominantly repetitive in nature $[18]$ . Planning and preparation for implementation, user training, management orientation, and system implementation testing are some of the activities which are very similar in nature from one implementation to the other. If the time spent by implementation teams on these activities can be isolated from the total time spent on a given implementation, an investigation into the nature of the organizational learning curve can be successfully conducted.

## Research Methodology

In order to contain the rapidly increasing costs of software design, development and implementation, many software houses have implemented comprehensive record keeping systems to account for the time and resources spent on various phases of software projects. Reliable and correct record keeping is also required as a contractual obligation on software projects undertaken for various agencies of the federal government, for appropriate cost allocation and accounting purposes. Properly designed project control and reporting forms can thus be analyzed to perform an ex-post-facto analysis of the time spent by software installation teams on different projects with a reasonable degree of accuracy.

Data were collected from five software vendors in the Atlanta metropolitan area. Reports for the implementations of forty off-the-shelf software packages completed from 1974 to 1981 by similar installation teams were analyzed. Time spent on implementation planning and preparation, user training, document preparation, minor conversions, management orientation, and implementation testing activities were collected from these records.

In the first part of the statistical analysis, simple linear regression was applied to estimate the parameters of the logarithmic transform of the general exponential model of organizational learning. The “b” coefficients of this transform, which represents a linear relationship of the form Y = a + bX, are estimated at this time. These represent the actual magnitude of learning for each package. The dependent variable in the regression model was the time spent on each subsequent implementation, and the independent variable was the cumulative number of implementations, for a given software package. Figure 2 contains a representative scattergram of the cumulative number of implementations (predictor variable) and time required for a subsequent implementation (dependent variable) for this part of the analysis. The regression parameters were tested in the model by a general analysis of variance approach.

In the second part of the analysis, multiple regression was utilized to identify the determinants of organizational learning from package characteristics. The coefficients of learning for individual applications packages, obtained from the first part of the analysis, constitute the observations on the dependent variable in the second part of the analysis. Since the objective was to identify the characteristics which determine the magnitude of actual learning, packages not showing statistically significant positive learning were excluded from this part of the analysis. In addition, four of the forty packages analyzed in the first part show a presence of serial correlation, as summarized in Table 1. These packages were also excluded from the analysis in part 2. Software package characteristics, identified in Table 2, were used as the predictor variables.

Similarly, the magnitude of organizational learning for implementations on mainframes and minicomputers also becomes significant at p = 0.10. The type of hardware on which an applications package is implemented is thus the only predictor variable which explains the differences in magnitude of learning for individual packages. Since the hardware type is a qualitative variable, a simple one way analysis of variance was performed on the model, and the results of this analysis, summarized in Table 3, were confirmed by multiple range tests. These results indicate that Hardware Type is the only independent variable which explains the variance in the magnitude of learning in software implementation, to a significant degree.

This analysis also indicates that more organizational learning occurs when applications packages are implemented on mainframe computer systems. This may be attributed to the fact that a greater degree of specialization in operating systems, equipment, and implementation procedures is required on the part of implementation teams for mainframe systems, and this provides a greater and continuous opportunity for organizational learning in software package implementation and support. Since the design, development and programming of software is not considered a part of the installation process, the design characteristics of the software package will not make a significant difference in the amount of learning on the part of implementation teams or organizational sub-units. Except in the case of turnkey systems, hardware acquisition and installation decisions in all of the companies which provided data for this study were made independently of the applications packages. Organization wide software implementation, maintenance and operation procedures were thus based on the previously acquired hardware and system software, rather than the specific applications software implementation. This is another reason why hardware type may be a significant determinant of organizational learning curve. It appears that the hardware type is a surrogate variable which encompasses not only the computer system in an organization but also the organizational structure, procedures, and management approach, all of which are likely to have a strong influence on the rate of learning.

Previous studies of the industrial learning curve have found 20% learning in most industrial tasks. The results of this study are in general agreement with these findings, in so far as significant coefficients derived in this study cluster around a 20 percent value, though the range of these coefficients is 6.0 to 36.2 percent. Therefore, the important conclusion is that organizational learning exists in the implementation of applications packages and the magnitude of such learning depends on the type of hardware on which the applications packages are implemented. Ample support is provided to the argument that organizational learning is a predictable and quantifiable phenomenon, similar to individual learning in industrial tasks, and that the general exponential model of the learning curve is applicable to both individual and organizational learning.

## Recommendations For Future Research

This study has demonstrated the applicability of the basic model of organizational learning to software implementation process. Improved models of progress, like Stanford's model with a productivity drift factor, can be tested for software implementation $[20,24]$ . The results of our study, which was conducted with historical data, probably should be verified in an experimental setting with a controlled learning environment. Research should also be undertaken in the area of model building for cost-benefit analysis of software development and implementation projects. These could include the long term benefits of organizational learning as an integral component in the organizational strategy for system development and implementation cost estimation.
