# Group processes and the development of information systems

A social psychological perspective

Israel Borovits, Shmuel Ellis

and Orly Ycheskel

The Marcel and Annie Adams Institute for Business Management Information Systems, Faculty of Management, Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv 69978, Israel

This article discusses the influence of intra-group processes on the development of information systems. Two alternative models of the Trinity Concept were compared in terms of the influence of communication patterns and group working procedures on productivity and intra-group relations. The first model has been characterized by individual work and circular communication, and the second by collaborative group work and multi-directional communication. Predictions are been made in favour of the latter over the former.

Keywords: Group Processes, Communication Patterns, Conflict Resolution, Group Performance, Trinity Concept.

## Introduction

Much research has been carried out to determine which variables affect the productivity level of the information systems development team and the quality of their work $[30]$ . Even though nobody denies or ignores the importance of personal or interpersonal variables on performance level $[40]$ , little systematic research has been done to consider these social-psychological variables in fields such as software development teams or GDSS $[5,20,21]$ . Underestimating the need, as reflected in the sparse research, is surprising in light of the fact that the process of information systems development is one in which social systems are involved, and communication problems, reciprocative influence, power relationships, and personal interests may take place.

Here, we will focus on a classical social system – the Trinity. We (a) review and analyze the social interaction processes in the Trinity (b) propose a research paradigm for investigating the effect of these processes on group performance, and (c) recommend different working methods.

## The Trinity: Designer, Decision Maker, and User

Churchman [8] distinguishes between three individuals who provide the prerequisites for a system to be successful in achieving its desired objectives: the client, the decision maker, and the designer. This is presented in Figure 1.

In an extreme case, all three may reside in one person. However, in most situations they are highly complex groups made up of interacting individuals. Each entity has its own purpose, and so has each individual within an entity. During the development life cycle of a system, it is possible to assume that the designer, decision maker, and client are represented, respectively, by the information systems analyst, the implementor, and the user for whom the application is being developed.

The composition of the development team varies with time, depending on the nature of the activity at different points along the system development life cycle [1]. During initial analysis, only information analysts are required. When the conceptual design is completed, system designers join the team to translate the conceptual framework into concrete specifications. These restrict the programmers in their writing and testing of programs; they also guide the preparation of procedures. The implementor (for example, the director of the data processing department) acquires and allocates resources, and controls their use. Thus, the director may influence the team's targets, working procedures etc.

Fig. 1. The Trinity Concept.

The information analyst, the implementor, and the user all play a role in the task of developing the system. They need to understand their roles as well as have a theory about the system. They must try to understand it, what influence they can have on its development and implementation, and where - and why they should exert influence or compromise.

The importance of mutual understanding and cooperation among the team in attaining a common goal suggests a need for substituting the Trinity concept. This is characterized by collaborative work of the team members in the three building stages: information analysis, building the conceptual framework, and translating it into procedures. The traditional model will be called model A and the alternative model B.

From a Social-Psychological perspective, one may argue that the Trinity members make up a group. In other words: the Trinity is an interpersonal interaction system where two or more individuals engage in behavioral exchanges in pursuit of a common goal.

People in groups may be classified in several ways. For example: their norms, patterns of role differentiation, leadership styles, level of cohesiveness, level of interpersonal attraction and mutual dependence, social power, communication patterns, working procedures, competition versus cooperation, etc. Among those, two variables are most important in differentiating the two alternative Trinity models:

(1) Communication patterns. The traditional model is characterized by transferring information from one person to another, and then on to another, with a general lack of common meetings among them (i.e., a circular communication). In contrast, the second model is characterized by inter-group communication among the three.

(2) Working procedures of the group. On one extreme of this variable each of the team members works independently, while at other extreme the group works collaboratively from the start to the end. In the traditional model, each member of the team works independently: few meetings take place for discussion, coordination, and feedback. The second model implies common performance of the task by all team members, using interactive feedback throughout all stages.

## The Influence of Group Work and Communication Patterns on Task Performance

Evidence in favour of cooperative team work using multi-directional and continuous communication is repeatedly found in papers dealing with the relation between team work processes and performance.

## Team work and group performance

Much research has been done in different fields of behavioral science comparing individual versus group performance $[17,22,32,41]$ . Empirical findings pointed to two prominent directly conflicting results: on the one hand, it was found that “best members” perform better than a group $[6,27]$ ; however, on the other hand, group’s performance is better than individual’s performance $[24,25,37]$ .

The lack of unequivocality in the research results led to classifications of conditions in which group performance is superior or inferior to individuals performance. These distinguish between group performance according to task type, the unique contribution of each group member to task performance, etc. [9,10,19,16].

These classifications were followed by much research comparing individual performance versus group performance under different situations and tasks. It was found, for example, that in tasks involving resolution of problems with one “right” answer, the group was better than the “average individual” [13] but less than the “best individual” [4].

In tasks involving learning and concept attainment, group performance was usually superior to average individual performance and inferior to the best individual [18]. In abstract tasks, groups took more time to get to solution, but had more correct solutions than individuals [26]. Also, in performing complex tasks, it was found that group performance tended to be superior to individual performance but inferior to statistical pooling of responses [35].

Douglas [12] summarized the conditions under which group performance is better than individual performance:

1. Tasks requiring more than one person.

2. Tasks requiring the division of labour.

3. By combining estimates, groups reduce possible error.

4. Groups produce more and better solutions to a problem.

5. Groups make a more efficient use of resources.

6. Groups eliminate inferior ideas more effectively.

7. Social motivation is higher in the presence of others.

8. The presence of others leads to increase productivity.

9. Where tasks involving random error are in process, groups tend to produce superior judgements.

10. Groups achieve more than even the most superior individual members.

11. Groups learn faster.

12. Groups tend to make more risky decisions.

13. Groups minimize the sense of responsibility of any one member for the outcome.

14. Groups ensure tenacity of purpose by committing member to group decisions.

15. The feedback of performance is from multiple sources and is therefore likely to be more accurate.

16. Involvement and participation produces a high level of commitment."

Developing information systems is a complex task involving creativity and analytic thinking, usually executed by more than one person and with division of labour among team members. Therefore, according to items 1–11 and 14–16 in this list, one can deduce that model B has an advantage over model A. The list also suggests that groups take more risks and group members may evade personal responsibility for their behavior. Inspite of these shortcomings, the advantages of model B (in items 1–11 and 14–16) constitute sufficient compensation for these possible disadvantages.

## Communication patterns and group performance

The group communication patterns affects group's performance. Douglas [12] stated that "group processes are first of all a result of the group's supervision on the communication within the group". The free flow of communication creates a comfortable climate within the group. It improves interpersonal relations among members, and is one of the major factors affecting its effectiveness in decision making processes [34].

Cartwright and Zander [7] summarized the relevant literature about communication networks. They concluded that group performance (measured by speed, accuracy, learning achievements, etc.) was highly influenced by the communication structure of the group. In simple tasks the group performance was found to be better in groups with a more centralized structure of communication. On the other hand, in complex tasks, centralized groups performance was inferior. It should be noted that members of groups with a decentralized communication structure expressed greater satisfaction with their conclusion than members of centralized groups.

Research concerning groups of experts engaged in highly complexes decision making found that the nature of the interaction, particularly the extent to which new information was exchanged, is the most important determinant of the quality of the decision outcome $[33,36]$ . Free paths of communication enable group members free participation in the problem solving process in expressing their opinions, evaluations, or criticisms. This is especially important when the group needs to reorganize to perform a complex task in a limited time.

One should note that most of these findings concern centralized versus decentralized communication patterns. Although the two Trinity models were characterized by circular versus multi-directional communication, it is still possible to find some characteristics that are common to the circular and centralized pattern. In both, information flow is not multi-directional and this imposes limits on free information exchange. It can therefore be expected that model B, characterized by free and decentralized communication compared to model A, may be more efficient in complex problem solving tasks, especially when a task is time limited.

## The Effects of Collaborative Group Work and Communication Patterns on Conflict Resolution and Interpersonal Attraction

Developing computerized information systems is a complicated process. In order to attain a common goal, the final information system, one should expect full cooperation among the three types of team participants. On the other hand, beyond this common interest, each of the team members has unique personal interest in the final product. For example, the decision maker might wish to minimize the cost of development (within performance limits), the designer will prefer well defined concept of the system including its purpose, measures of performance, etc. (or may be a system as simple as possible). Finally, the user, “ignoring” the other two, may desire very high system performance. Therefore, goal attainment requires a process of problem solving (successive negotiations and bargaining) pursuing satisfaction of or consensus in all aspirations. In other words, intra-group agreements should have an impact on the information system design. Thus, investigation of the perceived divergence of interests and the intra-group dynamics may contribute to a better understanding of the information system development process. More specifically, this suggests an effect on the team’s productivity.

Kelley and Thibaut [22] split problem solving situations to three types. The first two represent what is commonly investigated in studies of problem solving, group performance, or decision making. The third represents the typical situation studied by conflict researchers:

(1) Information distribution – who knows what. For example, the user knows the requirements, the designer knows how to translate the user's requirements into a design, and the programmer has the knowledge to convert the design into an executable program.

(2) Response distribution - who does what. For example, the user must define the requirements, the designer is responsible for the design, and the programmer for coding and unit testing.

(3) Conflict of interest – who gets what. For example, the user gets the working systems, the designer and the programmer have satisfaction in completing a new workable system, and other (financial) benefits.

Intuitively, the first two types may seem to have little to do with conflict in the ordinary sense. However, practically, if not theoretically, problems of information generation and response are inextricably intertwined with problems:

1. People tend to distort information in order to maximize their own prestige within the group [2]. Thus information is used as a means to achieve personal outcomes.

2. Information is not the only valuable resource in the inter team bargaining process. Social interaction outcome can be measured on several dimensions. For example: Material outcomes (such as money or possession); effective outcomes (hate, anger, insult, or regret); social outcomes (power, control or popularity), etc. Considering the problem of labour division, one may find that job satisfaction is a desired reward, but social status and power may also be seen as a “reward” and may influence the expected outcome.

The struggle for material profit reflects a clear-cut case of conflict of interest. The designer, for example, may economize on the project budget, desiring to maximize short term profit, but spoiling the final product quality and/or future relations with the client.

It is beyond the scope this paper to review the social psychological literature dealing with conflict resolution. However, to Pruitt and Rubin [29], direct contact among antagonists team members and the communication that often goes with it may have a number of beneficial effects: First, contact and communication allow the parties to explain actions and proposals 'that might otherwise elicit defensive reactions or retaliations. Second, they contribute to understanding the other party's motives, sensitivities, and the like; this makes it easier to deflate concerns with image loss and to act in ways that will not upset the other. Third, contact and communication permit problem-solving discuss ions in which substantive and procedural issues can be resolved. Without such discussions, the search for mutually acceptable formula must take the form of trial and error, which has many pitfalls. The surest way to achieve an integrative agreement is through discuss ions of needs and priorities. Finally, contact and communication usually contribute to interpersonal attraction and hence to the development of positive bonds. Research [14,39] shows that contact encourages attraction more often than antagonism".

Direct communication should not always be regarded as panaceas. It may be useless or even worse [31] in intense conflict situations [11]. Krauss and Deutsch [23] found that communication between people in conflict does help, but as long as the intensity of the conflict is relatively small, and the parties have large perceived common ground. Under conditions of intense conflict, people do not use communication channels in order to engage in problem solving, since they do not believe in each other and they really do not want to consider any solution that is acceptable to the other. By contrast, they use these channels to try tricks and to threaten rather than for resolving problems [38].

It should be noted that the information analyst – user – programmer contractual relation does not allow escalation of the relationship to open hostility, since the client may cancel the project and/or go to alternative professionals. Under this assumption, it seems that the level of conflicts within Trinity team members will be relatively low so that free communication will normally contribute to conflict resolution.

One of the main factors inhibiting concessions in the problem solving process is “loss of image”. One technique for entering negotiations without loss-of-face is by sending hints of willingness to make a particular concession” or to take some alternative cooperative action. These signals must be both noticeable and disavowable, because the hint should be received, but the sender should be able to deny this behavior, otherwise the person’s negotiation position is weakened. Nevertheless, the use of conciliatory signals is generally easier in successive, face-to-face communication, as characterized by model B.

This again suggests the superiority of model B over traditional model A.

## Experimental Paradigm

In the light of this discussion, four independent variables may differentiate between the two Trinity models:

A. Level of conflict (cooperation vs. competition):

1. distribution of resources.

2. type of resources.

B. Communication patterns.

C. Task structure.

The main dependent variable is, of course, group efficiency in terms of quality and speed of the information system development process.

It should be noted that those variables reflect the system's approach to group definition. In other words:

(1) The subject matter of the group (Trinity) research is the behavior of the system “group (Trinity)” as a whole. That is, the primary dependent variable consists of the behavior of the group and not the individual members.

(2) The primary independent variables in group (Trinity) research are characteristics of the system, i. e., the structure of interdependence and regular processes of interaction among its elements and not characteristics of the elements.

Social-psychological research on the structure of interdependence and interaction processes has been performed in: (a) research on bargaining and negotiations; (b) research on dilemmas, including the prisoner's dilemma and related two-person games, N-person dilemma games, and social traps; and (c) research on coalition formation.

The basic experimental paradigm applied in these research areas was experimental gaming: “... a laboratory task used to study how people behave in an interdependent situation, where (a) each individual must make one or more decisions that affect his own and the other’s welfare; (b) the outcomes of these decisions are expressed in numerical form; and (c) the numbers that expresses these outcomes are chosen beforehand by the experimenter. Most of the research has focused on so-called “mixed-motive” games, in which the interests of the parties partially coincide and partially conflict.” [28].

Mixed-motive games can be used to investigate dyadic interpersonal relations and/or N-person interdependence or conflict. The experimental paradigm which can better describe intra-group conflict and provide evidence for demonstrating the superiority of one of the above two Trinity models is the “N-person Prisoner’s Dilemma (NPD) [3]. In the NPD each member of a group is permitted to make one of, typically, two choices, either a “competitive” or a cooperative” choice. The competitive choice is the individually optimal choice for each subject, because that choice provides the subject a higher payoff than the cooperative choice, regardless of the choices of the other members of the group. The competitive choice also has the property that it reduces the payoffs to all the other group members. Moreover, it reduces the others’ payoffs; indeed, if all group members make the competitive choice they all have lower payoffs than if they all make the cooperative choice.

There are several reasons for the popularity of such experimental games: (a) This facilitate a precise definition of the reward structure Of the group; (b) They yield behavioral, as opposed to questionnaire, measures and hence appeal to the desire for objective observations shared by most experimental psychologists; (c) They are usually easy to employ and economical; (d) Many sources of variance found in more natural settings are absent in experimental games, enhancing the power of tests of significance.

Finally, it should be noted that the use of gaming is not unfamiliar in information systems research. Gray and Borovits [15], for instance, applied the “prisoner’s dilemma” paradigm for investigating a strategic GDSS. However, this application was a case study in decision making process and not an integral phase of systematic research on structural variable effects within the group.

## Conclusions

This article has focused on the comparison of two alternative models of the Trinity concept within a framework of social-psychological variables. Literature from social-psychological domains, such as individual vs. group performance, communication patterns and interpersonal relations, have been reviewed and discussed.

The superiority of the proposed alternative Trinity model over the traditional one was posited: The reasons for the relative advantages of model B over model A are:

(a) Complex tasks which require proper division of labor, creative and analytical thinking, communication and feedback, are better performed by groups than by individuals.

(b) Complex and/or time limited tasks are done better when communication channels in the group are open and multi-directional.

(c) Frequent contact and free communication may contribute to conflict resolution:

(1) by allowing the team members to explain their actions, proposals and express their attitudes or feelings;

(2) by enabling constructive discussions on substantive as well as procedural topics;

(3) by improving intra-group relations and strengthening interpersonal attraction;

(4) by making possible the transformation of conciliatory non-verbal signals among the team members.

In conclusion, it is our belief that model B will prove to be a better way of obtaining high quality information systems products.
