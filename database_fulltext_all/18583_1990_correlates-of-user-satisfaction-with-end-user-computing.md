# Correlates of user satisfaction with end user computing

An exploratory study \*

Magid Igbaria and Sidney A. Nachman
Department of Management, College of Business And Administration, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

The growth of end user computing (EUC) has been a phenomenon in the information management world of the 1980's. Our study, based on data from 104 end users in six large companies, examines the individual, organizational, and system factors affecting the success of EUC, as reflected in end user satisfaction. A key result is that the leadership style of information system managers is positively and significantly related to user satisfaction. Other findings of the study indicate that significant positive relationships exist between user satisfaction and hardware/software accessibility and availability, computer background of users, user attitudes toward end user computing, and system utilization. The results also demonstrate that computer anxiety and user age are negatively related with end user satisfaction. No significant relationships were found between user satisfaction and gender, education, and organizational level. Implications of the findings for practicing information systems managers are discussed.

Keywords: End user computing, Leadership style, Satisfaction, Computer anxiety, Attitudes.

## Introduction

The role of the end user has changed dramatically in recent years (Davis and Olson, 1985). Users have taken responsibility for interacting with applications, entering information, preparing outputs, and making decisions based on these outputs. in this context, users in many organizations are thereby considered active parts of the whole system. End user computing (EUC) has received attention in the MIS field, both from practitioners (Guimaraes, 1984) and researchers (Rivard and Huff, 1988). Although EUC has spread through entire organizations, where in some companies EUC utilizes 40–50 percent of the computing resource, “it is still poorly understood” (Rockart and Flannery, 1983). There has been tremendous growth in EUC (Benson, 1983), which is more dramatically described by Alavi and Weiss (1986) as “A rapidly growing and irreversible phenomenon”, thereby highlighting the need to investigate factors associated with the success of EUC.

Researchers have identified several criteria of MIS success as follows: system usage Pavri and Huff, 1989; Lucas, 1978); user satisfaction (Baroudi and Orlikowski, 1988; Rivard and Huff, 1988); and performance (Lucas, 1978). Thus far, user satisfaction and system usage have been used most extensively to measure MIS success. Although system usage has been recommended as an indicator of system success (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982); Ginzberg (1978) has argued against the system usage indicator, stating that it is not strongly related to the quality of decision making; thus, advocating the user satisfaction approach instead. in this vein, Tait and Vessey (1988) also recommend a user satisfaction measure as an indicator of system success.

User satisfaction has, thus far, been the most popular approach in the literature for measuring system success. This popularity is due, in part, to the difficulty of evaluating the economic outcomes of MIS impact and adoption, thus, user satisfaction provides a viable surrogate measure for system success. Given the dominance of the user satisfaction construct in recent MIS literature, it is applied in this study.

## Background on User Satisfaction with End User Computing

End User Computing (EUC) is defined as the process by which users develop applications where by they have access to computers, data, and support systems (Benson, 1983; Rockart and Flannery, 1983), and the capability to control their own applications and computing needs directly (Davis and Olson, 1985). Thus, end users range from command-level use, through parametric use, to programming (Rockart and Flannery, 1983). User control, or at least discretionary use, are the acid tests. Our study provides a broader definition of EUC which includes anyone who uses microcomputers or computers in general.

End User Satisfaction (EUS), in this study, is conceptualized as the affective attitude toward a specific computer application by any hands-on user of microcomputers or computers. End user satisfaction is evaluated in terms of (a) the information product; this focuses on the quality of output, independent of the information source (i.e., the application), (b) electronic data processing (EDP) staff and service, these reflect the attitude of the EDP staff as well as the quality of the EUC relationship with the EDP staff, and (c) knowledge or involvement; this depends on understanding the applications and EUC participation in its development (Baroudi and Orlikowski, 1988).

## Focus of the Current Study

Overall this study investigates the following factors that are considered correlates of system success as they relate to end user satisfaction: (i) behavioral characteristics (e.g. leadership style, system utilization by users) and individual beliefs (e.g., computer anxiety, and attitudes toward end user computing); (ii) system characteristics (e.g., end user computing software, applications, system accessibility, and availability) and (iii) demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education, user computer experience, and organizational level). Our hypotheses (as presented below) formally state the expected relationship of behavioral, system, and demographic characteristics with EUC success, as measured by user satisfaction.

Hypothesis 1: Leadership style, as reflected by greater consideration and structure of task for users, will have a positive relationship with end user satisfaction.

The traditional literature in leadership reports that greater initiation of structure and consideration, the components of leadership style, will be positively related to greater subordinate satisfaction (Bass, 1981). Leadership and managerial skills necessary in information systems environments remains an under-researched area in the MIS literature. Bartol and Martin (1982) highlight the need for research on management of information systems and people skills. They urge research on leadership style for a better understanding of the managerial activities that need to be matched to various information system activity needs. Turner and Baroudi (1986) suggest that the managerial practices for information systems may be more similar to general management practices than is widely held. In the present study, task and people skills of managers, as related to end user satisfaction, are examined. This orientation is consistent with Lucas (1989) when he emphasizes that leadership style, as reflected in task orientation and consideration, is important to the successful management of information systems. He also stresses the importance of examining leadership style at all managerial levels in the organization (e.g., CIO/ISD, manager of operations, project leaders, and end user support).

Hypothesis 2: Greater availability and accessibility of hardware/software resources will be positively associated with user satisfaction.

This hypothesis raises the question of the role of end user support. Since this depends upon the availability and accessibility of the computerized information systems resources (Camillus and Lederer, 1985), the degree of effectiveness of EUC, as measured by user satisfaction, will increase when users have a greater degree of availability and accessibility.

Hypothesis 3: Greater computer anxiety will be negatively associated with end user satisfaction.

End user computing brings with it major changes in the role played by end users in application development and system success. Limited use and dissatisfaction with the system may be ascribed, in part, to a lack of knowledge of the end-users of how they operate, or concerns about system capabilities (Howard and Smith, 1986). Furthermore, computer anxiety, or fear of computers, is described as a surprisingly powerful and widespread psychological phenomenon (Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1989), is a major factor associated with inhibiting broader system usage and a greater dissatisfaction with the system. A number of studies have documented the importance of computer anxiety as a key variable related to system success (Igbaria et al., 1989). Computer anxiety refers to the tendency of an individual to be uneasy, apprehensive, or fearful about current or future use of computers. It was suggested that computer anxiety is a factor in end users' reluctance to use this technology (Howard and Smith, 1986).

Hypothesis 4: Favorable attitudes toward end user computing will be positively associated with user satisfaction.

The importance of attitudes in influencing the eventual success of MIS, and specifically EUC, has been emphasized by Lucas (1978), Rivard and Huff (1988), Zmud (1979), and others. The significance of attitudes derives from the proposition of attitude theorists (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) that individuals' attitudes toward an object (in this case EUC) play an important role in influencing their subsequent behavior toward it. This suggests that, by measuring the attitudes of the affected individuals toward the change, one can assess the success of the outcome of that change (Rivard and Huff, 1988).

Hypothesis 5: Greater system utilization will be positively associated with user satisfaction.

Recent literature examined the relationship between system utilization and user satisfaction. Conflicting results have been reported, while Ginzberg (1984), and Sanders (1984) found that user satisfaction and system utilization have low correlations, and in some cases no correlations; Baroudi et al. (1986), and others found a strong relationship between them. These ambiguous findings lead to the conclusion that both system utilization and satisfaction may need further investigation, thus their inclusion in the current study.

Hypothesis 6: Individual characteristics are associated with user satisfaction.

The importance of individual characteristics in influencing the eventual success of MIS has been emphasized by Lucas (1978) and others. Salient variables in this category include computer experience, age, gender, organization level, and education. The experience that a user has received from using computers and interacting with the information system plays a very important role in affecting end user computing (Kasper and Cerveny, 1985). They concluded that greater end user experience increases the likelihood of EUC success. Age and education were found to be related to system success (Igbaria et al., 1989). In other studies it was suggested that women are likely to be more anxious about computers and more dissatisfied with systems (Dambrot et al., 1985). Zmud (1979) reported that individual differences created specific user attitudes, and he included demographic variables to test this phenomenon.

## Methodology

## Sample and Procedure

Participant in this study were end users from six large companies (above 1000 employees) with extensive and established operations (in existence for 40 years or more), all located in the northeast United States. The six participating companies represented the following types of institutions; banking (company 1), higher education (company 2), food production (company 3), high technology (company 4), and two manufacturing companies (companies 5 and 6).

Following the broad definition, EUC included essentially any hands-on use of microcomputers or computers (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1988). The MIS manager was asked to select about twenty five to thirty end users and send those individuals a letter encouraging them to participate in a study. The request emphasized the importance of their participation to the company, and ensured the confidentiality of their responses.

Through that procedure, 160 end users from the six companies were invited to participate in this study. Completed questionnaires were received from 108 end users (67.5% response rate). The final sample consisted of 104 end users (n =

19, 20, 15, 17, 19, and 14 usable questionnaires were received from companies 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively), excluding incomplete questionnaires.

## Characteristics of the Sample

An analysis of variance revealed nonsignificant differences across the six organizations for end users' age, education, and gender. Given the similarity in the demographic characteristics of end users across the six companies, and relatively small sample sizes of the companies, all analyses were conducted on the combined sample of 104 end users. Relatively minor effects were observed for education (the high technology organization had the highest education levels); for gender (a lower percentage of women in banking, and food production), and age (the educational institution had the oldest membership). Of the usable questionnaires, 54% were received from men, and 46% from women. Age ranged from 23 to 65, with the mean age for end users being 34 years old. Among the end users, 58% were professionals and the remaining 42% were managers. Approximately one fourth of the end users had completed some college, and an additional (67%) of the end users were college graduates. A wide variety of functions was represented in this sample: 38% in MIS area, 18% engineering and R&D, and 13% in accounting.

## Measures

End User Satisfaction. The short form measure of end user satisfaction designed by Baroudi and Orlikowski (1988) was employed, with appropriate modifications, as recommended by Ives, Olson and Baroudi (1983), to make it specifically relevant to EUC. The end user satisfaction items measure information about both overall end user satisfaction; and satisfaction in three specific areas of EDP Staff and Services, information product, and End User Knowledge and Involvement (Baroudi and Orlikowski, 1988). An overall end user satisfaction score was calculated by averaging the individual scales to determine the overall end user satisfaction. The alpha reliability coefficient of the 13 items was 0.89.

Leadership Style. The Leader Behavior Description Questionnaire Form-XII (Stogdill, 1963) was used to measure leadership style. This widely used measure (Bass, 1981) reflects two important constructs of managerial behavior: (1) initiating structure is represented by a ten item subscale representing task related activity on the part of the manager, such as assignment of tasks, scheduling work, maintaining performance standards, and encouraging use of uniform procedures; and (2) consideration, is also represented by a ten item construct, measures behaviors of the manager by way of some of the following examples; “how friendly and approachable” a manager is; “treats group members as equals; “is willing to make changes”; and “puts suggestions made by the group into operation”. Responses range from (1) never to (5) always, for the degree to which managers exhibit behaviors on the job. The twenty item Likert type scale had reliabilities of alpha = 0.81 for initiating structure and alpha = 0.83 for consideration. Overall the two managerial constructs, especially consideration, have been linked with satisfaction with work or satisfaction with supervision.

Hardware and Software Accessibility and Availability. The end users were asked whether they have free access to computer resources and whether they used their own computers. Accessibility has been perceived as a type of support from the MIS department and reflects the “freedom” to interact with ones own computer (Benson, 1983; Rivard and Huff, 1988). As perceived by the end user, in many cases MIS departments did not provide sufficient support in terms of resources and having free access to these resources can be beneficial.

Computer Anxiety. This reflects general anxiety about using computers and was measured by the Raub (1981) scale. The instrument asks individuals to indicate their agreement or disagreement with 10 statements reflecting anxiety, apprehension, confusion, hesitation, etc. In using computers in general. The response options, anchored on a five-point Likert-type scale, range from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. The internal consistency reliability of the scale as used in this study was alpha = 0.91.

Attitudes toward End User Computing. The attitudes of end users span the spectrum from positive perceptions toward EUC to feeling “forced” into user’s environment. The items used to construct the attitude scale were adapted from prior research (Goodhue, 1986), with appropriate modifications to make them relevant to EUC specifically. Individuals were asked to indicate the extent of agreement or disagreement with six statements concerning EUC. The mean of the six items was used as an overall index of attitudes toward EUC. The alpha reliability coefficient of the measure was 0.81.

System Utilization. Based on previous research on MIS usage (Srinivasan, 1985), a set of categories were derived to produce the system utilization measure. Twelve specific items used to measure system utilization. Sample categories include “budgeting,” “planning,” and “looking for trends”. The total number of categories in which end users reported system utilization was used as an overall index of system utilization. The twelve category scale had an internal consistency reliability of alpha = 0.74 in this study.

Computer Background. This was assessed by asking respondents to indicate whether they had experience in using different types of computer software, different computer languages, and development of computerized information systems. Responses were coded “0” for no experience, and “1” for some or more experience. The total number of categories in which respondents reported experience was used as an overall index of computer experience. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was alpha = 0.72.

Demographic Indicators. Single item questions were used to ascertain respondents' gender, age, education, and organizational level. Gender of respondents was coded (1) for men and (2) for women. Age was reported in years. Education consisted of five levels from (1) high school to (5) graduate degree. Level in the organization hierarchy consisted of 5 tiers: (1) professional; (2) first level manager or operations manager; (3) first level supervisor; (4) department heads and middle managers; (5) strategic management.

## Analyses

Prior to undertaking hypotheses testing, the reliability of the scales was determined. For each composite scale, Cronbach's alpha coefficient was calculated. Factor analysis was performed for the end user satisfaction measure. As described above, Cronbach's alphas, which vary from 0.72 to 0.91, satisfy Nunnally's (1978) suggestions that reliability coefficients of 0.70 or higher are adequate.

Factor analysis using the SPSS-X procedure was performed on the 13 items of end user satisfaction, as recommended by Baroudi and Orlikowski (1988). Principal components analysis and the method of varimax rotation was used to extract the factors and to determine the most meaningful factor solution (Kerlinger, 1986).

The factor analysis yielded 3 factors with eigenvalues greater than unity, accounting for 64.7 percent of the explained variance. Table 1 presents the results of the factor analysis. The three factors are listed in order of their importance, as determined by the magnitude of their eigenvalues, and the percentage of variance explained. Factor loadings of the items used to interpret a given factor are listed in bold face in Table 1. Factor 1, with an eigenvalue of 5.6, accounted for 43.2 percent of the expected variance and it was labeled "Information Product". Factor 2 had an eigenvalue of 1.6, and accounted for 12.3 percent. It was labeled "EDP Staff and Services". The third and final factor had an eigenvalue of 1.20, and explained an additional 9.2 percent of the variance, and was labeled "Knowledge and Involvement". This analysis suggests the factor structure of the scale is stable and consistent with Baroudi and Orlikowski's (1988) findings, and provides further evidence for the reliability and validity of their measure. The internal consistency reliability estimates of the three factors were: (i) information product, alpha = 0.83; (ii) EDP staff and service, alpha = 0.84; and (iii) end user knowledge and involvement, alpha = 0.75.

The next step, a second order factor analysis (Nunnally, 1978) was conducted to test for underlying homogeneity in the first order three factors that were extracted. The results showed a single factor solution, with an eigenvalue of 2.03, which accounted for 67.8 percent of the explained variance. Therefore, the 13 component items from the three factors were aggregated and averaged to create a composite measure of overall end user satisfaction, and the alpha coefficient of the resulting 13 item scale was 0.89, thus confirming the homogeneity of the items and the appropriateness of combining them. The reliability and validity of the measures satisfy Nunnally's guidelines. Following from that, the hypotheses of this study were tested using Pearson's correlational analysis.

## Results

## End User Satisfaction with Leadership Style

Results in Table 2 indicate significant, positive correlation between initiating structure, consideration, and all measures of end user satisfaction. These results are consistent with the stated hypothesis and provide support for earlier findings in the organizational behavior literature. The highest correlation (r = 0.32) was between consideration and the satisfaction sub-scale of knowledge and involvement, suggesting that the end users who are most comfortable with computer usage are those who receive consideration from their managers. The fact that all the Baroudi and Orlikowski (1988) measures of satisfaction correlate significantly with both leadership constructs, consideration and initiating structure, of the LBDQ Form-XII, reinforces the hypothesis that leadership behaviors are viable and important components of a successful information system implementation. These findings further underscore the need for continued research on leadership styles and user activity relative to information systems (Bartol and Martin, 1982; Lucas, 1989).

## End User Satisfaction with Availability and Accessibility of Hardware / Software Resources

In this study, we found an interesting relationship between end user satisfaction and computer resource accessibility and availability. Because of the lack of free access to computer resources and lack of support, the end users may be dissatisfied with the quality of the product, EDP services, and of their participation and involvement in developing the system, as well as dissatisfied with the overall system. Rivard and Huff (1988) emphasized the need to have free access to computer resources and being independent from EDP services. Note the presence of positive correlations among hardware/software availability and accessibility, user satisfaction, and its components. In practice, more accessible information resources may lead to more satisfied end-users.

## End User Satisfaction with Computer Anxiety and Attitudes toward EUC

Consistent with expectations, data show that computer anxiety is negatively related to information Product, EDP Staff and Services, Knowledge and Involvement, and Overall Satisfaction. End users with high computer anxiety were found to be less satisfied with the quality of system output, the EDP staff, and services; less involved in its development; and less satisfied with the system as a whole. The finding that attitudes toward EUC correlated positively, and significantly, with the three factors of user satisfaction and with overall satisfaction, confirmed the importance of attitude to MIS system success emphasized in prior studies (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982; Lucas, 1978).

## End User Satisfaction with Demographic Variables

The computer background of end users plays an important role in the context of EUC. There is a positive and significant relationship between this variable and overall user satisfaction, as well as their satisfaction with the quality of the product, and the EDP staff and services. Surprisingly, the relationship between computer background with knowledge and involvement was found to be positive, but nonsignificant. An awareness of the relationship of computer background to overall satisfaction might be helpful to MIS managers when selecting systems and developing training programs. Users with better computer background are likely to be more confident in their ability to use the system and more satisfied with the experience.

Also, education, organizational level, and gender are not correlated with end user satisfaction. The high level of education attained by the majority of the respondents in this study may provide a partial explanation for the generally low and nonsignificant relationships observed (67.7% of the respondents completed undergraduate degrees and had some graduate or professional study).

As expected, age is negatively related to Information Product, and overall satisfaction, which is consistent with Igbaria et al.'s (1989) findings. This indicates that younger users tend to report a higher degree of satisfaction with the system, than do older users. Non significant relationships were found between EDP Staff and Services, and Knowledge and Involvement, and age. A significant positive relationship was found between end user satisfaction and system utilization, representing that greater system usage is positively associated with greater satisfaction with EUC.

In our study, a significant relationship was found between end user satisfaction and system utilization. Thus the more the system is used, the greater the satisfaction with EUC; this supports strongly the work of Baroudi et al. (1988), where user satisfaction and system utilization are positively correlated. The point is that as end users use the system for more tasks, they feel more comfortable and familiar with it, and they will discover new uses for it, which may lead to enhance user satisfaction with the EUC system.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The results of this study of data from six different industries represent an important step forward in examining the relationships between individual characteristics, organizational characteristics, system characteristics and end user satisfaction. Firstly, following Baroudi and Orlikowski's (1988) suggestions, we modified their measure to make it more meaningful within the context of EUC. The instrument was factor analytically derived, and it revealed three areas underlying end user satisfaction: Information Product, EDP Staff and Services, and Knowledge and Involvement. This provided information about overall end user satisfaction with the EUC system, as well as information on specific types satisfaction in the three areas. The reliability and validity of this instrument, across the five different organizational types, provides future researchers with added confidence in the examination of user satisfaction and system success.

A major finding of this study, in support of Hypothesis 1, is that there is a significant relationship between leadership style and end user satisfaction. The continued study of leadership (Bartol and Martin, 1982) and supervisor relationships with users (Turner and Baroudi, 1986) with respect to measures of satisfaction, well being, and productivity continue to be of importance to the successful implementation and usage of information systems. Our study was an initial step toward systematically testing leadership style as it relates to the success of information systems. Further research in this area could move toward clarifying under what conditions a particular leadership style is appropriate for a particular set of end users with respect to satisfaction, motivation, and performance outcomes. This would begin to answer many questions facing practicing system managers today with regard to user resistance to information systems.

In accordance with Hypothesis 2, hardware/software accessibility and availability were found to be significantly related to overall end user satisfaction, as well as the other three areas of satisfaction. From an applied perspective, this underscores the responsibility of those providing user services, especially information centers, to provide unrestricted access to computer resources. Efforts to strengthen hardware/software accessibility and availability could reflect dividends in terms of increased end user satisfaction.

Computer anxiety was found to be negatively related with end user satisfaction, while attitude toward EUC is positively correlated with satisfaction. This brings with it a major change in the role played by end users in interacting with the system and/or developing their own applications. According to the proposition of attitude theorists (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975), attitudes toward the change play an important role in influencing the outcome of the change. The results also reiterate the importance of computer anxiety in inhibiting end user satisfaction.

The importance of prior computer background in promoting increased end user satisfaction was highlighted by the finding that computer background is positively correlated with end user satisfaction. This emphasizes the need to increase an individuals' familiarity with EUC applications, and thereby alleviate their anxiety. Additionally, the provision of opportunities to gain experience in using the system would be beneficial in encouraging increased end user satisfaction. Finally, system utilization was found to have a strong relationship with end user satisfaction. This finding indicated the importance of system utilization; this may lead one to be more familiar with the system and to discover new uses for it, thus increasing end user satisfaction.

The results obtained here carry some important implications for successfully managing EUC. Management, by way of their leadership style, should play a key role in assisting end users in utilizing a system. They should make greater efforts to create a climate that facilitates EUC, making resources accessible to support end user activities. In such organizations, the results of the study suggest that end users will be more satisfied with EUC, and thereby increase the effectiveness of computerized information and decision support systems.
