# Sources of software and levels of satisfaction for small business computer applications

Sufi M. Nazem

University of Nebraska at Omaha, College of Business Administration, Omaha, NE 68182, USA

The objective of this research is to investigate how small businesses are coping with the process of computerizing their business operations. Results of a sample survey indicate that sources of software vary with the nature of a business and types of application required. Although off-the-shelf packaged software is the most popular, other sources such as custom programmed and self-developed software remain prominent. The survey also shows that small businesses are generally satisfied with off-the-shelf packaged software, despite an apparent lack of vendor support services and training facilities.

Keywords: Microcomputer, Software, Small Business, Software Source, Software Satisfaction, Computer Applications.

## Introduction

The introduction of micro-computers in 1977 and the remarkable progress in both hardware and software technology in subsequent years has made micro-computer technology a desirable management tool for small businesses. The impressive growth of technology and decline in its price has made computerized information processing not only available but also affordable to small business. In fact, over the years, small business has become a substantial force in the micro-computer industry. The trend of computerization of small business is likely to continue in the foreseeable future. As technology matures and users become accustomed to it, opportunities have opened up for many applications of benefit to management of small business. Also, as technology becomes less expensive, computerization should become affordable to many small businesses particularly very small ones. Yet another force is fear of the unknown. Microcomputers are increasingly becoming a part of our lifestyle, both for pleasure and business. This changing environment eliminates resistance and more and more people will be knowledgeable in this technology and appreciate its potential benefits; they are therefore more receptive to its use in the business environment.

While the computer industry is reaching maturity the software industry still remains volatile. Much of the success of small business computerization depends on the availability of software that is both affordable and easily used. It is difficult to evaluate the software environment: sources of software vary substantially. Our knowledge of how small businesses use computers and what specific tasks are performed is still limited. Key questions remain unanswered: what kind of applications are small businesses currently pursuing? what are the sources of software, and how effectively are information technology resources utilized?

Only a few researchers have addressed the case in small business. For example, Nazem (1985) surveyed 598 firms of which 180 were computerized and another 131 were in the process of computerization. These two made up 52% of all firms in the survey, all of which had less than 100 employees. Among other things, the study revealed that small manufacturers have been the pioneers in computerization while construction businesses were the laggards. In general, many businesses felt that available support services, including installation, training and postinstallation assistance, were not particularly satisfactory; but they had learned to live with this situation.

Nickell and Seado (1988) undertook a major survey of small businesses in Minnesota: firms with up to 1500 employees were included in this survey and the authors analyzed responses from 121 small firms, 68 percent of which owned computers. The computerized firms used their systems principally for accounting, budgeting, inventory control and word processing. In general, respondents voiced satisfaction with their systems; but some user resistance was noted.

Several other researchers have focused on specific issues; for example, a survey undertaken by Farhoomand and Heryeyk (1985) found that accounting was the most common application and “information overload” was the primary incentive for computerization. A survey of 30 firms conducted by Cheney (1983) also established that computers in small businesses are used primarily for accounting applications.

Cooley et al. (1987) performed a comprehensive analysis of past research on small business applications. The authors, through an objective and highly critical analysis, attempted to determine the future direction of research for small business. Among others, the authors identified “the study of the role of computers in small business” as a critical area. While this study did set some objective guidelines for future research in small business, it did not address the issue of software availability and its related problems and promises. Small businesses, in general, rely upon others for software development.

Our survey intends to provide a profile of the wide variety of software and their sources currently being used by small businesses. In addition, qualitative and quantitative analysis is undertaken to evaluate the level of satisfaction of the users with respect to these sources. Such information can provide useful insight not only for academic research but more importantly for a variety of decision makers dealing with microcomputers such as users, buyers and vendors.

## Method

A mail survey was conducted of randomly selected small businesses in Nebraska. In this context they were defined as businesses with less than 100 personnel. The sample was selected from a mailing list comprising all businesses in the state; forms were mailed to 1800 businesses and 376 completed questionnaires were returned to become the source data in this study. The survey was undertaken during the spring and summer of 1988.

From past studies, it appears that transaction processing has been the major thrust of computerization of small businesses with the focus on the area of accounting applications. The questionnaire specifically asked for different kinds of applications, namely, General Ledger, Accounts Receivable, Accounts Payable, Inventory, Payroll, Purchase Order, Sales Order, Point of Sale, Fixed Assets Management, Job Costing, and Industry Specific Applications and, as a general category, "Other Applications". In addition, classificatory information such as size of business by number of personnel and type of primary trade classification was also collected.

Although it is conceivable that small businesses obtain their software from a variety of sources the major ones can be easily identified. They fall into four distinct categories:

(i) self-developed, which includes all application software that may have been developed and maintained in-house by the business;

(ii) “off-the-shelf” software obtained from discount or other computer stores; these come with little or no support from vendors but are generally inexpensive. Some support may be available from other organizations, such as community colleges, small accounting firms and small computer entrepreneurs offering training services;

(iii) custom programming; where the firm contracts with computer/software consultants to develop specific software. Generally speaking, contractors will also provide support and training although small providers may not be able to provide adequate and prompt support;

(iv) smorgasbord, including sources which are not included in the above, labelled as “others”.

## Analysis of the Survey

Of the 376 firms who responded with completed questionnaires only 136 indicated that they are using computers. Thus only about 36 percent of these small businesses are computer users. Our analysis is based on data from these 136 small businesses. The extent of computerization, as one would expect, varies according to need and expertise and the desire to exploit technology. A summary of activities is given in Table 1. General Ledger appears to head the list with over four-fifths of the sample firms currently reporting such a computerized system. Accounts Receivable is clearly the second most popular application, with three quarters of the firms indicating computerization. Accounts Payable captures the third place with about 60% utilization. Payroll Applications also enjoys a high level of computerization; just over half are currently computerized in this area. Fixed Assets accounting ranked fifth in application, with exactly one-half of the firms currently using computerized systems. All other types of application appear to trail behind with less than half of the firms currently using computers for these activities.

While the above paragraph summarizes the state of computerization in sample firms, our primary interest is aimed at the source of the application software. Statistics are also given in Table 1. Although the survey indicated 136 of the sample firms are currently computerized many did not divulge the types of application they currently use and many others did not disclose their sources of software for these specific applications. This resulted in a reduction of effective sample size for further analysis. Thus, the total number of firms reporting specific applications is considerably reduced in most cases. For example, General Ledger applications, the most widely computerized activity among small business, is confirmed by 110 firms but only 102 provided detailed information. Of these 102 firms, 15.7 percent developed their own software and another 11.8 percent obtained them from other unspecified sources. Off-the-shelf software is clearly the most popular with 43.1 percent of sample firms obtaining their software from this source while custom programs remains a distant second at 29.4 percent Off-the-shelf standard software leads the way for popular applications such as General Ledger, Accounts Receivable, Accounts Payable, and Payroll. In addition, the somewhat less popular application programs such as Fixed Asset Accounting, were also acquired “off-the-shelf”, making this source the number one provider. “Custom programs” was the second most popular source. The “self-developed” programs only ranged from 15 to 18 percent of applications in the top four areas. Order entry software was mostly custom produced, as was also Point of Sales and Industry Specific Applications.

Overall variation in the sources of software by type of application has been statistically tested utilizing Friedman's two-way Analysis of Variance using ranks. The results clearly establish the differences as significant at the one percent level. There is, however, little difference among the sources of software for the more popular applications, such as General Ledger, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable and Payroll. Another interesting feature should be noted with respect to order entry. There appears to be some difference between Sales and Purchase Order with respect to software sources. Major differences, of course, are concentrated among the more specialized software, as used in such activities as Job Costing, Point of Sales, Fixed Asset Accounting, and Industry Specific Applications.

## Source of Software and Level of Satisfaction

We also attempted to assess whether the level of satisfaction differs with the source of the software. To measure such differences, seven key questions were used, including the following:

1. Availability of Software Support Service.

2. Availability of Software Training Service.

3. Concern over Security.

4. Flexibility to Expand.

5. Ease of Use.

6. Accuracy of Output.

7. Reliability of Software.

Because of inadequate sample data, not all categories can be statistically tested. However, two major types of application: general ledger and accounts receivable have been analyzed to determine how source of software has affected performance. The results in Table 2 indicate the percentages of subject firms that agreed with the statements on the specific issues investigated; it also indicates the significance of the differences that exist among various sources of software. In general, any statistical differences in general ledger and accounts receivable are almost identical. Since general ledger is the most common application, critical analysis of the information on this application would be appropriate. The summary of test results suggests substantial differences in four areas: software support, training, flexibility, and security. No significant difference can be found with respect to the remaining three factors (ease of use, accuracy and reliability).

Among the 102 firms using computerized general ledger, 16 developed their own software, 44 acquired off-the-shelf programs, 30 used custom programs and 12 acquired theirs from other unknown sources. Almost one-half of the firms which developed their own software thought available support for software development was inadequate. But only a little over a third of the companies buying off-the-shelf software thought the support was inadequate. Contrary to common popular wisdom, about two-thirds of the firms utilizing custom-developed software were critical about software support. It appears that criticism of software support is directly related to expectations: the most critical firms are those using custom-developed software, expecting full support from the provider. At the other extreme are those who acquired off-the-shelf software at minimal cost; they do not expect much support and are less critical. A somewhat different situation can be noted with respect to available training. About one third of all firms appear to be unhappy with training opportunities. Half of these firms acquired off-the-shelf software. This can be rationally expected, with the low price of off-the-shelf software: a supermarket approach to software marketing does not lend itself to extensive training on the part of vendors. One can only speculate on the extent of available training from outside sources, such as educational institutions and private consultants, at the price small businesses are willing to pay.

Some differences can also be observed with respect to the flexibility of software and its source. In general, over two-thirds of the firms responded that their software were flexible enough for expanded applications. Differences are noted primarily between two types of firms – those who developed their own software versus those that acquired it off-the-shelf. Over three-fourths of the firms using off-the-shelf software regarded their systems as flexible while less than half of those developing their own software indicated this to be so. Also, only three-fifths of the firms using custom built software reported being flexible for further expansion. It appears that those with off-the-shelf software are becoming familiar with their systems and achieving competence; this, in turn, gives them confidence to expand applications. However, custom-built (turnkey) software users may not have attempted to understand the system and cannot expand without outside support. The most intriguing differences are noted in the perception of security. Those firms using off-the-shelf-programs are not generally concerned about security; less than one twentieth of these firms stated that they are seriously concerned; this compares with a fifth of the firms using custom-developed software. The firms which developed their own software are the group most worried about security; comprising three-fourths of all firms. This difference may again reflect perception. Perhaps small firms most seriously concerned with security develop their own systems.

The last three factors, ease of use, accuracy, and reliability, pertain to quality of output and system performance. Small businesses are generally satisfied with their software systems. Source of software does not appear to play any significant role and, irrespective of their source, most firms have indicated satisfaction with respect to all three attributes.

## Some Concluding Remarks

Small businesses obtain their application software from different sources: from developing their own to relying upon manufacturers' or distributors' software. While some of the variation can be attributed to the nature of the business, the major source of software was found to be off-the-shelf standard programs, particularly for basic accounting applications, the most dominant area of computer application. Generally speaking, users appear to be satisfied with these packaged software although many wish they could call on additional support and training. Ironically, the businesses that use custom designed software are more critical of the level of support and training than those using off-the-shelf programs. One positive feature that should be noted, however, is that off-the-shelf software users are very positive about the flexibility of their systems and feel comfortable in expanding applications. Maybe this group will ultimately become more proficient in the use of computers.

Firms using off-the-shelf programs do not appear to be overly concerned about security. In contrast, firms who develop their own are particularly concerned with it. Maybe businesses concerned with computer security build their own application software, and this could well hinder progress in computer applications. Opinions regarding performance and reliability of applications software, on the other hand, are found to be generally positive, irrespective of their source.

In the final analysis, it appears that some sense of maturity with respect to the use of computers is emerging in small businesses. As the process continues, more and more small firms will decide in favor of off-the-shelf, low-cost software and will use them with increasing effectiveness.
