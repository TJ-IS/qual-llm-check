# Utilizing GDSS Technology: Final Report on a Recent Empirical Study

Renée A. Beauclair

Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Detmar W. Straub

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

In recent years, Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) technology has attracted considerable research interest. Proponents argue that GDSS can improve group decisions by supporting face-to-face conferencing, remote site teleconferencing, and remote/local computer conferencing. Emerging capabilities in telecommunications technology have enhanced these prospects. A survey of 135 randomly-selected organizations determined that GDSS are gradually being incorporated into the organization's information system (IS) portfolio. Apparently organizations are increasing their commitment to GDSS and current implementations are heaviest in the remote/local computer conferencing arena, especially in electronic mail. A factor analysis of respondent data showed that GDSS usually involves planning, administrative, and data analysis tasks. Usage also varies significantly with certain organizational size variables.

Keywords: Group Decision Support Systems, Computer Supported Cooperative Work, Decision Support Systems.

## Introduction

The generation and implementation of solutions to a myriad of organizational problems are often conducted by groups; organizational leaders continue to recognize the need for effective strategies for augmenting and supporting the group process. Consequently, the use of computers to support group work in organizations is attracting a great deal of attention in the private, public, and academic sectors $[3,16]$ . The goal of those researchers and practitioners who work in this area is to provide effective computer support for group work, which may include problem solving, decision making, planning, or a host of other tasks.

Over the last decade, Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS), a specific type of computer technology, has attracted considerable research interest $[1,4,8,10]$ . These studies have dealt with managerial issues surrounding GDSS as well as its technical aspects. Prospects are high that GDSS will improve the quality of decisions made involving particular tasks $[6]$ and that user satisfaction with this form of DSS will grow $[7]$ .

We define GDSS as any technology used to enhance or support group decision making; computer support is often a primary part of the technology. Examples of GDSS include computer conferencing [9], interactive software packages, and decision room software. The potential for GDSS use in organizations is great [12]; beyond research and academic institutions, however, little is known of how GDSS are used in practice or the number of organizations actively using some version of the technology [13].

This paper is a final report of the results of an exploratory study of organizational GDSS use in which 135 randomly selected Data Processing Management Association (DPMA) members were surveyed [18]. Our data analysis suggests that GDSS are slowly being introduced and supported by IS departments. Based on our results and current interest in group and team work in organizations, we believe this pattern represents a trend for the near future.

We present here the final data analysis; the background, research methods, and analytical procedures from the preceding study are briefly outlined, but the focus is on the descriptive statistics and factor analysis of respondent data. Our exploratory findings show that GDSS are increasingly being found in larger financial institutions, computer service bureaus, and governmental organizations. GDSS that support computer conferencing are designed to structure and support planning decisions and share data analyses. Counter to suppositions of much of the prior research $[2,14]$ , the primary use of this type of GDSS appears to be for structured decision support. As might be expected, though, the evidence of this study indicates that GDSS used in decision room settings serve primarily as aids in unstructured, strategic planning tasks.

## Research Questions

While academic and research-oriented organizations have made great progress in the development and testing of GDSS, little is known of actual implementation patterns in organizations. The design, development, and implementation of these systems have an impact on, but also are influenced by, organizational adoption trends of GDSS. Currently, little is known about the kinds of GDSS being implemented or the organizations that have, or plan to have, these systems on-line. This leads to our first two research questions: What is the current level of usage of the different types of GDSS? And what are the future plans for use of GDSS technology? (See Figure 1.)

The third research question addresses the applicability of task and GDSS use. As discussed in our earlier work, there are several schools of thought on this question. The general goal of GDSS design is to improve the quality of the process and its outcomes, but there is disagreement as to how to achieve or measure these. Some researchers assert that GDSS are best used for unstructured or semi-structured problems, while others believe that GDSS are more appropriately designed to support unstructured or ill-defined tasks [17]. Also, little is known of how practitioners choose to use them, or in what circumstance they feel most comfortable. Thus, our third research question is stated: Are particular tasks associated with particular types of GDSS in practice?

The final research question deals with the organizational settings in which GDSS are introduced. GDSS implementation is costly in terms of time and money; therefore, most researchers believe that larger organizations will be most likely (and most able) to implement these systems first. It also is assumed that certain types of organizations will adopt earlier, as well. This research question is stated: What organizational variables are associated with particular types of GDSS?

## Research Methods

A questionnaire was developed in order to address these research questions. GDSS was defined for the subjects as “any technology used to enhance group decision making” and allowed for wide interpretation. However, examples accompanying the definition strongly implied that the investigation was restricted to computer technology.

Definitions of the three types of GDSS studied – face-to-face conferencing, remote site teleconferencing, and remote and local computer conferencing – were also enclosed for participants. These definitions were developed from prior work in GDSS and were intentionally discursive (see Figure 2).

The questionnaire was completed by 135 randomly-selected DPMA members, 45 of whom indicated that they were actively using 78 GDSS. Groups represented included profit and non-profit organizations in the private and public sectors, military and government installations, and educational institutions. It is important to note the possibility of non-response bias in the sampling. Nonrespondents were not compared with respondents; therefore, there may be unmeasured biases in the reported findings.

Descriptive statistics and exploratory factor analysis were used to address the third research question, which addressed the association between task type and type of GDSS used. The final research question dealt with both organizational variables and their association with types of GDSS; specifically we looked at size of the organization itself as well as its data processing department. ## Data Analysis

## Current GDSS Usage

The questionnaire instrument was designed to gather information on past and future GDSS usage by the organizations surveyed; it also collected specific information about each organization, such as industry type and size. Our analysis reveals that the majority of active decision rooms and interfaced systems have been implemented since 1984. All active teleconferencing systems, however, preceded 1984; this was an unexpected finding, especially since respondents related plans to implement three such systems before 1988.

The descriptive statistics also yielded interesting results. For instance, although this is a relatively young technology, the 3 out of 10 respondents who report utilizing one or more of the various types of GDSS is surprisingly high. Even though this result may reflect sampling bias, it is credible in the case of interfaced conferencing, since electronic mail can serve as a vehicle for group decision making (19% report using this type in batch or interactive mode), and therefore can be counted in the survey (see Figure 4). But the number of decision room GDSS (10% of the sample) and teleconferencing GDSS (4%) is remarkable.

## Current Interfaced Conference Usage

Organizations that had implemented interfaced conferencing GDSS used the technology as an aid in four different tasks, on average. Over half used the system as a means of aggregating data from group members. A second popular use was sharing the results of statistical reports, as highlighted in Figure 5. This suggests that data analysis results are being shared through interfaced GDSS systems like FORUM, PROFS, and other electronic mail systems.

Since a given GDSS may be used in multiple ways, the simple percentages presented here may not reveal underlying patterns of use. In order to understand better these patterns, we looked at the degree to which these tasks occurred together or covaried. Exploration of these dimensions was handled via a VARIMAX principal components factor analysis of the data. Loadings for factor one, greater than 0.50, stressed administrative functions, with strategic planning placed in the context of structured administrative activities that support planning, such as policy writing, hiring decisions, statistical support, and voting, as shown in Figure 6. The second factor appeared to center around planning tasks, such as forecasting and modeling. The third factor centered around simple data analysis, including aggregation of data and reinforcement of descriptive statistics. These three factors accounted for 58% of the variance in the data.

## Current Decision Room Usage

Among organizations that used decision rooms, the systems were applied to an average of four tasks. The primary decision tasks involved strategic planning, brainstorming, and forecasting, as highlighted in Figure 7. These data suggest that this type of GDSS is being implemented to aid high-level decision-making, as several researchers have predicted.

The decision room data set was factored into principal components to determine the tasks that respondents felt were appropriate for this type of GDSS, as shown in Figure 8. The covariance matrix indicated that tasks loaded on three factors: planning, administrative, and data analysis. The first factor included such individual tasks as aggregation of data, forecasting, and strategic planning. A strong loading by unstructured decisions indicates that many of these administrative tasks are associated with planning. A second factor stressed tasks that dealt with administration, specifically hiring decisions, policy writing, and modeling. A third principal component seemed to center around straightforward data analysis, namely the task of statistical support. Again, a strong loading by structured decisions supports this interpretation. All three factors account for 64% of the variance.

## Current Teleconferencing Use

Of the organizations surveyed, only five (or less than 4%) reported use of teleconferencing GDSS, the final category addressed by the questionnaire. Because of the low sample size, factor analysis would not be meaningful. Although descriptive statistics are used, interpretation of this data set is especially tenuous.

These systems were used for tasks similar to those for decision room GDSS, as would be expected. Highlighted in Figure 9, teleconferencing tasks differed from decision rooms only slightly, though placing a greater emphasis on team building. In terms of the composition of the data set, two respondents were in utilities, two in financial institutions, and one in government. A number of respondents commented that, as much as they would like to use teleconferencing, these systems were expensive or unavailable and implementation was not yet feasible.

## Future / Planned GDSS Use

In a series of three questionnaire items (numbers 8, 12, and 16), respondents indicated their intention to implement GDSS technology at some future date. Of the 135 respondents, 11% said they would be inaugurating a system between 1986 and 1990. Leading this group were government organizations that planned to introduce all three kinds of systems. Financial institutions and computer service bureaus intend to introduce face-to-face GDSS while utilities and financial institutions plan remote GDSS. These findings are depicted in Figure 10.

Of current non-user respondents, 8% reported they plan to adopt face-to-face conferencing by 1990, and 7% plan to introduce interfaced conferencing by 1988. Because of cost and complexity considerations, this type of GDSS seems least likely to experience much growth in the near future. Plans for GDSS types (other than teleconferencing) among those anticipating future implementation are shown in Figure 11. The data suggest, however tentatively, that interest in decision rooms will continue to run high while remaining steady for interfaced systems.

## Impact of Organizational Size on GDSS Implementation

The exploratory hypotheses predict that larger organizations and larger EDP shops implement more GDSS that smaller shops, and that this trend will continue into the future. In order to test these hypotheses, Kruskal-Wallis Analysis of Variance tests were used. The alpha level was set at 0.10 and the N of combined GDSS was 71.

Hypotheses 1a through 1c address current implementation of GDSS at the overall and local organizational and EDP shop levels. Results for test H1a were significant (p = 0.041). This indicates that more sophisticated GDSS are more likely to be found in larger overall organizations. The results for H1b were also significant (p = 0.075). Thus more sophisticated GDSS are more likely to be found in larger organizations at both levels. Analysis of H1c did not find significance (p = 0.243). These results may stem from the fact that differentiation between EDP shop size was either inaccurate or not an important factor in GDSS adoption and implementation.

Hypotheses 2a through 2c address future implementation of GDSS at the same levels as HI (overall/local organizational and EDP shop levels). Findings for H2a were significant (p =

0.08), indicating that, in general, larger organizations plan more implementations of sophisticated GDSS than smaller organizations. For H2b, results also were significant (p = 0.049); this suggests that larger local organizations are more likely to implement sophisticated GDSS in the future. Last, H2c was not significant (p = 0.224). Little or no distinction occurs between larger and smaller EDP shops in plans for GDSS implementation.

## Discussion

According to the exploratory findings in this study, GDSS are increasingly being found in larger organizations, particularly in financial institutions, computer service bureaus, and government. Interfaced GDSS, which now predominate, are being implemented to administrate, plan, and share data analyses, as summarized in Figure 12. The primary purpose of this type of GDSS, in short, is for structured decisions. Factor analysis identified administrative functions and pure planning tasks as important uses, but also data analysis tasks as a separate factor. Descriptive statistics reinforced the importance of data analysis tasks in this domain. These results are surprising in that they run counter to the suppositions of much prior research. As might be expected, decision room GDSS do appear to be used primarily as aids for unstructured strategic planning decisions.

What is also surprising is that brainstorming did not appear in the factor analysis and that voting appeared in only one factor, and at a marginal loading. Researchers in GDSS concur that brainstorming and voting are both critical design features, particularly for decision rooms [e.g., 6]. Thus, one of the challenges to GDSS system developers has been to create software that will allow groups to view and rank decision alternatives. Either this challenge is not being met in GDSS software now in use or decision makers do not fully understand the capabilities of these features. It may also be that decision makers do not need or wish to use these applications as much as predicted. In the former case, developers need to add these features; in the second, more education and training in use of idea exchange and anonymous voting may be in order. In the latter, perhaps these applications are not as crucial as had been believed.

Results from our study are limited in several respects. First of all, nonrespondents may differ systematically from those who did not respond to the survey. Since non-response bias was not measured, it cannot be ruled out.

Second, limitations in the robustness of one of the chosen statistical techniques, in this case principal components factor analysis, are another consideration. Third, much of the data are not useful because the bulk of organizations are not currently using GDSS technology.

Future research should address these limitations, as well as additional questions. For example, exactly how do interfaced conference GDSS promote data analysis? Another issue not dealt with in this study, and one that likewise limits the results, is software/hardware availability. A technology gap may currently exist between theory and practice; in other words, organizations may recognize the usefulness of interfaced GDSS for decision making but do not have access to the appropriate technology.

There are numerous practical implications for software design and implementation that emerge from these findings. First, it seems clear that GDSS will see more adoptions in the future. In parallel with much of the software development currently being carried on in universities and research institutions, software houses might be encouraged to move into commercial production and marketing of GDSS applications. Successful GDSS such as Malone, Grant, Turbak, Brobst, and Cohen's LENS system $[15]$ can serve as prototypes for interfaced support for structured and semi-structured tasks.

Second, the lag between researchers' models and practitioners' actual systems could be reduced by producing packages that support an even wider variety of tasks, as Huber [10] suggests. For example, voting/rating, brainstorming, and other rudimentary tasks are well represented in current systems, and yet, perhaps because of their limitations, seem to be underused. Software developers should look to applications that could serve the needs of decision makers faced with complex, multiple attribute, and unstructured problems.

Finally, software developers should base their work on systems already proven, as well as on theoretical bases, as guides for future systems development. The support capabilities of GDSS are well established; needless repetition of previous mistakes can and should be avoided. Alternatively, elaboration of already successful applications should be encouraged.

The major implication of this study for the administration of GDSS technology is straightforward: precedents for theory and practice have been set, and it is clear that GDSS – sophisticated and rudimentary – are beginning to find their place in modern organizations. However, the cost of these systems and potential for their misuse mean that care must be taken to choose a GDSS that will best fit the adopting organization.

It would appear that GDSS may one day become integral to organizational decision making. Given that GDSS is still in a seminal stage of growth and development, the survey results paint a positive picture overall. They indicate that GDSS can be used to solve both structured and unstructured tasks and are being initiated to produce better, higher quality decisions in organizations.
