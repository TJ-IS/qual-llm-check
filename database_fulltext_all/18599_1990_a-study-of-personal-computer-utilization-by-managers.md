# A Study of Personal Computer Utilization by Managers

Charles H. MAWHINNEY

CIS Department, Bentley College, Waltham, MA 02154-4705, USA

Albert L. LEDERER

School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309, USA

Personal computer (PC) utilization has become an important concern due to the PC's widespread and rapid implementation in the work place. A field study evaluated the responses of 105 managers who were PC users. The research model viewed PC utilization as a function of organizational, personal, computer system, and process attributes. Analysis was performed using Pearson correlations and one-way analysis of variance. The two items with the strongest and most consistent correlation with reported computer use time were user satisfaction with (1) the computer system's contribution to job performance and (2) their level of competence with the system. These findings suggest that PC utilization can be interpreted as a function of its perceived costs and benefits.

Keywords: Computer Usage, Personal Computer Utilization, End-User Computing, User Satisfaction, User Characteristics, Managerial Performance.

## 1. Introduction

It has been estimated that more than 21 million PCs have been sold, 41 percent of which were purchased for business purposes [30]. Keen and Woodman [20] suggest that a typical large company may buy a thousand PCs over a three year period, resulting in direct equipment and software expenditure of \$7 million or more. They suggest that “hidden costs” for training, connectivity, and support may add an estimated \$19 million to that direct cost. The effectiveness of this investment is coming under scrutiny as it becomes obvious that the majority of these machines will be used on an occasional rather than continuous basis.

The utilization of computerized information systems has long been of great importance to both researchers and practitioners $[13,22,31,36,37,38,41]$ . However, MIS research has lagged behind in its efforts to study PC use and effectiveness $[35]$ . This article reports on a recent field study investigating factors that were hypothesized to affect PC utilization by managers. The ultimate purpose of this kind of research is to help improve managerial performance through the effective use of PCs.

## 2. Factors Affecting Personal Computer Utilization

## 2.1. Previous Models

Many models and frameworks have been proposed to facilitate research in MIS. Chervany, Dickson, and Kozar [4] proposed four categories of variables in their framework, with “decision effectiveness” as their dependent variable and “decision environment”, “decision maker”, and “characteristics of the information system” as independent variables. Ives, Hamilton, and Davis [16] proposed a framework that subsumed several previously proposed models [9,25,28]. They used three major variable groups in their framework, including: “process variables” as dependent variables, and “environmental characteristics” and “information system characteristics” as independent variables. Lucas [21,22] proposed a model predicting MIS utilization and job performance; it used seven groups of variables, including “use” and “performance” as dependent variables, and “situational”, “personal”, “decision style”, “quality of system”, and “attitudes and perceptions” as independent variables.

## 2.2. The Research Model

Several of the variables from these models were adapted to the context of PC utilization to develop a model to describe variable groups that were expected to affect such utilization:

$$
\mathrm{U} = \mathrm{f} (\mathrm{O}, \mathrm{P}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{R}),
$$

where U = Utilization of PCs, O = Organizational attributes, P = Personal attributes of user, C = Computer system attributes, R = Process attributes. The variable groups and the individual variables within each group are presented in Figure 1.

## 2.3. The Dependent Variable

Numerous variables have been suggested and employed as dependent variables in the literature. Lucas utilized managerial “performance” as one of his dependent variables. Snitkin and King used “perceived effectiveness” of the system as a dependent variable. Chervany, Dickson, and Kozar used “decision effectiveness” as the dependent variable in laboratory experiments, but they went on to note that it is particularly difficult to measure the quality of decision-making when conducting field studies. Consequently, it is common to use a surrogate instead of managerial performance.

Two different surrogates have received rather widespread attention. "User satisfaction" is one of these. Bailey and Pearson [2] developed a satisfaction measurement instrument which was subsequently modified by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [17]. Although this instrument has been used [24,32] or advocated [5] by other researchers, alternative user satisfaction measures are quite commonly used, as exemplified by the recent work of Rushinek and Rushinek [34] as well as Gremillion and Hopkins.

“System use” is an alternative dependent variable which has also received attention. Ives, Hamilton, and Davis mentioned system use as a possible variable. Lucas also employed system use as a dependent variable in his model. His “use” variable dealt with how the system was being utilized. Raymond measured the level of system utilization through a seven point scale on which “users rated their perceived frequency and regularity of MIS usage”. Swanson measured MIS utilization by counting the number of database queries made by managers. He employed this variable as a “co-producer” with user satisfaction. It has also been a common practice to employ system “use time” as a variable. In one empirical study of PC users conducted by Gremillion and Hopkins and another performed by Snitkin and King, self-reported use time was used as the dependent variable.

PC system utilization served as the dependent variable in this research. Computer utilization was measured through self-reported use time in hours per week. The use time measure was an overall estimate of the amount of time normally spent using a computer during a seven day time period.

## 2.4. The Independent Variables

The research model includes many specific independent variables that have either been used in prior empirical research or suggested by previous authors. Concrete measures of these variables were used wherever possible in the blind survey questionnaire. However, in numerous instances the methodology precluded the use of concrete measures. For the most part, a surrogate measure (user satisfaction) was utilized. The word satisfaction was intended to focus the survey respondent on the subjective aspect of these questions in contrast to the more objective nature of other questions.

## 2.5. The Hypotheses

The hypotheses are grouped according to the model; very little has been suggested concerning specific relationships, particularly with respect to PC utilization. These hypotheses are listed in abbreviated form in Table 1.

## 2.5.1. Organizational Factors

01: Lower level managers use PCs more than higher level managers. The evolutionary path of information systems usage within organizations has been from the lower levels (data processing systems dealing with transaction processing and operational control problems) toward the top (decision support systems dealing with strategic planning problems) [1,8]. The tasks performed by managers at the lower levels tend to be more structured and thus more easily assisted by computers, whereas the tasks at higher levels tend to be less structured and thus less easily assisted by computers [9]. Consequently, it was expected that lower level managers would use PCs more than higher level managers. This variable was measured by asking the respondent to indicate the level of his/her position in the organization hierarchy (1 for CEO, 2 if reporting directly to CEO, etc.)

02: Managers with smaller spans of control use PCs more than managers with larger spans of control. As span of control increases, more time is required to deal with the corresponding geometric increase in the number of interpersonal relationships [11]. Consequently, less time is available to use a computer. This variable was measured through: (1) the number of direct subordinates and (2) the number of indirect subordinates for which the person was responsible.

03: Persons in technical and quantitative jobs use PCs more than those in non-technical or non-quantitative jobs. Computers have traditionally had their greatest application for problems involving mathematics and/or data manipulation. Positions which emphasize these skills (e.g., research, engineering, accounting, and finance) were expected to use PCs more than non-technical/non-quantitative positions (e.g., marketing and personnel). Type of position was assessed by three external “experts” (a high school guidance counselor, a university career counselor, and a personnel management professor) on the basis of job title, supervisor’s title, and department.

04: Managers use closely controlled systems more than systems not subject to external controls. It was anticipated that external control would encourage managers to justify the need for the system and thus encourage its use. External control was assessed through: (1) budget paid for the system (user's budget versus someone else's budget), and (2) accountability for system use (no accountability versus accountable to someone else).

05: Persons who are more satisfied with their PC's contribution to job performance utilize them to a greater extent. Lucas as well as Bailey and Pearson have suggested that an association exists between "job performance" and system use. Lucas expected that the direction of the relationship depended upon the decision making situation, but found the relationship to be rather weak.

## 2.5.2. Personal Factors

P1: Younger persons use PCs more than older ones. The age of the user has been thought to play a role in the managerial utilization of computers. It is also a common belief that older persons exhibit higher levels of computer anxiety [15].

P2: Males use PCs more than females. Zmud suggested that gender influenced MIS usage. Traditionally, the data processing professions have been dominated by males [12,40]. It is also a common belief that females have higher levels of computer anxiety than men.

P3: Persons with lower levels of education use PCs more than persons with more formal education. Lucas attempted to use education to predict system use but did not find a significant correlation. It was anticipated that users with higher levels of education would hold positions at higher levels in the organization where it was expected that PCs would not be used as much.

P4: Persons with greater typing ability use PCs more than persons with less. Typing ability has not yet received much attention as a determinant of direct computer use. Although Card, Moran, and Newell [3] state that typing speeds may vary by a factor of up to fifteen, the consideration of this variable seems to be restricted to laboratory studies [18,23] and appears to have been ignored in field research. It was anticipated that a lack of typing ability would inhibit the use of PCs. Typing ability was assessed through: (1) estimated typing speed, and (2) completion of a typing/keyboarding course.

P5: Persons who are more satisfied with their level of computer competence utilize PCs to a greater extent. The development of computer skills requires substantial learning. Hence, it was expected that a positive correlation would exist between use time and competence with the system. A satisfaction measure was used for system competence.

## 2.5.3. Computer System Factors

C1: Persons who are more satisfied with the ease of learning a PC system utilize PCs to a greater extent.

C2: Persons who are more satisfied with the ease of using a PC system utilize PCs to a greater extent. A system that is difficult to learn and use would tend to frustrate the user and thus inhibit its use. Paisley [29] considered ease of use of an MIS to be as important to MIS usage as the quality of information received. Both variables were assessed for hardware and software.

C3: Managers use PCs that are more accessible. A manager who is inconvenienced by either having to move physically to a distant workstation or wait for other users to finish with a shared system will tend to use the system less. Bailey and Pearson included “ease of access” as a dimension of user satisfaction. System accessibility was assessed through: (1) distance from PC to user’s desk, (2) number of other users sharing the workstation, (3) number of PCs used, (4) whether the user had a PC at home, and (5) satisfaction with accessibility.

C4: Persons who are more satisfied with the response time of the system utilize PCs to a greater extent.

C5: Persons who are more satisfied with the accuracy of the system utilize PCs to a greater extent. System response time and accuracy are commonly used performance measures of computer systems. Dissatisfaction might inhibit system use. Satisfaction with response time was assessed separately for software and the overall system.

## 2.5.4. Process Attributes

R1: Persons who participated more in the acquisition process use PC systems to a greater extent. User involvement in the implementation process is one of the more widely accepted concepts in systems design [7,39]. Swanson found a significant relationship between a priori involvement and system utilization. User participation in the acquisition process was assessed through the identification of the person who: (1) initiated the request to acquire the system, (2) decided which system to select, and (3) paid for the system purchase. It was also assessed through (4) satisfaction with participation in the acquisition process.

R2: Persons who are more satisfied with their systems use them to a greater extent. Bailey and Pearson, Swanson, and Zmud have all reported positive relationships between system satisfaction and system utilization. Satisfaction was assessed separately for hardware, software, and the overall system.

R3: Persons with more computer training use PCs to a greater extent. Training of system users is a traditional part of system implementation. It is expected that the vast majority of users will require formal training in order to gain proficiency in use. Computer training was assessed through: (1) number of computer courses completed, (2) the number of different languages in which a computer program had been written, (3) hours of computer training in the previous six months, and (4) satisfaction with the availability of training.

R4: Managers who are more satisfied with the support provided by data processing departments and vendors utilize PCs to a greater extent. Technical support provided to the user by the data processing department and/or the vendor has been suggested by several authors to be important to MIS success. Technical support was assessed through user satisfaction with: (1) hardware support by the data processing department, (2) software support by the data processing department, (3) hardware support by the vendor, and (4) software support by the vendor. An assessment was also made of responsibility for maintenance.

## 3. The Methodology

This research was a field study of managers. Several previous MIS researchers have recommended that more field studies be conducted using managers as subjects [33,39,41].

## 3.1. The Instrument

The instrument used was four pages long and included 134 items arranged in three major sections: (1) Background Information, (2) Personal Style Inventory, and (3) System Characteristics. The instrument was tested in two pilot studies; one involved graduate business students, and one involved managers in a medium-sized organization.

The Background Information included questions pertaining to personal characteristics, organizational characteristics, and the implementation process. Three questions involving computer use were also included to provide information to determine the extent of the respondent's utilization of computers.

The “Personal Style Inventory” was developed by Hogan and Champagne [14] to measure the Jungian personality typology [19]. This aspect of the research has been reported elsewhere [26].

The System Characteristics solicited a detailed description of the hardware and software in use, as well as the acquisition process and various aspects of the user's satisfaction with the implementation. The Likert-type response to the satisfaction measures incorporated a seven point scale. Figure 2 contains some sample items.

Computer use was assessed through several items contained in the Background Information and System characteristics. Some sample items appear in Figure 3.

## 3.2. Characteristics of the Subjects

Two groups served as subjects for the field study. Both groups were “convenience samples”. Group A was the set of graduates of a special “executive” MBA program at a large university in the northeastern USA. Group B was the membership of a chapter of a national professional organization that deals with production and inventory management. Group A can be viewed as a “horizontal” sampling of managers at higher levels across all functional areas, whereas Group B can be viewed as a “vertical” sampling across more managerial levels within a specific functional area. Group A had 373 members and Group B had 475 members. The two groups had no members in common.

Questionnaires were mailed to all members of the two groups. Responses were received from 123 members of Group A and 126 of Group B, resulting in an overall response rate of 30%. The 249 responses were screened to remove those that were not both managers and PC users. Non-managers were removed from the sample on the basis of "Current Job Title" and "Number of employees directly supervised". Thus, the "managers" used in this analysis were consistent with McLeod's [27] definition. The treatment of non-users posed a dilemma. To include them in the analysis would have skewed the use time distribution so as to preclude the use of parametric statistics. However, to simply compare the users to non-users in a dichotomous fashion would effectively equate "occasional users" to "power users". Consequently, persons who were non-users, or whose primary use was of mainframe and minicomputers were also excluded. Both groups were predominantly male. Only seven of the 105 respondents were female. The age distributions for both groups were very similar, ranging from 24 to 65 with an overall average of 40.6 years. About one third of the respondents were employed by organizations that exceeded one billion dollars in annual revenues. Overall, the organizations for which the respondents worked employed an average of fifteen thousand persons. Both groups represented a broad range of levels in the organizational hierarchy, with a substantial representation from the higher tiers of management.

## 4. Results

Many of the hypotheses were tested through multiple items on the questionnaire. These individual items are listed in Table 2. Two basic statistical testing methodologies were used. Equality of means was tested using analysis of variance in instances where the independent variable response was categorical in nature. The test statistic for these items is listed in Table 2 as “f”. Correlation was tested using Pearson's product moment coefficient, “r”, in instances where the independent variable response was on a continuous scale. The levels of significance reported are for one-tailed tests.

Although the two groups were similar in that they were both predominantly middle managers, anticipated differences might have been expected to affect personal computer use characteristics. One such difference was in educational background, since all members of Group A had completed a graduate business degree. A second difference was the more “functional” orientation of Group B (production and inventory control) compared to the more “general” orientation of Group A. Consequently, the analysis was performed for each group separately. Table 2 provides a detailed listing of the statistical results for each item within each hypothesis for each group.

## 5. Discussion

The following discussion of the results will focus on three aspects: (1) the overall findings, (2) sample group related effects, and (3) the effects that were strongest and most consistent. The term “very highly significant” will be used to describe instances where the p-values were 0.001 or better, “highly significant” for instances where the p-values were 0.01 or better, and “significant” for instances where the p-values were 0.05 or better.

## 5.1. Overall Findings

Altogether 41 different items were tested for each of the two sample groups. Thirteen of these 41 items showed significance for at least one of the sample groups. This resulted in seven of the nineteen research hypotheses being supported by one or more significant statistical tests. However, none of the four variable groups (organizational, personal, computer system, and process) in the research model appeared to dominate. The significant items were fairly evenly distributed among the four variable groups. Hence the findings generally support the model but not in a particularly strong or consistent fashion.

## 5.2. Sample Group Related Effects

Some results were significant for one sample group and not the other, and vice versa. These are probably due to differences in characteristics of the two samples.

Three hypotheses (C3, R2, and R3) were supported by significant tests for Group A alone. First, system accessibility (C3) was found to be highly significant when assessed through distance from the user's desk, and significant when assessed through the number of other users who shared the system and when assessed through the number of PCs used. Higher use times were consistently associated with systems which were more accessible. Second, satisfaction with the system (R2) was found to be significant for all three items: satisfaction with overall system, with hardware, and with software. Higher use times were associated with higher satisfaction. Third, computer training (R3) was found to be significant for two items for Group A. These items included the number of computer courses completed and satisfaction with availability of training. Higher use times were associated with more training.

One hypothesis was supported by a significant result for Group B alone: satisfaction with software accuracy (C5). Higher use times were associated with higher satisfaction.

Some general differences between Group A and Group B with respect to their educational backgrounds and managerial orientations (“general” versus “functional”) may account for these results. In addition, another very important subtle difference might have existed. Despite the fact that all the subjects were managers, it is possible that there was a difference in the proportion of the use which was “discretionary” [10] or “optional” versus mandated. Although the degree of voluntary use was not included as a variable, Group A probably had a substantially higher degree of discretionary computer use, and this may have accounted for many of the observed differences in significant items. Specifically, when PC use is optional, increased use might be associated with: (1) making systems more accessible by placing them closer to the users’ desks and not sharing them with others, (2) increasing user satisfaction with the system, and (3) the amount of computer training.

## 5.3. Strongest and Most Consistent Effects

Three hypotheses (02, 05, and P5) were supported, with varying degrees of strength, in a fairly consistent fashion by significant results for both sample groups. First, direct and indirect span of control (02) were found to be significant. The direct span of control was significant for Group B while the indirect span of control was significant for Group A. In both cases those with larger spans of control reported less computer use. This suggests that managers who have fewer subordinates to supervise have more time to use a PC.

The final two items showed the strongest and most consistent correlations with reported use time. These were user satisfaction with contribution to job performance (05) and user satisfaction with level of competence with the system (P5). The former implies the importance of the occupational benefits derived from system utilization. The latter implies the importance of the costs of developing skill with the system. Together, these two attitudinal measures may suggest a focus on a portion (05 and P5) of the model.

Hence, without discounting the importance of other items, it may be argued that PC utilization can be interpreted as a function of the perceived benefits (expected contribution to job performance) and perceived cost (expected cost of developing competence with the system). Managers perhaps develop a mental model of a task. If the expected job performance benefits of using the PC outweigh the expected efforts at developing sufficient competence, the user chooses the PC assistance. Otherwise, the user chooses another means.

## 6. Summary and Conclusion

This research takes an important step forward in the examination of PC utilization. Most of the variables were suggested or found to be important by previous researchers, but before the advent of personal computers. Thus prior theory has been extended to a new context: personal computing by managers.

Seven of the nineteen research hypotheses were supported by one or more statistical tests. PC utilization was found to be associated with: (1) system accessibility, (2) satisfaction with the system, and (3) computer training, but in a way that may be related to voluntary use. Furthermore, the items which showed the strongest and most consistent associations with PC use were the user's: (1) span of control, (2) satisfaction with system competence, and (3) satisfaction with contribution to job performance.

Future research should validate the cost/benefit model proposed at the end of the previous section. It should identify a comprehensive list of costs and benefits and explore where costs can be decreased and benefits increased.

Although all the variables in the model were expected to be significant, several were not substantiated by this study. This is also an important result. Failure to confirm their significance may indicate their lack of importance in personal computing by managers. Future researchers should consider these variables and determine whether situations exist where they might be significant.

Practitioners who are involved in developing, implementing and supporting PC systems used by managers should take careful note of this research.

They should observe that in many cases computer training and system accessibility play a large role in system use. It also suggests that if the objective of those practitioners is to encourage the managerial use of PCs, then they should demonstrate how the PCs provide job-related benefits of greater value than the costs of learning a new technology.
