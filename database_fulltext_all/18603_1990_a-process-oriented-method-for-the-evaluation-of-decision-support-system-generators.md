# A process-oriented method for the evaluation of decision support system generators \*

Dennis A. Adams

Decision and Information Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, TX 77204-6283, USA

James F. Courtney, Jr.

Business Analysis and Research, College of Business Administration, Texas A & M University, College Station, TX 77843, USA

George M. Kasper

Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences, College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409-4320, USA

A process-oriented DSS selection and evaluation criterion and method are proposed and empirically supported. The relationship between DSS tools, the services they provide the user/decision maker, and stages of the decision-making process is developed. A model of DSS-aided decision making is advanced and the characteristics of DSS support are extended. The decision-maker's perception of the amount of support provided by the DSS to different stages of the decision-making process is the criterion upon which the selection and evaluation approach is based.

Questionnaire data was collected from the users of two leading DSS generators and analyzed using the developed approach. These results show that comparing the needs of the different stages of the decision-making process to the support provided by particular DSS generators provides valuable evaluation and selection information. Confirming the usefulness of the process-oriented method, the results show that the model-oriented DSS generators surveyed provide support for the alternative development and selection stages of decision making, but very little for the problem identification and diagnosis stages of the process.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Evaluation Methodology, Evaluation Criterion, Human Problem Solving

The purpose of this paper is to propose a process-oriented method for the selection and evaluation of DSS generators. The method relates a set of user-perceived DSS support characteristics to a similarly derived set of decision-making stages. The relationship between the stages of the decision-making process and the support provided for these stages by a DSS forms the primary evaluation criterion. Empirical support for the proposed method is presented, as it is applied to two popular DSS generators: Lloyd Bush and Associates' MODEL and Execucom's IFPS.

## 2. Background

Almost from its inception, the special evaluation needs of DSS have been recognized. Kcen [20] suggests that evaluation is a process-oriented activity that must deal with qualitative issues. While much of the research on DSS evaluation has concentrated on measuring qualitative benefits, the perspective has remained productivity or output oriented rather than process oriented. Typically, DSS evaluation research has consisted of relating qualitative support benefits of a DSS generator to some measure of productivity or output (e.g., decision performance, confidence, and satisfaction). However, there are no empirical studies that relate these same qualitative support benefits to the process or stages of decision making. Because the objective of DSS is to improve decisions by supporting the process of decision making, the absence of a process-oriented approach to DSS evaluation is surprising, and inconsistent with the conceptual foundation of the area.

## 2.1. DSS Evaluation Literature

Keen has argued that DSS differ enough from traditional computer-based systems to warrant a special evaluation approach. He proposes a method he calls Value Analysis that consists of four logical and sequential steps: (1) establish value by assessing benefits, (2) establish a cost threshold, (3) build an initial prototype, and (4) assess the prototype [23]. As he points out, key to the development of these prototypes, or specific DSS, are DSS generators such as IFPS. That is, the value of a specific DSS is directly dependent upon the breadth of decision-making support that its underlying DSS generator can provide.

Meador and Mezger [27] propose a method for the selection and evaluation of end-user DSS languages. Their approach begins with an assessment of end user and organizational needs (problem diagnosis) as a means of establishing the functional requirements and specifications of the DSS generator. Next, a language features analysis is conducted to match the capabilities of the candidate DSS language with the end user and problem diagnosis requirements. Meador and Mezger then propose that the selected software be used on a trial (demonstration prototype) basis, that a survey of current users be conducted, that the software be benchmarked, and that programmer productivity and end user orientation (“user friendliness”) be analyzed. This process results in identifying the product’s functions and features, ease of use, vendor support, operating requirements, and general market posture.

Sprague and Carlson [38] define four measures that can be used to evaluate the impact of DSS. Each of these measures is used to evaluate a different aspect of DSS effectiveness. Productivity measures evaluate the impact of DSS on decision outcomes; perception measures assess the impact of DSS on decision makers; product measures evaluate the technical merit of the DSS, and process measures are used to evaluate the impact of DSS on the decision-making process. Using these categories, Keen's Value Analysis is productivity oriented, Meador and Mezger's method is product oriented, and by far the majority of evaluation tools reported in the literature use perception measures [for reviews, see 17, 41]. Because each of these is an important measure of DSS effectiveness, Sprague and Carlson as well as Meador and Mezger recommend that a combination of these approaches be used. However, lacking development of a decision-making process approach to DSS evaluation, complete implementation of this recommendation is currently impossible.

Consistent with Sprague and Carlson's classification, Rohrmann [32] argues that two principal perspectives are possible in the evaluation of decision-aided support: output and process orientation. Viewed as an output, a number of DSS researchers have suggested that a consumer behavior perspective be taken with respect to evaluation.

One benefit of this is that a major focus of consumer behavior research has been the development of methods for identifying attributes that are important in influencing product preferences and choice. Several approaches have evolved for this purpose within the disciplines of consumer behavior and social psychology $[18]$ . One of these approaches, conjoint measurement techniques $[12, 13]$ , has been applied to DSS evaluation and information requirements analysis. As an approach to DSS evaluation, the application of conjoint measurement techniques was originally proposed by Stabell $[40]$ and later discussed by Keen and Scott Morton $[24]$ and Sprague and Carlson, and again suggested by Money et al. $[30]$ . Conjoint measurement techniques have also been proposed for eliciting information requirements $[14]$ . However, because no attempt is made to relate the attributes identified by conjoint measurement techniques to decision making, Keen and Scott Morton conclude that this approach is essentially an output-oriented approach to evaluation. Other methods have also been attacked as output oriented. For example, Srinivasan $[39]$ suggests a method similar to that used here but fails to recognize the importance of information system service characteristics $[16]$ and their association to stages of the decision-making process.

In terms of providing a process-oriented perspective to DSS evaluation, a number of frameworks implicitly suggest that information systems researchers and designers should recognize the stages of the decision-making process; however, these have exclusively addressed DSS design considerations. Similar to the taxonomy proposed by Gorry and Scott Morton [11], Lerch and Mantei [26] develop a prescriptive framework that classifies computer-based tools based on the characteristics of decision activity they support. Decision activity consists of decision stages and decision structure; the last is expanded into four levels (unstructured and three levels of structured: under certainty, under risk, and under uncertainty). Combining these with three decision stages (intelligence, design, and choice), they develop a twelve category framework for classifying decision support tools. Proficiency in any one of the decision-making stages depends upon the combination of the requirements for that stage and the decision aid [33].

## 2.2. DSS Support

The decision support provided by a system can be measured by the value, benefits, functions, and features of the DSS. Welsch defines properties of DSS support as consisting of computer-based tools and overall support. The former consists of DSS product properties, while support “...provide[s] service, rather than the installation of a product” [43].

Selecting and evaluating a DSS as supporting a process, rather than producing an output, requires an assessment of the “manager’s subjective opinion that he or she now has ‘a better grasp of the problem’ and is able ‘to consider more alternatives’” [24]. This perspective implies some broad criteria for measuring the effectiveness of a DSS, including: accessibility, availability, reliability, etc. [24]. When DSS are viewed as providing decision support, the decision maker becomes a consumer of this support, and his or her attitudes and perceptions become important selection and evaluation measures.

From this support perspective, a DSS provides a set of benefits that affect its use and usefulness. The amount of decision-making support that the manager believes the DSS provides is the criterion upon which the usefulness of a particular system is judged. Thus, the relationship between the stages of the decision-making process and the manager's perception of the support provided by a DSS is an important selection and evaluation measure. To develop this relationship further, models of the decision-making process must be considered.

## 2.3. Decision-making Process

There are numerous models of decision making, each consisting of a series of steps that identify different stages of the process [e.g., 8,19,29,37]. A comprehensive review of these models can be found in [33].

Mintzberg et al. is possibly the best known empirically based model of decision making. Based on twenty-five case studies, they found that the decision-making process for strategic planning consisted of three core stages. Consistent with the conceptual models of others, they labeled their stages identification, development, and selection, and identify seven central “routines” or operations that support these core stages. Identification, which consists of decision recognition and diagnosis routines, addresses the importance of identifying the correct problem. The core of the decision-making process, development, consists of search and design. The first is invoked to find ready-made solutions, whereas the latter is intended to develop tailor-made solutions. The logical last step is selection, consisting of a screen routine, which is concerned with eliminating infeasible alternatives, an evaluation-choice routine, involving judgement, analysis, and bargaining, and an authorization routine.

Collectively, models of the decision-making process suggest that the stages of this process can be identified and measured. Witte [44] found empirical support for this contention and the Mintzberg et al. model is derived from empirical case study data. These models suggest that the overall decision-making process is independent of decision complexity and setting; however, movement within the stages of the process and the length of time spent in any particular stage are dependent upon the problem and solution-generating tools. Thus, the solution-generating capabilities of a DSS directly affect the decision-making process and, therefore, the quality of decisions. Despite the prescriptive literature, no model of decision making explicitly recognizes the steps needed to incorporate DSS support into the decision-making process. A model of DSS-supported decision making forms the basis for our process-oriented approach.

## 3. A Model of DSS-supported Decision Making

Our approach can be used either as a selection tool or an after-the-fact evaluation tool. Within Keen's Value Analysis, it can be used to establish value and assess the benefits of the prototype: it therefore complements the value added perspective. In fact, our approach assumes that the value added is the degree of support provided to the decision-making process.

Within the Meador and Mezger method, our approach can be used to assess end user and organizational needs (problem diagnosis), and establish the DSS generator's functional requirements and specifications. It can also be used as a model for surveying existing users of candidate DSS generators. Combined with problem diagnosis and end user assessment information, results from externally collected survey data can help evaluate the DSS generator's user/problem match.

Figure 1 depicts the basic model of the process-oriented approach: the stages of the decision-making process are indirectly matched with the tools and capabilities of the DSS generator. Different types of DSS generators provide varying degrees of support for the decision-making stages. For example, a model-oriented [2] DSS generator would be expected to provide greater support for the generation and evaluation of alternatives than would a data-oriented system. Conversely, a data-oriented generator should provide greater support for the intelligence or data-gathering stage.

Before a DSS can support the decision-making process, it must be explicitly integrated into the process. The DSS-aided decision-making model specifically recognizes the stages required to include a DSS in the process. For model-oriented DSS, the steps needed to identify, formulate, and test a model are recognized and included. For data-oriented DSS, the steps and tools that summarize, analyze, and identify structure and relationships among data [7] must be integrated into the process of making these decisions.

Ginzberg and Stohr have argued that the imposition of structure is an essential contribution of DSS to the decision-making process. The integration of a DSS into the decision-making process of any decision imposes structure. The explicit recognition of where and how DSS impose this structure is essential to formulating computer-based support for the process of decision making.

Examining the tools and support provided by a DSS, the right-half of Figure 1 depicts the relationship between the physical tools of a DSS and the decision-support they provide. The functional capabilities of DSS tools have been discussed [3,4]. Implicit in these discussions is the provision of a tool kit with sufficient breadth to support the user's decision-making process.

The left-half of Figure 1 shows the relationship between unaided and aided decision making. The stages needed to produce effective DSS-aided decision making must be explicitly included and integrated into the decision-making process. Before aided decision making can be investigated, models that specifically recognize the stages needed to integrate DSS support into the process are necessary. While this integration of DSS and process stages is not limited to model-oriented systems, for illustrative purposes, only the model-oriented DSS-aided decision-making stages are presented in Table 1.

In this model, aided decision-making has 12 stages; five are unique and seven common to many of the decision-making process models. For comparison, Table 1 also shows the first six stages of the Mintzberg model of the decision-making process; the seventh and last stage of this model, authorization, resides solely with the decision maker and therefore is not directly part of the aided decision-making process [5]. The five added stages define the essential steps of aided decision making. The additional steps are the identification and naming of variables, the translation of equations and report formats into DSS generator statements, the entry of these generator statements into the computer, the testing and debugging of these statements, running the specific DSS, and the summary and presentation of output. Many of these steps coincide with those of Keen [21] as part of the chauffeur's responsibilities or the intermediary function. The model also maintains the decision maker's control of the decision-making process, yet it explicitly recognizes the additional steps needed to integrate DSS development into the decision making process.

The stages and names used here are no more exact than any model of the process. Furthermore, the name aided decision-making was selected to recognize that both DSS development and DSS use must be included in any model that integrates DSS support into the process. Just as factors influence the amount of identification, development, etc. required in a specific decision-making process, the extent to which a specific step in the model is developed and applied will also vary.

To summarize, the basic proposition of our approach to DSS selection and evaluation is that the criterion should be the amount or degree of support provided by the DSS to stages of the process. This requires comparing the capabilities of DSS tools to the support needs at different stages. To make this comparison, the process must explicitly recognize the steps needed to integrate DSS support. It must also be recognized that the decision maker is not interested in computer-based tools per se, but in their decision support. From the decision-maker's perspective, the usefulness of the system is defined by the relationship between the support provided by the DSS and the decision-making needs.

## 4. Validation of the Model

Empirical support for the process-oriented model developed above is now presented. A self-administered questionnaire was developed making it possible to increase the volume and range of the collected data and the potential applicability of the results after mailing the questionnaire to actual users/decision makers. Pretests were used to hone and confirm the questionnaire's ability to capture the desired data.

## 4.1. Survey Questionnaire

A questionnaire containing thirty-six, seven point Likert scale questions was developed to assess the relationship between the stages of aided decision making and the level of support provided by the DSS generator. Twenty-four of the questions concerned the users' perception of the DSS generator's level of support, while the remaining twelve questions asked the respondent to report the degree of his or her involvement or experience with each of the steps in the aided decision-making model. The vast majority of questions pertaining to the DSS generator's level of support were taken from instruments developed and validated by Welsch and Sanders [43,35]. Questions consistent with DSS support were selected from these instruments. In addition, demographic data about the respondents was collected and a few questions regarding the importance of different stages in the DSS-aided decision-making model were included. Response to the latter was used to compare the perception of the user/decision-maker about the importance of specific stages in the decision-making process with the level of support provided by the DSS generator.

## 4.2. Procedures

Questionnaires were mailed to users of Execucom's IFPS and Lloyd Bush and Associates' MODEL. Where required, questions were adapted to reflect the specific terminology of the respective DSS generator; otherwise, the two hundred questionnaires mailed to both IFPS and MODEL users were identical. In total, eighty-seven responses were received, yielding a 22% response rate; 59 IFPS and 28 MODEL users responded. While skewed toward IFPS users, this sample size and response rate are consistent with those of previous survey research [e.g. 28], and the exploratory nature and supporting purpose of such work. Moreover, there was no significant difference in the responses of the IFPS and MODEL users.

## 4.3. Respondents

The average respondent held a bachelor's degree, and had occupied his or her current position just over three years with six years of experience in the particular industry. On average, the respondent's immediate span of control was two persons, and he or she was from three to four levels below the CEO of the company. Respondents reported moderate computer and mathematical training and experience, and substantial involvement in non-routine, ad hoc managerial decision-making activities. While these DSS users may not be of top management status, they are the true decision makers or key decision influencers – “the criterion for defining proper DSS users” [10].

## 4.4. Analysis and Results

The data analysis follows that of Green and Tull and Srinivasan. First, responses to the survey questions were divided into those measuring the degree of user involvement in the stages of the DSS-aided decision-making model and those recording the level of support provided by the DSS. Next, factor analysis was applied as a data-reduction technique to each of the data sets. We were not interested in the underlying dimensions per se, but in summarizing the data into a smaller set that retains most of the information in the original set. Finally, univariate and multivariate analysis was performed on the factor scores to determine the association between variables in the reduced data sets.

## 4.4.1. Data Reduction

To reduce the two original data sets, factor analysis was performed on the responses to the aided decision making questions and the DSS support questions, individually. Factor verification was computed using Chronbach's coefficient alpha, a widely used measure of reliability for testing the internal consistency of multiple item additive scales. Table 2 presents the results of the varimax rotated factor analysis for the DSS-aided decision-making data set.

While the primary purpose of the factor analysis was data reduction, composite names were selected to reflect the questions constituting the factors. Not wanting to introduce any new terminology, and because the terms used by Mintzberg et al. seem to describe the four factor analysis derived dimensions well, the DSS-aided decision-making constructs were labeled: identification, development, selection, and choice. The standardized coefficient alpha for each of the first three factors ranged from 0.87 to 0.91, which indicates a high degree of internal consistency, and these four factors explain almost 85% of the variance in the original data. Because the choice factor consisted of a single question, the coefficient alpha could not be computed. At the bottom of Table 2 is a test of the adequacy of four factors to describe the original data. Based upon the p-value of the chi-square result, the null hypothesis cannot be rejected; that is, four factors are sufficient to describe the original data set. Collectively, these results indicate that the four factors describe the underlying data set very well.

## 4.4.2. Association Between Data Sets

There are two related but distinct aspects of the study of association between variables. One involves prediction and forecasting; the second, correlation analysis, has the objective of determining the “degree of the relationship” between variables [6]. Because our objective is to determine this relationship between the aided decision-making stages and DSS support factors, correlation analysis is the basis for the data analysis. Both simple and multiple correlation analysis is presented.

The value of each variable in the reduced data sets was computed as the sum of the responses to questions included in its respective factor. Using this data set, Pearson product-moment simple correlation analysis was conducted to determine the univariate association between the aided decision-making factors and the derived support factors. Treating the DSS support factors as a set and using each of the aided decision-making factors as single variates, multiple correlation was conducted. These results are presented in Table 4.

Measured simply by the number of significant coefficients, the results of the Pearson product-moment analysis shows that most of the support or benefits provided by these generators is associated with the selection stage of the DSS-aided decision-making model, and the identification stage is the least serviced. Of the eight service characteristics factors, six are significantly correlated (p < 0.05) with the selection factor; four are correlated with the development factor; three with the choice factor; and only two are related to the identification factor. Because the non-significant coefficients are assumed to be no different than zero, these results show that most of the benefits provided by these DSS support the alternative selection stage of decision making, and very few are associated with the problem recognition and diagnosis stages.

The support or benefits associated with the stages of DSS-aided decision making is dependent upon the extent to which the names selected for the factors are representative of the actual constructs. Recognizing this limitation, Table 4 shows that the support provided to the identification stage of decision making is through the generation of alternative solutions and improved decision-making performance. Correlation analysis of the individual questions constituting these factors shows that the relationship between the identification stage and the generation of alternative solutions is the result of clarifying cause and effect relationships and with decision-making performance by encouraging the consideration of more areas and issues related to the problem. Because the development stage of decision making involves specific DSS development, results showing that the perceived benefits of system reliance, the generation of alternative solutions, and decision-making efficiency are consistent with this objective. To support the screening and selection of alternatives, the support provided to the selection stage of the decision-making process by these DSS include:

decision confidence and system reliance, efficient access to effective tools, and better decisions. Finally, the correlation coefficients show that the support provided by DSS to the choice stage are decision confidence, search and decision-making efficiency, and better decisions. These results are consistent with the model-oriented nature of the DSS generators surveyed; i.e., it is clear that for these DSS the number of support factors associated with the identification stage is much less than the number associated with the selection stage. The multiple correlation analysis also supports this finding.

These correlation coefficients were computed using the more general canonical correlation analysis. “A canonical correlation involving one y- or one x-measure is identically the multiple correlation of that measure with the other set” [9]. The multiple correlation results are presented at the bottom of Table 4. These results show that model-oriented DSS support factors, taken as a unit, are significantly related to the development, selection, and choice stages of the DSS-aided decision-making process. The F-statistics for these three stages all have p-values less than 0.05. However, the identification stage is not significantly supported by either of the surveyed DSS generators. Because identification tends to be a very data intensive activity, and because both IFPS and MODEL are model-oriented DSS generators, this result is not surprising.

## 4.4.3. Comparison of DSS-aided Decision-making Stages

The survey questionnaire also asked respondents to assess the difficulty, importance, time, and thought required of various stages of the aided decision-making process. Consistent with the earlier analysis, responses were collapsed into identification, development, selection, and choice categories as defined in Table 2. Results are summarized by these categories in Table 5.

These results show that almost 78% of the users felt that the most difficult parts of the process involved steps in the identification stage. They also felt that the identification steps were the most critical (82.5%) and required the most thought (74%). However, the majority (58.1%) felt that the model development stage took the most time. This stage consisted of equation and report specification, creation of specific DSS statements, and testing and correction of the DSS model(s). Despite overwhelming recognition of the importance of problem identification and advances in the abilities of DSS generators, most of the time is still spent in the development of specific DSS. Because it is intended to provide a means to improve decision making, one would like the majority of the user's time to be spent on the decision itself rather than developing the specific DSS. However, this does not mean that time spent in the development phase is unproductive; in fact, the iterative nature of the decision-making process relies upon the relationship between the identification and development phases.

Lastly, comparing the response means for the identification, development, selection, and choice aggregates suggests that the respondents were not the decision “ratifiers,” but were key decision influencers or stakeholders – the true decision makers and “the criterion for defining proper DSS users” [10]. On the Likert-scale (ranging from 1, “never involved,” to 7, “always involved”) used to capture the respondents’ level of involvement in the DSS-aided decision-making stages, the respondents reported that they were involved in the identification (4.72), development (5.38), and selection (4.76) stages of the decision-making process more than half the time. However, their involvement in the choice (2.82) stage was much less. These results support Ginzberg and Stohr’s contention that the true decision makers may not hold top managerial status; that involvement in the identification, development, and selection stages of the decision-making process reduces the choice to a ratification decision. This would explain the fact that our respondents were not top management status, yet reported substantial involvement in non-routine, ad hoc management decision-making activities.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

Because the objective of DSS is to improve decisions by supporting the process of decision making, the lack of a process-oriented perspective to DSS selection and evaluation is surprising and inconsistent with the conceptual foundation of the area. Used in concert with other approaches, a method based on the amount of support that the DSS provides to the different stages of the decision-making process recognizes and adds a critical criterion to its selection and evaluation.

Here, the criterion and a method for selecting and evaluating the degree of support that a particular DSS generator provides its users are proposed, developed, and empirically assessed. Before a DSS can support the process, it must be explicitly integrated into it. A DSS-aided model is proposed that specifically recognizes the stages required to include a DSS in the process. The support provided to each of the stages of this model serves as the selection and evaluation criterion.

The proposed process-oriented approach consists of identifying the different decision support needed at various stages of the decision-making process. Alone, this information may be used pre-scriptively or by comparing decision-support needs to those provided by a particular DSS. An efficient means of collecting this data is through a survey instrument, such as that used here.

To assess the usefulness and practicality of this approach, questions concerning DSS support were taken from instruments reported in the literature and combined with questions assessing the respondents' level of involvement in different stages of the decision making process. These questionnaires were mailed to IFPS and MODEL users. Analysis of this data supports the usefulness of the proposed process-oriented approach. Based on the number of significant associations, the results show that the support provided by these DSS to the selection stage of decision making is much greater than that provided to the identification stage. Because these are model-oriented DSS generators, these results were expected and serve to confirm the usefulness of the proposed procedures.

For the practitioner, this research presents a process-oriented DSS selection and evaluation method that complements other techniques. The process-oriented approach can be used for prescriptive, descriptive, or comparative assessment and evaluation. This information may be used to evaluate a DSS already in use or as selection information, by surveying the proposed systems current users. The decision-making stages supported by different DSS generators can be compared and the one(s) supporting the most, or the most critical, stages selected. In addition, knowledge of the service needs of a specific level of management and/or problem structure can provide a prescriptive guide for defining DSS generator requirements. Matching decision-making process requirements to the service provided by a DSS generator can also help evaluate the system's hardware/software tools by identifying decision-making stages where support is weak or lacking.

For the researcher, the DSS-aided decision-making model provides a vehicle for both prescriptive and descriptive research. Before a DSS can support the decision-making process, it must be explicitly integrated into the process. The aided decision-making model specifically recognizes the stages required to include a DSS in the decision-making process. In addition to describing the support currently provided by a DSS, such a model can be used to prescribe what is needed to support the different stages of the decision-making process. Concentrating on the services needed at different stages of the decision-making process as a criterion for DSS selection and evaluation is not only normatively appealing, but provides the DSS researcher with a means of understanding how DSS services can best be delivered.

From the user's/decision-maker's perspective, the level of support provided by the system is critical. Knowledge of the nature of this support, understanding the support needs of different stages of the decision-making process, and the design of DSS-based tools to provide this support are all needed if DSS is to fulfill its promise of improving decisions by supporting the decision-making process.
