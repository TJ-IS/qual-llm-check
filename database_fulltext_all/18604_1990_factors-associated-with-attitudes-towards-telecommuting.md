# Factors associated with attitudes towards telecommuting

Chee Sing Yap and Helen Tng
Department of Information Systems and Computer Science,
National University of Singapore, Singapore 0511

A survey of the attitudes of female computer professionals in Singapore towards telecommuting was carried out. Seventy-three percent of the 459 respondents were in favour of telecommuting. Most would prefer to work at home 1 to 3 days a week and at the office on the other days, rather than working at home full time. They would telecommute only in times of need (e.g. when they have young children) and were concerned with work and interaction-related problems which might arise from telecommuting. The findings suggest that telecommuting will be of particular interest to employees who are married, those with a high proportion of work that can be done at home, those who find their journey to work frustrating, and those with supervisors and coworkers who are supportive of telecommuting. Respondents who are single and have a study room at home are also likely to favour telecommuting. Implications are discussed.

Keywords: Remote Work, Telecommuting, Telework, Work at Home.

## 1. Introduction

The term “telecommuting” refers to the substitution of computer and telecommunications technologies for physical travel to a central work location [11]. Other terms, such as flexiplace, homework or work-at-home, telework, and remote office work, have also been used to refer to telecommuting [3,12]. In this paper, the term “telecommuting” is used to refer to work carried out at home during regular office hours by employees of organizations; it is used interchangeably with “work-at-home”. This paper presents the findings of a survey carried out in Singapore on the attitudes of female computer professionals towards telecommuting.

One would not expect telecommuting to be of particular interest or relevance to Singapore, because it is a small island nation with a good public transport system. The maximum commuting time by car from one end of the island to the other is less than one hour. Nonetheless, there has been considerable interest in telecommuting as an arrangement to allow working mothers to keep their jobs and take care of their children at the same time. Could some of these women have continued to work if their jobs were brought to them instead of them travelling to their jobs?

## 2. Previous Studies

Previous studies which examined the attitudes of employees and managers towards telecommuting include DeSanctis [5], Duxbury, Higgins, and Irving [6], and Vitalari, Hamilton, and Ramsower [18].

DeSanctis examined the attitudes of 51 managers and 129 programmers in the US. Her findings suggested that attitude towards work-at-home is primarily related to a person's work position and job characteristics: programmers tended to be more interested than project managers. Both groups perceived telecommuting as promoting better morale and productivity. Neither group, however, mentioned easier child-care, better recruiting, reduced turnover, or problems of career development. Her findings indicated that preference for telecommuting was significantly related to supervisory responsibility. The more frequently the respondents had to interact with others to perform their jobs, the less likely they were to prefer telecommuting. Persons in lower salary grades and those with less seniority were more likely to prefer the work-at-home option. Most demographic variables, such as age, education, and number of children, were not related to attitudes towards telecommuting.

Duxbury, Higgins, and Irving carried out a survey involving 78 managers and 63 employees in Canada. They found that employees are more attracted to the benefits of work-at-home than are their managers. Significant differences in views were found for three variables: productivity, stress, and quality of work life. Managers took the view that telecommuting decreases productivity. Employees perceived that job-related stress will decrease and quality of work life will improve. However, both groups did not feel that work-at-home would improve an organization's morale; this contradicts the findings of DeSanctis [5]. Both groups felt strongly that personal interaction and "being visible" were important to their careers, and that telecommuting would impede professional development and threaten career opportunities. Both groups indicated that their organizations were not supportive of the idea of telecommuting.

Vitalari, Hamilton, and Ramsower used Fishbein and Ajzen's [7] theory as a framework for understanding the attitudinal and normative beliefs behind the desires and intentions of 60 programmers and analysts to work at home. Their findings showed that while there is generally a strong desire to work at home a few days each week, there is little intention to do so. The programmers and analysts generally felt that their supervisors, companies, and co-workers would react unfavourably to work-at-home.

These studies have provided useful insights into the perception of employers and employees towards telecommuting. They show that although technology has made it feasible for people to telecommute, it is not the force driving the work-at-home movement. Organizational culture, economic and social concerns of both employers and employees seem to have a greater influence on the choice made $[13,15,16,17]$ . The population of telecommuters is likely to be limited to those who have strong personal reasons for wanting to telecommute.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

According to Fishbein and Ajzen's [7] theory, a person's intention to perform a behaviour is determined by two factors: the attitude towards the behaviour and the subjective norm. Attitudes are composed of the person's behavioural beliefs about outcomes of performing the behaviour, and the values of these outcomes for the individual. The subjective norms concern the person's normative beliefs about what relevant others think should be done, and the person's motivation to comply with those relevant others. In the context of telecommuting, behavioral beliefs are likely to be associated with home situation (e.g. availability of a home computer), job characteristics (e.g. the amount of work that can be done at home), and commuting situation (e.g. the daily commuting time). Normative beliefs refer to beliefs about supervisors' and co-workers' attitudes towards telecommuting.

## Home situation

Availability of a home computer may add to the convenience of working at home as it can be used as a link to the office computer. Those with such hardware at home are likely to have used or be using their computers for work after office hours, and hence may be in favour of telecommuting during regular office hours.

H1: Those with personal computers at home are more likely to favour telecommuting.

For some women, the home may be perceived as a more desirable place to work, with more privacy and fewer interruptions, especially if there is a room set aside for work and study. Those who have such a room may find home a more conducive place to work.

H2: Attitude towards telecommuting is positively correlated with the availability of a study room used for work at home.

## Job Characteristics

DeSanctis [5] found that the decision to work at home is primarily related to a person's work position and job characteristics. Those with supervisory duties are more likely to feel they need to interact in person with their subordinates in order to manage and evaluate their work performance.

H3: Those who have supervisory duties are more likely to be against telecommuting.

Programming is an activity that requires full concentration over an extended period of time with little interpersonal interaction with others. Generally programmers would like to have their own terminal all the time, for instance, when fixing problems.

H4: Those whose main work activity is programming are more likely to favour telecommuting.

Tasks that involve mainly manipulation of information and little face-to-face interaction with others can be more easily accommodated in telecommuting arrangements. Thus, attitude towards telecommuting is likely to be related to the amount of work that can be done at home, as perceived by the respondent.

H5: Those who perceive that a high proportion of their work can be done at home are more likely to favour telecommuting.

## Commuting situation

Early research on telecommuting focused mainly on telecommunications-transport substitution as a means of energy conservation during the “oil crisis” years. However, findings have not been consistent. One study found no significant relationship between transport variables and attitudes towards telecommuting, but another found that the commute distance to work was highly correlated with intention to work at home. Reduced commuting time and costs were frequently cited advantages of working at home.

H6: Those who take longer to travel to work each day are more likely to favour telecommuting.

H7: Attitude towards telecommuting is related to the degree to which the individual perceives the journey to work as being frustrating or relaxing.

## Normative beliefs

As previously suggested, the respondent's intention also depends on how she perceives others feel about telecommuting. This study examines the relationship between a respondent's attitude and the perceived attitudes of supervisors and coworkers.

H8: Attitude towards telecommuting is related to beliefs about the attitude of supervisors towards telecommuting.

H9: Attitude towards telecommuting is related to beliefs about the attitude of coworkers towards telecommuting.

## 4. Research Method

The study was directed at only female computer professionals because (i) they are a large group of workers who are “information technology literate", (ii) much of the work of computer professionals can be done remotely using computer and telecommunications technologies, and (iii) in Singapore, telecommuting is of particular interest to women as an alternative work arrangement. Hence, female computer professionals are conceivably the early adopters of telecommuting in Singapore.

Forty organizations were selected at random from the 1985 Asian Computer Directory. The sample included private firms, government bodies and statutory boards, with business activities such as banking, housing, telecommunications, shipping, petroleum, beverages production, education and taxation. A pilot survey was carried out in May 1987 to test the questionnaire with 178 female computer professionals from 11 organizations. Based on the returns, the questionnaire was revised. It was then sent to 758 female computer professionals in 29 other organizations in June 1987. A total of 459 useful returns were received, representing a response rate of 61%. The sample represented about 15% of the total number of female computer professionals in Singapore [10].

Telecommuting is a very new concept in Singapore and is conceivably a hypothetical consideration for most respondents. The dependent variable, attitude towards telecommuting, was measured using a six-point scale. An even numbers scale was chosen to “force” respondents to indicate their preference however slight it may be.

Table 4 shows the major concerns of the respondents in favour of telecommuting. Some were concerned that telecommuting might have negative implications for their career development. These findings are in line with those reported in other studies [5,6].

The majority (83%) of the respondents in favour of telecommuting would want to work 1 to 3 days a week at home, while maintaining full-time organizational employee status. Only 17% would want to work at home 4 to 6 days a week (full-time telecommuting). One possible explanation is that respondents felt that working at home for less time would not significantly affect their visibility at the office, the lack of which might have a negative effect on promotion prospects. In addition, many other work problems arising from working at home are likely to be ameliorated by being at the office part of the week.

Table 5 shows the main reasons cited by respondents for being against telecommuting. These respondents indicated that they would be interested in telecommuting when they have children (36%), when they are pregnant (26%), or when they get married (5%). Sixteen percent indicated that no event would cause them to be interested in telecommuting.

## 5.4. Factors Associated with Attitude

The nine hypotheses were tested using either the Chi-squared test (for nominal data) or the Kendall rank order correlation test (for ordinal data). Because attitude towards telecommuting is significantly correlated with marital status, separate analyses were carried out for married and single respondents. The results are summarized in Table 6. Consistent results were obtained for both groups in all analyses, except for H2 where the result was significant for single respondents but not for married respondents. The survey data did not support four of the hypotheses (H1, H3, H4, H6) at the 0.05 significance level.

H1: The survey data did not support the view that those with personal computers at home are more likely to favour telecommuting. Perhaps the respondents assumed that computer and communication equipment would be provided by the employers. It is interesting to note that 47% of the respondents had a personal computer at home, but 37% did not use the computer for work-related purposes.

H2: For single respondents, attitude towards telecommuting was significantly correlated with the availability of a study room set aside for work purposes. Those who worked in the study room while at home tended to be more enthusiastic about telecommuting. A similar trend was observed in the data for married respondents, but the relationship was not significant at the 0.05 level.

H3: No significant relationship was found between attitude towards telecommuting and supervisory content in job. This finding contrasts with DeSanctis' [5] results that managers feel they need to interact directly with subordinates in order to manage them and evaluate their performance. The disagreement could be due to differences in the samples; here only female computer professionals were included, whereas DeSanctis included both sexes.

H4: There is no significant correlation between amount of programming content in job and attitude towards telecommuting.

H5: Attitude was significantly correlated with the respondent's perception of the amount of work that can be done at home.

H6: No significant relationship was found between attitude towards telecommuting and the length of commuting time. This is probably because most of the respondents (90%) took less than one hour to travel to work, and this was felt to be acceptable.

H7: Attitude towards telecommuting was significantly correlated with the degree of frustration/relaxation of the journey to work as perceived by the respondents. Respondents who found commuting to work stressful and a hassle were more likely to favour telecommuting.

H8 & H9: Attitude towards telecommuting was significantly correlated with the respondent's beliefs about the attitudes of supervisors and co-workers towards telecommuting. Those who perceived their supervisors and co-workers as being supportive tended to favour telecommuting. This is consistent with the findings of Vitalari et al [18]: programmers and analysts had little intention to work at home because they felt that their companies, supervisors, and co-workers would react unfavourably towards those working at home.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Telecommuting As An Alternative Work Arrangement

The generally positive response from the respondents suggests that telecommuting is an alternative that employers should consider. Most respondents indicated that their interest in telecommuting would change in future. Those who were against telecommuting said that their interest in telecommuting might change when they got married or had children. This implies that telecommuting is desired only for particular periods in their lives to accommodate particular needs, for example, when they have small children.

Working at home part of the time is more popular than full-time. This suggests that an ideal arrangement is one which affords personal autonomy and control over timing of work, while maintaining a presence at the office. For some professionals, life-style and balance are more important than the single-minded emphasis on career advancement, and location and timing of work are important to the extent that they allow them to meet their personal needs [1].

A flexitime scheme that allows an employee to make decisions on when to turn up at the office, subject to exigencies of work, may be ideal. One suggestion is for employees to “log” a minimum number of hours at the office and be free to work anywhere else the rest of the time.

## 6.2. Major Concerns

Work-related problems cited as major concerns of respondents in favour of telecommuting were also given as reasons for opposing telecommuting by others. This suggests that employers planning to allow telecommuting should pay attention to such issues as communication between homeworkers and others, access to reference material and resources, suitability of job tasks, and fostering team spirit between homeworkers and on-site employees [3]. Policies should include arrangements for time at the office to facilitate interaction and team building.

Many respondents were concerned about problems that might arise from integrating work and home activities. Role conflict problems may arise. One respondent felt that working at home would not work, because her children would demand all her time and isolating herself at home would make her feel guilty.

Another major concern is with career advancement. Employers should ensure that telecommuters are not made to feel their chances of advancement will be lessened. A performance evaluation process perceived as being fair to all employees, on-site or off-site, may help to address these concerns. Telecommuting programmes must not result in the homeworkers becoming “second class corporate citizens” [2].

## 6.3. Attitudes Of Supervisors And Co-Workers

On-site supervisors and co-workers must be perceived to be supportive. Supervisors in particular must actively support alternative work arrangements. If an individual believes that supervisors and co-workers may react negatively, the individual is likely to reject telecommuting. On-site employees are more likely to be supportive of off-site colleagues if they do not perceive them as being especially favoured or privileged.

## 6.4. Pilot Telecommuting Programmes

Employers should evaluate telecommuting programmes in small pilot studies before widely offering the option $[4,8]$ . The highly positive response suggests that female computer professionals may be willing to participate. The government should take the lead and initiate programmes to evaluate positive and negative impacts of working at home. Based on experience, appropriate policies and guidelines can then be formulated. Because cultural characteristics and management motivations in implementing telecommuting programmes vary from organization to organization, policies appropriate to one may not be right for another. Each organization should look into its own situation and adopt appropriate policies.

## 6.5. Telecommuting For Both Parents

While female participation in the labour force should be encouraged, it should not be pushed without consideration for quality of life. Caring for the family and children is a full-time job. Working as an employee is a separate full-time commitment. Public policies must not be anchored on the traditional view that women's priorities center only on their families and homes, and that they can afford to put their careers on hold. Men, too, can be encouraged to telecommute to participate actively in caring for family and children.

## 6.6. Child-Care Facilities

It should be noted that most respondents who were in favour of telecommuting preferred to work at home only 1 to 3 days a week. Therefore, reliable and affordable child-care arrangements must be available so that working mothers can be at the office on the other days. These facilities may also be required when the mothers are working at home: previous studies indicate that working at home is not a good substitute for child-care [14].

## 7. Concluding Remarks

The study suggests that most Singapore female computer professionals would like to telecommute only 1 to 3 days a week, and in times of need, for example, when they have young children. Because the sample comprised only female computer professionals, the results obtained may not be generalizable. However, they are likely to be representative, to some extent, of the attitudes of other female professionals in Singapore who use computers in their work and whose work, either part or all of it, could be done at home.
