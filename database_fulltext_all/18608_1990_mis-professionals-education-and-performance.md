# MIS professionals: Education and performance \*

M.B. Khan and Sal Kukalis

California State University, Long Beach, CA 90840, USA

Individuals from various disciplines have joined the MIS profession and have been successful in varying degrees. Our study involved identifying the educational background of MIS professionals (managers and non-managers), analyzing the performance of these professionals, and investigating the perceptions of both managers and non-managers with regard to the factors that contribute to professional advancement. According to the results of this study, MIS professionals come from different backgrounds and have different levels of education. Also, it seems that a significant number of MIS professionals are performing well on-the-job though they possess no formal academic training in traditional computer-related disciplines. Both MIS managers and non-managers agree that nontechnical skills are more important than technical skills for higher professional advancement; however, both also feel that technical skills are required for professional success in certain MIS areas. There are no major differences between the responses given by MIS managers and non-managers in those factors that contribute to successful MIS careers.

Keywords: MIS Professionals, Education, Performance.

## Introduction

Spurred by the demand and opportunities for the MIS profession, individuals from various disciplines and backgrounds have joined this profession and have succeeded. There is a lack of material in the MIS literature on the relationship between MIS professionals' type of training and level of position attained. One study [SIM, 1988] has been conducted on the educational profile of senior MIS executives (CIO, VP, Asst. VP, Directors, and Managers). It gave the highest degree earned by these executives and their field of specialization. However, no studies have been conducted to analyze the educational background of MIS managers and non-managers, and how their education is related to job performance skills and abilities.

The objective of this paper is to provide a breakdown of the educational background of both MIS managers and non-managers and an analysis of the performance of these professionals on the basis of their education and experience. ## Methodology

The research methodology consisted of mailing out a questionnaire to 600 MIS professionals (managers and non-managers) who are members of the Data Processing Management Association (DPMA) in region 1 (consisting of California, Nevada and Arizona). Of the 600 questionnaires that were sent, 17 were returned to the authors due to incorrect or insufficient addresses; thus, 583 questionnaires actually reached the addresses. Of these, 225 were returned to the authors with valid responses: a response rate of over 38 percent.

The questionnaire consisted of 17 questions: two were personal (age and sex), three were training-related, three were company-related, and the remaining nine were related to education, job performance, and perceptions relating to successful MIS careers. The data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics.

The questionnaire was validated by sending it to a group of 10 DPMA members in California to see if they had difficulty in interpreting the questions. No difficulty was experienced by these respondents.

## Respondent Profile

The respondents were divided almost equally between managers and non-managers: 49.3 percent managers. They represented all types of organizations: manufacturing (28.3%), government (2.3%), financial (43.8%), food-related (10.0%), distribution (5.0%), transportation (0.5%) and others (9.6%). Their experience level ranged from less than one year to over 10 years; their annual salary was from less than \$25,000 to over \$55,000. Their educational background ranged from no degree to masters degree; none of the respondents had doctorate degrees. This is in contrast to the earlier study [SIM, 1988] in which 5.0% of the senior MIS executives (directors and vice presidents) were found to have doctorate degrees.

## Results

The results of the study are presented in Tables 2 through 9. All data are organized and presented in three ways (if appropriate): all respondents, only managers, and non-managers. The educational background of the respondents is shown in Table 2. The entries in the table represent the percentage of respondents.

This table shows some interesting facts: almost one third of the undergraduate degrees are in fields other than business, information systems, engineering, and computer science. The corresponding figure for a masters degree is 13.2%. Thus a large number of MIS professionals come from non-computer related fields, although the number of such professionals with masters degrees is much smaller than those with bachelors degrees.

It is also found that more MIS non-managers have masters degrees than MIS managers. This may indicate that there is a trend among MIS professionals to get masters degrees, in the hope that their advanced degrees will help professional advancement. To investigate the relationship between educational background and the likelihood of being a manager or non-manager, a number of t-tests were used. The results indicated that at the bachelor's level, both computer science and business majors were significant at t = 14.3, p < 0.001 for computer science, and t = 4.6, p < 0.05 for business. At the master's degree level, computer science majors were significant at t = 16.2, p < 0.001, and engineering was marginally significant at t = 3.1, p < 0.10. According to these tests, MIS professionals majoring in computer science are more likely to be managers than non-managers.

As already mentioned, almost half of the respondents were managers. The managers got their first supervisory position after working for varying periods of time in non-supervisory positions: 37.9% of them got their first supervisory position after having worked for less than three years in an MIS non-supervisory position, 28.3% after having worked between three to five years, 15.5% after having worked between five to eight years, and 13.2% after more than eight years.

Of all respondents, 56.6% felt that their formal education provided a strong foundation for their on-the-job performance, while 28.3% did not. Considering only managers, 53.7% felt positively about the contribution of formal education on their on-the-job performance, and 27.8% felt negatively. Considering only non-managers, 59.5% felt that formal education had a positive impact on their on-the-job performance, whereas 28.8% did not. That more than one-fourth of the respondents answered negatively on the question of the contribution of their formal education on their on-the-job performance is expected, since many of them came from disciplines that are not related to information systems.

## Conclusion

The study found that a little more than 20 percent of MIS professionals without formal education have attained a certain level of job status; this may imply (or be assumed to be indicative of) a degree of successful job performance.

Over 30 percent of MIS professionals come from disciplines other than business, computer science, and engineering. The fact that 25 percent of MIS managers do not have a degree in computer-related fields raises an important question if traditional education is necessary for success. Adequate training may prove to be an effective substitute for formal MIS education.

According to a recent study [Rouse and Hartog, 1988], MIS jobs are undergoing fundamental changes and MIS organizations are becoming more business-oriented. It would be interesting to see if these changes affect the educational profile of future MIS professionals. The authors plan to study this trend. The study found that there are no major differences between the opinions expressed by MIS managers and non-managers with respect to the factors that affect their advancement potential. Although there is a general consensus that non-technical factors will play an increasingly important role in professional advancement, technical capabilities and knowledge will still be required to perform well in certain areas. Both MIS managers and non-managers agree on this point.
