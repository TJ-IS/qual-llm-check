Case Study

# Information support for sales and marketing

A case study at a small grocery manufacturer

David K. Goldstein

Boston University, Boston, MA 02215, USA

As more managers are using data and analytic tools, such as spreadsheet software, organizations are attempting to determine how to best support them. In this paper, the impact of the information support on the work carried out by sales and product managers at one company is examined. Interviews were conducted with members of the MIS, product management, and sales organizations at a small grocery manufacturer. Information support consisted mostly of reports obtained from the company's sales system and of SAMI (supermarket warehouse withdrawal) reports. Product and sales managers used the information to learn about their business, to communicate to brokers and supermarket buyers, and to monitor performance. The principal benefit of the available data and analytic tools was enhancement of the sales and product managers' knowledge. Managers, however, expressed frustration at the lack of available data and analytic tools and felt that they could be at a disadvantage with respect to their competitors and buyers.

Keywords: Management Information Systems, Management Support Systems, Decision Support Systems, Marketing Applications, Sales Applications, Consumer Products Marketing.

In 1971, Michael Scott Morton wrote, “Computer technology has advanced at a rapid rate, but thus far has had little, if any, direct impact on managerial action [13].” After nineteen years, we are starting to see evidence that this statement is no longer true. Two technological changes are affecting the degree to which managers are using information technology. First, computer-based data analysis tools, specifically LOTUS 1-2-3 and similar software packages, are being used with increased frequency by managers and staff. Second, the amount of data available to managers and staff is growing rapidly, due to a greater use of computers throughout organizations and an increase in the amount of data available from information services vendors. Given these changes, it is important to provide answers to two questions: how are managers using information technology in their day-to-day work and how can we effectively provide data and analytic tools to managers?

David K. Goldstein received his Ph.D. in Management Science from the Sloan School of Management at M.I.T. He also received a Master of Engineering degree in Information Science from Harvard and a Bachelor of Science degree from McGill University. He has extensive MIS teaching experience in MBA, doctoral, and executive education programs both at Harvard Business School, where he was an Assistant Professor, and at Boston University. He has published articles in the

Management Information Systems Quarterly and the Journal of Management Information Systems. For the last four years he has studied the approaches organizations take in supporting marketing managers with information technology, focusing on the relationship between information technology, managerial knowledge, and management effectiveness. He also conducts research examining the management of the information systems function and the use of qualitative research methods in MIS.

Through a case study in the marketing and sales organizations at a small grocery manufacturer, we will answer the first question and shed some light on the second. Specifically, we will examine the relationship between the information support environment for sales and marketing managers and their use of data and analytic tools. Further, we will study the impact of using these tools on managers' work. We define information support environment as: (1) the data and analytic tools directly available to managers, (2) the assistance provided by support staff in organizing data and analysis tools, and (3) the data gathered and analyzed by others and provided to the managers.

Research using the organizational information processing paradigm provides insight into how managers use data and analytic tools. Daft and Weick [3] view organizations as systems that gather and interpret information to increase knowledge. They noted that organizations enact cycles of information gathering, interpretation, and learning. According to their model, increased monitoring of the environment leads to the development of a better understanding of events, and an increase in knowledge about the relationship between an organization's actions and the outcomes of the actions. Hence, we might assume that the availability of more information about the environment should facilitate information gathering and that the availability of easy to use analytic tools should simplify information interpretation. These two factors should then lead to increased learning.

Even though there is a massive amount of MIS research, very few studies have examined managers' day-to-day use of information technology or the impact of information technology on managers' work. The exceptions include a study by Morrell [10] examining the use of personal computers by naval personnel officers. He found that spreadsheet and data base software were used for budgeting, planning, and ad hoc inquiry and that the PCs provided information that improved both matching of personnel to openings and long range planning. Goldstein and Zack [5] observed that higher levels of information support, including the use of PCs and decision support systems, led to more knowledge acquisition among product managers at grocery manufacturers. In a study of executive support systems, Rockart and De-Long [12] found that these systems had an impact in three areas: improving communication, better planning and control, and enhancing senior manager's mental models of the organization and its environment. Keen and Scott Morton present examples of how DSS use leads to “better understanding, richer insights, and more extensive assessment and synthesis of data [7, p. 218].” These last three studies support Daft and Weick's model by providing evidence that using information technology increases learning.

## Subjects and Procedures

To begin to understand how managers are using data and analytic tools in their day-to-day work, a case study was performed within the marketing and sales groups at a small grocery manufacturer. These groups were chosen for three reasons. First, their work is information-intensive. Product and sales managers analyze data from internal order processing systems and inventory systems and from several external sources (e.g., Nielsen). Second, there is evidence [8] that they perform similar functions in different companies and that different approaches to providing information support can be found at these companies. This permits comparisons to be made between this case study and others done in similar firms (for example, [5]). These comparisons allow us to examine the effectiveness of different approaches to information support. Third, the specific problem of information support to sales and product management is an important one for both marketing and MIS managers, due to both the complexity of providing information support and the importance of the sales and marketing tasks [9].

Quelch, Farris and Olver [11] provide insight into the work done by product managers. They identified nine categories of tasks they performed. Three of these, developing executing and evaluating consumer and trade (supermarket) promotions, forecasting and budgeting, and market research and analysis, require extensive analysis of quantitative data.

Interviews were conducted in the spring of 1987 with three members of the marketing organization, two members of the sales organization, and one member of the MIS organization at a small national grocery manufacturer. The guideline presented in Appendix 1 was used in interviews with product and sales managers. The MIS manager was asked to describe the MIS organization, the hardware and software used at the company, and the support provided for marketing and sales. Each tape-recorded interview lasted approximately one hour.

The case study presented below is based on an analysis of the transcribed interviews, and on notes taken by the researcher during the interviews. A preliminary draft of the case, in a slightly longer, undisguised version, was circulated to the subjects for comment. This version incorporates their comments. In addition, it is disguised at the request of the company and the subjects.

## Case Study

## Company Background

Barrow Company, a privately-held consumer goods manufacturer, was founded in the 1960s. Its 1986 sales were in excess of \$40 million. Its products were produced in 5 plants, shipped to 20 warehouses, and sold through over 50 brokers. Barrow was described as a conservative and bottom-line oriented. Its primary emphasis was on increasing profits and secondarily on increasing sales.

The MIS organization consisted of three people. The MIS manager had been with Barrow 9 years and reported to the VP Finance. A computer operator and a data entry clerk reported to him.

Barrow's principal computer was being converted from an older small mainframe to a mini-computer. The computer's software handled financial activities – accounts receivable, accounts payable, and general ledger – order processing, inventory, billing, freight allocation, and raw materials.

While Barrow competed in several product categories, the case focuses on its largest, which accounted for most of its sales and profits. The category (as measured by SAMI) had sales of \$400 million, of which Barrow's share was about 10%. The category was growing about twice as rapidly as the average supermarket category (the average category grew 1–3% per year). Supermarkets stocked about 30–40 items in the category. Barrow's principal competitors were divisions of large diversified grocery manufacturers. Being smaller than many of its competitors, it had the ability to sell \$4–5 million products profitably.

Barrow sold its products through 50 brokers. These were independent businesses that represented several manufacturers. The brokers were paid on commission, which was based on sales. Barrow's sales represented a significant portion of the business for some brokers, but not for others. Broker turnover was not a problem.

A typical supermarket buyer to whom the brokers sell handled Barrow's category, along with several others. Its category represents about 10% of the buyer's business, and Barrow's products make up less than 1% of those for which the buyer was responsible. Hence, the company was a relatively small seller to its major accounts.

Barrow competed in markets that accounted for about 80% of supermarket ACV (stores that made up 80% of the total U.S. supermarket sales volume), with its sales strongest in the region in which it was based. Promotions were kept to a minimum. They were done semi-annually for most brands; competitors promoted quarterly.

BrandA, Barrow's oldest and largest selling product, was introduced in 1973 and was the 4th best selling item in the category with about \$20 million in sales. BrandA came in two sizes. In 1984, a premium-priced line extension, was introduced (also in 2 sizes). The introduction was a success, increasing overall business by 15%. Both versions of BrandA were sold nationally. A second line extension, was being introduced and was available in 40% of the country. BrandA had no direct competition, although another manufacturer was test marketing a similar product.

The principal thrust of the marketing strategy for BrandA was to increase consumer awareness and gain new users. Toward that end, a combination of trade promotions, advertising (both print and spot TV), and consumer couponing were used.

BrandB had sales of under \$5 million and was ranked between 20 and 30 in category market share. It was a new brand available in 50% of supermarket's ACV. BrandB had direct competition from one product.

The objective of the marketing strategy for BrandB was to increase distribution. It was available in 50% of supermarket ACV, with a goal of 75%. There were regional differences in sales with much depending on the effort made by the broker. Barrow was running into some difficulty in gain-

Marketing Organization Use of Data and Analytic Tools

The marketing organization consisted of four people, the Marketing Vice President and three Brand Managers. All had extensive previous experience at large consumer goods firms.

The brand managers described their tasks as managing everything to do with the brand, including working with sales to design trade programs, working with the ad agency to build brand awareness, and working with production and purchasing to insure adequate supply and quality. They developed and implemented a marketing plan for the brand, which included product improvement, packaging, positioning, and distribution. In addition, they monitored sales and profits to meet planned goals. The job was described as a classical brand management position – the hub of the brand with limited authority.

The marketing organization relied on data from internal transaction processing systems and on purchased data. (See Table 1 for a summary of data sources used by marketing and sales managers.) All data reached the managers as reports. MIS provided 27 reports based on internal data – mostly from the sales history file. The marketing group worked directly with vendors to obtain the purchased data. MIS was not involved in the purchased data.

The internal data were principally used to monitor sales. Subjects indicated that the data allow them to see if their products were in stock, to find out “where we’re selling displays and where we’re not,” to determine how much money was spent on a particular trade deal, to evaluate programs (e.g., TV, coupons, or local promotions), to insure production schedules were adequate, and to develop volume forecasts for the coming year. Analysis of the data was carried out either with a calculator or using LOTUS 1-2-3. To use LOTUS, product managers manually entered data from the reports into a personal computer.

One brand manager described the use of sales data to evaluate promotions, "I first dig out monthly sales data for the 3 months before the promotion, the month of the promotion, and the three after it. I then get the same data for the previous year. This process is time consuming, because the data is on different printouts. I then compare the sales data. I have found that promotions help in a few markets, but not in others."

The BrandA manager described how she used sales data that she entered into a spreadsheet, stating “I manipulate sales data to try and understand it. For example, a line extension was introduced in three phases starting two years ago. I track sales for the markets in each phase to see if they were following the same trend. For example, I look at sales in the phase one markets six months after introduction to determine what the sales level will be in the phase two markets six months after introduction. I also examine cannibalization of our existing brand by the line extension."

SAMI data were used as an indicator of how well Barrow, its competitors, and the category as a whole were doing, nationally, regionally, and by market. One brand manager indicated that the data were used to “assess the effectiveness of advertising and promotions, to assess the impact of competition on our business, to make volume forecasts for next year, and to revise the current year’s forecasts.”

For BrandB, data were used to monitor and improve distribution. Shipment, SAMI, and NRTI data were used to calculate sales per point of distribution (sales for a given market normalized for the number of stores in the market in which the product was available). The brand manager found that per point of distribution sales were almost at par with BrandA, indicating that the product sold relatively well once it was stocked by a store.

The brand manager used the data to identify accounts that stocked BrandA and not BrandB. He then estimated, based on BrandA sales in the account, how much yearly sales to expect if BrandB were introduced. The brand manager then generated a list of the top 25 accounts for the brokers to target. Plans were developed to approach the most important accounts. The brand manager commented, “We went after the accounts even if it costs us money we wouldn’t generally want to spend. If we can estimate potential sales, we will deal even if the account wants \$25,000 in slotting allowances, free goods, and deals, because the incremental volume will return the money in 18 months.” All the brand manager’s analyses for these tasks was carried out on a calculator.

One brand manager used a personal computer for several tasks. She kept budget and actual data in a LOTUS spreadsheet to monitor performance. LOTUS was also used to produce reports for regional sales managers and to test ‘what if’ scenarios. For example, she used a spreadsheet model to estimate the sales volume needed given a certain level of marketing expenditure to reach a profit goal. It was also used to break out budgeted volume by month, product, and market to facilitate volume planning.

## Evaluation of Marketing Information Support

Using the available data and analytic tools, brand managers learned about factors that affect sales of their products. This knowledge changed decisions that they made. In some instances, these changes led to measurable benefits. For example, the BrandB Manager, who carried out the sales per point of distribution analysis, gained distribution in 40% of the targeted 25 stores. The same brand manager indicated that the promotion analysis that he performed identified markets in which this year's promotion were a success. In these markets another promotion might be added next year.

Brand managers also noted that the available data and tools facilitated several tasks. One brand manager indicated that the use of spreadsheet models made it easier to monitor the introduction of a line extension, to compare actual performance to budget, and to break out budgeted data by product and market.

For the most part, the managers focused on what could be done with more data and tools available. They felt that they would learn more about their products and their decisions would improve.

One area of frustration was promotion analysis. A brand manager commented, “The promotion data is difficult to use. We are trying to control expenses, but all we get is raw data. Analysis must be done by hand. In addition some of the data, such as percentage sold on deal, can fool you.” Another added, “We don’t have a good understanding of competitors’ activity during promotion. We barely know what they are doing nationally and don’t know their regional exceptions.” He did not, however, think that lack of promotion data was a major problem, noting, “This does not put us at a huge disadvantage, since we would rather be proactive than reactive. And even the sophisticated companies can’t track promotions well.”

One manager conjectured that the availability of scanner data could have a significant impact on brand managers. It would allow them, for example, to learn more about competitors' promotions. If a competitor ran a successful promotion, Barrow might be able to copy it. The availability of scanner data would also allow a brand manager to test a program in one city before using it nationally.

Another manager commented on the potential impact on Barrow of its lack of data and knowledge. He noted that Barrow could be at a disadvantage with respect to larger competitors and retailers given the amount of data they were using. He noted that competitors who purchase more scanner data and retailers who have direct access to the data might know more about Barrow's brands than Barrow itself.

Another theme mentioned by several managers was the difficulty in using existing data and tools. They had to spend more time doing analysis than they would have liked. One subject commented, "The EDP [MIS] reports are formatted wrong. All the information is there, but we can't use it. I would like to look at my data on a regional basis, but it is too time-consuming under the current system. With better support, I could spend less time looking at data. I'd be able to detect problems faster and improve my efficiency."

Two managers commented that they were spending too much time on data analysis either because they were keying numbers into the PC themselves or because they were relying on a calculator for analysis. One mentioned that he “hates to do all the analysis on the calculator”. The other mentioned that keying in data was “awful” and that he was “spending a lot of time doing grunt work”.

Some managers discussed the reasons behind the lack of information support at Barrow. One commented, “Barrow has been slow to get into EDP. We have focused on providing reports, with little or no analysis of data. On the market research side, we are using the same techniques that our competitors use. I feel much more confident in market research than in MIS. We could be at a disadvantage with respect to our competitors. They have sales information that is presented differently and is more timely and more useful.” Another manager mentioned that MIS didn’t understand that the marketing area needs to get more involved in its data.

When asked to compare the marketing information support at Barrow to other consumer packaged goods firms, one manager said, “Barrow is investing in baseline systems. We need to invest more, but it is hard to justify spending, because of the small number of people. On the same topic, another commented, “More analysis is required at Barrow than at other companies that I’ve worked for. The company is conservative and numbers-oriented and we make frequent comparisons to plan.”

The managers also discussed differences among the brands in information needs. One stated, “Among the brands, less analysis is required for a simpler brand, such as BrandB. With one product, less emphasis is placed on cannibalization and on the impact of dropping a product. Both of those tasks require a great deal of analysis.” Another noted, “The emphasis should be placed on the products that are key to the company’s sales. We need lots of information to track them.”

Sales Organization and Its Use of Data and Analytic Tools

Barrow's sales organization consisted of a Sales Vice President and five Regional Sales Managers (RSMs). The VP Sales directed the RSMs, monitored performance against budget, and developed sales plans. These plans included details on trade promotion activity, information on the timing of marketing support, and shelf-merchandising objectives. He described the task as “taking what we want done and putting it in a format where we can explain it to the brokers.”

The RSMs acted as a conduit, moving information from headquarters to the brokers and vice versa. They spent a great deal of time traveling, usually three 3-day trips per month.

In a typical trip, the RSM visited about 40 stores, accompanied by the broker's account executive responsible for the Barrow account. In each store, the RSM checked which Barrow and competitor items were being stocked. He filled out a form noting their price, location, and number of facings. The information was used by the RSM, but not put into any computer system or formally shared with anyone else. The RSM also met with the account executive and, possibly, with the president of the broker, reviewing sales, distribution, pricing, and promotion issues.

The RSM participated in a 20 minute presentation to the supermarket buyer at the end of the fiscal year. He reviewed Barrow's national sales and its sales in the supermarket. He also discussed Barrow's programs for the coming year.

Much of the analysis done by RSMs was in preparation for their meetings with the account execs. The RSM used sales history data to produce a status report which compared this year's actual sales to both this year's budgeted and last year's actual sales, by brand for the entire region.

The RSM compiled the status report from various MIS reports spending, on average, an afternoon in its preparation.

One of the RSMs described two reports based on data from SAMI that he took on a recent visit. In one, the top products in the category were ranked. In the other, sales trends in the market were noted.

In presenting analyses to supermarket buyers, the RSM stated that he reported numbers to put Barrow in the best light. For example, he would report volume gain for Barrow, if share fell and the category grew. However, under the same circumstances he would report a share loss for a competitor.

The VP Sales utilized both internal and purchased data. He used sales reports to track volume trends and to monitor new item sales. He examined both weekly and monthly sales reports.

The VP Sales used SAMI to monitor Barrow's performance and to communicate to the brokers. For example, he compiled a list of the top 40 category items and sent it to the brokers. He described the list's message as, "Accounts have about 40 items in our category. Since Barrow has 4 items in SAMI's top 40, your accounts should have 4 items in every supermarket." The VP also produced a list of "SAMI losers." These were category items that weren't selling well nationally and, hence, were ones that the brokers can suggest that accounts remove in favor of Barrow's items.

## Evaluation of Sales Information Support

Learning and communication were the key benefits to the sales managers of the available data and analytic tools. The VP Sales commented, "We are managing sales more closely and therefore we need more information. We need to find out what works and communicate it to the field, and what doesn't work and communicate it to marketing. We've learned that programs that put incremental cases in the stores on display work well. Cents off promotions don't work as well.

“Different things work for different accounts. For example, one major account likes half price sales. In a recent program, it purchased ten times normal volume. The same program wouldn't bring a large increase in volume at a different account.

“Sometimes a program surprises me. For example, a recent program at one of our major accounts which combined coupons, TV advertising, and display increased our share in the region by a point and it has held steady at this higher level. In contrast, a half price sale in another region generated a great deal of short term volume increase, but had a very small long term impact. One of the greatest attributes for someone in this business is the ability to bounce a lot of information into your head.”

As with the marketing managers, the sales managers focused on the problems caused by poor information support. One noted that lack of data led to lack of knowledge about the environment. He said, “If we had better knowledge of where we stood and of the conditions in the marketplace, we should have better results. We might more quickly know when we have problems. We might know more about our marketing condition and we could challenge ourselves more. For example, if we thought we had 90% distribution and an accurate monthly survey tells us we are at 78%, we could say, ‘let’s get on the case of the people in charge.’”

One RSM felt that the available data and analytic tools made it more difficult for him to provide brokers with the information they need to better sell Barrow products. He commented, "Now it takes me six hours to complete a report ranking items by sales per point of distribution. If I had a more powerful tool, I could produce the report in 5 minutes. The report could be one of the strongest sales tools we use. Overall more ranking reports at a more detailed level (for example, by market rather than nationally) would be helpful. In addition, I should be able to get SAMI data laid out in a neater format for presentation to brokers. We now have enough information, but it isn't in the appropriate format."

The sales managers also commented on the impact of their lack of knowledge on Barrow's competitive position. One noted, “We are at an advantage with respect to our competitors, because we are more concerned about our category. The larger competitors probably don't spend the amount of time analyzing the category that we do. We are at a disadvantage with the trade when examining detailed sales data. We can, for example, say we are the tenth best selling item in a given market, but the buyer can say, ‘you're not tenth best in my account’ and we have no way of checking this.” Another added, “We are terribly unsophisticated. We just don't have the resources that larger companies have. It's hard to say whether our lack of sophistication is hurting our sales, but it probably is."

The managers focused on the time it took to assemble data and the quality of the available data, especially with respect to data needed for field trips. One RSM stated, “I should be able to request a status report for a given market, and get it. Now I generate it myself. The computer could generate the report faster and it would be more accurate than my manual report. The computer-generated report, for example, could have more up-to-date distribution information.”

The VP Sales concurred, noting that if he could assemble these reports, he could better monitor the relationship between RSMs and brokers. He said, “I would like to have the information needed for a RSM broker meeting on the PC. I’d like to be able to run Dallas on a Friday before the RSM’s trip. I’d like the status of volume versus budget and versus last year, and current distribution status – any changes in last 90 days. There would be no question about what the RSM should do in the meeting.”

When asked why there was less sophistication at Barrow, the VP Sales noted, “We run a lean ship with respect to overhead. In the past, the business was easy to manage. There were very few items and there were just 3 sales people. They didn’t have time to worry about information. Now, however, there are 6 people and we can go back to market more often. We have more time to get in more depth and we need more data.”

## Discussion

In discussing the case study, we will address the two questions presented in the introduction: how are sales and marketing managers at Barrow using information technology in their day-to-day work and how can we effectively provide data and analytic tools to these managers? In answering the first question, we will also examine the impact of information support on the managers' work. In answering the second, we will make specific recommendations for Barrow's management to improve its information support environment.

## Impact of Information Support

The managers identified three ways in which they used data and analytic tools: to learn about their environment, to communicate more effectively both within and outside Barrow, and to tighten management control.

To learn about their and competitors products was the use of data and analytic tools most frequently mentioned by managers. Brand managers wanted to learn how effective their marketing programs were, how successful a new product introduction was and how greatly it reduced sales of existing products, how well competitors products were doing, how well their marketing programs were doing, and in which regions their and their competitors products were doing well. RSMs also wanted to learn which programs were successful and which weren't. Their focus, however, was more at the region and account level than the brand managers.

The answers to one question might lead to asking several others. For example, upon learning that his product was not selling as well as he had planned, the BrandB manager wanted to know why. He then performed an analysis by region and identified lack of distribution as the problem.

Learning, at times, had a direct impact upon both managers decisions and their effectiveness. For example, the sales per point of distribution analysis carried out for BrandB provided valuable information on sales performance. This information led to specific actions, including targeting key accounts for special attention. The actions, in turn, led to measurable results; 40% of the targeted accounts added BrandB to their shelves.

In other instances, the increased knowledge did not have any immediate impact. For example, data about the success of a promotion might not be used until the next planning cycle. This is consistent with the observations of Feldman and March who noted that, “most organizations and individuals collect more information than they can use in the making of decisions,” and that, “the link between decisions and information [is] weak,” [4, p. 174]. Weiss [15] observed that information from social science research is often not directly used by practitioners in their decision making. Rather, research provided practitioners with knowledge which later influenced decisions.

The brand managers and RSMs also noted that without data they could not learn things about their environment, which they wanted to know and which could affect both the decisions they made and Barrow's performance. The brand managers stated that they wanted to learn more about competitor's programs and their impact in different regions. Getting this data could lead to both changes in how brand managers react to competitor's actions and to how they design their own programs. RSMs observed that lack of accurate and timely distribution data hampered their ability to quickly identify problems at the regional and account level.

The importance of learning has been overlooked by many previous information systems researchers. Researchers in other areas, however, have discussed the benefits of learning and increasing the organization's knowledge. Weitz, Sujan, and Sujan [16] proposed that greater knowledge of sales situations and strategies will lead to more effective selling. In a similar vein, Szymanski [14] hypothesized that the number and types of categories used by sales people to classify prospects, customers, presentations, and closings will affect sales performance. Our findings fit well with Daft and Weick's [3] view of organizations as systems that gather and interpret information to learn about the relationship between actions and their outcomes. Jaikumar and Bohn [6] discussed the importance of an intelligent system, combining information technology and people, for improving the effectiveness of manufacturing.

Among sales managers, data and analytic tools also played an important communication function. RSMs used sales and SAMI data to communicate performance successes and failures to brokers. They also used data to convince accounts to stock a specific item or to participate in a particular promotion. They also noted that lack of data and poor analytic tools made it difficult to develop effective presentations and hence to communicate Barrow's programs to brokers. We would expect sales managers to use data to improve communication, since they viewed their role as one of communicators – taking information from brokers to headquarters, and from headquarters to brokers.

There were two ways in which the available data and analytic tools were used for management control. First, RSMs and the VP sales used information to monitor the performance of brokers. Second, brand managers used data and analytic tools to follow the sales of their products. In both cases, managers could adjust their actions to meet budgeted goals. Bruns and McFarlan [1] noted that computer systems can play an important role in providing information used in management control.

## Recommendations for Barrow

To increase the effectiveness of its information support, Barrow must move away from its reliance on reports that either are purchased or are by-products of internal transaction processing systems. It must begin to view providing information support to sales and marketing managers as a critical task. With respect to data and analytic tools, Barrow must provide an integrated and flexible system so that the managers can fully explore the impact of marketing actions. With respect to assistance provided to sales and marketing, Barrow must increase the resources it expends on marketing information support and view this task as an ongoing, evolving one.

Barrow must develop a system that integrates internal and external data and must provide its managers with flexible and easy-to-use analytic tools. Integrated data will allow managers, for example, to examine the impact of promotions (internal) on both sales (internal) and market share (external), or the impact of a competitor's price change (external) on sales at specific accounts (internal). Barrow must also formally capture the data collected by RSMs in their field trips. This valuable data on product distribution, facings, and pricing should be available to all sales, marketing, and senior management.

Spreadsheet software on a personal computer is a viable choice as a primary analytic tool. This software is flexible and easy to use. Barrow could develop a series of spreadsheets from an overview of performance at the national level to separate spreadsheets for each broker territory with data at the account level. Spreadsheets containing promotion data could also be developed. In addition to the spreadsheets themselves, macros could be created to perform standard analyses, such as monthly reports against budget or regional status reports.

Managers could then trace problems from the national level down to the account level to learn about the impact of their and competitors' actions. This would eliminate some of the frustration stemming from getting data in the wrong format for the type of analysis they want to perform. With the data in a series of spreadsheets, the managers could easily manipulate it and create new reports, reducing the burden faced by the MIS manager to produce reports for marketing and sales.

There are some differences in the data and analytic tool needs for brand and regional sales managers. Brand managers need more data and more powerful tools than RSMs. Since, they have some PC experience, the tools do not have to be as easy to use. The ability to explore the data and track problems and their impact in a variety of ways is critical to their task of designing marketing programs. They want to figure out what works and what doesn't in the marketing of Barrow's products. Further, promotion data and tools to analyze promotions at both national and regional levels are critical to brand managers.

RSMs will benefit initially from more structured and easy-to-use tools. They prefer to be able to 'press a button' and get a regional status report for an upcoming field trip. Accessing these reports quickly is a critical requirement of the RSMs. Hence, they will benefit more from a smaller set of data and a series of macros to carry out simple, repetitive analyses. They also place more emphasis on communication and hence would benefit from a tool that can produce presentation graphics.

The needs of both groups will evolve. Once they become familiar with a set of data or a tool, they will realize that a new capability will provide them with additional valuable insights. This will be especially true of the RSMs, since their knowledge of information technology is relatively weak. Barrow, therefore, must set up an ongoing information support capability to meet both current and future needs.

Given its small size and limited budget, it is not feasible for Barrow to hire a marketing information manager or establish a marketing information support organization. Barrow must, therefore, meet its needs with existing staff. Both the IS manager and the marketing and sales managers must play critical roles.

The IS manager and the IS organization at Barrow would be characterized as Era I by Cash, McFarlan, McKenney, and Vitale [2]. Its focus is on developing transaction processing systems to improve organizational efficiency. It must move to Era II, where more emphasis is placed on providing managers with the data and tools needed to improve their effectiveness.

While it is not technically difficult to take data from the Barrow minicomputer and move it to managers' personal computers it must be made an important task for MIS. The MIS manager must familiarize himself with marketing data and how it is organized and with tools that managers could use to analyze the data. In addition, he must work with vendors and marketing managers to obtain external data in the appropriate format so that it may be integrated with internal data.

The marketing and sales organizations should each appoint a manager to act as a liaison with MIS. These managers, as part of their responsibilities, should identify marketing information needs. These managers, along with the MIS manager, should form a task force that meets on a regular basis to discuss ongoing marketing needs and review potential hardware and software solutions. This task force should also meet regularly with the President, VP Sales, VP Marketing, and VP Finance. Senior management approval is essential for obtaining the financing needed to purchase hardware, software, and data. In addition, senior management commitment is needed to encourage marketing and sales managers to use available data and tools.

Barrow need not be at the leading edge with respect to data and analytic tools. This would require too much money and would move critical people away from managing sales and marketing. They must, however, expend more effort in this area than they currently are.

Barrow is at a turning point. It has not invested in sophisticated information support tools, limiting the amount of analysis that the company can carry out. If nothing is done, this problem will grow as the importance of data and analytic tools grows. Barrow could find itself at a significant competitive disadvantage, if its buyers and competitors know more about its products than its own managers do. Competitors could, for example, adjust marketing programs in a more effective and timely manner leaving Barrow perpetually one step behind.

Barrow is a small company in an industry dominated by larger ones. If it invests in information support, it can obtain a significant competitive advantage. Barrow already has a great deal of qualitative data on its products, markets, and competitors, which it obtains from interactions with brokers and buyers. This data can be expanded by adding detailed quantitative knowledge obtained through analyzing available data.

In summary, despite a lack of information support, data and analytic tools play a crucial role at Barrow in three areas: increasing knowledge, improved communication, and tighter management control. There is, however, a mismatch between the demand for information by members of the sales and marketing organization, which is relatively high, and the supply of information and analytic tools, which is lower. This mismatch is leading to frustration on the part of some managers, and possibly to missed sales and marketing opportunities.

## Appendix 1

Guidelines for Barrow Sales and Product Manager Interviews

1. Introduce self and project stressing confidentiality of the study

2. Tell me a little about your education and work background – before Barrow and now

3. Tell me about the product you are responsible for [for product managers only].

a. Is the category growing/shrinking and at what rate?

b. Who are the main competitors? What is their share? What are they doing?

c. How is your performance measured (e.g., volume, share, profit)

4. Tell me about the sales region you are responsible for [for RSMs only].

a. Who are the principal buyers?

b. Who are the main competitors? What is their share? What are they doing?

c. How is your performance measured (e.g., volume, share, profit)

5. What are the most important tasks that you perform? For each task:

a. Describe it.

b. What information do you use in carrying out the task?

c. Where does the information come from? Probe to find out what information is coming from people and what is coming from systems.

d. What analytic tools do you use (if any) in performing the task? What tools are used by others to provide you with the information that you use?

e. What is the impact of having this information on the performance of the task? How would you do the task differently with less or more information?

f. What is the most important information that you use for each task?

6. Are there other ways that you use information or analytic tools in your work? Gather information on the use of personal computers, PC software, mainframe computers and software, electronic mail, etc..

7. Is there other information that you receive from others that you use in your work?

8. What factors influence your use of IT?

a. What aspects of the company influence your use of IT? What aspects of your division (or business unit)? What aspects of your product? What aspects of yourself?

9. What, if any, changes have occurred in your work due to the increased availability of data and analytic tools?

10. Has the availability of data and analytic tools had any measurable affect on your performance? Has it led to increased market share or sales volume, or to reduced costs?

11. What, if any, changes do you expect to occur in the next few years?
