# Reorganization of the work system for successful information systems implementation

A case study

Kailash Joshi

School of Business Administration, University of Missouri-St. Louis, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

This paper presents a case study of two attempts to implement a computer based materials management system in a large engineering company. It presents the details of the failure of the first attempt, and an analysis of the problems encountered in the first attempt. The details of the corrective action undertaken, and the successful second attempt are also presented. The analysis of the two implementation attempts suggests the important role of personnel with functional expertise and cross-functional business perspective on design teams. This should facilitate the integrated work-information system design stressed in this paper. The case study illustrates the nature of interrelationships between the work system and the information system and the dependence of the information system on the work system for successful operation. Thus, in some instances the need for reorganization of the work system for successful implementation is highlighted. The analysis also identifies the role of top management in creating the necessary organizational structures for involving users and assigning the responsibility for the success of the implementation to them. The paper also makes recommendations for the development of systems designers through appropriate recruitment, training, and job rotation programs. The paper concludes with a recommendation that systems designers should focus on the integrated work-information system design, rather than limiting the boundary of the systems design to the information processing system and information technology related tasks.

Keywords: Implementation, Implementation Failure, Systems Design, Integrated Work-information System Design, User Involvement, Top Management Involvement, Purchasing and Materials Management Systems.

## Introduction

Information systems technology has been identified as the most promising vehicle for improving productivity in organizations. Almost all organizations have embraced this technology in an effort to increase their productivity and competitive position in the marketplace. However, the results of MIS implementation have been mixed. A review of MIS literature suggests that there have been many failures in the implementation of information systems (Kumar and Welke, 1984).

Implementation of management information systems (MIS) has been a major area of inquiry for MIS researchers. Researchers have examined implementation problems and failures, and have assessed the reasons for these problems and failures from different perspectives. Different user groups use their power and influence to control information resources (Danziger, Dutton, Kling, and Kraemer, 1982; Kling, 1980; Pfeffer, 1981), to obtain greater share of computing resources (Danziger et al., 1982; Kling, 1980; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Markus, 1981; Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975), and to achieve preferred task allocation (Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975).

Other researchers have viewed implementation as a sequential process. Various antecedent factors at all levels of analysis – individual, group, and organization – and the quality of the implementation process have been examined as determinants of success or failure of implementation (Lucas, 1978; Ginzberg, 1980). Factors considered by these researchers include: quality of systems, user attitudes and perceptions, user decision style, management support, situational and personal factors (Lucas, 1978), time spent in the analysis phase (Mckeen, 1983), impact on users' work lives (Nichols, 1981), user involvement in development effort (Ives and Olson, 1984), management of the process of change (Ginzberg, 1979), prior user expectations (Ginzberg, 1981), and the credibility of the MIS department (Doll and Ahmed, 1983).

MIS researchers have generally identified the primacy of behavioral problems in the implementation of information systems. Lucas (1976) reported the results of his empirical investigation covering 2000 users in 16 organizations by stating, "It is our contention that the major reasons most information systems have failed is that we have ignored organizational behavior problems in the design and operation of information systems" (page 6).

Systems may also not work optimally due to technical problems in areas such as programming, database or file design, hardware and software selection, and telecommunication links. Failure to identify user requirements and poor design of reports, screens, and input procedures can also result in implementation problems and failure. Some of these problems have also been attributed to users' failure to conceptualize, clearly articulate and communicate their own requirements (Dickson and Wetherbe, 1985). Some researchers have viewed these problems as a communication gap between users and systems designers (Guinen and Bostrom, 1986).

While MIS researchers has focused on the behavioral and other factors identified in the preceding discussion, little attention has been paid to work system design prior to or during implementation as a contributor to successful implementation. Researchers with a Socio-Technical systems (STS) perspective have acknowledged the strong interrelationship between the design of work systems and the design of information systems (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977a, 1977b). However, the STS researchers' stress appears to be on the impact of MIS design on the work environment of users, rather than on the design of the work environment itself as a prerequisite to successful implementation.

Many information systems designers take a very narrow view of design activity and define the boundary of the system being designed to include only data collection, storage, processing, and output generation activities. This means the design effort concentrates on information technology related tasks, such as design of outputs, inputs, forms, files, programs, and procedures for data collection. However, the organization of the existing work system for which the information system is being designed, is overlooked in the design process. Thus, structural weaknesses and problems in the existing work system are not addressed. These weaknesses may cause implementation of the new system to fail even if there are no other technical, behavioral, or information requirements identification problems associated with the new system. The structural weaknesses of the pre-implementation work system may limit the accuracy of the data being collected, which may ultimately undermine the usefulness of computer-based systems that rely on inaccurate data. Unless the work systems are redesigned with the objective of ensuring suitable discipline, procedures, and division of responsibilities to obtain accurate data, it may not be possible to implement computer-based systems successfully.

This paper presents a case study of an implementation failure. It identifies the importance of structural reorganization of the work system during implementation of MIS, and the need for system designers to focus on the total work-information integrated system design rather than just the information processing system design. The importance of availability of functional expertise in systems design teams, user responsibility, and top management's role in creating appropriate organizational structures for design teams is also highlighted.

The case describes the introduction of computer-based systems for materials management (which includes purchasing, materials planning and inventory control, and warehouse operations) in India. The case presents the details of the first implementation attempt which was unsuccessful. The problems identified in the first implementation and the corrective action taken during the second implementation are discussed. The next section presents the case study and describes the two implementation attempts. The following section presents an analysis of the two implementation attempts. The final section presents the conclusions of the study and makes some recommendations for future implementations.

## Case Study: XYZ Engineering Company

XYZ Engineering Company is a large diversified, professionally managed, and privately owned company in India. It manufactures a range of automobile and general engineering products. As part of its expansion plans, it acquired an existing company that manufactured general purpose machine tools. After the take over, the quantity and variety of machine tool production rapidly increased to include special purpose machines and sophisticated transfer lines for mass production of automobile components. At this plant site, the company also located its new automobile divisions and a new research and development center.

The head of machine tool operations controlled machine tool design and production, and reported to the Chief Executive Officer at the plant. The automobile production divisions and other functions such as administration, accounting, human resources, MIS, materials management, plant engineering, quality control, and research and development also reported to the Chief Executive Officer at the plant. The marketing, finance, and legal functions were located at the Head Office, about 150 miles away.

The Management Information Systems Department had a staff of about twenty-five in the systems design and programming area (excluding data entry and computer operations personnel). Most of the systems designers, programmers, and managers had computer science or technical backgrounds. The initial thrust of systems development had been in the area of accounting and payroll applications. Payroll processing, budgeting, costing, assets accounting, and some general ledger and cash accounting applications had been implemented successfully. Sufficient computing capacity was available to take up additional batch processing applications in areas such as accounts payable, materials management, production planning and control, and manufacturing cost control. The available computing equipment limited the development of systems to batch type systems only.

The materials management function, headed by a manager, was responsible for purchasing, material planning and inventory control, and warehouse operations. With the rapid expansion in machine tool manufacturing, a number of problems surfaced in the materials management area. These included nonavailability of materials in time for production, high inventory levels, and a rapid increase of staff in all of these areas. The increase in staff did not result in any significant improvement in the operations. These problems also resulted in a rapid turnover of managers in the materials management function. It was against this background that top management decided to bring in professional management and introduce information systems in the materials management area. The objective was to improve productivity by streamlining purchasing, materials planning and inventory control, and warehouse operations. The next section describes the first attempt at introducing information systems for materials management.

## First Implementation Attempt

The new management in the materials area decided to use computer-based information systems to keep track of materials requirements, stock levels, and purchase order schedules to maintain better control over inventories and materials availability. Materials accounting was already being carried out on a batch processing type transaction processing system. Whenever a material was received a goods-inward-note document was prepared, which was later certified by the quality control staff for accepted quantity. Similarly, for each withdrawal of materials from the warehouse, a material-issue-note was prepared. Goods-inward notes (GIN) and material issue notes (MIN) were batched up in the warehouse and sent for processing on a daily basis under a batch control sheet to update materials accounting master files. The master file was updated once a week. Up to this point, the materials accounting data was being used only by the accounting department for semi-annual inventory valuation. The materials area made no use of computer-based materials accounting data. It relied on a manual Kardex system, where receipt and issue data were posted on individual cards for each item maintained in the warehouse. Two sets of Kardex records were maintained, one in the warehouse and the other in the materials planning and inventory control section.

Information systems designers developed a materials planning and inventory control system. This system monitored stock levels of regularly consumed items, and triggered an order whenever the stock levels fell below reorder levels. The order size was based on the economic order quantity ordered. Past consumption was analyzed for all commonly consumed items and an A, B, C classification was carried out. Reorder levels, minimum safety levels, and economic order quantities were established for each class of items. Some items needed special treatment and their reorder levels and order qualities were adjusted accordingly. In addition to the commonly consumed items, the system also facilitated materials planning and procurement of special items that were machine specific. The system included provisions for consolidating parts lists for machine tool work orders, and matching with current stock levels and quantity on order to determine the additional quantities needed for the special items. The system was also designed to keep track of purchase orders and the receipt of materials against each order. The reports also permitted materials planners to review the overall status of an item, and highlighted critical items and excess inventory items for necessary action. The format and information content of the reports were developed in consultation with the materials management function and approved by them.

The new system was introduced with great enthusiasm on the part of materials management and the MIS department. However, a serious problem arose soon after the new system produced its initial reports. Most of the stock balances reported by MIS differed from physical quantities in the warehouse. In addition, many items had either no descriptions or incomplete descriptions in the computer records. It was also found that a large number of transaction errors had not been corrected. Even after correcting and updating the transaction errors, there was no appreciable improvement in the situation. The new system was not used, and the materials management function concentrated on improving its manual systems to meet production requirements.

Systems designers in the MIS function took the stand that there was nothing wrong with their system. Systems designers expressed the view that if the warehouse personnel did not make so many mistakes in the data being sent for processing and would correct the error reports in a timely manner, the new system could work effectively. The system designers put the responsibility for the failure of implementation on the materials management function, maintaining that the information system designed by them had worked effectively. Systems designers discussed the problems with warehouse personnel and their response was to improve the layout of error reports, provide more descriptive information on error reports, and provide additional information such as batch control numbers to facilitate the error correction process.

Meanwhile, some adverse reports by the internal and external auditors drew the attention of head office management to the problems being experienced in the materials accounting system. A special team was set up headed by a management graduate with the authority to completely reorganize the entire system. The team's objective was to successfully implement a computer-based materials management and materials accounting system.

The next section describes the state of the materials accounting system after the implementation attempt described above, and the steps taken to overcome some of the problems identified in the total system.

## An Assessment of the Materials Accounting System

The organization chart in Figure 1 describes the functional division of responsibilities in the warehouse. Warehouse staff was organized into a receiving section, binning section, materials issue section, Kardex posting section, and error correction section. The receiving section was responsible for receiving and unloading materials supplied by vendors and checking them against authorized purchase orders. It was also responsible for preparing GIN (goods-inward-notes) and offering the material for inspection to the quality control staff. After inspection, the accepted materials were handed over to the binning section staff for storage in the warehouse. The issue section was responsible for picking up materials from the warehouse against MIN (material-issue-notes) and handing over the materials to the representatives of the requisitioning departments. GIN and MIN documents were forwarded to the Kardex section, where they were posted on individual cards maintained for each item stocked in the warehouse. After Kardex posting, separate copies of the documents were forwarded to the MIS data entry section and the materials planning Kardex section for updating.

Over time, the number of transactions and errors in transactions had become so large that it was not possible for the existing staff in the Kardex section to handle the regular posting of documents as well as error correction. In addition, the existing Kardex posting staff found it difficult to understand the error reports and error correction procedures. Therefore, a new error correction cell was created by recruiting additional staff.

With the expansion of machine tool product range and output, the number of items in the warehouse had exceeded 20,000. A vast variety of electrical, electronic, hydraulic, pneumatic, and hardware items had been added to the materials in the warehouse, creating an acute shortage of space. This resulted in a number of problems in warehouse operations that seriously affected the materials accounting process. The problems that were identified and the action taken to overcome them are summarized here.

## 1. Materials Part Numbering Problems

Before the machine tool plant was acquired by XYZ Engineering, it was using a seven-digit part numbering system, borrowed from its British collaborator. In addition, the previous company had introduced its own nine-digit item numbering system for some new items which were not covered in the British collaborator's coding scheme.

After the Machine Tool company was acquired by XYZ Engineering, XYZ introduced its own 11-digit significant part numbering scheme, which was developed for materials used in automobile production. The 11-digit part numbering scheme was based on a systematic classification and grouping of items and was generally limited to hardware, bearings, raw materials, and consumable items used in automobile production. The introduction of the 11-digit part numbering scheme resulted in the existence of seven, nine, and eleven digit part numbers for the same category of items. As the number of new items increased rapidly, an eleven-digit temporary part numbering scheme was also introduced to provide temporary part numbers for hundreds of new items, so that they could be purchased and recorded in the materials accounting system. The temporary part numbers were just serial numbers with the number 9 as a first digit to identify them as temporary part numbers. The temporary serial part numbers were to be replaced with permanent significant part numbers in due course.

Eventually over 4000 items were assigned temporary part numbers. However, efforts to study and classify these items were very slow. Many part number requests contained incomplete descriptions, yet a number had to be assigned due to pressure to meet production requirements. These problems with temporary part numbers resulted in a new temporary part number being assigned to the same item every time a request for part numbers was made to the Standardization Department by either the Design or Purchasing Department. The regular 11-digit significant part numbering scheme was developed by the Standardization Department, which reported to the head of the research and development function. The Standardization Department was responsible for company wide standardization of all materials, drawings, and engineering specifications. The main unit of the standardization department was located at another plant about 800 miles away. At the machine tool plant, the Standardization Department was represented by a small cell of junior level staff who were unable to cope with the rapidly increasing work load and had yet to fully study and classify the new groups of items.

For the warehouse personnel, these part numbering problems meant that they could not use part numbers to organize the materials or the Kardex cards. Almost all the material issue requests also did not contain any part numbers. Warehouse operations were mainly conducted on the basis of descriptions. However, because of the absence of any technically qualified staff in the warehouse, these part numbering problems spilled into nearly every aspect of the warehouse operations.

## 2. Materials Description Problems

The materials accounting system had a 40-character materials description field. As there was no special procedure for handling new items, the goods-inward-notes received from the warehouse were used to update the description field. However, only the first 40 characters could be key punched, while many items had a full paragraph description. The goods-inward-note description itself was condensed from purchase order descriptions or supplier descriptions. This meant that most of the descriptions on the computer files were incomplete. In the absence of proper auditing and controls, warehouse personnel made short-cuts of their own while transcribing descriptions. Therefore, the descriptions on the Kardex cards were inadequate in many cases. For example, on some cards electric motors were described as electric motor 1 HP. There were at least 10 types of motors that could fit this description. Important specifications of speed, voltage, current (AC or DC), mounting (foot or flange), insulation class, continuous or intermittent rating, etc., were left out.

As stated earlier, in the absence of a significant and reliable part numbering scheme, descriptions had become the main identification method and almost all warehouse operations were organized around descriptions. However, for the generally non-technical staff of the warehouse, the reliance on incomplete and poorly understood descriptions created severe problems in the storage, issue, and accounting operations as described below.

## 3. Problems in Materials Storage and Issue

Over a period of time the number of items and variety of items had increased tremendously. Because of this the current storage arrangement of items became completely inadequate. As explained earlier, part numbers were of no help in organizing the items. As the congestion increased, the items were put into bins wherever space permitted. The binning section staff wrote inadequate descriptions on identification tags and attached them to items with rubber bands. Most of these tags came off over a period of time. This also resulted in a mixing of different items (with similar appearance) in one bin in many cases. When staff from production, maintenance, or other departments came to the warehouse, they did not know the part numbers for the items they wanted. They came with their own descriptions; but with the inadequate space, poor physical organization of items, and the lack of a location numbering system in the storage area, warehouse personnel could not locate the needed items. Most of the time, personnel from the Purchasing, Materials Planning, and Production Departments had to personally search the warehouse for the items they needed. Sometimes they inadvertently removed existing identification tags. When the items were found, they took them, leaving the materials issue clerk a material-issue-note without a part number or complete description. In view of their own weaknesses and the powerful position of other departments, there was little that warehouse personnel could do. In some cases, senior production supervisors or managers would walk away with materials without any documentation.

## 4. Problems in the Materials Receipt Section

Some urgent items were dispatched by the vendors on a verbal request from the Purchasing Department. No purchase order was issued and no purchase order number was assigned. Some items were ordered without any part numbers. The goods-inward-notes prepared for these items could not be sent for processing without part numbers or purchase order numbers. Some of these documents were kept in desk drawers, and ultimately, some of them were lost.

## 5. Problems in Units of Quantity

Many items were received in one unit of quantity and issued in another unit of quantity. For example, steel products were received in tons or kilograms, but these were issued in meters or millimeters. Liquids were received in liters or kilograms, but issued in numbers of bottles. For example, acids were ordered and received in liters. However, different suppliers packaged them in different size bottles, e.g., 1 liter, 2 liter, or 5 liter. When the acids were issued, the issue personnel recorded only the number of bottles issued. Sometimes, the Kardex posting clerks updated such quantities without regard to their units. Another similar case was that of electrodes, which were received in kilograms but issued in numbers. Some of the issuing staff did not even know that some of these items were being received and accounted for in different units.

## 6. Problems in Manual Kardex Record Keeping

The problems in the materials part numbering scheme, descriptions, units of quantity, and material issue had a cumulative impact on the manual Kardex sections' record keeping. The first problem was the organization and sequencing of cards. It was clear that cards could not be arranged by the part numbers, for it would have been nearly impossible to locate a specific card in the absence of the appropriate part number, which was generally not available. Therefore, the Kardex cards were arranged by descriptions. However, the descriptions were either incomplete or non-standardized, making it impractical to arrange the cards alphabetically. For example, most items could be described in alternate ways such as Hex Bolt or Bolt Hex. There were also multiple names for the same item, e.g., Allen head screw or socket head cap screw. Another example of multiple descriptions would be brake motor, electric motor brake type, motor electric brake type, brake type electric motor. The usual tendency was to prefix the type of material first, which meant that similar items could fall under different alphabets, e.g., hex bolt, square bolt, round bolt, hex screw, socket head screw, counter-sunk screw, etc. Therefore, the cards were arranged in general groupings without regard to part numbers or first letter of description (alphabetic order). However, many new electronic, electric, hydraulic, and hardware items posed challenges to the non-technical Kardex clerks and had no proper sequence or location. When the cards as well as the material issue and receipt documents contained incomplete descriptions, it was difficult to find the right card and post the receipt and issue documents correctly. The Kardex clerks were under great pressure to post more than 300 transactions each day and made many wrong postings under these circumstances. They were also required to fill in the part numbers on these documents, so that they could be forwarded to the MIS Department for processing.

## 7. Problems of Variety of Items. Non-moving and Obsolete Items

In the absence of pressure for standardization, different design groups were free to specify any materials for the equipment designed by them. Other departments were also free to order any material through the Purchasing Department. Over a period of time this resulted in a very large variety of items being stocked in the warehouse. For example, there were eleven different shades and types of gray paint. In hardware, almost every shape and size of bolts and screws were stocked, such as hex screw 8 mm (diameter) in more than fourteen lengths (8 × 10, 8 × 12, 8 × 15, 8 × 18,

$8 \times 20, 8 \times 22, 8 \times 25, 8 \times 28, 8 \times 30, 8 \times 32, 8 \times 35, 8 \times 40, 8 \times 45, 8 \times 50, \text{etc.}$ . Similarly, many sizes and types of raw materials were stocked; for example, steel angles of almost every size (15 mm × 15 mm, $18 \times 18, 20 \times 20, 22 \times 22, 25 \times 25, 30 \times 30, 35 \times 35, 40 \times 40, 45 \times 45, 50 \times 50, \text{etc.}$ ) in different thicknesses and specifications.

Many items were ordered by users on a one-time basis, however, when the materials arrived, no effort was made to issue the items to their respective users. Many users did not pick-up these items due to lack of information or failure to locate them once they had been put in bins.

Still other items had been ordered for old machines whose production had been discontinued or whose designs had been modified. These items continued to occupy space in the warehouse. Many currently used items had lost their identification tags and remained in the warehouse unidentified. Countless spare parts and maintenance items were lost in the warehouse and were reordered whenever breakdowns occurred.

## 8. Problems in Stock Verification

All these problems had a cumulative effect on the annual stock verification. There were many discrepancies between the physical stocks, the Kardex record, and computer-based materials accounting data. Many similar looking items were mixed up in the warehouse. While many items could not be found, many unidentified items could not be linked to any Kardex card or materials accounting part number.

Whenever discrepancies were found between physical stocks and Kardex records or computer-based data, it was impossible to assign responsibility to any one person or section. These discrepancies could occur due to nondocumentation, incomplete or incorrect part numbers and descriptions, mixing of similar materials, wrong posting on Kardex cards, inability to locate materials in the warehouse, etc.

After identifying these problems, it was clear that a materials accounting and materials management' system could not be successfully implemented without a substantial redesign effort in the warehouse. Procedures, controls, division of tasks, and assignment of responsibilities needed to be redesigned to ensure that accurate data can be collected. Part numbers and descriptions needed to be standardized. The work organization needed to be redesigned so that accountability could be assigned to a specific group which would have full control over a limited area of operations. The next section describes the steps taken to redesign the warehouse systems as a first step in the implementation of a computer-based materials accounting system.

## Redesign of the Warehouse System

After a thorough investigation of all the underlying problems in the current warehouse system, the following steps were taken to prepare for the introduction of the computer-based materials management system.

1. The first step was to launch a crash program in the standardization department to study all the new items in the electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, electronic, bearings, and hardware categories and develop the significant 11-digit code numbering schemes for them. Immediate steps were taken to increase the staff in the standardization department to cope with the additional workload. Booklets of code numbers and standard descriptions were printed and widely circulated for each major group of items. All design releases and purchase orders were required to contain the significant 11-digit part numbers. The practice of assigning temporary part numbers was discontinued.

2. A new procedure was devised under which the descriptions and part numbers of all new items were provided by the standardization department for both computer files and warehouse records. A data control department was established in the warehouse to audit and certify every new Kardex card created in the warehouse for part number, standard description, unit of quantity, and location of material.

3. In order to reduce congestion in the warehouse, maintenance items, spare parts, and accessories were removed to a separate warehouse area directly under the control of the Plant Maintenance Department. A team of technical personnel attempted to identify and tag all the remaining items with standard descriptions and 11-digit code numbers. Nearly 500 items that could not be identified were removed to a separate room in the design office area, where technical staff and machine tool designers attempted to identify them.

![](/api/attachments/QB2NDF7Q/fulltext/images/20b36911e87639a9e628a6222acaf983abab565a0b18d51f3341c8fb2366a86c.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Warehouse Organizational Chart after Reorganization.

About 200 items were no longer required for any current or future product, and were disposed of.

All the remaining materials in the warehouse were reorganized into different groups; hardware and bearings, electrical and electronic, hydraulic and pneumatic, and consumable and miscellaneous items. Steel, castings and forgings were already stored in an outside area. Within each group, items were organized on the warehouse shelves according to the 11-digit significant part numbering scheme. Similar items that were formerly mixed, were segregated. Each bin and a sample part were tagged with a correct part number, standard description, unit of quantity, and location. The tags used for this purpose had steel eyelets and were tied to the bins with steel wire. All new items were to be tagged the same way. In the materials storage area sufficient space was left after every five bins in each category to accommodate new items.

4. All warehouse operations were reorganized around materials groups with the exception of the receiving section and the data control section (see Figure 2). Each material group was responsible for binning, issue, Kardex posting, and correction of error reports for the assigned group of items. The staff member who binned or issued any material was also required to immediately update the Kardex record. The function of the data control section was to coordinate all data flow from the warehouse to the MIS data entry section. They were to coordinate error correction and monitor the discipline of each section in maintaining the new system and its procedures. They were also to act as a resource in helping the materials groups in solving problems such as obtaining code numbers, standard descriptions, identifying items, and suggesting warehouse locations for new items.

5. The Kardex records for each material group were moved from the office area to the entrance of the respective storage area. The part numbers, descriptions, and locations were corrected on all Kardex cards. The cards were also organized according to the 11-digit significant part numbering scheme.

6. A special stock verification audit was conducted to count all the items and update Kardex cards with correct quantities. Descriptions, locations, and quantities were also updated on computer files during this process. Procedures were established for transferring the stocks under new part numbers and deleting obsolete part numbers from the active file to a separate file. From this point onwards, each materials group was held fully accountable for any discrepancy between physical stock, Kardex, and computer records. This was feasible because each group had full control over the warehouse operations for the group of items handled by them.

Instead of annual stock verification, a perpetual stock audit system was introduced. Under this system all A-items, B-items, and C-items were to be audited at least three, two, and one time(s) per year, respectively.

7. All the GIN forms were pre-numbered so that any missing document number could be identified. The receiving department was also requested to withhold material-issue-notes for any urgently needed material issued from the receiving section to avoid the problem of a negative balance.

8. Personnel from other departments who came to the warehouse were not permitted to enter the storage area. They were required to fill in materials-issue-note(s) with correct part numbers and standard descriptions of required item(s). For this purpose each department was provided with standard code number manuals. In addition, the data control section was required to assist them in this task. A security guard was posted at the gate to ensure that no material left the warehouse without proper documentation (MIN). The guard was also instructed to count some items on a spot check basis to ensure that the quantity being carried out was in agreement with the documents accompanying it.

9. The next phase involved reducing the variety of items stocked in the warehouse. For each group of items, a list of standard items was prepared with a view to reducing variety. These lists were circulated to all the concerned departments, and after some consultation and negotiations, lists of standard items were finalized and circulated. For example, 8 mm hex screws were stocked in more than fourteen different lengths. After standardization only seven varieties were stocked. Out of eight shades of gray paint only one type was stocked. Upper level management approval was needed if any nonstandard item was to be procured. All specially requested and ordered non-production one-time items were required to be directly issued from the warehouse receipt section itself. However, both receipt and issue documents were processed together to maintain a record.

All non-moving, slow moving, and obsolete items were identified through a major exercise involving personnel from the Machine Tool Design, Planning, Purchasing, and Materials Planning Departments. Action was taken to write off, and dispose of the items that were not required for current or future production.

10. Separate error lists were produced for each materials group so that they could be directly passed on to the concerned section. After the reorganization, the error rate dropped significantly within four months. Direct accountability, as well as competition with other materials groups for accuracy in record keeping, spurred each group to maintain their section in a well organized fashion. The sense of helplessness and low morale that prevailed earlier was overcome. Each group had full control over their assigned responsibility and felt motivated to maintain high standards. They were also monitored and audited on a continual basis. In addition, they had resources in the data control section to help them in solving any problems beyond their scope of understanding.

11. A detailed warehouse operations manual was prepared and distributed to all the warehouse sections. It specified procedures for all warehouse operations, including some critical tasks such as handling of new items, handling of rejected items, and settling of discrepancies between physical stocks, Kardex records, and computer files. The manual also clearly specified the steps to be taken in case of any unforeseen problems and listed the sources of help.

## Final Implementation

These structural changes in the overall materials accounting system made it possible to introduce computer-based purchasing, materials planning and inventory control systems over the next six months. The duplicate Kardex system being maintained in the Materials Planning Department was discarded in favor of daily and weekly computer reports. Purchase orders were also printed by the computer. The system permitted the Purchasing and Materials Planning Departments to monitor the receipt of materials against the schedules to ensure that the shortages for machine tool production were minimized. Over the next year, the average inventory was reduced from a nearly 5 month to 3.5 month supply.

The possibility of disbanding the warehouse Kardex was also considered, but a decision was made in favor of retaining it for a number of reasons. There was a strong objection to removing the warehouse Kardex by materials planning and warehouse personnel, who felt that the Kardex system provided a strong backup in the event of any failure. The confidence in the computer systems was still shaky after years of bad experience. It was also pointed out that Kardex provided much help in analyzing and correcting any discrepancies between the physical stocks and the computer-based data. The limitations of the batch type sequential file processing environment also did not permit immediate access to information stored on computer-files. Therefore, it was decided to continue with the warehouse Kardex system until an online system could be introduced at a future date.

## Discussion

This case study presented the details of two implementation attempts for a materials management system. The first attempt was considered a failure; however, after reorganizing the work system, the materials management system was implemented successfully in the second attempt. Analysis of the implementation attempts suggest the factors that may have influenced the success/failure of implementation and provides insights for future implementations.

The details of the first implementation and an analysis of the problems identified suggest that some of the traditional implementation problems discussed in the MIS literature were not dominant in this case. The details of the case suggest that there were no major behavioral problems in the implementation. Users in purchasing and materials planning functions were enthusiastic about implementing the new system. The warehouse personnel were also willing to cooperate in the implementation of the new system, though, they were not viewed as having a major role in the first implementation attempt.

There were no major political issues involved in the implementation. It is true that greater attention was paid to accounting oriented applications in the past; however, in this case, the interests of accounting, materials management, and production functions coincided. Top management also supported the implementation. There were no technical problems either. Computer hardware, software, and data files were performing adequately. There were no major information requirements identification problems from the perspective of the systems designers. Users in purchasing and materials planning were consulted on a regular basis about the details of the system. The format and information content of all the planned reports were approved by users in advance. Warehouse personnel were also consulted about the new system and the procedures to be employed for filling in and forwarding the documents to the data entry section in the MIS department.

Yet, the first attempt failed to identify many structural weaknesses in the operation and the organization of the warehouse system as well as the nature of dependency of warehouse operations on other functional areas such as design, standardization, purchasing, materials planning, and other consuming departments.

This leads us to the discussion of reasons for the failure of the first implementation attempt and the success of the second implementation attempt. Table 1 provides a summary of differences in the first and the second implementation attempts.

In the first attempt, the MIS function bore the primary responsibility for the success of the system. The design team was staffed by only MIS personnel and reported to the MIS manager. The key design staff had M.S. (computer science) degrees with previous experience primarily in developing accounting applications (e.g., payroll). The design team had a high understanding of information technology but lacked sufficient understanding of the functional areas involved. The role of the design team was viewed primarily as carrying out a technical task in which users had little role or responsibility except to provide the needed information. MIS personnel were expected to develop expertise and make decisions about the key components of the system.

The technically oriented systems designers viewed the boundary of the system being designed by them to include only data collection, storage, processing, and delivery related tasks. At the same time they excluded the work organization in the warehouse and its interrelationships with other functions as being outside the boundary of the system, possibly due to their technical background, lack of functional expertise, and lack of a cross-functional business perspective.

In the second attempt, there were a number of significant differences. These differences arose primarily on the initiative of the top/upper management. In addition to the support for computerization, the top management assigned the responsibility for the success of the project to the user departments. Middle level executives from the concerned user departments were assigned to the design team on a full time basis. The leadership of the team was assigned to a user department executive with an M.B.A. degree. Thus the head and some key members of the design team had a cross-functional business perspective (M.B.A. degrees) and previous experience in purchasing, materials planning, inventory control, and warehouse management. These personnel could understand the requirements and the interrelationships of the different functional areas involved (including auditing, accounting, production, and standardization departments) as well as the MIS function. These personnel were able to identify and comprehend the problems and specify the required structural arrangements and changes needed to overcome the identified problems.

The responsibility for the success of the implementation effort was diffused among functional area managers and upper management, instead of being assigned to the MIS manager. The user departments were represented on the design team by middle level executives as the head and key members of the team on a full time basis, and therefore bore greater responsibility for its success. The design team reported to a Deputy General Manager responsible for non-production functions. Therefore, the control over the design team was formally in the hands of upper management, however, informally user managers exercised most control over the design team in view of the composition of the team. User representatives worked diligently on the design effort in view of their perceived responsibility for the success of the implementation effort. This was unlike the first attempt during which users volunteered information only if asked, and remained somewhat uninvolved in the progress of the implementation on account of their preoccupation with other responsibilities.

The second implementation attempt was also more successful in obtaining resources and favorable decisions from the top management after the nature of problems and the required help had been clearly identified. This facilitated allotment of necessary manpower resources for the reorganization, support from different functional areas such as standardization and design, and establishment of suitable guidelines for the ordering, stocking, and issue of materials. Thus, the second attempt also involved more effective utilization of the top management support for implementation.

## Conclusions

The analysis of two implementation attempts presented in this study suggests the important role of personnel with functional expertise and a cross-functional business perspective on the design teams. The analysis also suggests the importance of user involvement and responsibility for the success of the implementation and provides insights into the nature of user participation for successful systems design. The role of top management also appears to be important for creating the necessary organizational structures for the systems implementation attempts and the roles assigned to various participants in such efforts. The analysis also suggests that top management support can be more effectively utilized if the design team can properly identify the problems and the necessary resources and management decisions that are needed for the implementation.

These conclusions put to question the effectiveness of the role of systems designers as “requirements assessors”. The details of the study suggest that it may be difficult for systems designers to gain full knowledge of a functional area and design effective systems without significant previous experience in the relevant functional area(s). If users with significant experience in the relevant functional areas can be trained in information systems and developed for roles in systems design teams, such user/designers may be able to play more of a “requirements specifier’s” role.

Many future design efforts may involve cross-functional integration (e.g., integration of design, engineering, and manufacturing and extension of integration to include functions such as sales, purchase, and finance, etc.) and greater reorganization of the work system to obtain the full benefits of productivity improvement potentials of the information technology. A review of the literature suggests that such developments are already under way (Gold, 1989; Datamation, 1989a, 1989b). In such circumstances, the need for functional expertise and an understanding of cross-functional issues is likely to be even more important for members of the design teams.

It appears that organizations need to pay greater attention to the development of functional and cross-functional expertise in systems designers through suitable recruitment, training, and job rotation programs. Organizations are already hiring increasing number of business school graduates with an understanding of different disciplines in the role of systems designers. Organizations can arrange for a greater flow of staff from the MIS function into the user departments and from user departments into the MIS function as part of an organized training and job rotation plan. This would increase the availability of personnel with significant functional expertise for systems design teams. Greater understanding of information technology in user departments (through job rotation and training as discussed above) should also contribute to more effective utilization of information technology in organizations. The presence of users and systems designers on the design teams with better understanding of the information technology as well as the functional areas should contribute to better designs.

The analysis presented in this study provides support for the assertions of previous MIS researchers. MIS researchers have identified the role of top management involvement (Lucas, 1978), training of systems designers in functional areas (Vitalari, 1985), and user involvement in the design effort (Ives and Olson, 1984). This study illustrates the nature and the role of these factors in influencing the success of implementation.

This study also illustrates the nature of interrelationships between the work system and the information system in the context of a batch processing system. Such links are likely to be even stronger in the case of online systems, though the type of implementation problems encountered may be different. Thus the study supports the importance of an integrated work-information system design approach. This approach will enable systems designers to examine the processes and the organization of the work system, and redesign it to suit the requirements of the integrated work-information system. Any structural weaknesses that exist in the work system can be identified and corrected during implementation. The systems designers can also ensure that the division of responsibilities, procedures, and controls are adequate to ensure that any data generated and collected from the work system will be a fair and true representation of reality. An integrated work-information system design approach could also provide an opportunity for optimizing productivity improvements in the total work-information system.
