# A Comparison of Information Management Issues in the United States of America and the Republic of China \*

William L. Harrison

Oregon State University, Corvallis, OR, USA

Cheng-Kiang Farn

National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan, ROC

A number of research efforts in the United States of America in recent years have provided a look at issues in information management. The important factors change with time. The degree of importance of individual issues also changes as new technologies enter into information management activities. In this study, rankings of the relative importance of information management issues in both the United States of America and the Republic of China are compared. Most issues share a common level of importance. There are, however, some important differences. Those in the rankings are, to some extent, explainable by differences in the economic development and cultural environment of the two countries. Additional efforts are needed to provide more rigorous explanations.

Keywords: Information management issues, Information systems, International issues, R.O.C. information management, U.S.A. information management.

## Introduction

Numerous prior studies of the relative importance of information management issues within the United States of America have been published. These studies have indicated that the issues judged to be most important change with time. Changes reflect not only the successful resolution of previously troublesome issues, but the arrival of new issues brought about by the introduction of new technologies.

Some issues, on the other hand, seem surprisingly stable over time. These are found on the lists produced by virtually all research efforts. Sometimes their ranking is also quite stable over time.

## Prior Work in the Area

Several of the earlier efforts to determine the relative importance of information management issues have been done with the cooperation of the Society for Information Management (SIM) (formerly known as SMIS, the Society for the Management of Information Systems). The first of these studies was published in 1982 [1]. Their ranked ordering of issues was:

1. MIS long-range planning and integration with corporate plans;

2. Gauging MIS effectiveness;

3. The developing role of the information resource manager;

4. Decision support systems;

5. User education;

6. Data security and information privacy issues;

7. Technical recruiting and training;

8. Use of personal computers in the organization.

Another study was completed in 1983 and published in 1984 [2]. Again the target population was the membership of SIM. Such a target is appropriate, since SIM is an organization whose members are upper-level MIS managers, predominantly from firms in the U.S.A. The results of the 1983 study were:

1. MIS long-range planning and integration with corporate plans;

2. Gauging MIS effectiveness;

3. Use of personal computers in the organizations;

4. The developing role of the information resource manager;

5. (Application) development productivity;

6. Management development for MIS managers;

7. Decision support systems;

8. User education;

9. Structure of MIS functions;

10. Being identified as a business manager;

11. Network planning;

12. Managing the development backlog;

13. Data security and information privacy issues;

14. Technical recruiting and training.

The two most important issues were the same. In the three years between them, very little progress had apparently been made with respect to these two issues. Another issue, the use of personal computers, increased in importance. The period between these two studies is when the IBM PC was introduced. In another work, it was found that only ten percent of companies had microcomputer policies prior to 1982 and approximately sixty percent had such policies before 1984. Clearly this was a period of increased visibility and concern over the use of personal computers and it was captured by the issues studies.

Another study of issues was also conducted then [4]. Dickson et al. used a technique which allowed respondents to rank the importance of issues in four different rounds (a basic Delphi method). This gave respondents an opportunity to compare their own beliefs about importance of the issues with those of the other respondents. The ranking results indicated need for:

1. Improved I/S planning;

2. Facilitation & management of "end user" computing;

3. Integration of I/S, office automation, and telecommunications

4. Improved software development and quality;

5. Measuring and improving I/S effectiveness/productivity;

6. Facilitation of organizational learning and usage of I/S technologies;

7. Aligning the I/S organization with that of the enterprise;

8. Specification, recruitment, and development of I/S human resources;

9. Effective use of the organization's data resource;

10. Development and implementation of decision support systems;

11. Planning and management of the applications portfolio;

12. Planning, and implementation, and management of office automation;

13. Planning and implementing a telecommunications system;

14. Information security and control;

15. Increased understanding of the role/contribution of I/S;

16. Determination of appropriate I/S funding;

17. Effective use of graphics;

18. Impact of artificial intelligence;

19. Management of data and document storage.

Interestingly, the list of important issues is much longer than the previous two. Perhaps the technique of multiple rounds allowed respondents to work away the ambiguities of the list and create new, more explicitly-stated ones.

New technologies seem to enter in an interesting way. Their first appearance may be as a very general issue, such as the use of personal computers. Later these are separated into several, more precise statements as managers begin to focus more accurately on aspects of the new technology.

No less that three studies were completed in the 1986–87 timeframe. Brancheau and Wetherbe [3] used a five-part Delphi study to identify and rank as follows:

1. Improving strategic planning;

2. Using information systems for competitive advantage;

3. Facilitating organizational learning and use of I.S.;

4. Increased understanding of I.S.'s role & contribution;

5. Aligning the I.S. organization with that of the firm;

6. Facilitating and managing end-user computing;

7. Promoting effective use of the data resource;

8. Developing an information architecture;

9. Measuring I.S. effectiveness and productivity;

10. Integrating DP, OA, factory automation and telecommunications

Planning remains the most important, unresolved issue.

One study should be mentioned, published in 1986 [8]. In this questionnaires were sent to 1500 managers in the Fortune 1000 firms. The following ranking resulted:

1. Aligning MIS with Business Goals;

2. Data Utilization;

3. Educating Senior Personnel;

4. Software Development;

5. Productivity;

6. Planning;

7. Integration of Technologies;

8. Telecommunications Technology;

9. Quality Assurance;

10. Office Automation;

11. Data Security;

12. End-User Computing;

13. Recruiting and Training;

14. Information Centers;

15. External Data;

16. Decision Support Systems;

17. Centralization;

18. Telecommunications Deregulation;

19. Measuring Productivity;

20. Fourth Generation Languages;

21. Strategic Systems;

22. CIM;

23. Expert Systems and Artificial Intelligence.

These then are the issues in the United States of America. Are they stable across cultures? Would they be the same in an economy quite different from that of the U.S.A.? Do they represent a growth and maturity curve that could be used to predict the emergence of issues in environments which lag behind the U.S.A. information technologies?

## Details of the Current Study

Two phases of our research effort have now been completed. In the first, a questionnaire was used to identify information management issues in an area of the U.S.A.: the Pacific Northwest. The annual conference of the SIM, held in Seattle in 1987, provided an opportunity and the results of this study were used to form the program conference. A questionnaire was designed to allow practitioners to rank a set of sixteen issues derived from the earlier efforts. It was tested by administering it to a small group, modifying it, and again administering it on a pilot basis. The final versions of the questionnaire, in English and Chinese, are given in Appendix A.

Questionnaires were mailed to practitioners, who were to rate the relative importance of the issues using a scale from 1 (unimportant) to 10 (very important). Respondents were asked to rate issues of current importance, their importance to 1989 and to 1992.

## Administration of the Questionnaire in the U.S.A.

The questionnaire was mailed to 1160 information management professionals. SIM's mailing list for the Pacific Northwest Chapter was used. A total of 133 responses were received with 17 being discarded as unsuitable for tabulation. The remaining 116 represent a response rate of only $10\%$ , a fairly low rate. Interviews after the fact indicated that the questionnaire was too long and the task of judging relative importance too difficult.

Respondents were given an opportunity to suggest issues not captured by the questionnaire. These are given in Appendix B.

Administration of the Questionnaire in the Republic of China

Several changes were made prior to administration in the Republic of China. The most obvious change was translation into Chinese, and rankings were requested only on current importance and importance five years into the future.

Questionnaires were mailed to 250 professionals in Taiwan. A total of 94 usable responses were received, a response rate of 39%. Improvement in the response rate is attributed to the easier-to-respond questionnaire and better follow-up due to the smaller mailing list. Cultural and economic differences may also have contributed. The rapid rate of growth of the Taiwan economy and the emphasis, in particular, on matters concerning computing causes managers to be hungry for more information about the field. They were assured of a copy of the results, information they seemed eager to receive.

## Comparison of Results

There are interesting differences between business firms in the U.S.A. and R.O.C. There are interesting differences in their supporting information systems as well. In “explaining” the results of this work, the authors hope to focus attention on other research which needs doing in order to verify or counter our “explanations.”

The Impact of lower R.O.C Wage Rates on the Substitution of Capital for Labor

To the extent that the substitution of capital for labor is a function of rational, economic criteria, it is heavily dependent upon prevailing wage rates. A high wage rate provides much greater tangible benefits in the elimination of labor intensive operations. A low wage rate provides substantially less opportunity.

A computer is a capital good that is frequently justified on the basis of labor savings. The difference in wage rates between the U.S.A. and the R.O.C. is substantial. Statistics from 1986 indicate that the average industrial hourly wage in the R.O.C. was US\$1.71 while in the U.S.A. the figure was US\$13.50 [6].

It is not, therefore, surprising that computerized information processing systems would have been implemented in the U.S.A. than in the R.O.C. The wage rate ratio of \$13.50 to \$1.71 indicates that equivalent investments in computers would require displacement of 7.89 times as much labor in the R.O.C. as in the U.S.A. One could expect, therefore, that the degree of utilization of computers in the R.O.C. would be much less than in the U.S.A.

The “Lag” in Applying New Information Management Technologies in the R.O.C.

The difference in wage rates also causes a difference in the types of tasks undertaken. The conversion from manual to automated processing has followed a fairly predictable path. The first to be converted were those which were fairly straightforward, had good cost/benefit ratios, and employed tested technologies. As these were completed, conversion moved to tasks with less obvious justifications.

In Taiwan, only the most beneficial of the early technologies have been implemented. Technologies at the margin of justification in the U.S.A. are not even close to being justified in Taiwan. As a result, applications at the leading edge of the technology do not receive the same emphasis in the R.O.C.

## Smaller Average Firm Size in the R.O.C.

Taiwan's reputation is that the strength of its economy comes from the efforts of its many small enterprises. Indeed, $94\%$ of all companies in Taiwan have nineteen or fewer people [9]. Analysis of the employment figures for the top 500 firms in both countries indicate a significant difference in firm size. For the Republic of China the average number of employees for the top 500 industrial firms is 923 [12]. In the U.S.A. the comparable figure is 27,025 [5].

Small firms seem less likely to turn to computerized systems. The very small firms of Taiwan seem even less likely to turn to computerized systems, given the lag in implementation of new technologies.

## Concern for Professional Advancement in the R.O.C.

The structure of wages in the lower ranks of MIS professionals in the R.O.C. is such that there is great economic pressure to move up. There is also considerable social/cultural pressure to advance.

Significant advancement within the field of information management is possible only for those who work in companies with large MIS departments. The average number of employees in the MIS department of those responding companies in Taiwan was nine. The opportunity for advancement within MIS is therefore limited. MIS employees in Taiwan are, therefore, quite sensitive to the opportunities for advancement which might exist outside MIS. For these reasons, career path opportunities are critical in the R.O.C.

## Comparing the Studies

An interesting initial conclusion can be drawn: there is much agreement between them. For both the current and future periods, three of the top four issues are the same. Thus, in many ways, managers face the same challenges. There are, however some differences.

Table 6 expresses the difference in rankings between the two surveys. A positive difference indicates that the issue was rated more important by R.O.C. respondents. A negative difference indicates the opposite. Thus, the difference in rankings for Issue No. 1 for the current period was -5. This was, therefore, considered to be relatively more important to U.S.A. respondents for the current period.

## Current Period Differences

Rankings of the majority of the issues differ only slightly, indicating considerable agreement concerning the issues. For the current period, thirteen differ by an absolute value of 2 or less.

The greatest difference for any rankings is for Maintenance of goal agreement between the organization and the information services group. This is ranked second in the U.S.A. but only seventh in the R.O.C. (Interestingly, this difference disappears in the future period rankings.)

This is probably due to the difference in firm size. Small firms would provide much less opportunity for conflict. In the extreme, of course, corporate planning and information systems planning are both accomplished by the owner or the owner and a close family member in the R.O.C. Goal conflict or the drifting apart of organizational and information services goals takes place when information systems staffs begin to manage their own enterprise independently of the corporation itself. Such problems sometimes occur in large organizations.

Two other issues with significantly different ranking for the current period are Information service staff development and career path maintenance, and Better integration of systems through better interface and interconnectivity standards. The former is more important in the R.O.C. while the latter is the opposite.

In independent interviews with MIS managers, they often expressed their concern for providing reasonable career paths for their employees. Their employees expressed the same concerns. This concern reflects the small size of the MIS departments. The median was only nine people, which includes the professional and operating staff. Since most firms in Taiwan are family-owned, career path considerations for non-family members become even more important.

Improved system integration in the current period is more important in the U.S.A. This difference is to be expected, given the difference in the “state of the art.” It should disappear in later periods and, in fact, the rankings for the future period show it to be of approximately the same rank.

This issue is also a function of firm size. Large firms have a greater need for interconnectivity. Firms in the U.S.A. would be much more likely to seek interconnection between units within the firm than Taiwan. As Taiwan firms grow this issue will grow in importance.

## Discussion of Future Period Differences

There is significantly less agreement in issues for the future. Here only nine have differences in ranking of two or less, while eleven have three or less.

The greatest difference in rankings is for Information service staff development and career path maintenance, for which there was some disagreement in current period rankings. This difference is not surprising, given the difference in emphasis on career paths. Such topics are of paramount importance in the R.O.C. and lower level information systems professionals keep the issue alive with their superiors.

Two issues had differences of minus eight; (No. 8 and No. 9), having to do with the development of expert systems to enhance information management activities and functional activities respectively. Both issues were ranked low in the current period in both countries. For the future they are expected to be much more important in the U.S.A. than the R.O.C. This is undoubtedly related to the lag in the application of new information systems technology. Information management professionals in the U.S.A. expect expert systems to “come of age” in the relatively near future. Information management professionals in the R.O.C. think that expert systems will become more important but not enough to displace other issues.

Some of the difference is also due to reduced capital for labor substitution which results from the lower wage rates in the R.O.C.

As an aside, the authors tried very hard to find an MIS Director in Taiwan to present a lecture on the uses of expert systems in Taiwan. No one could be found who admitted knowledge of any expert systems in use in Taiwan.

The improvement of productivity of maintenance activities had a difference of six. It was ranked much higher in the R.O.C. than in the U.S.A. This is difficult to explain. Maintenance is likely to continue to be important in both countries. New technologies in use in the U.S.A. and under research in the R.O.C., for example Computer Assisted Software Engineering (CASE), may reduce maintenance.

There is evidence that CASE techniques have not yet found significant application within the Republic of China. Professor Sung's work [11] indicates that the R.O.C. may be well behind in the use of software engineering tools.

The R.O.C. respondents ranked Development of standards for information system activities and control mechanisms to foster compliance with them, as more important (by four places) than did respondents in the U.S.A. This would seem to be explained by the lead of the U.S.A. in matters of information management. Standards for programming and applications development are reasonably well developed in the U.S.A., but R.O.C. companies have not reached this same level of maturity. Eventually progress will bear fruit and this issue can then be expected to decline in importance.

Two issues had differences in rankings of three: The assessment of the economic viability of replacing old applications with new ones, and Establishing more effective communications with top management. The first is more important in the R.O.C. Perhaps the explanation is that information managers in the U.S.A. expect that this problem will be solved in the time period. The second is more important in the U.S.A., it is most likely due to the difference in firm size.

## Concluding Remarks

The purpose of this research has been to explore the extent to which information management professionals in the United States and the Republic of China share a common interest in unresolved issues. The results indicate that most issues are, indeed, common to both. There are some important differences. Possible explanations were advanced. Further research is needed to assess the extent to which these explanations are true.
