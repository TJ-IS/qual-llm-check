# Software Piracy and Responsibilities of Educational Institutions

Jin H. Im and Clifford Koen

Department of Management, University of New Orleans, New Orleans, LA 70148, USA.

Use of microcomputers for educational purposes has increased enormously, but heavy dependency on microcomputers exposes several issues to educational institutions. One of them is legal responsibility for software piracy by faculty, staff, and students. This paper addresses the current situation of software piracy in educational institutions, laws for software protection, and responsibilities of educational institutions.

Keywords: Software piracy, Microcomputers, Educational institutions, Legal responsibility, Copyright law.

## 1. Introduction

The number of microcomputers in business schools doubled in the two years from 1985 to 1987 [9]. Virtually all business schools now have microcomputer labs or facilities [15]. However, such heavy dependency has several effects on educational institutions. First, there are financial issues concerning funding for hardware and software, establishing and operating microcomputer labs, and staffing them, etc. Second, there are managerial issues concerning selection and maintenance of hardware and software, assignment of appropriate personnel to the labs, and security of hardware and software, etc.

Funding for hardware purchase is the most urgent problem, due to proliferating demand for microcomputer computing facilities. Many educational institutions have vigorously expanded their computing facilities. During the same two year period, the average student-per-microcomputer ratio in a business school dropped to about 42.7, a 50% improvement, and the average faculty-per-microcomputer ratio to 2.8, a 64% improvement. Although it is hard to specify appropriate ratios, it is clear that the current ratios are not satisfactory, as witnessed in the crowded computer labs of many business schools. However, most basic needs have been met by the rapid expansion of microcomputing facilities.

The proliferation now raises new managerial issues. Among them, security against unauthorized copying of software requires special attention, because it is a new issue to most administrators of educational institutions, and it may entail legal responsibility. Here, we discuss the increase of software piracy in educational institutions.

## 2. Software Piracy in Educational Institutions

For a number of years, industry analysts have estimated that for every legitimate program purchased there are between two and ten illegitimate copies made [5]. Fully aware of the economic losses in the record industry due to use of the cassette recorder, and the motion picture industry's calculations of the impact of videotaping, software copying has become an increasingly emotional topic within computer circles.

The potential economic impact of unauthorized copying on the software industry is considerable. The consequence of unauthorized duplication and distribution may be lost revenue for the computer industry of at least \$800 million annually [10]; some experts even estimate that the annual loss could exceed \$2 billion. Because of this financial incentive, software vendors have initiated a number of ways to halt software piracy, including prosecution of those who copy software illegally.

Most of the lawsuits that have been filed have focused on illegal copying in the business community. However, some software publishers think that piracy is a bigger problem at colleges and universities than elsewhere, because there is no on-campus enforcement of the copyright law [4]. Therefore, they have recently shifted their attention to halting software piracy within educational institutions by faculty, staff, and students.

According to our 1988 field survey of the 630 member schools in the American Assembly of Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB), 181 respondents (75.1%) out of the 241 responding schools agreed with our statement that software piracy occurs in their schools. Two hundred and thirty respondents (95.5%) said that their schools were aware of the legal responsibilities involved in software piracy, and one hundred and ninety-one respondents (79.2%) said that their schools had taken appropriate measures. Eight-six schools (35.7%) responded that they had no formal policies against unauthorized software copying. Although 155 schools (64.3%) had formal policies, only 63 schools (26.1%) had policies which involved disciplining copyright violators. However, those policies were mainly intended to punish copyright violation by students, not by faculty and staff: only 37 schools (15.4%) had policies to punish unauthorized software copying by faculty and staff as well as students.

## 3. Laws for Software Protection

There are three principal methods by which a creator of computer software may seek legal protection for intellectual property: copyright, patent, and trade secret law. Of these forms of legal protection, the most direct and easily attainable protection is through the law of copyright $[14]$ .

## 3.1. Copyright Law

Copyright protection is available for computer software under the Copyright Act of 1976, as amended by the Computer Software Act of 1980. The Copyright Act enumerates both the copyright owner's exclusive rights and the limitations on those rights. Under section 106 of the Act, the author of a copyrighted computer program has the exclusive right to reproduce copies, prepare derivative works, distribute copies, and perform and display the copyrighted work. Copyright law protects the author's right for lifetime plus 50 years for individuals and for 75 years for corporations. The law provides an adequate protection period for a copyright holder in view of the fairly short life span of most microcomputer software.

The Copyright Act limits the owner's rights in several ways. These statutory exemptions listed in section 107 through 117 provide possible defense to claims of copyright infringement. Relevant statutory exemptions for educational institutions would include copies made for use by a library, for use in a display, for archival purposes, and for “fair use” of the computer program.

Under section 108, the school library could arguably make and distribute a copy of the copyrighted program for scholarship purposes. This section permits a library to reproduce one copy of a copyrighted work if the reproduction or distribution is not made for commercial advantage, the library is open to the public or persons doing research in a specialized field, and the reproduction includes a copyright notice. Section 110 would probably allow the instructor to display or demonstrate the computer program during teaching activities in a classroom. The exemption allowing for archival purposes permits the user to make one copy of a purchased software to keep as a backup copy. Thus, most of the statutory exemptions are of little help to university faculty and administrators who may be charged with copyright infringement.

The fair use exception appears to be the best defense available to an individual educator or an educational institution against a claim of infringement. According to section 107, the fair use of a copyrighted work, including such use by reproduction in copies for purposes such as criticism, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright. The U.S. Supreme Court in Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios [16] noted that fair use is an “equitable rule of reason” that must be applied on a case-by-case basis. One commentator has noted that “although generally the doctrine of fair use is more liberally applied in non-commercial and non-profit educational situations, an educational setting does not insure a fair use determination.” [7].

## 3.1.1. Remedies for Copyright Infringement

Sections 502 through 505 of the Copyright Act provide a variety of remedies to restrain infringement, compensate the copyright holder, and punish the infringer. The court can issue an injunction to prohibit further infringement. The Act also gives the court power to issue impoundment orders at the pre-trial stage and order the destruction or other reasonable disposition of all copies made in violation of the copyright. One commentator has noted that, if read literally, the statute authorizes a court in a software copyright case to impound the defendant's entire computer system on the day the case is filed [12].

Section 504 of the Act provides a copyright plaintiff a choice between the traditional legal remedy of an award of actual damages and the special copyright remedy of statutory damages. If plaintiff chooses to receive actual damages, this award will usually include plaintiff's lost profits. In lieu of actual damages and profits, statutory damages may be awarded in the discretion of the court for a sum of not less than \$250 up to a maximum of \$10,000 for all infringements with respect to any one work. In other words, an infringer may be liable for up to \$10,000 for each copyrighted software that is copied. If the infringement is “willful”, the court may increase the award to as much as \$50,000 for each infringement.

In one lawsuit filed by Lotus in 1984 against Health Group Inc. (HGI), Lotus alleged that HGI had made several unauthorized copies of the Lotus 1-2-3 disks and delivered them to its hospitals and nursing homes [18]. The suit was settled by consent decree two months after it was filed. HGI paid an undisclosed sum of money and agreed to a permanent injunction against unauthorized copying.

Lotus alleged that since the infringement was willful, it was entitled to attorney's fees and statutory damages of \$50,000 for each act of infringement (i.e., each unauthorized copy). Most legal experts agree that only one amount of statutory damages can be awarded for all infringements of a single work. However, it must be noted that a court has considerable latitude in determining the actual dollar amount and that there is a reasonable argument that the Lotus interpretation is appropriate. Since this issue has apparently not been conclusively resolved, it is possible that the Lotus interpretation could prevail in a court to the detriment of some unsuspecting copyright infringer. Either way, copyright infringement is a costly proposition.

Individual faculty members and administrators may take some comfort in section 504 which limits the amount of statutory damages that can be assessed against them for copyright infringement. To qualify for this reduction in damages, the employee or agent of a non-profit educational institution must have had a reasonable belief that his or her use of the copyrighted work was a fair use as defined by section 107.

## 3.2. Patent Law

Patents, unlike copyrights, are designed to protect the idea behind an item, not merely the particular form in which the idea appears. Patent law protection is available for any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any improvement thereof that is new and useful. The holder of a patent has rights to exclusive use of the subject matter of this work for seventeen years. In spite of the availability of patent protection for computer programs, there are several factors that discourage programmers from seeking patent protection. In contrast to copyright law, which requires only that programs be original, patent law has a relatively high standard of novelty that must be met for protection [11].

The patent application process also presents several barriers to those who may seek patent protection for computer programs. The process is costly and time consuming. The cost of obtaining a patent may easily reach several hundred thousand dollars, and it could take two to three years for issuance. Also, the patentee must make substantial public disclosure if a patent is granted. Consequently, the granting of a patent will terminate trade secret protection for any secrets disclosed by the patent application. Generally, patent protection, while important for the best and most novel ideas, is simply not available for much software. For either legal or practical reasons, many of the ideas developed with a computer program will remain unprotected by patent $[13]$ .

## 3.3. Trade Secret Law

State statutes and common law may be used to protect trade secrets embodied in computer software. Trade secret law also protects against misappropriation of other valuable ideas and items that are useful to a company's business. This area of law varies from state to state. Generally, a trade secret is any formula, pattern, device, or information used in the operation of a business to provide the business an advantage over competitors who do not know or use it. The owner of a trade secret must take measures to prevent it from becoming known to individuals other than those selected by the owner to access it. To maintain this element of secrecy so that the software will be protected by trade secret law, software vendors use license agreements.

The typical license agreement will include restrictions by which the user states that he will not copy the program or disclose it to any third party without the vendor's consent. An exception is sometimes made for archival copies for backup purposes or for a limited number of copies for use on multiple computers at the same site [6]. A violation of this agreement will subject the customer to a lawsuit.

## 3.4. Shrink-wrap License

In addition to these traditional methods of intellectual property protection, some software developers have turned to creative ways of restricting a buyer's use of purchased software. One such innovation is the shrink-wrap license; this is becoming increasingly popular. A license grants permission to use or to do a thing which the licensor could otherwise prevent [1]. The shrink-wrap license purports to bind the buyer of software when the seal (or shrink-wrap cover over the software) is broken. Typically, the disk containing the software purchased by the customer is inside a package, and the license is printed on the outside. The license claims that a buyer's opening of the software package is effective as acceptance of the license terms. Although each software manufacturer furnishes its own “agreement”, most mass marketed software licenses exhibit similar characteristics. The terms generally provide that (1) title to the product remains with the manufacturer and the product is licensed, not sold; (2) the product may be used only by the licensee on a single central processing unit; (3) copying, modifying, or transferring the program is restricted or prohibited; and (4) the licensee disclaims warranty protection and agrees to limits on remedies that might otherwise be available.

These agreements, if effective, would preserve many of the interests important to the software creator. However, their enforceability is subject to serious debate. Even when the license is found to be legally enforceable, the licensor (seller) may have a problem meeting the burden of proof required to pursue enforcement. For example, most purchasers do not read the licensing agreement before opening the package. Proof that the claimed acceptor read the license before opening the package is likely to be difficult. Such proof would be necessary to show that the claimed violator accepted the license.

Several states, including Illinois and Louisiana, have responded to the software protection problem by passing statutes that declare the license agreements binding. These laws, however, may be pre-empted by federal statutes. Generally, a state law is pre-empted by a federal law, and therefore unenforceable to the extent that the state law is inconsistent with the federal law.

The Louisiana's statute has been rejected by one court which cited conflicts with federal copyright law. In Vault Corp. v. Quaid Software Ltd. [20], the plaintiff manufactured software designed to prevent the copying of software programs and the defendant devised a system which unlocked the plaintiff's security software and allowed the program to be copied. The court rejected the plaintiff's request for an injunction against the sale of defendant's unlocking software. The court held that Louisiana's law "invaded the exclusive province of the federal Copyright Act," and that the provisions of the licensing agreement were "unenforceable to the extent they are contrary to the policies of the federal Copyright Act".

## 4. Legal Responsibilities

## 4.1. Environmental Changes

In earlier days, most software vendors protected their software with technical schemes, such as making laser-burn holes on floppy disks, writing data between sectors, and imbedding counters to keep track of number of copies made [8]. However, two major changes in the environment of PC users have been pushing vendors to drop their protection.

The first change is that more microcomputers nowadays come with hard disks. This provides substantially larger memory storage and much faster access speed than a floppy disk. Protected software cannot be copied into a hard disk unless the protection scheme is removed. It is really troublesome for a user to change software frequently in a floppy disk drive rather than use his or her hard disk because of software protection. For large software, it is often necessary to use a hard disk.

The second environmental change is that many organizations have been installing local area networks (LAN) to integrate their computing equipment. Major reasons for a LAN are data sharing, software sharing, hardware sharing, and communication. Among them, software sharing means that all the necessary software are stored in the powerful microcomputer termed a “file server”, and each user has access to the software through the LAN. Only unprotected software can be used in a LAN.

In addition to the environmental changes, protection-breaking software is available at modest prices, so that a user can break virtually any protection scheme. It is designed to protect the interests of a software developer, but does so at the expense of legitimate user's convience. After increasing outcries by users, many vendors have started to drop their protection schemes.

However, they have not given up protecting their copyright. Major software vendors established the Software Protection Fund in late 1983 for user education and software piracy prosecution. In an effort to educate users in educational institutions, EDUCOM (a consortium of over 450 colleges and universities committed to the use and management of information technology in higher education) and the Association of Data Processing Organizations jointly have published and distributed a pamphlet titled “USING SOFTWARE: a guide to the ethical and legal use of software for members of the academic community”.

## 4.2. Vicarious Liability

If a university employee, either faculty or staff, were to make unauthorized copies of software available through the university, the university as well as the employee may be held liable for copyright infringement (i.e., the university may be held vicariously liable). Also, other administrative officials, including the person responsible for software purchases, may be held liable. Liability may befall certain individuals who have the power to police the conduct of employees, such as the manager of the computer lab or dean even though those persons may have no actual knowledge that the copyright is being impaired. It is also possible for such individuals to be held liable for the infringing actions of an employee, in spite of policies or orders of the university designed to prohibit copyright infringement [19].

In one recent decision, the court stated that “vicarious liability may be imposed when an individual has the right and ability to supervise the infringing activity and also has a direct financial interest in such activities.” [17]. Most courts tie the liability of the employer to a financial loss due to action of the infringing employee. However, even when required by a court, the “financial interest” can be found simply from the employment relationship.

The concept of vicarious liability generally would not apply to situations where a student violated a copyright since there is no employment relationship. However, it is conceivable that a university could be held negligent because it failed to implement proper safeguards or because it failed to inform students of the obligations created by copyright law.

## 4.3. The UCLA Case

A recent case involving the University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) caused fear among software publishers that they would have no legal recourse against illegal copying at state colleges and universities [2,3]. The plaintiff, BV Engineering, sued the University for \$70,000 when the company discovered that a UCLA employee had made more than one copy of each of seven programs that the company had sold to the university. UCLA defended its case with two propositions. The first was the Eleventh Amendment's protection of state governments and agencies from being prosecuted by federal laws. The second was the fair use exception in the copyright law. While not mentioning the fair use exception, the court ruled that the Eleventh Amendment protects state agencies from being sued under copyright law.

Prior to this decision, only four federal court cases had addressed the issue of whether the Eleventh Amendment provides a state and its agencies with immunity from liability for copyright infringement suits. Two circuit courts of appeals are divided on this question. Until a more definitive decision is reached, software publishers have good reason to be concerned. It should be remembered, however, that copyright holders may still sue individual employees of state institutions for copyright infringement. Also, a state's Eleventh Amendment immunity from suit in federal court will not prevent a state or its employee from being sued on some other theory, such as breach of a license agreement.

## 5. Conclusions

It is necessary for an educational institute to make a policy against software piracy by faculty, staff, and students with consequences and then diligently enforce it. For instance, a written copyright policy may be included in employee handbooks and personnel policy manuals. All employees should be informed of restrictions on the use of computer software and informed of penalties for violating those restrictions. Students likewise should be educated on the legal and ethical aspects of computer software piracy. Notifying, training, and educating employees and students will reduce the likelihood of accidental as well as intentional acts of copyright infringement, though such efforts are no guarantee of avoiding liability.
