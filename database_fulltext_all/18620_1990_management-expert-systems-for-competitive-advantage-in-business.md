# Management Expert Systems for Competitive Advantage in Business

Liang Thow-Yick and Ta Huu-Phuong

Faculty of Business Administration. National University of Singapore, Kent Ridge, Singapore 0511

While some areas of information technology are gradually slackening into the position of competitive necessity, Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology is emerging as a competitive weapon. The three areas of AI that have immediate economic values are robotics, natural language processing, and expert systems (ES). ES have the potential of becoming useful managerial tools in decision-making. They can handle ill-structured problems and exhibit features that will complement existing management information systems and decision support systems. Recognising the potentials of ES in management, this study investigates their present state of development, competitive implications, depth of penetration in business organizations, and future application areas. The trend and level of management expert systems application in Singapore are compared with those in the US.

Keywords: Information technology, Information systems, Expert systems, Management expert systems.

## 1. Introduction

Traditional computer applications employ algorithms that are precise: rules and procedures that will lead to the correct solution. A typical example is a payroll system that calculates salary according to a set of rules that take care of all the usual variables as well as special conditions that may arise. The key features in traditional systems software applications are ‘computation’ and ‘correct solution’.

Expert systems (ES) on the other hand are different. They do not necessarily solve well-structured problems. Their problems may be too complex to be solved perfectly and do not necessarily have optimum answers. The development of ES applications requires a revolutionary approach where heuristic techniques are used to provide a ‘good solution’. ES in general are “computer programs that use specialised symbolic reasoning to solve difficult problems well” [1] in a narrow domain. Therefore, their emphasis is on the use of specialised knowledge and symbolic reasoning rather than numerical computation.

A classical example of ES is the Dipmeter Advisor, a geological expert system. This is able to make inferences about the probable location of oil in a region by analysing oil-well log data and utilising the interpretive abilities of some geological experts stored as facts and rules in the system. ES are software systems that are designed to mimic the way human experts make decisions; basically a form of intellectual clone [2], comprising a knowledge base and an inference system. The knowledge base contains the experience of the expert(s) while the inference system is made up of an interpreter and scheduler. The two components are created separately and independently, allowing flexibility for future enhancements.

The most important component of an ES is its corpus of knowledge acquired by the ‘knowledge engineer’ through repetitive interviews with experts. The expertise, a combination of facts and heuristics, is then structured and codified. The three most common knowledge representation techniques are rule-based, semantic net, and frame-based. Usually a high-level language software known as an expert system shell, is employed. Languages such as LISP, PROLOG or even BASIC are used, depending on the nature and complexity of the problem.

Stored knowledge will be retrieved by a user when specialized advice is needed. The system will also make deductions and provide a possible conclusion, advising the user on the logic that leads to the conclusion, if requested.

## Categories of Problems and Informations Systems

Early DP systems have gradually been upgraded into management information systems (MIS). With this improvement, some routine and repetitive decisions could be made, providing aids in managerial decision-making. However, as well-structured problems are solved, the need is for decision support systems (DSS). These required operation research models and a database, with interactive support to decision-makers attempting to solve semi-structured problems. ES deal with heuristics and ill-structured data; they emerged in the early 1980s as a by-product of Artificial Intelligence (AI) research. The ES will function as advisors to decision-makers. ES have only been successful when they solve specific problems. The ability to identify an appropriate domain is therefore significant and essential.

## Research Approach

Our study focuses on the state of development of ES in Singapore as compared to the US. Literature on survey of MES utilisation and development experience $[5–9]$ are available for analysis and comparison. These were used to compare with the stages of MES development in Singapore. The data and information for Singapore were mainly gathered through interviews with knowledge engineers, users and owners of ES. Even though this study was primarily intended to assess the development of MES, other ES are included because of the sparseness of material since this technology was introduced into Singapore about four years ago.

## Competitive Implications and Strategic Areas

Although most successful ES today are in non-management application areas, many organisations are beginning to realise the possibility of MES in providing a competitive edge. As more tools of information technology evolve into strategic necessities, identification of a new area as a sustainable competitive weapon is encouraging. Today, information and its related technology have developed into a commanding position where analysis of a strategic business plan should incorporate an IS plan. Hence the use of MES to solve strategic problems by management is gaining momentum.

Several competitive implications of MES have been identified. When ES technology allows systems to be built in entirely new application areas, an organization will be able to exert competitive advantage. Thus the task of identifying new opportunities for MES is a challenge.

The ability of accumulating expert knowledge and integrating it for use in different areas is another advantage: thus the MES can evolve with time. The knowledge base can be updated and refined occasionally to keep it in phase with new strategic criteria.

The strategic capabilities of MES will be enhanced if the limitations of humans are a factor; e.g., most managers cannot make good decisions when the area involves too many factors and thus the problem must be simplified before a decision can be made. A MES, on the other hand, can take care of many factors, giving each a different weight, and recommend a better solution.

Often, top management and specialist expertise are scarce and their time is extremely valuable. Such shortages can be overcome by utilizing an MES, which can provide expertise more readily and widely. ES can also provide other advantages, such as simple transfer of systems and data, consistent and easier documentation, reduced cost, and simple training. Our study has identified some general characteristics of MES application areas in organisations. Their contribution and impact will probably be greatest in areas with these characteristics. The application of MES could also enhance the strategic advantage to their user. These are:

a. Core functions or critical areas in the organisation;

b. Top level and rare expertise areas;

c. Areas where higher levels of expertise are required;

d. Areas that handle a large volume of transaction through many people at different levels of expertise;

e. Areas that are already well-defined and well-understood.

## Industrial Sectors with Immediate ES Applications

A side interest of this study was to determine the industrial sectors that have immediate MES applications. The investigation revealed that the financial services sector is a potentially significant user. Banks and credit card organisations perform many analyses, brokerage firms provide much investment advice, and insurance companies do a great deal of underwriting and claims. As this sector deals with judgemental transactions, ES technology is naturally useful. According to a survey [5], there is tremendous interest in this sector also in the US.

Several application areas have been identified as potential MES application targets. They exhibit strategic characteristics indicated earlier. The areas are:

a. Credit analysis;

b. Investment planning;

c. Auditing;

d. Underwriting; and

e. Claims analysis.

In fact, prototype development has started on these in several large organizations.

## Development Trend

According to “The Commercial Application of Expert Systems Technology”, in 1984 there were 138 ES projects in the US [1]. Thus US has a 5-year headstart in MES technology relative to Singapore. However, the intensity of ES applications in Singapore per ten thousand people is large and will be increasing rapidly.

In general, the spread of ES technology occurs in two waves. The first has two characteristics: the availability of small knowledge engineering systems building tools and the ability of non-AI specialists to use the techniques to solve problems. The US has progressed towards the end of this wave, while Singapore is somewhere in the first half. The second wave has rather different characteristics. Here large, hybrid ES that could rival human experts are involved. These may have intelligent workstations, use sophisticated natural language interfaces, and several knowledge bases. They could be common organisational tools by the late 1990s.

At present, there are several ways in which ES technology can be utilised. ## Difficulties Encountered in Management Applications

The utilisation of MES is more complex than application in other areas. As managerial decisions are normally multi-disciplinary, involved a large number of factors, the derivation of a ‘correct’ answer is usually impossible. An MES in the strategic planning area will have a wide variety of factors to consider and integrate. The process of ‘mining’, representing, and ranking the factors in order of importance requires extra caution. An exhaustive analysis including behavioural variables must be attempted.

The study also found that the set of factors common to the development of other ES must also be considered and justified. These factors are: development costs, frequency of use, and immediate benefits, such as competitive advantage derived from the system. Additional considerations, such as the effect of other IS and how the MES would complement or supplement them are also significant.

## Conclusion

We also found that “many interesting problems solved by human experts are not expressable in terms of algorithms and mathematical formulas, and by focusing on a narrow domain many new problems became candidates for ES approaches” [10]. Hence using the ES techniques, some essentially unsolvable problems become tractable. The capability of IS would be further enhanced if the two techniques could be integrated [11].

Our feelings are that an ES is a promising tool for making better and faster decisions. The trend is that “environmental intelligence is no longer a fashionable obsession of business academics; it is now on the agenda of corporate board meetings” [12]. When organisations are determining their corporate strategy and considering a technology strategy plan, ES technology should also be considered. New departments, such as AI/ES and an Information Centre, will be common.

However, the full impact of ES will not be felt in the next few years. As the utilization of ES in commercial organizations is still relatively new, many side-issues must be explored and rectified. "The state of the art is such that everyone building an ES must endure this primitive start-up phase to learn what is involved in this fascinating new field" [1]. There are predictions that ES will dominate the HIS field by the year 2000 [13,14]. Considering the rate at which all branches of information technology is advancing, a decade could be sufficient for the second wave of ES to reach maturity.
