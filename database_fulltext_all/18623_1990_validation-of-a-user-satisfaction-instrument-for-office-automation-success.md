# Validation of a User Satisfaction Instrument for Office Automation Success $^{1}$

Tan Boon Wan

Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 0511

Lo Tak Wah

Strategic Planning Associates Inc. (Singapore Branch), Singapore

By viewing an office automation system as an information system, user information satisfaction can be applied to measure its success. The Bailey and Pearson instrument was modified for an OA system. The validity of the modified instrument was tested and refined using responses from its users in an academic institution in Singapore. The data were further factor analysed and the result compared with two previous studies.

Keywords: OA, MIS, User information satisfaction, System success.

## Introduction

Office automation (OA) is fast becoming an important element in modern business. Vendors of OA are offering their products as the means to achieve a long overdue increase in office productivity; applications such as electronic mailing and word processing are being implemented on a wide scale. With the recognition of its growing importance, operational measures of OA success are required by both practitioners and researchers; organizations need measures to evaluate OA systems and its effectiveness after implementation, whereas researchers often include OA success as the dependent variable of their studies.

According to Raymond [15], there are four basic types of surrogate measures of system success in the literature: (1) user satisfaction [5], (2) level of system usage [10], (3) user decision performance [4], and (4) organizational performance [17]. A number of researchers have used more than one type of measure in a single study. However, most of these types of measures were developed with respect to management information systems (MIS). Although OA can be considered as one of the subsystems of MIS, there could be subtle differences in the measurement of their respective success.

This paper attempts to answer the question of measuring user satisfaction in the context of an OA system. A previously developed user satisfaction instrument was adapted and validated. Further, the adapted instrument was used to determine the dimensionality of OA success.

## Office Automation

The concept of OA can be traced to as far back as the 1950's; e.g., Leavitt and Whisler [11] and

Hoos [7] speculated on how computers would affect administrative functions in the office. But the concept did not take off until the development of microelectronics, networking, and distributed computing.

Although OA is easy to recognize, it is hard to define. Definitions of OA offered in the literature range from the very broad and general to the specific. In a broad sense, OA refers to the incorporation of appropriate technology to help people manage office activities. Stouder [16] defined OA as:

“…the use of information processing techniques in an office environment to create, process, store or retrieve, use, and communicate information to improve the performance of managerial, professional, technical, administrative, and clerical tasks.”

## User Satisfaction Construct

User satisfaction is a subjective measure of system success; it measures how users perceive their information system, as opposed to the technical quality of the system. Frequently, it can serve as a substitute for objective determinants of information system effectiveness which are not available.

The concept of user satisfaction as a surrogate for system success was first proposed by Cyert and March [3], who posited that the ability of an information system to meet the needs of its user will wither, reinforce, or frustrate user satisfaction with it. If the user perceives that the formal information system should provide required information, that source will first be queried. If the information is readily available, satisfaction with the system is reinforced. If it is not, the user must look for alternative sources; frustration and dissatisfaction are reinforced. Evans [6] suggested that there is a lower limit to satisfaction below which the user will stop all usage of the system and seek alternative sources. Powers and Dickson [14] concluded that user satisfaction is the critical criterion in the measurement of system success and failure.

## Measuring User Satisfaction

A review of the literature reveals that user satisfaction has been operationalized in many different ways. An evaluation of these previously developed user satisfaction instruments led to the selection of that of Pearson and Bailey [1]. This instrument has been proven to be both reliable and valid by a number of researchers [8,15].

Pearson developed a list of “factors” that contribute to information satisfaction. It was derived from existing research on computer interactions and was then reviewed for completeness and accuracy by three data processing professionals. This list was later compared to an analysis of critical incidents collected through interviews with thirty-two user managers; thirty-nine distinct factors were identified. Pearson adopted these factors as the basis for a user information satisfaction instrument which utilized the semantic differential technique; four adjunctive pairs were provided for each factor, plus a satisfied-dissatisfied pair and an important-unimportant pair. A scoring method which used the “important” rating as a weighting factor when calculating the overall satisfaction was also proposed.

Only 33 of the full 39 the factors were included in the current study; organizational competition with the EDP support unit, priorities determination, vendor support, organizational position of the EDP support function, language, and chargeback for services were excluded. Some of the factor titles were also modified, and a seven-point Likert-type rating scale was used instead of the original semantic differential scale. A measurement study was then undertaken to investigate the reliability and validity of the OA version of the user information satisfaction instrument.

The modified instrument was administered as part of a mail questionnaire survey of OA users in an institute of higher learning in Singapore. Only those members who used OA in the course of their work were surveyed and they are restricted to the Heads in Central Administration, Dean of Faculties, Heads of Academic Departments, and their secretaries. Of the 160 questionnaires that were sent out, a total of 68 were returned. Overall, the returned questionnaires accounted for 42.5% of the mailing. Of the returned questionnaires, 64 were usable; the remaining 4 were incomplete and hence had to be omitted.

## Validating the Instrument

## Reliability

Reliability of a measurement instrument refers to its stability over a variety of conditions $[12]$ . Its reliability was tested using the split-halves and internal consistency methods. For the split-halves method, the reliability coefficient was 0.94. The corresponding inter-item reliability coefficient using the internal consistency method was 0.96. These coefficients agree with results published previously by other researchers. For purposes of basic research a reliability of 0.80 or better is deemed adequate $[12]$ . Thus, it can be argued that the instrument is reliable.

## Content Validity

Content validity implies that all aspects of the attribute being measured are considered by the instrument. In the absence of well-defined objective criteria, Nunnally noted that content validity depends mainly on appeals to reason about the adequacy of the content, as cast in the form of test items. Hence, evaluating content validity is basically a question of judgement. We can first refer to Bailey and Pearson, Ives et al. and Raymond. The rigorous manner in which these researchers developed a list of scales both complete and short plus the results of their analyses lend credence to a claim of content validity. Scales that were eliminated from the final questionnaire were removed for sound reasons: non-relevance to the context, or non-importance to the current measure, or both.

## Predictive or External Validity

Predictive or external validity implies that the instrument is consistent and agrees with other measures. Basically, it consists of correlating scores on the predictor test with scores of the criterion variable. The size of the correlation is a direct indication of the degree of validity. Due to the difficulty in finding another established independent measure, no direct attempt was made to evaluate this category of validity. However, based on the results obtained by earlier researchers, it can be concluded that the measurement instrument has predictive or external validity.

## Construct Validity

Construct validity asserts that a measure is valid for a construct if it relates to other measures of this construct and to measures of different constructs in the expected manner $[13]$ . One of two methods of construct validation proposed by Kerlinger $[9]$ is an examination of the correlations between the total scores and item scores: this approach assumes the total scores to be valid. Thus, the extent to which the item correlates with the total scores is indicative of construct validity for that item. In this study, each item score was subtracted from the total score in order to avoid a spurious part-whole correlation $[2]$ . The ‘new total score’ was then correlated with each item score. The correlation coefficient for the measurement instrument ranged from 0.39 to 0.79, with only one correlation coefficient being below 0.55 (Table 1). All the correlations were significant at the 0.0001 level. If it can be assumed that the total scores do measure user satisfaction, these results could indicate support for construct validity of the measurement instrument. The other method of construct validation proposed by Kerlinger is factor analysis. The results from factor analysis also supported the construct validity of the measurement instrument.

## Dimensions of Office Automation

To identify the underlying dimensions of user information satisfaction and to verify the construct validity of the measurement instrument, a factor analysis was performed. The scores of the instrument scales were intercorrelated and a product-moment correlation matrix was obtained. An examination of the correlation matrix revealed numerous correlations in excess of 0.30, some considerably higher. 516 of the 528 coefficients in the correlation matrix were significant at the 5% level. Patterns in responses to variables were therefore expected and the matrix should be factorable. These product-moment correlation coefficients were then factored using the method of principal component.

An examination of these eigenvalues showed there to be only 8 meaningful orthogonal factors with eigenvalues greater than one underlying the user satisfaction construct. Accordingly, 8 factors were extracted and rotated to enhance the interpretability of the factors. Varimax rotation was used. For the purpose of interpretation of factors, a loading cutoff of 0.55 was adopted in this study. At that loading level, a total of 26 scales were included. Table 2 shows the rotated factor matrix with scales having loadings of under 0.55 being omitted. Interpretative labels are suggested for each of the factors.

The first factor carried with it scales related to information product and service quality. The second contained scales representing the user's attitude towards OA support staff. The third was drawn from scales related to user's knowledge and involvement with the system, such as user's understanding of the system, user's feeling of participation and degree of training the user received. The fourth only included two scales: security of data and error recovery. This is termed security of the system. The sixth contained scales related to the user interface. The last two factors, seventh and eighth, each had only one scale and they were subsequently termed as integration of the system and top management involvement.

A logical structure among the scales underlies the results of the factor analysis. This structure reflects the characteristics of an OA system such as an end-user perspective, the importance of top management support and integration. This logical structure indicates that the instrumental definition of the user satisfaction construct is valid. This is another piece of evidence supporting the construct validity of the instrument.

This also allows comparison of our results with those of Ives et al and Raymond which had, as their bases, management information systems for production and small organisations respectively. Our result revealed more dimensions, some of which merged to form the fewer dimensions in the other two studies. For example, the output quality and user-system relationship dimensions in Raymond's study are distributed among three of our dimensions: information product and service quality, user's knowledge and involvement, and user interface. In a management information system, the product of the system is focused on its output while the services provided and the knowledge and involvement of the user are submerged in the user-system relationship. But in an end-user system which makes office activities more convenient, the product of an OA system will include not only the output but also the services provided, like electronic mailing. User knowledge and involvement is also distinct from the quality of the user interface.

While the absence in our results of the vendor support dimension in the Ives et al study is self explanatory, that of the support factor dimension in the Raymond study warrants explanation. All the three scales of this dimension: processing of request for change, time for systems development and timeliness of output are not significant in our result. As an integrated end-user system supporting an established set of office activities, the request for change and system development are minimal. As electronic communication is instantaneous, the timeliness of output is also not an issue. In fact timeliness depends very much on the end-user.

The presence of the two dimensions of systems integration and top management are further evidence of the validity of the satisfaction instrument. Integration was not important in both the results of Ives et al and Raymond because networking was probably not as important an issue to MIS in 1983 or to small organisations. In an OA system integration is critical to its communication role. If Ives et al were to repeat their investigation today, the probability of integration appearing as a dimension will likely be higher. The emergence of top management support as a dimension is also not surprising since the OA system investigated was used by top administrators. These are further evidence supporting the validity of the Pearson and Bailey instrument.

## Conclusion

This paper has extended the work of earlier researchers in the development of a standard measure of user satisfaction: the instrument previously developed by Pearson and Bailey, improved upon by Ives et al., and modified by Raymond for small organisations. The study has provided further evidence for its reliability and validity.

The factor analysis revealed eight dimensions: information product and service quality, user's attitude towards OA support staff, user's knowledge and involvement, security of system, utility of system, user interface, integration of system, and top management involvement. The logical structure which underlies the scales and dimensions reflects the characteristics of an OA system. The factor analysis also confirmed the multi-dimensional nature of user satisfaction.

This study has broadened the applicability of the measurement instrument into the domain of OA and it allows us to understand the factors affecting the success of an OA system better. An increased understanding of the factors of OA success is important: more and more organisations are looking towards OA systems as a tool for improved productivity and better decision making.
