# With or without microcomputers

A dilemma

Telahun Abebe and Janez Grad
Boris Kidric Faculty of Economics, University of Edvard Kardelj,
61000 Ljubljana, Yugoslavia

The emergence of microcomputers in the early 80s created a confused atmosphere in the information technology (IT) users' world. Especially, in developing countries, the decision on what type of hardware to acquire for day-to-day data processing needs of organizations was at the mercy of somewhat inadequate hardware vendor specifications. This being the case, a study was conducted in a composite organization of associated labour (COAL - a Yugoslav terminology for complex business organization - equivalent to corporation), to determine whether microcomputers (micros) could or could not be adapted to support data processing in conjunction with the existing mainframe. The research is based on samples gathered from a COAL that consists of nine different work organizations (WO), equivalent to middle size business organizations, which in turn consist of 27 firms or basic organizations of associated labour (BOAL). The study involved 350 employees whose day-to-day work assignment is linked with the existing data processing system, and those whose work assignment could have been supported by IT. The results are analyzed and implications outlined and discussed.

Keywords: BOAL, WO, COAL, IT, MIS, Data Processing Center, Local Area Network, Mainframe, Microcomputer.

## 1. Introduction

Unlike East European communist countries, Yugoslavian business organizations have a decentralized character. The destinies of organizations depend on the decisions made and the actions executed by “self-managers”. In short, in a self-managed business organization, the workers manage themselves or are the sole owners of that organization $[10,15]$ . This being so, the business organization that the self-managers form can either be BOAL, WO, or COAL (see Fig. 1). BOAL is the basic building block of the COAL, while WO is the basis for the formation of the COAL. In other words, WO must consist of at least two BOALs; while a COAL must consist of two or more WOs. To summarize $[1]$ , a WO can be considered a federation of BOALs, and a COAL to be a loose confederation of WOs.

Whether we like it or not, we are moving from a “Postindustrial Society” to an “Information Society” backed by an IT Revolution characterized by mini and microcomputers [13]. Regardless of the social formation, a need to install an integrated computer based information system

Even though there is little encouragement for extensive implementation of micros by computer center management in certain COAL's, it is expected that this will not last long. The domination of minis in COAL computer centers played an important role in stopping micros being installed as supplementary IT for data processing needs. Meanwhile, the rapid increase in the number of microcomputer possessions by educational and governmental institutions, provided a changing atmosphere in the installation and acquirement of micros in business organizations. It was expected that the implementation of micros in COALs in the next two years would increase tenfold. The role that local area networks (LANs) play contributed by decreasing the dilemma encountered by managers of WOs and BOALs. Along with this, the continuous decrease in the price of micros could be considered one of the reasons for their extensive adoption [6]. Besides, "minis thrived in the 1970s because they cut costs by sharing expensive resources such as processing power and memory among dozens of office workers, each with a terminal. I wish this has been done before”.

As long as the IT revolution continues to be aggressive, end-user power to keep pace with changing circumstances by and large will depend on the management of data processing centers, and those who are responsible for the functional areas. Even though attempts have been made to describe the need for micros in data processing, concerned management of business organizations must consider: (1) the “where and where-not” in application of micros; (2) the anticipated benefits and drawbacks; (3) the role to be played by management of the data processing center, and (4) the indispensability of these devices.

## 2. Research Methodology

Prior to the formulation of a questionnaire, an analysis of the business process and contact with management of the data processing centers was considered appropriate for collecting preliminary data. In the meantime, randomly selected end-users have been asked the following type of questions:

(1) In your opinion, where and which work assignments seem appropriate for handling by micros?

(2) If your BOAL acquires some micros to augment the present data processing center's computer configuration, what benefits/drawbacks would you expect?

(3) Suppose you were one of the data processing center personnel, what type of help do you thin end-users will expect?

(4) Do you see any justification for the introduction of microcomputers? If so, what could they be? What are your suggestions?

This contact and the observations not only helped by collecting the necessary data, but also allowed us to determine the users and classify employees into one of the following functional groups (levels of management) [3]:

(1) Clerical-operational: consisting of bookkeepers, storekeepers, secretaries, data entry operators and computer operators.

(2) Technical-(non)professional: consisting of systems analysts, programmers, data base administrators, salesmen, engineers, and accountants.

(3) Departmental-managerial: including top management, directors, supervisors, department heads and line management.

Based on these groups and the preliminary data, a questionnaire made up of three parts was formulated:

(1) The Degree of Microcomputer Application: This part of the questionnaire was targeted at obtaining data/information from those employees (participants) whose work is supported by micros. Data and information collected includes: the type of activities they are performing with micros, the type of software packages they are using, the number of departments using the available micros in each BOAL, the type of activities they wish to see aided by micros, and the time needed to process using micros compared to that of the mainframe.

(2) The Degree of Acceptance: This part was designed to collect information on the features of micros as a means for data processing such as: costs of data processing, benefits and drawbacks, the role that micros play along with the existing mainframe, whether or not a LAN can be installed and connected with another network, security and privacy controls, etc.

(3) The Degree of Support: This was drafted to draw out information about the support that data base administrators, programmers, systems analysts, and information systems experts (MIS/DP staff) are giving to the end-users in order to accomplish their work assignment using micros. It was also designed to collect information about the plans, perspectives and possibilities of organizing seminars, courses, and the like to increase end-users productivity.

## 3. Research Findings

Of the 350 respondents 156 (41% clerical-operational, 28% technical-(non)professional, and 31% departmental-managerial level of management) supported both mainframe and micros as a means for data processing and for the executing the different work assignments. 56 respondents (12% clerical-operational, 64% technical-(non)professional, and 24% departmental-managerial) supported only a mainframe, while the remaining 138 (51% clerical-operational, 22% technical-(non)professional, and 27% departmental-managerial level of management) respondents supported only micros. The average number of micros that BOALs possessed was 9 and the median was 4. The average number of microcomputer users was 23, while the maximum number was 48 and the minimum 5. In one BOAL, 12 micros were attached to a single LAN. It was found that two BOALs had their 7 microcomputers connected to the mainframe.

Respondents in the different functional areas, as shown in Figure 2, were identified to discover the most significant activities performed by micros.

Respondents were asked to indicate the reasons why they decided to use a microcomputer intensively instead of the existing mainframe computer (see Figure 3). The most important factors were: (1) quick and easy to implement the application, (2) software is available, (3) it is user friendly, (4) it improves productivity and efficiency, (5) there is quick response, (6) it enhances privacy, and (7) lower cost is rated significant. About 12% of the respondents further specified other reasons, such as developing specific applications (decision support and expert systems). Respondents used micros an average of 12 hours per weeks (median = 3 hours), while the maximum time was 25 hours and the minimum 2 hours per week. Respondents were also asked to indicate the type of activities they are performing using micros and the time they spent per week on each of ten major category of applications. In order of their significance, they are: word processing, spreadsheet, data entry, transaction processing, own program developing, graphics, inquires and responses, other package program manipulation, electronic mail, and mainframe connection. Above all, the respondents used an average of 4.25 of applications (median = 3). Table 2 shows the average time (hours) spent per week on each application for the functional areas of the entire COAL. Figure 4 shows the percentage of respondents who reported usage of each type of application in descending order of use.

## 3.1. Microcomputer Application

Although end-users mainframe usage had been playing an important role in computing business data, the emergence of micros appears to have introduced low cost computing with easy to enter data; the user is often self-taught and is now independent from the central COAL information system bureaucracy. Hence, it is not surprising to find extensive application of micros. Being so, 95% of the respondents indicated that word processing is the most dominant application used to accomplish their day-to-day activities. Above all, the clerical-operational level of management staff spent significantly great amounts of time (an average of 4.75 hours per week) on this application. Spreadsheet came next with 92% of the respondents: the technical-(non)professional level management staff were the most frequent users. They spent an average of 4.72 hours per week on this application. It was also found that departmental-managerial level of management spent greater amount of time on spreadsheet and graphics (an average of 2.96 and 2.94 hours per week respectively). Along with this, the results in Table 2 show that the staff of the clerical-operational level of management averaged 10.35 hours micro applications usage per week. Technical-(non)professional averaged approximately 2 hours per week less than the clerical-operational staff. Although the departmental-managerial level of management spent a great amount of time on the different application (4.23 hours per week), differences with the other two levels were vivid and significant.

Respondents from 14 BOALs confirmed that they have direct access to the mainframe through terminals, while the remainder are subjected to batch operation. Respondents of the BOALs with direct access to the mainframe accounted for 62.3%. These respondents were found to spent significantly more time with micros than with mainframe manipulation and computing (an average of 5.4 vs. 2.6 hours per week respectively). It was also found that respondents from the MIS/DP center were the only staff who spent significantly more time with the mainframe than with microcomputer computing (a median of 22 vs. 3 hours per week, respectively).

## 3.2. The Degree of Acceptance

Consciously or unconsciously, when new technologies are introduced, a few employees try to build stumbling blocks in order to prevent successful implementation $[14]$ . This, in many cases, is due to worries about job security and inadequate understanding of how these new technologies are capable of upgrading efficiency and productivity. The same also happened with micros. Acceptance of micros as support for data processing and execution of day-to-day work assignments, as perceived by respondents, is due to several factors that can be classified as follows:

1. Cost of Data Processing: Respondents were asked to indicate whether it is or is not advisable to process data using micros. Although none of the respondents performed a cost-benefit analysis to justify the implementation of micros, about 27% thought that the use of micros has to some extent added extra expenses due to new applications development, and purchase of software. These, they commented, could have been eliminated by using existing applications already developed and housed with the mainframe. On the other hand, about 68% of the respondents perceived micros as more cost effective for their kind of applications. Respondents whose data processing activities were based on a batch access mode perceived microcomputers as cost effective only in terms of response time and cost of communication.

2. Benefits: When IT has been introduced as support to the data processing needs of end-users, it has been done with the expectation that its benefits will overweigh its drawbacks. Being so, respondents are asked to state in descending count the benefits that micros offer. Almost 99% of the respondents agreed that the very first benefit that they identified was quick and easy implementation of micros, followed by software availability, user friendliness, improvement of productivity and efficiency, quick response time, independent and self-development, simple self-teaching procedure and lower costs and economics. About 53% of the respondents believed that micros could be used not only by one single user but by a group of users for implementing DSS and expert systems thereby providing a direct control to each one of the users.

3. A Role to be Played in Conjunction With the Mainframe: Respondents were asked to indicate the role that the micros played along with the mainframe. 90.3% of the respondents indicated that they found it easier to manipulate spreadsheet, word processing, and graphics with micros than with a mainframe. This, they indicated, will minimize mainframe's workload and response time to a large extent. One marketing first-line supervisor said: "After all, we are buying and selling time". Besides this, 76% of the respondents indicated that the micros at the top of their desks would have been more effective had they been linked as intelligent node to the mainframe in a LAN, thereby providing access to the COAL and external data bases.

4. Installation of LAN: In organization like COAL, where top management is indifferent to the thorough introduction of micros, it is unlikely that end-user are aware of LANs unless some beneficial “sales” demonstration has been given. This was true in some organizations surveyed. Respondents were asked to state the importance of installing LAN in order to integrate all micros across the WOs. Only 18.8% of the respondents felt that LAN is extremely important, if an organization possesses 5 or more micros. The remaining 81% were indifferent to LANs. The manager of one BOAL that possesses a LAN said, “we receive a significant amount of data from every corner of this BOAL and make it available to others who will use it for different purposes. We do this efficiently through our micros, which are tied together by the single LAN”. He further commented that, “as long as micros are not integrated through a LAN, they are not in a position to upgrade multi-user capability, and I can’t be sure that they are cost-effective. To eliminate this dilemma they must be tied together by a LAN”.

5. Drawbacks: Regardless of the numerous advantages, respondents were aware of some drawbacks that micros brought. In spite of this, respondents were asked to indicate the significant drawbacks they noticed. About 21.6% of the respondents indicated that the available memory, storage capacity, and lack of more complex programs were very significant drawbacks. About 27.3% were extremely concerned with the problems of security and privacy. 18.8% commented on the lack of LANs that could have tied together the dispersed micros. “Micros must be tied together by a LAN if we want to share disk storage space, data, and peripherals, otherwise the investment should be written off (in advance) as a bad debt”, said a head of marketing department of a tourist WO. 13% of the respondents were concerned about the impossibility of direct manipulation of some of the software packages to and from the mainframe, while the remaining 19% were worried about the lack of specialists who could have dealt with the large variety of problems they and other end-users were facing.

## 3.3. The Degree of Support

Since the support which MIS/DP staff offers play an important role in end-user micro computing and usage $[7]$ , it is worthwhile to look at other characteristics of MIS/DP personnel support. Interestingly enough, while much resistance to the introduction of micros had been expected from the clerical-operational management staff due to the technological surplus that micros could impose, it was found that about 38% of the respondents from the managerial-departmental hierarchy were totally against the introduction of micros, even though these same people agitated for more efficiency and productivity. It would be paradoxical to expect progress in micros usage unless some immediate actions had been taken in support of the changing technological adaptations and adjustments to the environment $[2]$ . It was noticed that respondents were not overwhelmingly satisfied with other support for their micros. 15.2% of the respondents indicated that they were “completely dissatisfied” with their micros. 26.7% indicated “not quite satisfied”, 32% stated “quite satisfied”, and 25% indicated that they were “completely satisfied”. Respondents indicated that their dissatisfactions were the result of not being offered even minimum help from the MIS/DP personnel. Only 13% of the respondents appreciated the help they received from the MIS/DP center. About 42% were able to satisfy themselves from manuals and documentation materials offered to them by hardware vendors and software suppliers. 33% indicated that they received support from professional journals and magazines. 11% of the respondents indicated that they got help from (or useful exchanges with) colleagues within the same BOAL or WO. As far as technical assistance was concerned, about 52% of the respondents relied on vendors, whereas whereas 31% indicated that they relied on organizations that offer microcomputer services outside their organization, and 15% indicated that they relied on in-house MIS/DP staff experts.

It was found that the majority of the respondents surveyed possessed limited or no prior microcomputer or other computer knowledge. 63% of the respondents did not even participate in any one of the many seminars organized by the COAL. 17% of the respondents indicated that they attended at least one, whereas 19% indicated that they attended more than two seminars or short-term courses.

## 4. Suggestions for End-User

In spite of the various advantages enjoyed and the stumbling blocks experienced, respondents were asked to give suggestions they believe important to other end-users who are intending to introduce micros for data processing. The highlighted suggestions include:

1. Determine the Type of Task to be Handled by Micros: It was established that partial dissatisfaction was the result of not knowing what type of applications (activities) should be handled by micros and what type by the mainframe. 43% of the respondents preferred their data processing activities completely removed from the mainframe, regardless of the difficulties they might encounter. One experienced mainframe/micro user suggested that, “only word processing, spreadsheet, and electronic mail could be handled by micros, while others should be handled by the mainframe, due to security reasons”. A manager of one WO suggested that, “we must first determine what tasks we should process with micros. Users think that every job could be done with micros: this is wrong. The MIS/DP department must plan and explain to end-users the pros and cons of totally micro based data processing”.

2. Select the Right Hardware and Suitable Software: Due to the many types of micros that respondents possessed, the relationship between hardware and users satisfaction appeared complicated; major factors were the variety of tasks each BOAL wanted to process and insufficient hard disk capacity of each of the micros intended to handle users data. "I wanted to use the SPSS package extensively, however, I can't use it because the RAM of my micro is only 256K. I suggest that users must first check the characteristics of micros before they plan to buy", said one respondent from a research and development department. Even though there was no significant difference in the degree of user satisfaction with the available software, respondents suggested that end-user should buy those software packages that have easily available complete reference and text books. “You can’t use your software package efficiently by only relying on vendor’s software tutorials”, said one member of a MIS/DP department personnel.

3. General Support: About 81% of the respondents suggested that end-user microcomputer computing and usage could be successful, when top management at least tries its best to give support in general. This should happen particularly when a MIS/DP department designates personnel to advise and aid end-users in converting data bases, testing application programs, installing networks, etc. It should also help through training session on how to use micros and how to select hardware/software.

4. Security Control Requirements: Micros and data that stored on them need to be protected from physical damage, theft, and intentional disclosure of data by unauthorized users [8]. Since data is more exposed to damage and danger than the hardware itself, it is important to establish tight security control mechanisms. All respondents agreed that the loss of fundamental records, files, customer and creditor accounts, payroll and research records, DSS models and the like might even cost more than merely that of recovering the lost data. Respondents also indicated that such a loss might even endanger the existence of the entire COAL. To summarize, their suggestions include: (a) end-user should be made responsible for the hardware, the data, and the software they utilize, (b) implement the use of password mechanism, (c) keep duplicate files of important data and application programs, and (d) build suitable safeguard mechanism to protect data from unauthorized users and uses.

## 5. Conclusions

The growth of end-user computing through micros demands that more attention be paid by management. In this experiment, an attempt was made to figure out the potential dilemma of the end-users as well as management of 27 BOALs. The findings point to three key issues for top management in general and MIS/DP personnel in particular: (1) The most important reason for using micros is to satisfy the end-user computing needs.

Micros are nothing more than tools to support user data processing activities. It is important to find a way to integrate this IT effectively and efficiently into user activities or applications. (2) Micros and mainframes can coexist, mutually interact, and be integrated within ever larger network by means of a LAN. Also, the implementation of a LAN could lead to local integration, with data exchange between the mainframe and micros. (3) It was recognized that top management and MIS/DP personnel support is necessary if micros are to achieve their intended benefits of increasing productivity and efficiency. Thus, management should give every necessary support to upgrade end-user usage and computing by organizing seminars, short-courses, etc.

The research has attempted provide to future microcomputer implementors with necessary guidelines for introducing them into the organizations. In this regard, the research has significant implications, and has also identified the important issues for future research on the dilemma of microcomputer acquisition for data processing activities.
