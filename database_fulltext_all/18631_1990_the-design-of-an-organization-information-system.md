## Introduction

The primacy of Management Theory among the schools of thought which provide the IS discipline with its present foundations (Culnan, 1986 and 1987; Culnan and Swanson, 1986) explains why the term management information systems (MIS) is still considered almost synonymous to that of information systems (IS). When IS's are regarded as MIS's, an information system is defined in terms of the informational needs of the organization and of the managers that it serves. MIS's, DSS's and other information systems are mere “adjuncts” to the organization. As noted by Le Moigne (1973, 1984, 1985b), this partial view of an information system fails to take into account the multifaceted aspects of information technology and disregards the true role which an information system plays, or will play, in the context of the modern organization.

This paper shows how the traditional MIS view of an information system can be modified to encompass the whole organization in which the IS is embedded, to the point that organization and information system operate as one intelligent system.

## Designing intelligent artifacts

Until recently, the IS discipline seemed to be “technology driven”. It is as though it lay in wait for events to happen in the industry, instead of leading the way and spearheading the development of innovative IS designs that could enhance and bolster the human being’s ability to reason and think. To date, with a few exceptions, present day artifacts have merely provided humankind with greater and faster data processing capability than were previously available. In spite of noteworthy recent cases, it has not ventured to “extend” human intelligence or take the human element into account (Le Moigne, 1974; Simon, 1977; Landry & Le Moigne, 1977; Le Moigne and Sibley, 1986c).

This paper stems from a desire to correct the present imbalance and to recognize the fundamental importance of recent developments in Organization Theory and in IS design. To start this process, it is timely and fitting to turn for new inspiration to Simon's (1981) Sciences of the Artificial.

(a) H.A. Simon's “Information Processing Paradigm” underlies his Theory of Design by which “the artificial” can be created. We shall describe this line of inquiry by citing Simon's results (1977, 1979, 1981a, 1982).

In most systems, according to Simon, natural attributes “are inextricably blended” with artificial attributes. Natural properties are those that actually pertain to, and can be observed in, real world phenomena. Artificial properties are the result of human intervention: we design an artifact which imitates and behaves like a natural system.

(b) When this approach is applied to the design of IS's, Simon postulates hypotheses that govern the information and cognitive processes of natural systems (i.e., human beings). Knowledge about these processes is then used to imitate these natural systems with artificial, information processing systems which exhibit the same behaviors, capabilities, or skills.

If one adopts this approach, IS's are not defined by “what they are” in the organization. Instead, we focus attention in acquiring an understanding of the logics underlying information processing (as exemplified in human thinking) and then try to formalize this knowledge in the form of computing processes and programs. The result is to design IS's which “imitate” the information processing functions of human beings or of social organizations. An information processing system is an artifact designed to carry out symbol-manipulating processes similar to those in representations of complex systems. Simon's entire approach rests on Newell and Simon's (1976) hypothesis that complex systems, such as the human brain, can be modeled and operated as “a physical symbol processing system”. The hypothesis asserts “that [our understanding and] the availability of the basic symbol-manipulating processes [that are used by computers], provide the necessary and sufficient conditions for an [artificial] system to exhibit [and to be endowed with] intelligence”. (Simon, 1981b).

It is clear, that the ultimate aim is to devise information systems artifacts which can help social organizations attain the same (or better) goals as they do (or would do) in reality. In other words, an IS must be an artifact which incorporates the organization's intelligence.

Once Simon's outlook is adopted, with the IS deliberately designed to exhibit the information processing functions and thinking skills of the individuals in the organization, the information system appears to have acquired a life of its own. At the “representation level”, the IS becomes synonymous with the organization: instead of being considered separately and independently from it, by virtue of deliberate design, it is now endowed with the individual and collective intelligence of the organization.

(c) In Simon's conceptualization of the design process, knowledge of human thinking and reasoning processes can be modeled through the design of intelligent systems. Simon's approach allows a quantum leap in the conceptual mechanism of inquiry. Heretofore, the properties and "behavior" of organizational IS's are inferred from a study of their implementation and operation in the organization. On the other hand, the line of inquiry suggested by Simon's Theory of Artifacts, implies a system's design with capabilities beyond those that exist in any system already built: by inferring what the system ought to be like (the prescriptive mode) instead of what it is like or what we observe (the descriptive mode), we postulate the existence of IS's with greater capabilities than any yet implemented or even invented or designed. Simon's approach looks for generalizable processes that can be programmed in artifacts to exhibit "natural behavior". In particular, it opens the way to consider an "Organization Information System" (OIS) as much more than a "management information system" (MIS). It allows us to assume the existence of capabilities such as memorization, communications, control, self-regulation, maintenance, self-testing and other cognitive functions which cannot be conceived in the traditional MIS. (Le Moigne, 1985a, 1986a; Le Moigne and Pascot, 1979).

In summary, Simon's Theory emphasizes the importance of design (instead of analysis), of endowing artifacts with thinking and reasoning skills and organizations with teleogical cognitive behavior. Instead of relegating the IS to the role of an organizational subsystem, it makes the IS and the organization coincident. Thus the organization can be represented by its information system which, by deliberate design, is “artificially” endowed with intelligence and design capabilities. Therefore: the organization becomes an intelligent artifact.

## The New Systemic Model Versus The Cybernetic Model

## The Organization as a Complex System

In order to better understand the evolution from MIS to OIS, it is important to recall that the traditional cybernetic model is slowly being replaced by the systemic organization model. See Figure 1.

In the latter, the organization is a complex system. It is, at the same time: (a) operating and transforming itself; (b) organizing and self-organizing; (c) informed and informing; (d) autonomous and dependant upon its environment; (e) goal-seeking as well as establishing its own teleology; (f) ordering as well as self-ordering (Morin, 1977 and 1980; van Gigch, 1978).

In this new view, the organization is not merely considered an entity or a state, but must be understood as resulting from the irreducible, recursive and conflictual conjunction of three fundamental functions:

1. To maintain and self-maintain (or maintain itself),

2. To produce and self-produce (autopoiesis),

3. To relate and self-relate (self-reference).

This new view affects Organization Theory as well as the paradigm of the IS discipline (van Gigch and Pipino, 1986; van Gigch and Le Moigne, 1988).

The Organization: A Process Rather Than a Product

Traditionally, the organization has been viewed as a highly structured “social construct”. Actors have attempted to formulate a control system to manage their “territorial imperatives” where they exercised their capacity to bargain and negotiate. According to the modern view, the organization is an “organizing social construct” which defines itself as a complex interaction, and on the basis of this, the organization establishes its own autonomy.

This autonomy is ephemeral. It constantly and continuously, reestablish itself in a different configuration. The organization is no longer defined or identified in terms of the traditional management functions, such as decision making or problem solving. Rather, it is considered as a process through which it is formalized, transaction by transaction, in the course of its operations.

## Circulating versus Generic Information

The organization establishes the “traces” of its own activities: a shipment, an order, an invoice, a bank draft, or a sales receipt are all deliberate and artificial traces, whose production are indispensable to the smooth enfolding of the organization’s activities. These traces are information which evolves or emerges, as the enterprise carries out its daily activities.

Generic information is that which is “generated” by the organization’s own actions and which, initially, does not necessarily exist. In contradistinction, circulating information originates from outside the organization.

Generic information is characterized by the organization which creates it: as a result of the organization's own activities. OIS's have a fundamental role to play in the elaboration of generic information. Indeed, this may be one of the OIS's most important functions; in particular, if we agree that the formulation of a piece of information by a system and its subsequent memorization, constitute an inseparable demonstration of artificial organizational action. (Le Moigne, 1986b).

Research in knowledge representation also becomes relevant. It is important to understand how an organization represents itself with a code particular to itself; it is shared by all its actors and participants, transaction by transaction, transformation by transformation. The OIS paradigm considers how these transactions can be represented by a symbol--a question which the old MIS paradigm readily ignored. Representing a transaction by a symbol not only creates a code that can be transmitted (in Shannon's sense), it also creates something new and generates meaning: producing generic information, as well as developing a new semantic and pragmatic field, in which intelligence can move without constraints.

Memorization of Generic and Circulating Information

Organizations grow progressively more complex. As actions and interrelationships among actors take place, organizations require information to carry out such functions as regulation and control. The production of information necessitates its memorization as well as the operation of a storage system, which grows more complex with the life of the organization. Therefore, it is clear that an OIS not only involves the production of generic information, but also the design of models for memorization. To date, such processes are still not entirely understood. Many questions remain unresolved: Can we have an organization without memory? Can we have communications without memory? We have an imperfect knowledge and understanding of the effect of recent advances in memorization on the amplification and degradation of organizational functions.

Le Moigne (1981) coined the phrase: “To organize is to co-memorize”, by which he meant that as the organization evolves it generates its own history. We still know very little about the conditions and modes of co-memorization.

It is interesting to note that memorization is not merely the retention and storage of circulating information. It also involves addressing, association, accessibility, and actualization of information--all problems of the type which, nowadays, are related to the realization and use of modern data bases. (Le Moigne and van Gigch, 1989).

## Changes in Paradigm and Technology

Progressing from the cybernetic to systemic view, leads from a conceptual model of the organization centered on the control system, to one centered on memorization. This progression from the MIS to the OIS paradigm also implies a change of view which favors almost exclusively computational functions and ignores the memorization function – which was merely considered an adjunct to calculation (Ashby, 1956).

## Some Implications for OIS Designers

Whereas, on the surface, transition from MIS to OIS paradigms may appear to be purely conceptual, it has a profound effect on the behavioral aspects of the IS discipline. These can be summarized as:

## 1. Broadening the universe of discourse

Given that “management” is only one of the aspects of an organization, a change of emphasis of the discipline from focusing on the management aspect to one that encompasses the whole organizational perspective, broadens the universe of discourse. (Le Moigne and van Gigch, 1989) (van Gigch, 1989a and 1989b).

As a direct corollary, the discipline should adopt the broader perspective which considers IS's as OIS, rather than strictly as MIS.

## 2. Emphasis on the design of artifacts

The application of Simon's ideas of the Artificial emphasizes the importance of endowing the organization with cognitive behavior and representing it by its IS. As a result, the organization becomes an intelligent artifact.

## 3. Replacement of the traditional cybernetic organization model

Modern Organization Theory considers the organization as a complex system resulting from the conjunction of recursive functions that define it as a process rather than as an fixed entity or state. As a consequence of this epistemological shift, the organization (and by derivation the intelligent artifact which represents it) cannot be modeled in terms of a well structured, well behaved control system.

Rather, the organization and the OIS must be represented as ill-defined, conflicting, operating systems, where heuristics replace algorithms, possibilities replace probabilities, and ambiguities dominate over uncertainties (van Gigch, 1987a).

4. Organizations design their own teleological behavior

OIS designers must recognize the cognitive capabilities of the organization as a man-made artifact and as a complex system with reflexive power over its own behavior, organization, autonomy, teleology, and order.

## 5. Organizations utilize generic and circulating information

Designers of OIS must consider the emergence of generic information generated by the daily activities and transactions of the organization. It must be formalized and formatted through conceptual models of internal data; the organization memorizes generic as well as circulating information.

## 6. The complexity of the traditional organizational model

The classical interpretation of regulation and control, with built-in check-and-balance computations, must be augmented by the knowledge that the organization is capable of determining its own behavior and computing its own equilibrium, balancing abrupt changes with adaptation.

## 7. Memorization versus computational processes

Whereas, the MIS paradigm emphasizes the computational and data processing roles, the OIS paradigm places increasing emphasis on the role of memorization in communication processes. Improved technologies will draw attention to the importance of memories for effective organizational competence, in spite of the fact that, as organizations grow more complex, our understanding of how they function may diminish.

## 8. Role of symbol representation in knowledge acquisition

OIS designers will internalize, in their IS Newell and Simon's Physical Symbol System Hypothesis. This stipulates that the design of such special artifacts as intelligent machines must endow them with the following capabilities: a) knowledge representation through symbols and symbolic structures and, b) operating processes by which these symbols and structures can be manipulated and modified, in order to obtain valid semantic interpretations leading to intelligence.

## Conclusions

Designers of information systems must abandon the restricted MIS perspective in favor of the OIS perspective. The former is grounded in obsolete notions and narrow views of the organization and of the roles and functions that IS's play in these organizations. The OIS perspective is grounded in the theory of intelligent artifacts and in the systemic models of organizations. OIS designers must ponder the various implications of these advances when formulating the design of intelligent information systems to cope with the complexities and challenges of the 21st Century.
