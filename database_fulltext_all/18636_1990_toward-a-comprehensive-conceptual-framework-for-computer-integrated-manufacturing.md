# Toward a Comprehensive Conceptual Framework for Computer Integrated Manufacturing

William I. Bullers, Jr. and Richard A. Reid
Anderson Schools of Management, University of New Mexico,
Albuquerque, NM 87131, USA

Effective Computer Integrated Manufacturing (CIM) requires computer software to control automated processes, regulate production facilities, and generate information to support operational, tactical, and strategic decision making. An analysis of decision problems, managerial functions, and information systems is a prerequisite for the design of a CIM system. Four basic types of IS are analyzed as potential components in a CIM design methodology which seeks to integrate computerized manufacturing systems and management information systems.

Keywords: Computer Integrated Manufacturing (CIM), Computerized Manufacturing Systems (CMS), Electronic Data Processing (EDP), Management Information Systems (MIS), Decision Support Systems (DSS), Expert Systems (ES), CIM and MIS Relationships, Manufacturing Information Systems Architecture, Conceptual CIM Framework, CIM Control and Decision Support.

Conceptually, integration in a manufacturing system warrants consideration from several perspectives. Horizontal integration involves coordination among the manufacturing functions, such as raw material procurement, production scheduling, quality control, and product distribution. Multi-level operations encompassing manufacturing facilities, shops, cells, work stations, and equipment characterize CIM systems $[18]$ . They require complex, highly coordinated, hierarchical control $[9–11,13,16,17,23]$ . Vertical integration allows the capabilities of the four major types of IS: electronic data processing (EDP), traditional MIS, decision support systems (DSS), and expert systems (ES), to be accessed by managers throughout the organization. Temporal integration allows consideration of the past, present, and future so that decision making insights gleaned from one production planning period can impact planning in subsequent time intervals. Finally, physical integration promotes cooperation between geographically dispersed production facilities.

Competitive advantages accrue to those organizations that are able to design and implement a comprehensive CIM architecture. The purpose of this paper is to evaluate the capabilities and limitations of the four types of IS for possible inclusion in a comprehensive framework of CIM.

## CIM Control and Decision Making Characteristics

The determination of control and decision support objectives in CIM involves the direct consideration of regulatory and decision making activities. A three dimensional framework will be used to descriptively analyze organizational decision making processes $[2,5,8,19,26]$ . In particular, the dimensions allow decisions to be classified by type, process stage, and hierarchical level.

Decision type ranges from structured to unstructured, depending on whether the decision making process can be described in detail before a decision is made [24]. Structured decisions are routine and repetitive decisions for which the problem can be anticipated and an appropriate response programmed. Such decisions often involve predefined objectives, constraints, data requirements, and data flows. A set of predefined responses can be assembled into a standard operating procedure. Unstructured decisions involve problems which cannot be preformulated, because they have not arisen before, are unusually complex, or are so important that special considerations are required. Human judgment plays a critical role in unstructured decision making. Semi-structured decisions span the continuum between highly structured and completely unstructured problems. Significant problem solving expertise is often required in identifying an effective solution to a semi-structured problem, and, as such, forms the basis for the development of many “expert systems” [15].

Process stage refers to the specific sequential step of interest within the decision making process. Traditionally, the decision making process has been partitioned into four stages [24,19]: intelligence, design, choice, and implementation. Intelligence refers to problem identification and definition, often a result of a manager's search of the organization's internal and external environment for conditions calling for decisions. Data are gathered, processed, and evaluated in a search for potential problems and/or opportunities. Design represents the generation of alternative courses of action to solve the problem. The decision maker attempts to understand the problem, synthesize potential solutions, and test the solutions for feasibility. Choice is the selection of a particular course of action. The choice phase includes detailed analysis and evaluation of the alternative solutions. Implementation requires putting the chosen solution into effect. Although this last stage can be viewed as part of choice, it is considered separately because of its significance in differentiating between control and support functions.

From a classical perspective, the CIM control function involves monitoring the outcome of a CMS process, comparing the results to some previously established standard of performance, and making necessary adjustments to bring the system's output more closely in line with desired performance. Thus, control in a CMS application focuses on those situations in which the IS automatically executes all four stages in the decision making process. In essence, monitoring of the CMS output is a particular type of intelligence activity, while making necessary decision making adjustments requires activities in design, choice, and implementation. Therefore, if all four stages can be completely automated so that human intervention is not required, it is appropriate to design an automatic control IS. In contrast to typical CMS operations, however, control is not relinquished to machines for many MIS applications. In typical MIS, one or more stages in the decision making process is usually delegated to a human decision maker; thus a decision support IS is required. Figure 2 illustrates the difference between automatic control and decision support in IS design.

Hierarchical level of managerial activity corresponds to a widely-used planning and control taxonomy [1], with its levels of strategic planning, management planning and control, and operational control. Strategic planning at the manufacturing facility level involves the determination of the manufacturing system goals, the identification of production resources required to achieve those goals, and the development of organizational policies required to manage the manufacturing resources. Management planning and control of manufacturing operations in the organization's facilities, shops, and cells involves the effective selection and utilization of production resources (including CMSs) required to accomplish the manufacturing system goals. Operational control is concerned with efficient operation for all production capabilities, from the facility level through individual work station and equipment items, as required, to insure appropriate material and process flow.

As an integrated whole, this three dimensional framework for specifying the type, stage, and level of decisions involved in manufacturing management allows the nature of CIM system decision requirements to be readily identified. Once specified, the CMS capabilities required to control shop floor and manufacturing cell tasks, or MIS capabilities required to support decision processes can be examined and the overall CIM system design process can commence. A process framework for partitioning CIM system requirements into CMS control and MIS decision support capabilities is critical in the identification of an appropriate CIM conceptual system structure.

## Critical Features of the Four Basic Types of IS

Meaningful information processing is a prerequisite for effective decision making in a CIM environment. A descriptive analysis of IS functions, components, and automatic control or decision support implications is essential to the prescriptive design of the MIS components of a CIM system. Specifying the proper IS type will promote the provision of effective CMS control and support efficacious decision making in production management for CIM.

## Electronic Data Processing (EDP) Systems

Almost exclusively, EDP systems automate structured clerical tasks within an operational control field. In general, they automate the transaction processing, record storage and retrieval, and periodic report generation functions.

In contrast to the highly structured nature of many decisions in these transaction dominated applications, EDP systems usually do not control their host organizational system. Since control requires that the system execute the intelligence, design, choice, and implementation stages in the decision making process without human intervention, EDP systems rarely qualify as automatic control systems. Lacking a true control capability, many EDP systems can be appropriately considered as narrowly focused DSS. Two examples are presented to illustrate the decision assistance nature of EDP systems.

Employee Scheduling System. The basic task of such a system may be the assignment of employees to work stations to meet production demands. The intelligence stage, recognition of the need for an employee to be assigned to a given work station, must have been carried out prior to invocation of the system. Upon activation, it may generate a list of possible workers (design) who meet feasibility criteria. Yet the final selection may be made by the shop scheduler in conjunction with the scheduling system. Thus it provides decision support for the choice stage, but does not provide automatic scheduling.

Inventory Control System. The focus of this system is the identification of items which need to be reordered (intelligence), the production of a list of possible suppliers (design), and the preparation of a purchase order to a particular supplier based on predefined criteria (choice). However, final implementation of the order is usually subjected to human review prior to being sent to a vendor or placed on the master production schedule. Although such a system comes close to providing automatic control, it must be considered a low level DSS, because the order is not automatically issued. Thus, implementation remains an unautomated stage in this decision making process.

The principal components of an EDP system usually include data collection procedures and a library of programs for data manipulation. More recent EDP systems may take advantage of a data base management system (DBMS) to support more sophisticated data organization and manipulation. In all EDP systems, the vast majority of the transaction processing is performed on a periodic basis by specially written programs designed to perform highly structured tasks.

A CMS performs some EDP tasks, but unlike most administrative data processing systems, the CMS provides complete control, at least under normal operating circumstances, even though certain decisions may be overridden by a human. ## Management Information Systems (MIS)

Traditionally, MIS have provided the capability to perform highly structured tasks as well as limited support for decision making. The major difference between production-oriented MIS and EDP systems resides in the managerial orientation of the MIS output and the degree of integration across the manufacturing functional areas. Whereas EDP system output is regularly utilized by operations management personnel, MIS output is generally intended for middle managers at the shop or facility level within a factory.

Requirements in CIM for increased decision support have redirected MIS toward a greater information focus. The management-by-exception principle has been used to provide information to managers without overloading them with irrelevant data. The implementation of information filters has helped to identify conditions calling for decisions (intelligence). MIS reports provide significant decision support activities under the auspices of the intelligence stage in a manager's decision making process. For example, a budget variance report only identifies a situation requiring managerial attention. Frequently, support for the final three stages in the decision making process (design, choice, and implementation) is not provided by the MIS. As an example, it does not provide support in the design of alternative strategies to alleviate work station cost overruns, nor does it assist in the evaluation and selection of an appropriate response when carrying out a corrective action.

The integration of management science approaches, statistical analysis methods, and operations research techniques into automated manufacturing systems $[7,25]$ can help provide direct support for manufacturing management decisions. Analytical tools contribute valuable assistance in the choice stage. Determining the optimal product mix with linear programming, manufacturing resource planning, assembly line balancing, and production process simulation are a few examples of models that can be embedded in an MIS to identify preferred choices from among alternative actions in a CIM environment.

In MIS, a greater emphasis is directed toward incorporating a DBMS. Its use is necessary to support those data relationships needed in the integrated context of CIM. Besides providing data relatability, a DBMS is also useful in supporting special reporting requests and inquiry response. ## Decision Support Systems (DSS)

Improved decision making has provided the impetus for allowing DSS to emerge as a distinct class of IS for manufacturing management. DSS tend to be more flexible, allowing management science and operations research models to be embedded in the IS for use in a variety of situations not anticipated when the IS was initially designed. Much of the DSS flexibility is the result of IS designers focusing attention on the three dimensions of decision making. The resulting DSS has a greater capacity to handle those less well-structured problems which are prevalent in strategic planning for CIM facilities, as well as in managerial control of CIM shops and cells.

From a comparative perspective, DSS incorporate a more sophisticated set of software. This upgraded capability often involves both data base and model base components, with an easy-to-use communication network designed to promote active involvement by the typical manager. Observed characteristics include [26]: (1) a definite emphasis on supporting the resolution of semi- or unstructured problems that are typically encountered by upper level managers; (2) the availability of combinations of models and analytical techniques with traditional data access and retrieval functions; (3) a focus on user-friendly features in an interactive mode; and (4) an orientation toward flexibility and adaptability to accommodate changes in the environment and the decision making approach of the individual manager.

The structure of a DSS within a CIM environment is illustrated in Figure 5. The dialog manager accepts either a data entry request or a model creation request from the CIM user. Data base entries update historical records or environmental information profiles. The model base provides interactive design capabilities which allow an analytical representation of the CIM problem to be considered. Interaction between the data and model bases provides the capacity to cycle through the normal building, validating, experimenting, and evaluating process. Predicted results and/or decision making guidelines are finally extracted via decision support reports.

DSS can make significant contributions in the automated factory by assisting managers to carry out their responsibilities. Assistance associated with the intelligence stage include: structuring, producing, and storing specially designed reports to monitor CMS work station, manufacturing cell, and facility performance; conducting descriptive statistical analyses on shop floor operational data to identify conditions that may warrant further investigation, etc. DSS functions related to the design stage may involve constructing alternative analytical models for forecasting or performing statistical analyses on historical performance data with regression models. Decision support activities related to the choice stage generally involve assessment functions, including the development of solutions with operations research models or the evaluation of alternatives via Monte Carlo simulation. Examples in the choice stage include: conducting a financial analysis of different automated flexible manufacturing systems [21] and assessing operational performance under a variety of designs using automated guided vehicles in plant modernization [3]. The final stage of decision making, implementing a selected course of action, usually requires some human involvement. Thus, most DSS do not support automated control in the factory, since implementation is rarely performed by the DSS.

## Expert Systems (ES)

The application of artificial intelligence techniques in narrowly defined, yet difficult, problem areas have produced ES. These systems are often considered to be knowledge-based, because they incorporate previously identified and carefully selected facts, beliefs, and heuristics in analyzing a problem situation. The effective application of knowledge to issues of planning and control in manufacturing should address the kinds of relatively unstructured problems encountered in CIM [4,10,11,13].

The basic characteristics of a knowledge-based ES include [15]: (1) the effective performance of critical activities that are usually assigned to highly experienced and acknowledged experts; (2) focus on narrowly defined decision making strategies in contrast to more general problem-solving frameworks; (3) the utilization of knowledge to resolve dilemmas or develop solutions through inference processes with conclusions being explicitly justified by expert systems; and (4) the solution of problems associated with the following general activities – interpretation, prediction, diagnosis, debugging, design, planning, monitoring, repair, instruction, and control.

The three major ES components for CIM are a data base, model base, and problem processor for the manufacturing system. An ES problem processor dynamically combines data and models through a combination of general inference control strategies and explicit knowledge related to the manufacturing facilities. Thus, the data base, model base, and processing capability constitute a CIM knowledge base that may be applied to solve problems. The general structure of an ES for CIM is shown in Figure 6. In particular, the knowledge base is partitioned into declarative knowledge (reference data or facts about the manufacturing and organizational systems), control knowledge (general control strategies for problem solving), and procedural knowledge (operational rules describing operation of the CMS and managerial decision making processes). The emphasis on knowledge processing, in contrast to data or information processing, is the major difference between an ES and other IS components in a CIM system.

Most manufacturing ES have focused on decisions at the operational level; however, major benefits are also available from an ES component in a CIM system at the strategic planning and manufacturing management control levels. Decisions faced by senior level executives are often complex and non-routine, and therefore, are frequently unsupported by existing information systems. Thus, significant opportunities exist for the developers of higher level manufacturing-oriented ES.

## Summary and Conclusions

Control and decision support, as idealized in CIM, require integration at a variety of levels and with a number of perspectives; for example, manufacturing cells and work stations need to be integrated into CMS in order to perform automated manufacturing tasks. Furthermore, the integration of CMS with various functional area information systems is required to support planning and control activities throughout every manufacturing organization. Still other dimensions associated with the CIM environment also need to be considered from an integrative standpoint when designing a CIM system.

First, the successful integration of CMS and MIS requires the consideration of control and decision making activities. Explicit examination of decision type, decision making process stage, and hierarchical level of managerial activity in CIM system design has led to the development of somewhat disparate information systems. However, it is necessary to recognize that CIM is best embodied by a synthesis of all types of information systems with their corresponding manufacturing system elements. The integration of the various IS capabilities is paramount to achieving a truly comprehensive CIM system.

A common database, integrating data from functionally-oriented information systems, provides the basis for standardized MIS reports. These are certainly necessary in a CIM environment, but they do not provide complete support for manufacturing management in the dynamic environment of CIM. DSS overcome some of the MIS shortcomings by integrating data and model management components to provide more flexible, decision-oriented information processing capabilities. Dialog management components, which are also a part of DSS, provide user-friendly access to the integrated data-model components. However, DSS still requires the manufacturing manager to select the proper combination of operational data, analysis techniques, and reporting programs for a given problem situation encountered in the factory. ES combine knowledge with the data and model management components of DSS to enable the IS itself to select the data and models appropriate to solving a given production problem.

True integration in CIM requires a tightly woven federation of EDP, MIS, DSS, and ES capabilities to provide the information required for managerial decision making at various levels throughout the manufacturing organization. In particular, the EDP and CMS control system processing of manufacturing transactions, the data integration and standardized managerial reporting provided by traditional MIS, the decision support orientation and flexibility afforded by DSS with its integrated data-model components, plus the effective application of knowledge processing in an ES are all required in a comprehensive approach to the implementation of CIM. The resulting CIM system combines both the CMS and the MIS support for manufacturing management planning and operational control functions that will be required in the factory of the future.
