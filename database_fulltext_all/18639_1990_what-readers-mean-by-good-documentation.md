# What Readers Mean by ‘Good Documentation’

Ronald A. Guillemette

Department of Management, University of Texas-El Paso, El Paso, TX 79968-0544, USA

Application software documentation is critical in developing a good image of a system. Although several empirical studies suggest the importance of documentation, few have sought to identify and measure different subjective factors in reader-based evaluations. This paper reports on a comprehensive study that employed a semantic differential technique in the empirical assessment of documentation. Readers use a number of different adjectives in describing what they consider good documentation; a number of reliable, specific evaluative factors are also identified. These factors are shown to be largely uninfluenced by demographic/situational variables.

Keywords: Documentation, User Interface, Semantic Differential, Subjective Measure, Field Study.

## 1. Introduction

Documentation is organized, descriptive information concerning purposes, methods, logic, relationships, capabilities, and limitations [31]. Documentation serves a number of useful purposes, including a means for communication during software development, historical and instructional reference, and quality control [32].

Documentation costs constitute a significant portion of the software development budget [27]. A critical question in the management of documentation costs is how to assess the effectiveness of documentation. Effectiveness may be judged from two different perspectives: macro (organization-wide) and micro (individual/group) [25].

The traditional approach to assessing documentation has been from the macro perspective; typical goals are cost containment and the realization of expected benefits in software use. Documentation contributes to software planning and control by reducing entropy in software development and maintenance and facilitating the sharing or reuse of system resources and technology $[17]$ , $[20]$ , $[43]$ . Consequences of inadequate system documentation include software redesign and recoding costs, duplication of development effort, more extensive audit and control activities, and higher software operational and maintenance costs $[1]$ , $[4]$ , $[7]$ , $[18]$ , $[29]$ , $[35]$ . Costs associated with poor user documentation include the inefficient use of or failure to use available software, errors in interacting with the software, and the need to provide additional user assistance and training $[21]$ , $[28]$ , $[51]$ .

A second approach to assessing documentation is through the perspective of the individual reader. The basic rationale for this approach is that traditional software effectiveness goals are achieved through the behavior of individuals. Documentation is a major part of the system image; it is a critical portion of an individual's learning experience with software and is often the primary interface when difficulties occur in using the software [33], [44].

A number of problems have been noted with existing software documentation [9], [54], [55]. These problems often surface across several dimensions of user behavior, including performance (task completion, time, and errors), learning, and acceptability [37]. Relevant data are obtained through various objective and self-report methods [34], [36]. For instance, the activities of documentation users may be unobstrusively observed [38], and verbal protocols of users can provide additional evidence concerning the processing of documentation [8].

An empirical relationship between observable user behavior and acceptability or satisfaction with computer-based systems has been explicitly recognized by a number of researchers [48], [49], [53]. One widely referenced user satisfaction measure includes documentation as an empirically-derived factor [3]; other studies have noted a significant relationship between overall user satisfaction and subjective measures of documentation [11], [46].

Few studies have focused on measuring subjective factors which readers use in assessing software documentation. Furthermore, little prior empirical research has been reported on whether documentation judgments differ among various groups of readers or along other demographic and situational variables. This paper discusses a semantic differential scale designed to measure subjective factors relevant to documentation, including an exploratory investigation of significant difference of ratings for a number of study variables.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Other Subjective Measures of Documentation

Two multi-item documentation scales which have been reported in the literature are (1) an IBM instrument measuring usability of software manuals and (2) another instrument assessing the quality of user documentation.

## 2.1.1. IBM Usability Scale

An IBM task force concluded that there are three basic aspects to usability in software manuals: information should be easy to find, easy to understand, and task-sufficient [6]. Each of these dimensions is measured by 3 eleven-point scale items [5]. Empirical data for the composite measure were gathered through a survey of some 600 randomly chosen users from different organizations (20 for each of 30 randomly selected manuals). Survey results were used to define baseline figures against which the usability of future software manuals could be evaluated.

Criticism of the IBM study includes the judgmental derivation of scale items, the difficulty in interpreting scale item scores, and the inadequate reporting of psychometric properties.

## 2.1.2. Quality of User Documentation Scale

The developers of this scale conceptualize documentation as having one attribute (readability) and three functions (describing the functions of the software, how the software works, and how to use the software) [12]. They present a short-form, 15-item measure, heuristically derived from full-scale (37 Likert-type items) data collected from 81 users in five organizations. Reported psychometric evidence includes high internal consistency, significant concurrent validity with a global documentation scale item, and partial support of construct validity through confirmation of an expected relationship between user satisfaction and documentation scale scores.

A number of criticisms apply to this study. The conceptualization of user documentation seems incomplete; the quality of documentation is multidimensional in nature, and theoretical relationships with external variables are unstated or unexplained. There is an inadequate discussion of measure development, including domain sampling procedures and the identification of deleted full-scale items. All the items in the derived measure are directed to the same end of the attitude continuum [30]. A single-item criterion measure is not considered reliable [2].

Two other objections, i.e., the limited number of study subjects for purposes of psychometric analysis [41] and the lack of independent evidence presented for the derived short-form measure, have been addressed in a subsequent measure validation study [52].

## 2.2. Current Study Measure

## 2.2.1. Definition and Conceptual Framework

Here, application software documentation is defined as all transmissible information that facilitates the analysis, planning, acquisition, transfer or development, operation, use, control, and maintenance or enhancement of computer-based applications. This definition includes online as well as offline sources of system information and recognizes alternate groups of readers who reference these sources for differing purposes.

The reader requires software information for various purposes, such as orientation, need satisfaction, or problem solution [22]. The documentation user seeks information about application software products and processes in order to facilitate performance of software-related tasks in the context of some individual/organizational environment.

Reader-based problems with documentation can be classified along three levels of communication: technical, semantic, and effectiveness [47]. Technical problems focus on traditional ergonomic concerns involving physical characteristics of the documentation, such as its legibility and bulkiness. Semantic problems include difficulties in understanding written material; these result from characteristics of the text (e.g., vocabulary or grammatical complexity) or of the reader (background or reading skills). Effectiveness problems relate to difficulties that readers encounter in using the documentation to achieve their goals. These include (but are not restricted to) understandability issues; for instance, written information which is fragmentary, inaccurate, irrelevant, or inaccessible also adversely affects reader performance and attitude [56].

## 2.2.2. Construction of the Study Questionnaire

The goal of the scale development process was to compile a list of polar adjectives broadly descriptive of the relationships among reader, documentation, software and task. The three principal inputs to scale construction were an interdisciplinary literature review, a series of interviews and an expert review.

Candidate adjectives were extracted from research and practitioner sources in technical communication [19], data processing, human factors, educational psychology and reading. In addition, relevant adjectives used in prior semantic research were also included.

Other terms were obtained from recorded interviews with experienced users, writers and reviewers of documentation from 12 different organizations [24]. The final list of semantic differential (bipolar adjective) scale items reflected the addition, modification and deletion of compiled terms by a group of eight American university professors in computer-related disciplines.

The 70 semantic scale items were presented in random order and with reversals in direction of polarity. $^{1}$ The subject was asked to evaluate the documentation for a software product with which he or she was most familiar in the performance of software-related occupational tasks. This was done in order to ensure saliency of the related object to the respondent and to control for extraneous sources of variance (i.e., selection of a particular type of documentation). Readers usually access documentation in a reading-to-do mode [10], [23].

Additional questionnaire items focused on the nature and use of the software and its documentation and respondent background data.

## 2.3. Description of the Study Sample

The target population consisted of individuals who reference application software documentation in the performance of software-related occupational tasks. The author solicited responses from organizations representative of different industries in a large city in the southwestern US. The initial contact was made with an MIS manager expected to have access to a number of application software users in the organization. The author sought a heterogeneous sample of documentation readers differing by function and by level in the organization.

The final sample consisted of 483 usable responses from over 30 organizations. A plurality of responses (42%) came from eleven companies in the energy industry; other types of organizations represented in the sample with at least 3% total responses include banking, manufacturing, utilities, education, financial, health, and local government.

Approximately 45% of the sample consisted of end users. Nearly 25% of the respondents were managers, 60% were knowledge workers, and the remaining 15% were clerical/support personnel. The majority of subjects reported holding at least one college degree, were older (exceeding 25 years) and/or had several years of prior full-time work experience, including exposure to computer-based products and services.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Exploratory Factor Analysis Process

The goal of the factor analysis process was to identify semantic dimensions which efficiently explain covariation among the 70 semantic scale items. The exploratory process adopted in this study is consistent with the general pattern used by other applied researchers. First, principal components analysis (PCA) with varimax rotation was conducted in early runs, primarily to address assumptions and limitations of the data prior to factor analysis [50]. The results obtained from this stage were not intended to be final: PCA includes the effects of specific and random error variance.

Maximum-likelihood (ML) analysis, a preferred common factor extraction technique [16], was used at the next stage of analysis. A seven-factor solution, obtained from split-sample data after removal of outlying cases and variables, was subsequently validated against remaining cases.

## 3.1.1. PCA Solution

An evaluative dimension generally emerges and accounts for most explained variance in a factor solution over a heterogeneous group of semantic scale items [42]. This observation was found to hold for the PCA conducted over all scale items and retained cases in this study. The first component of the default (eigenvalue criterion) twelve-factor, varimax-rotated solution explained 31.48% of the variance among scale items and 73.12% of the covariance. The evaluative nature of this component is indicated by the high-loading nature of three marker items (good, helpful, and successful) on the unrotated component. Scale items with rotated loadings above 0.6 (36% variance) on the evaluative component are listed in Table 1; these items are terms which readers in this study associated with ‘good’ documentation.

## 3.1.2. ML Solution

A general evaluative dimension often includes clusters of specific evaluative scale items. ML analysis over retained cases and variables revealed seven reliable, stable factors, including a number of specific evaluative factors: task relevance, understandability, systematic arrangement, credibility, demonstrativeness, and fitness. Scale marker variables and internal consistency reliabilities are shown in Table 2. Coefficient alpha for all 22 variables over the sample is 0.96.

## 3.2. Demographic / Situational Variable Analyses

Prior research suggests that reader perceptions of documentation are mostly unaffected by demographic variables. The author conducted a series of exploratory analyses designed to test whether reader judgments of documentation are affected by an alternate set of external variables (Table 3). These analyses used factor scores computed using the Anderson–Rubin method [40], a more accurate alternative to the use of summation estimates [45]. An alpha level of 0.05 was chosen for establishing significance, and Duncan's multiple range test was applied to subsequent comparisons between group means.

Significant results are summarized in Table 4. Overall, demographic/situational variables do not seem to explain reader judgments of software documentation. However, some interesting observations can be made regarding the results. Specific factor scores provide certain insights that are not apparent from general evaluative scores. For instance, readers did not differ in their overall evaluation of documentation between internally developed and packaged software; however, they rated the documentation of packaged software as more appealing and of internally developed software as more convincing.

Current study results also differ in some degree from findings of the IBM study. The latter study concluded that data processing personnel evaluate their documentation as less usable. This study did not find a difference in overall evaluations between data processing personnel and end users. It did indicate that the former rate their documentation as less credible; however, they also consider it better arranged and more understandable.

## 3.3. Global Item Analyses

Single-item Likert-type scales were used to measure the helpfulness of the documentation, the ease of use of the software, the importance of documentation for the application software, and the potential effect of improvements of documentation on future reader behavior with the software.

The helpfulness scale scores correlated 0.5645 (p < 0.001) with the general evaluation factor scores; this finding verifies an expected relationship between the theoretically linked measures.

A low, significant correlation was found between ease of software use ratings and the general evaluative factor scores (0.2281, p < 0.001). This confirms a similar finding of the IBM study; this result is interpreted to reflect the impact of complexities of the software product on its corresponding documentation.

Ergonomic research suggests a moderating effect of software usability on the need for documentation. The instructions of easy-to-use products are often not read by users [57]; a usable human-computer interface limits the need of the user to consult external information sources [13]. Thus, users wo rate their software as very easy to use should rate the importance of documentation less than those who rate their software as only slightly easy to use; this hypothesis was confirmed (t = 2.52, 121.19 df, 1-tailed p < 0.007).

The user appears to apply an implicit cost-benefit assessments towards the use of software [14]. Poor documentation increases the user's cost of using the software and thus can limit the degree to which software is actually used. Improvements to the documentation which minimize user effort in using or learning to use the software may lead to increased software use as the user is able to accomplish software-based tasks. However, if the user is already able to accomplish those tasks with existing knowledge and documentation, modifications to the documentation may not affect software use patterns. The author hypothesized that discretionary users of software who are dissatisfied with their documentation would indicate comparably greater use of the software if the documentation is improved. (Mandatory software use results in a significant base rate of activity which may not distinguish between assessments of documentation [15].) Discretionary software users in this study who rated their documentation negatively indicated a greater change in future software use than those who rated their documentation positively (t = 3.06, 146 df, p < 0.002).

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

Documentation facilitates achievement of overall software goals, such as economy, maintainability, and reusability. These goals are achieved through the efforts of individuals. Poor documentation may adversely affect different dimensions of reader behavior, including performance with or acceptability of the documentation.

Documentation has been cited in a number of empirical articles as a variable contributing to computer user satisfaction. Recent research has focused on the development and validation of instruments designed to measure subjective factors in the reader-based evaluation of documentation. The development of standardized measures can result in several advantages, such as objectivity, quantification, communication, economy, and scientific generalization.

This paper focuses on an instrument which attempts to measure the meaning of the concept of documentation through a broad collection of 70 semantic differential items. Questionnaire results obtained from a large, heterogeneous group of documentation readers indicate the dominance of a general evaluative factor among the scale items. Additional analysis shows that this general factor includes a number of reliable, stable specific evaluative factors, such as task relevance, credibility, systematic arrangement, and demonstrativeness. The general evaluative factor and seven specific factors were shown to be largely uninfluenced by demographical/situational variables.

The development of baseline scores for general evaluative and specific factor scales allows for the comparative interpretation of mean scores associated with a particular piece of documentation. For instance, the IBM study used survey results involving 30 manual to establish benchmarks for the comparative assessment of newly written manuals.

It should be noted that self-report measures are vulnerable to respondent bias and focus on one dimension of reader behavior; additional data may be obtained from observations of reader-documentation-software interaction.

Finally, analysis of certain global measures lends support to claims in the literature regarding the impact of software usability on documentation and the adverse effect of poor documentation on discretionary software use.
