# Using the Analytic Hierarchy Process for Information System Project Selection

Krishnamurty Muralidhar

and Radhika Santhanam

Department of Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Florida International University, Miami, FL 33199, USA

Rick L. Wilson

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lincoln, NE 68588-0491, USA

Information system project selection plays an important role in planning for information systems. This paper presents an improved methodology for information system project selection using the analytic hierarchy process (AHP). The AHP method adopts a multicriteria approach to information system project selection unlike the single criteria approach used by existing methods. It also provides the capability to establish the relative importance of criteria in-line with organizational objectives. Existing methodologies fail to provide this capability. The application of the AHP method is illustrated through an example.

Keywords: Analytic Hierarchy Process, Cost/Benefit Analysis, IS Planning, IS Project Selection, Group Decision Making, Ranking Model, Scoring Model.

One of the important activities of the IS planning process is the selection of appropriate IS projects that further organizational objectives $[7,10]$ . Typically, organizational resources are limited and the selection of one project may preclude the selection of other projects. The organizational interests are best served by choosing those projects that provide maximum contribution. In other words, projects chosen to be developed must: (1) be in accordance with organizational objectives, and (2) maximize the benefit to the organization.

The objective of this study is to present a new methodology for IS project selection using the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) proposed by Saaty [20]. The AHP methodology is well suited to address the different issues in IS project selection and is an improvement over existing methodologies.

## 2. Issues in IS Project Selection

Selection of IS projects is a difficult, yet important task faced by an organization. The ultimate goal of any IS project selection methodology is to choose a set of projects that maximize the benefit contribution to the organization and are also in accordance with organizational objectives. Cost/benefit analysis, ranking, and scoring are commonly used to select IS projects. However, there exists no standard accepted technique for IS project selection. This has led to dissatisfaction and frustration among IS managers over the manner and method in which IS projects are to be chosen [13].

The selection decision is complicated due to the existence of a large number of factors that have to be included in the process. Some of the criteria identified by researchers include user satisfaction, cost of development, probability of success, cost of operation, etc. [8,13,19]. It is clearly seen that while some of these factors (cost of development, cost of operation, etc.) are objective, measurable criteria, others (user satisfaction, probability of success, etc.) are subjective criteria that are difficult to measure. Though subjective criteria cannot be easily measured, it is necessary that they be included in the selection process, because, in some cases, they may be more important than objective (tangible) criteria [3]. The existence of several criteria mandates the use of a multi-criteria approach for IS project selection.

The IS project selection decision is also characterized by differing priorities among the various factors. For example, an organization may place a greater priority on the “user satisfaction” criteria than on the “cost of operation” criteria. Therefore, a greater emphasis must be given to “user satisfaction”. As the growth of computing in organizations goes through different stages, priorities for IS development may also change $[10,18]$ . Hence, an IS project selection methodology should be capable of evaluating the relative importance of the multiple criteria and account for changes in relative importance over the life cycle.

The nature of the different factors and priorities will vary among organizations. However, the characteristics of the IS project selection problem dictates that the methodology used must have three capabilities: to handle multiple criteria, to evaluate projects on tangible and intangible criteria, and to establish the relative importance of each criteria. Furthermore, it must be flexible and easy to use, so that current dissatisfaction regarding IS project selection methodologies can be reduced. Existing methodologies fail to satisfy one or more of these factors.

## 3. Existing Methodologies for IS Project Selection

Among the several existing methodologies for IS project selection, one of the traditional approaches is a cost/benefit analysis [11]. To use this analysis, it is first necessary to express the benefits arising from each project in dollar units. These benefits are then aggregated and evaluated against the costs of developing the project. The net contribution (benefits - costs) is used to make the selection decision.

To express the contribution of every project is not easy. It is especially difficult in IS project selection, since the problem is characterized by the existence of many intangible benefits. Attempting to quantify this is, in itself, a difficult task, and it will be difficult to associate dollar units to such benefits. For example, consider a situation where one of the criteria used in selection is “the degree to which a project promotes organization learning.” This is an intangible criteria that is extremely difficult to quantify and it is virtually impossible to associate dollar units to its contribution.

Another problem with cost/benefit analysis arises because there is no way to establish the relative importance of the different type of benefits. Knutsen and Nolan [12] also argue that using cost/benefit analysis leads to the selection of IS projects that have a high monetary benefit while ignoring projects which have high intangible benefits.

Scoring and ranking techniques are also commonly used to select IS projects $[3,13,14]$ . In using the ranking approach, the available IS projects are ranked based on their relative contribution to a given criteria. The process is repeated for every criteria. The ranks are then aggregated and used in the decision. The scoring approach is very similar, except that available IS projects are scored (on a scale of, say, 1 to 10) rather than being ranked as in the ranking approach. The aggregated scores or ranks are used in making the selection decision. Ranking and scoring approaches avoid one of the problems associated with cost/benefit analysis, namely, the evaluation of intangible criteria.

While ranking and scoring approaches are capable of handling both tangible and intangible criteria, they still fail to provide the decision maker with a means to evaluate the relative importance of the criteria. Aggregation of the scores or ranks is an attempt to convert the multi-dimensional problem into a single dimensional problem. However, such aggregation completely ignores the varying levels of importance placed upon each criterion. Thus, existing methodologies for IS project selection fail to address all aspects of the problem.

## 4. The Analytic Hierarchy Process

The Analytic Hierarchy Process was introduced by Saaty [20]. The process addresses how to determine the relative importance of a set of activities in a multi-criteria setting. One of the distinguishing features of this approach is the use of hierarchical structure to represent the decision problem, independent of problem complexity or the number of criterion [26]. Hierarchical composition is one of the most commonly used methods by which decision makers factor complex problems into more manageable subproblems [24]. AHP also makes it possible to incorporate judgements on intangible qualitative criteria alongside tangible quantitative criteria [21]. The process incorporates pairwise comparisons (as contrasted against simultaneous comparisons) of the project alternatives as well as pairwise comparisons of the multiple criteria.

The attributes of the AHP satisfy the requirements of a good IS project selection methodology. It allows factors to be specified in a multi-criteria setting, provides the ability to express the relative importance of the multiple criteria being considered, and uses pairwise comparisons in extracting information.

The AHP has been used extensively in practice, including some areas similar to IS project selection. Zahedi [27] developed a decision mechanism for microcomputer data base software selection, using the AHP to quantify the evaluation. The method was then applied to the comparison of two micro-based DBMS products: dBASE III and Condor 3. Seidmann and Arbel [23] used the AHP in selecting among four alternatives for a microcomputer based accounting information system. The objective was to select the best microcomputer for accounting information management in the firm. They used the AHP in selecting a microcomputer for process control and data acquisition [1]. Johnson and Hihn [9] identified the usefulness of the AHP in selecting among potential projects in the field of energy storage. A comprehensive list of major applications of AHP can be found in Zahedi [28].

As with any methodology, the first step in using the AHP for IS project selection is to identify the set of multiple criteria which merit consideration. The next step is to evaluate how well each project addresses each criterion by comparing the projects in pairs for all possible project combinations for a given criterion. The process is repeated for each criterion under evaluation in the project selection problem.

The use of pairwise comparisons to collect data from the decision maker offers some advantages. It allows the decision maker to focus on the comparison of just two objects, making the observation as free as possible from extraneous influences. Additionally, pairwise comparisons generate meaningful information about the decision problem, improving consistency (compared to simultaneous comparison) in the decision making process [4,21].

When comparing the alternatives for each criterion, the decision maker will respond to questions such as: “In comparing Project 1 and Project 2, on the basis of cost reduction which project is preferred?” The numeric scale in the comparison matrix is identified in Table 1 [21]. The basic rationale for a 1–9 scale is based on the psychological experiments of Miller [17]. Further discussion on the scales’ appropriateness can be found in Saaty [20].

The matrix will be reciprocal in nature, reducing the number of needed comparisons by half. The rationale for using reciprocity is intuitive; once a response is gathered for a particular comparison, the exact “opposite” response should be true for the same comparison when reversing the order.

After all project comparisons are made for each criterion, their relative importance are then elicited from the decision maker using the same pairwise comparison process as was used in evaluating the project alternatives. When comparing the importance of the individual criteria, the typical question asked of the decision maker would be: "In comparing the benefits obtained by cost reduction and the benefits obtained by increased productivity, which is more important to the entire organization?" As before, all possible pairwise comparisons are made, with the responses being represented numerically in another comparison matrix, using the same 1–9 scale identified previously.

Once comparison matrices are constructed for both project and criteria comparisons, the final step is to determine the overall ranking of the IS projects.

Establishing the overall ranking of the projects involves three steps. The first is to determine the relative importance of the criteria using the comparison matrix constructed by the decision maker. The largest eigenvalue and the corresponding principle eigenvector of this matrix are calculated. The principle eigenvector is normalized such that its entries sum to one. The normalized eigenvector represents the relative importance of the criteria [21].

The second step is to determine the relative importance of the projects with respect to each criterion. The project comparison matrix for a given criterion is used to prioritize (i.e., establish the relative importance of) the projects. As in the previous step, the normalized principle eigenvector of the project comparison matrix for a given criterion represents the priority of the projects for that criterion. The process is repeated for every criterion, each resulting in a distinct prioritization of projects.

Finally, the relative importance of the projects for each criterion and the relative importance of the criteria themselves are used to determine the overall ranking of the projects. Assume that the relative importance of m projects have to be established using n criteria. Let $C_{i}$ ( $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$ ) represent the relative importance of criteria i, and let $P_{ij}$ ( $j = 1, 2, \ldots, m$ ) represent the relative importance of Project j with respect to criteria i.

Table 4

The overall relative importance of Project $j(R_{j})$ is determined as

$$
R _ {j} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} C _ {i} P _ {i j}.
$$

The larger the value of $R_{j}$ , the higher the relative importance of Project j. Thus, the composite values of $R_{j}$ represent the relative ranking of the projects under evaluation [26].

## 6. Advantages of Using the AHP

## 6.1. Multi-Criteria Approach

The AHP was designed specifically for decision making in a multi-criteria environment. None of the existing methodologies (cost/benefit, ranking, or scoring) were designed for this situation. A review of the literature indicates, without a doubt, that IS project selection is a multi-criteria decision making problem. Some authors have suggested that individual criteria be assigned weights and these weights be used when aggregating the contribution to individual criteria. In the example problem used in this study, the relative priorities were 0.04, 0.53, 0.30, and 0.13 for Criteria 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively. It is unreasonable to expect that any decision maker can determine the weights (relative priorities) to this level of accuracy. Yet, the degree of precision used to determine the weights will have a significant impact on the selection of projects. The AHP approach aids the decision maker in achieving this high degree of precision through a simple yet effective technique.

## 6.2. Different Types of Criteria

As mentioned earlier, the IS project selection problem is characterized by the existence of both tangible and intangible criteria. The AHP approach can easily handle both types of criteria, unlike cost/benefit analysis. For tangible criteria, it is also possible that the contribution of each project be used directly in constructing the response matrix.

## 6.3. Pairwise Comparisons

The response matrices are constructed based upon pairwise comparison of projects and benefits. This is easier to implement than comparisons that require simultaneous evaluation of several projects. For example, if the ranking approach was used, it would be necessary for the decision maker to consider all projects simultaneously to determine their relative ranking. Research evidence indicates that it is extremely difficult to do this simultaneously $[4,21]$ . The AHP approach avoids this problem by using a pairwise comparison technique. It also provides meaningful additional information about the decision problem under evaluation.

## 6.4. Judgmental Responses

The scale used in eliciting pairwise comparisons in the AHP is judgmental rather than purely quantitative (as in the scoring approach). In some cases, it may be difficult for a decision maker to assign a quantitative value to an intangible variable, even on a simple 1–10 scale. The scale used in the AHP process, on the other hand, is judgmental. Rather than asking the decision maker to determine a quantitative value, it only requires the decision maker to choose between nine comparison levels. These range from “both projects are equally important” to “one project has absolute preference over another.” Most decision makers would be able to respond to this scale more easily than trying to associate quantitative values to projects. Thus, using the AHP in place of the the scoring approach can prove of significant advantage.

For example, consider a tangible criteria like cost saving. Assume that projects 1 through 4 will result in cost savings (in 000's) of \$10, \$50, \$20 and \$30, respectively. Thus, the AHP approach can easily be applied to both qualitative and quantitative factors. It also allows the judgement of the decision maker to be used in the selection process.

## 6.5. Group Decision Making

Several authors have suggested the use of a steering committee for IS project selection [16]. The AHP process can be adapted very easily where decision making is performed by a group rather than an individual. Saaty [21] has discussed the protocol to be used in an AHP session and different methods that can be used to obtain consensus in using the AHP in group decision making. In the IS project selection situation, two methods can be used by steering committees.

The first is to elicit a response matrix (for each benefit type and between benefit types) from each decision maker in the group. The response matrices of all the decision makers for a given benefit type can then be aggregated to determine the average response matrix for that benefit type [21]. The process is then repeated for all benefit types and the comparison among benefit type. The resulting average response matrices can be used to determine the ranking of the projects.

The second method is to use the expertise of specific members of the group for creating specific response matrices. For example, if a systems analyst is a member of the committee, then his or her expertise can be utilized to create the response matrix for implementation time. An end user in the committee can be queried to create the response matrix for user satisfaction. If there is more than one end user, the first method can be used to create a single response matrix (by averaging) for end user satisfaction. The second method allows for the incorporation of the specialized expertise of individual committee members.

With the increasing importance of IS, it is reasonable to expect that project selection decisions will be made by a group rather than an individual. The use of the AHP approach allows an organization to incorporate group decision making in selecting IS projects.

## 6.6. Ease of Use and Implementation

One of the attractive features of the approach is its ease of use. It can be designed as a part of a decision support system. The computations are very simple and can be performed using any matrix manipulation software.

## 7. Conclusion

IS project selection has received considerable attention in the IS literature. This reflects the degree of importance placed upon the process, both by researchers and practitioners. This study presented a new, multi-criteria approach using the AHP; it provides several improvements over existing methodologies and is also easy to use. Sowder [25] observed that a good project selection methodology must be realistic, flexible, easy to use, easy to understand, and inexpensive to implement. The AHP approach possesses all these characteristics and hence will provide an excellent alternative to existing methodologies for IS project selection.
