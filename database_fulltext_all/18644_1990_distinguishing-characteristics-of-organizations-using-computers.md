# Distinguishing Characteristics of Organizations Using Computers

Chee Sing Yap \*

Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Singapore 0511

Organizations have been using computers and other advanced information technologies for more than a decade, yet there remain many which are slow in adopting these technological innovations. This paper identifies characteristics that distinguish organizations using computers from those that do not, based on data collected from a survey of the U.K. service sector. The survey findings indicate that large organizations and organizations in the financial sector are more likely to use computers. Organizations using computers tend to have more formalization in communications and procedures, a higher growth rate, and a higher percentage of secretarial and clerical workers. A classification scheme based on the findings is proposed for identifying the potential for applications of information technology in an organization.

Keywords: Adoption of Computers, Computer Usage, Organizational Characteristics.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) provides an opportunity for organizations to improve their efficiency and effectiveness. Yet, whilst organizations have been using computers and other advanced IT for some time, there are still many which are slow in taking up these technological innovations.

Several factors, such as technological availability, competitive imperative, and the need to improve the productivity of clerical and managerial workers, have been suggested as the motivation for organizations to invest in IT [21,25,34]. But there has been little reported research that attempts to identify the distinguishing characteristics of organizations using computers. Based on data collected from a mail survey in the U.K., this paper identifies organizational characteristics that distinguish organizations using computers from those that do not. For convenience, organizations using computers will be referred to as computer users, and those not using computers as non-users.

## 2. Research Framework

## 2.1. Research Variables

The research framework is based on Leavitt's diamond model of the organization; it comprises four main components: technology, task, people, and structure [19]. The study examined the effects of organizational characteristics, which include the task, people, and structure components of Leavitt's model and organizational size, sector, performance, and environmental factors, on the use of computers.

A survey of the existing literature identified a large number of potentially relevant organizational characteristics. It was, however, not possible to cover all or even most of them in a single study. Ten organizational characteristics were included as the research variables based on two main criteria (see Table 1):

(1) The characteristics had been found in previous studies to be relevant to the use of computers; and

(2) There must be a simple yet meaningful operational measure that could be incorporated in a mail questionnaire.

The operational measures employed are shown in the Appendix 1. A dichotomous measure was adopted for the use of computers: an organization is either a computer user or non-user.

## 2.2.1. Size

One of the factors often found to be relevant is organizational size [30]. For example, Moch and Morse [23] found a small but significant positive correlation between the size of hospitals and the number of innovations adopted; this included use of electronic data processing. DeLone [8], in a study covering 74 manufacturing firms in Los Angeles, found that small firms had been using computer systems for less time than larger firms, that is, large firms were more likely to be early adopters of computer systems than smaller ones. Organizational theorists suggest that as the size of an organization increases, the task of coordination becomes more complicated and its information processing requirements increase [13]. Hence, the use of computers is expected to be related to organizational size. The annual turnover of the organization was used as a measure of size, because this data could be obtained relatively easily from the organization itself or through a commercial agency. Furthermore, government statistics relevant to the study are grouped under turnover ranges [9].

## 2.1.2. Sector

The sector to which an organization belonged was included because it is an aggregate measure describing the basic function of the organization. Organizations in different sectors are expected to have different information processing requirements. Certain sectors, such as the business and financial services sectors, are more information intensive, since their main functions are to process and package information. These sectors would be expected to use computers extensively.

Five subsectors of the U.K. service sector were identified: transport and communication, wholesale distribution, retail distribution, business and financial services, and miscellaneous services, including hotels and catering, repairs, and recreational services. These groupings were derived from the 1980 Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) for the United Kingdom [6].

## 2.1.3. Performance

Organizations which have above average performance are likely to be in a better position to invest in technological innovations [5]. Two performance measures were included: average return on capital employed over the last three financial years, and growth of business activities over the last three years. The first is a profitability measure, and was defined as profit before interest and tax divided by net assets. The second provides a general indication of the change in business activities over a period of time and was measured on a subjective 5-point scale, from large contraction to large expansion.

## 2.1.4. Task

Task refers to the organization's raison d'etre: the production of goods and services, including operationally meaningful subtasks that might exist in a complex organization. An important characteristic is how routine the main work activities are [27]. When the work activities are routine, rules and procedures can be developed and implemented, hence simplifying the job of coordination and control. The decision processes are generally better understood and structured, or programmable [31]. Conversely, if activities are non-routine or decisions are dissimilar from day to day, the administrative work is more complicated. The decision processes are likely to be less structured or unprogrammable.

## 2.1.5. People

One major motivating factor for office automation is the desire to improve productivity of clerical and managerial workers. Thus, an organization with a large number of office workers is more likely to invest in computers and other IT than an organization with fewer office workers [26].

The study focused on the relative number of workers employed in different job categories. The workforce was divided into six broad categories: managers, professionals, secretarial workers, clerical workers, technical support staff, and other staff. Information workers, defined as secretarial and clerical workers, are likely to be the group that is most closely associated with the use of computers. Thus, the research variable was the percentage of information workers in the total workforce.

## 2.1.6. Structure

Organizational structure refers to systems of communication, authority, and work flow. It has attracted much research effort in organizational studies and some interesting dimensions have been identified [22,28]. Two, degree of formalization of communications and degree of centralization of decision making, were included as research variables because they have been found to be relevant to the adoption of innovations [3]. Both variables were measured on a subjective 5-point scale, from very informal to very formal, and from highly decentralized to highly centralized.

Organizations which are more formalized have written procedures for various functional activities, decision and control processes. To implement computer-based information systems, these organizational processes must be well understood, and amenable to processing and analysis [12].

Organizations which tend to favour centralized decision making are likely to be attracted by the capability of computer systems to facilitate centralized structure and control through central corporate databases [20]. However, computer systems also facilitate decentralized control through the use of distributed processing technology [7].

## 2.1.7. Environmental factors

The environment can be divided into two levels. The general environment includes social, economic, political, technological, legal, and cultural factors. These are assumed to have similar effects on all organizations within an economy and were not considered in this study $[15]$ . The specific environment concerns factors such as customers, suppliers, and competitors; these have a more specific effect on individual organizations $[10]$ .

In this study, two such variables were considered: competitiveness of the market and predictability of customers' requirements. Computer-based information systems are expected to be used more extensively by organizations with a high degree of marketplace uncertainty $[16]$ . These organizations have to be more responsive and adaptive to changes. Predictability of customers' requirements is another measure of environmental uncertainty which may be directly related to information processing needs. When the requirements are highly unpredictable, more information gathering and processing activities, such as market research and forecasting, have to be carried out. These were measured on a scale ranging from relatively not competitive to very competitive and from easily predictable to very unpredictable.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

Hypotheses relating the variables to the use of computers are:

Organizations are more likely to use computers when they:

H1: are bigger in size,

H2: are in an information intensive sector,

H3: have higher profitability levels,

H4: have a higher growth rate,

H5: have more routine work activities,

H6: have a higher percentage of information workers,

H7: are more formalized in communications,

H8: are more centralized in decision making,

H9: operate in a more competitive environment,

H10: operate in an environment where their customers' requirements are more unpredictable.

## 2.3. Methodology

Data for the research were collected through a mail survey carried out in the U.K. service sector. This sector was chosen because it is basically information-based, handling and processing a vast amount of information. Some subsectors, such as financial and business services, are of course more information intensive than others, such as retail and transport. The service sector is also a sector which has been undergoing rapid changes $[4,14]$ .

The sample was stratified along two dimensions: sector and size. It was divided into five subsectors, and into three size groups on the basis of turnover: organizations with an annual turnover of less than £1 million, between £1 million and £5 million, and more than £5 million. Organizations in these ranges are referred to as small, medium, and large respectively. These ranges were chosen because relevant government statistics are published in this form. Thus, there was a total of fifteen cells. The actual sample size for each cell was calculated from published statistics, with more weight given to larger organizations. A commercial agency was engaged to select 3000 organizations randomly from its databases.

The questionnaire was tested in two pilot projects. The first took the form of personal interviews with senior managers from six companies. In the second, mail questionnaires were sent to one hundred companies chosen at random from Kompass [18]. A response rate of 22% was obtained. These resulted in a slight modification of the questionnaire. The final questionnaire was divided into two sections: the first contained questions related to organizational characteristics and the second was devoted to the use of IT.

## 3. Research Findings

## 3.1. Correlations

The individual hypotheses were tested by correlating each research variable with the use of computers. The overall analyses referred to analyses for the overall sample. The subsequent analyses controlled for the effects of both turnover and sector. Details of the analyses and the results are summarized in Appendix 3. The analyses showed that the use of computers was highly correlated with annual turnover and sector. Overall, about 70% of the sample were computer users. The data show a wide variation in the percentage of organizations using computers across turnover ranges; large organizations were more likely to use computers than small and medium organizations. Also, there was a significant difference between the financial sector and the other four sectors. More than 90% of the sample in the financial sector were using computers, compared with between 54% and 72% for the others. Moreover, the financial sector was the most highly computerized sector in all three turnover ranges.

Return on capital employed was found to be related to the use of computers for the overall sample. The relationship was still significant after controlling for the effects of turnover. However, none of the correlation coefficients in the subsequent analyses, which controlled for the effects of both turnover and sector, were significant. Thus, the correlation was spurious, due to the effects of turnover and sector. Hence, one can conclude that the survey data did not provide evidence to support the hypothesis that organizations are more likely to use computers when they have higher profitability.

Nonetheless, there was strong evidence to suggest that the use of computers was related to the growth of business activities. Significant relationships were obtained in the overall and the subsequent analyses. Organizations that have expanded greatly over the last three years were more likely to be computer users.

Significant correlation coefficients were obtained for routineness of work activities, even after controlling for the effects of turnover. However, in the subsequent analyses, the correlation coefficients were not significant, except for the retail sector: the relationship obtained in the overall analysis was probably a spurious one. Hence, it was concluded that the survey data did not provide enough evidence to support the hypothesis that organizations with more routine work activities were more likely to be computer users.

The use of computers was found to be highly correlated with the percentage of information workers (r = 0.252, p < 0.001) in the overall analyses. In the subsequent analyses, the partial correlation coefficients for the transport and financial sectors were significant at the 0.05 level; that for the miscellaneous sector was significant at the 0.1 level. The coefficients for the wholesale and retail sectors were not significant. Further analyses of the data showed that computer users had a significantly higher proportion of information workers than non-users of a similar size. About one third of the workforce of computer users were information workers, compared with less than one quarter for non-users.

The relationship between formalization and the use of computers was found to be highly significant in the overall analyses. In the subsequent analyses, significant relationships were obtained for the wholesale, retail, and financial sectors. Apparently computers users tend to be more formalized in communications and procedures than non-users.

The relationship between the degree of centralization of decision making and the use of computers was found not to be significant in the overall analyses. Subsequent analyses showed that for the transport sector, computer users had less centralized decision making than non-users. However, computer users in the retail and miscellaneous sectors tended to be more centralized than non-users. Thus, the direction of association between the degree of centralization and the use of computers was inconclusive.

No significant relationship was found between competitiveness of the market and the use of computers in the overall analyses. In the subsequent analyses, the correlation coefficients were also not significant, except for the miscellaneous sector, which was significant at the 0.05 level.

For predictability of customers' requirements, no significant relationship was found in the overall analyses and the subsequent analyses. Thus, there was little evidence to support the hypothesis that the two environmental variables included in the study were related to the use of computers.

## 3.2. Discriminant Analysis

Discriminant analysis was used to identify characteristics that distinguish organizations using computers from those not using them. Because earlier analysis showed that the use of computers was highly correlated with annual turnover and sector, we would expect these two variables to be good discriminants between computers users and non-users. The aim of this analysis was to identify organizational variables other than annual turnover and sector which distinguish computer users, so annual turnover and sector were purposely excluded. The other eight research variables were used as candidates for the discriminants.

The discriminant function was estimated using the Statistical Packages for the Social Sciences (SPSS-X). A stepwise procedure was used because the aim was to select variables which contribute most significantly to the discrimination ability of the linear discriminant function. It was found that the same discriminant function was obtained by using either the Mahalanobis' D-squared distance criterion or the Wilks' lambda criterion. Summary results are shown in Table 3.

## 4. Discussion

The survey data did not provide enough evidence to support the hypotheses relating the use of computers to return on capital employed (H3), routineness of work activities (H5), competitiveness of the market (H9), and predictability of customers' requirements (H10). Significant relationships were found for size (H1), sector (H2), the growth of business (H4), the percentage of information workers (H6), and the degree of formalization (H7). Inconclusive evidence was obtained for the degree of centralization of decision making (H8).

## 4.1. Size

The survey data showed that large organizations are more likely to use computers than small and medium organizations. This is much as expected and is broadly in agreement with other findings on the adoption of innovations [17]. Size is probably a surrogate measure of several dimensions that lead to the adoption of innovations: resources, organizational structure and so on. Another explanation is that as the size increases, organizational structure becomes more complex, and coordination of work activities more complicated.

Another line of reasoning is that increasing size leads to economies of scale which enhance the feasibility of adopting innovations. It is likely that the large volume of information processed by large organizations gives them the advantage of scale. There is some evidence to suggest that large organizations enjoy some cost advantage, as the price of computer systems increases at a rate equivalent to the square root of their power $[11,36]$ . This is less true today with the advent of mini-computers and microcomputers.

It is also likely that small organizations have more difficulty justifying investments in IT. They face a number of problems arising from limited financial and human resources. For example, Raymond [29] suggests that small organizations are unlikely to have an in-house expert and usually have fewer resources to allocate. Other empirical studies show that small firms have less computer related experience, are more dependent on external support, and are concerned about different kinds of problems in relation to their use of computers [35].

## 4.2. Sector

The survey data showed that organizations in the financial sector are more likely to use computers than those in the other four sectors studied. Barras and Swann [1] explained the differences in diffusion rates between the sectors in terms of three factors: opportunity, capacity, and market pressure. They argue that the opportunity for innovation has always been present in the financial sector because of the suitability in banking, insurance and accounting. Furthermore, ready access to capital has provided the capacity to meet the investment outlay, firstly among the large institutions and then among the smaller firms. Finally, the financial sector exhibits an oligopolistic market structure which provides the best competitive conditions for innovation.

The results can be seen from an organizational perspective by focusing on the nature of work in these organizations. In banking, insurance, and some other business services organizations, the bulk of the activities are concerned with information. Money, for example, is a form of information and a large amount of money is transacted daily as electronic information $[33]$ . Most of the workers employed in the financial sector are engaged in activities related to IT, and this is reflected in the manpower structure. The survey data showed that about 45% of their workforce were managers and professionals, 35% information workers, and 20% technical support and other staff. The corresponding figures for the other sectors taken together were 35%, 27% and 38%. It is worth noting that within each industrial sector, there are many types of organizations which can be engaged in rather different activities. For example, removal firms and travel agents are both classified under transport, yet their main activities are very different.

## 4.3. Performance

Two specific variables were studied: return on capital employed and growth of business over the last three years. Return on capital employed was found not to be a good discriminant between computer users and non-users. There was little evidence to support the hypothesis that more profitable organizations are more likely to use computers. However, it was found that organizations with high growth rates are more likely to use computers. As the organization grows and the volume of business transactions increases, there is a need to expand the capacity to cope with the accompanying increase in complexity and information processing requirements. This is particularly attractive in the case of computers, because labour costs are rising while equipment costs are declining.

## 4.4. Information Workers

The survey data showed that organizations with a high proportion of information workers were more likely to use computers. Although several authors have suggested that organizations with high proportions of information workers tend to use IT heavily, there is no published empirical data to support this. Thus, this study has contributed by providing empirical data to show it. It is implied that the higher percentage of information workers is a possible cause rather than a result of using computers.

## 4.5. Structure

Organizations which are more formalized are usually those which are more mature or have been rationally structured and organized. According to Ein-Dor and Segev [12], mature organizations are those in which systems are formalized, quantified and produce data appropriate to their decision and control processes. Thus, the degree of formalization can be seen as a surrogate measure for organizational maturity. The survey result suggests that less mature organizations are less likely to use computers.

Inconclusive results were obtained for the degree of centralization of decision making. The expectation that more centralized organizations would be more likely to use computers was not supported. An increasing number of organizations are using microcomputers which facilitate delegation of responsibility and decentralization of decision making, rather than centralization. Child [2] suggests that the choice of whether to use IT to assist centralization or decentralization is likely to be influenced by the managerial ethos of the organization and the task contingencies, such as variability of production and the number of environments served.

## 5. Summary: Potential for Application of Information Technology

The survey results show that in the U.K. service sector, large organizations and organizations in the financial sector are more likely to use computers. In addition to size and sector, the use of computers is correlated with a high degree of formalization, a high growth rate, and a high percentage of information workers.

## Correlation Analysis

The Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients (zero-order) between each research variable and the use of computers were computed, except for the sector variable. For the sector variable, which was a nominal measure, only the Chi-squared statistic was computed. These analyses were carried out for the whole sample (the overall analyses). The correlation coefficients and their significance levels are shown in Table A3.1. A negative value for the correlation coefficient implies that computer users tended to have a smaller value than non-users. For example, the correlation coefficient for routineness of work activities was negative, implying that computers users tended to have less routine work activities than non-users.

The analyses showed that the use of computers is highly correlated with turnover and sector. Thus, further analyses were carried out to control their effects. The first-order correlation coefficients, controlling for the effects of turnover, were computed. However, similar analyses could not be used for the effects of sector because it was a nominal-level measure. To eliminate the effects of both, the first-order correlation coefficients, controlling for the effects of turnover, were computed for each subsector (the subsequent analyses). The results of these analyses are shown in Table A3.2.
