# Enhancing Computer-Mediated Communication: An Experimental Investigation into the Use of a Group Decision Support System for Face-to-Face Versus Remote Meetings $^{1}$

R. Brent Gallupe and James D. McKeen
Queen's University, School of Business, Kingston, Ontario, Canada K7L 3N6

A controlled laboratory experiment was undertaken to examine the effects of the use of a Group Decision Support System (GDSS) on face-to-face versus remote meetings. Some expected and some unexpected results were obtained for decision performance (quality, speed, choice, shift) and group member perceptions (confidence and satisfaction). Use of a GDSS, for example, increased the time it took groups to reach a decision yet had no impact on the decision quality of the groups. Also, there was a substantial interaction effect regarding choice shift. The ramifications of these results are discussed fully.

Keywords: Group decision support systems, Computer-mediated communications, Face-to-face versus remote group decision making.

## Introduction

A significant amount of research is currently being conducted into the use of computer-based systems to support collaborative group work. This research has followed two major streams. One is in the area of computer messaging or computer-mediated communication (CMC) to support geographically remote group members. The second has attempted to investigate means for supporting the group's actual decision processes and this area has come to be known as group decision support systems (GDSS). This study represents an attempt to bring these two fields together.

## Computer-Mediated Communications (CMC)

Communications has long been recognized as an important ingredient in organizational decision making. In fact, the ability to work in meetings (face-to-face), over the telephone, and through the mails has largely been taken for granted. Because of this, interest in communication lay dormant until the introduction of the computer as an active component of the communication medium used by individuals faced with a problem. With the computer, it became possible to structure the transmission and accumulation of information in ways never before possible. Research into computer-mediated communication has approximately a 15-year history; a summary of the full range of CMC results can be found in Kerr and Hiltz [26]. The promise of these systems is best expressed by Hiltz and Turoff [16] where they state that “the ability of a computer-based communication system to allow the right individuals anywhere in the formal organization to be gathered together and focussed on a single task is paramount. The ultimate goal of these systems is collective intelligence”.

Using student subjects, an experiment to compare the process and outcomes of face-to-face meetings versus unstructured computerized conferences $[17]$ found that face-to-face groups were more apt to reach consensus during the allotted decision period but that there was no relationship between quality of decision and consensus. A second experiment by Hilt, Johnson and Turoff $[18]$ in a field setting with managers found that: (1) there was more explicit agreement with face-to-face groups, (2) there was more opinion-giving in computerized conferencing and (3) the nature of communication in computer conferences devoted to decision making tended to be oriented more towards the achievement of high quality decisions and less towards the social-emotional types of communications which aid consensus.

More recently, three controlled experiments were conducted by Siegal, Dubrovsky, Kiesler and McGuire [33]. All used student subjects faced with “choice dilemma” problems. The results indicated that groups using computer-mediated communication, as compared with groups using face-to-face communication, experienced some inefficiencies in communicating, participated more equally, were more uninhibited, and reached decisions which deviated more from their initial preferences (i.e., greater choice shift). This demonstrates that communication structures, different from those experienced in face-to-face exchanges, result in significant differences to the process of decision making. The usage of these communication structures with appropriate GDSS capabilities promises even more dramatic results.

## GDSS: The Concept

Group decision support systems combine computer, communications, and decision technologies to support problem finding, formulation and solution in group meetings. Because decision making in groups occurs through interpersonal communication $[4,31]$ , the most fundamental goal of a GDSS is to support and structure the exchange of ideas, opinions, and preferences within the group $[6,8]$ . A GDSS that provides communication-support features (beyond simple computer messaging), such as electronic brainstorming, alternatives organization, or voting support, is a Level 1 system (for a thorough explanation of GDSS levels, see DeSanctis & Gallupe $[10]$ ). A Level 2 system is one where the GDSS is enhanced by the addition of decision modeling or mathematical techniques.

Decision support technology aims to support the decision-making process by, among other things, reducing the cognitive load, or mental effort, associated with group meetings. In the case of Level 1 systems, this means that the system facilitates interpersonal sharing of ideas, opinions, and preferences by providing structure and automation. Even a basic Level 1 GDSS should ease some of the communication burden of open verbal exchange, thus simplifying the decision task.

## GDSS: Current Research

The development of systems to support the activities of face-to-face group meetings is a relatively new application of computer technology. Huber [19,20], and others [1,24,28,35] have outlined the design, functioning, and expected impacts of these systems. Only a small number of empirical studies related to group decision support systems (GDSS) have been conducted, however. Anecdotal evidence and case studies have been reported by Kull [29] and Gray et al. [14]. In these, the authors conclude that computer systems can improve the quality of decisions made in face-to-face meetings.

Steeb and Johnston [34], Turoff and Hiltz [37], Lewis [30], and Beauclair [3] have employed more rigorous experimental methods. These studies have been conducted in the laboratory and have used “crisis management” settings (e.g. survival in arctic conditions) in which small groups of people have been asked to generate action plans or choose from alternative solutions. GDSS software has been made available to decision making groups for soliciting and aggregating members’ ideas and/or preferences, and the performance of these groups was then compared to groups without access to the software support. Each of these experiments has demonstrated a performance advantage for groups using group decision making software compared to groups not using GDSS software.

The results of research on group processes that use GDSS must, in the long run, account for a myriad of variables that may affect group meetings and the organizations in which they occur. The impact of computer-based information systems has been shown to depend on a variety of factors such as: (1) the stage of computerization, (2) management policy, (3) the particular task involved, (4) existing organizational structure, and (5) amount of user experience with the system, and/or the type of system used (see Attwell & Rule, [2] and Rice [32]).

Knowledge regarding the impacts of a GDSS on users is less understood at this time. The initial empirical research has been confined to laboratory settings which provide the control necessary to begin understanding the relationship between the presence of GDSS technology and group outcomes. Thus a number of potential influences such as organizational contexts and group learning have, to this point, been avoided.

The purpose of this study is to investigate the effects of using a GDSS in a non-crisis decision situation which involves the usage of a CMC system.

## Hypotheses

The major question addressed by this research can be stated as: How does a GDSS affect group decision making for choice tasks where group members are either face-to-face or remote? This question was examined in terms of five main hypotheses. These are organized into two groups: decision performance and group members' reaction to the group decision process. All hypotheses predict an interaction effect between GDSS use and dispersion of group members on group outcomes. In other words, the effects of GDSS on group outcomes is expected to vary as a function of type of meeting (face-to-face versus remote). The hypotheses are stated in the null form, except where prior research indicates an effect.

## Decision Performance

Decision performance refers to those variables which attempt to measure the attributes of the “finished product” of the decision making process. For this study, decision performance includes decision quality (defined later), decision speed, and choice shift (the amount of change from individual choice to group choice). The hypotheses are described in terms of the impact of the two independent variables (GDSS/nonGDSS, Group member dispersion) on three dependent variables (decision quality, decision speed, choice shift).

H1: Decision Quality

H1a: In face-to-face meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will make decisions of higher quality than those not supported by a GDSS.

H1b: In remote meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will make decisions of higher quality than those not supported by a GDSS.

Previous research studies into GDSS support for face-to-face decision making [e.g., 12] have shown that those groups supported by a GDSS have made higher quality decisions. A variety of decision tasks were used in these studies (choice, problem finding) but the results indicated that the computer support helped structure the groups' deliberations and aided in higher quality analysis. Studies into the effects of computer-mediated conferencing have shown that such systems do not detract from the decision quality of the groups. Since we were adding the structure of the GDSS onto a computer-mediated conferencing facility, it was anticipated that remote GDSS groups would benefit from that structure and make higher quality decisions than remote groups without GDSS support.

H2: Decision Speed

H2a: In face-to-face meetings, there will be no difference in decision speed between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

H2b: In remote meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will take longer to reach a consensus decision compared to those groups not supported by a GDSS.

Previous studies of GDSS support for face-to-face meetings have shown differing results in terms of decision speed. Steeb and Johnston, and Lewis found the computer-supported groups took longer than unsupported groups. Gallupe [12] found no differences between groups in terms of time. Here, it was anticipated that in the face-to-face setting the task efficiencies that computer support would offer would be offset by the “overhead” of keyboarding and interfacing with the system and hence no difference in speed would occur. For remote groups, research has shown that decision groups take longer than face-to-face groups, mainly because of the extra messaging that must be made for consensus to occur [27]. Despite the fact that this extra effort will be minimized due to the structured facilities of the GDSS, it was hypothesized that remote DSS groups would take longer.

H3: Choice Shift

H3a: In face-to-face meetings, there will be no difference in choice shift between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

H3b: In remote meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will experience greater choice shift than those not supported by a GDSS.

Choice shift has been studied in the face-to-face meeting situations $[11,36]$ and in computer-mediated remote decision settings. Research has not yet been conducted into the impact a GDSS might have on choice shift. It is a well-known phenomenon that individuals are influenced by other individuals in the group when making a decision where a number of alternatives must be considered. A theory to explain this shift has been developed by Vinokur and Burnstein $[38,39]$ . This, the persuasive arguments theory, suggests that choice shift is caused by partially shared arguments for the initially favored alternative. It was anticipated that GDSS would minimize the persuasive aspects of communications within the group and hence no difference in choice shift would occur. For remote groups, Kiesler et al. found that they deviated further from initial individual preferences than face-to-face groups. They attempted to determine the reasons for this result but could not come up with any clear reasons. By using a GDSS in the remote setting, persuasive aspects of computer-mediated communication should be heightened by the structured features of the software and hence greater shift would occur.

## Perceived Reactions to the Decision Process

The perceived reactions to the decision process are defined as the group members' feelings about the decision process and the decision they have made. For this study, perception measures were decision confidence, and satisfaction with the process.

H4: Decision Confidence

H4a: In face-to-face meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will have less confidence in the decision made than those not supported by a GDSS.

H4b: In remote meetings, there will be no difference in decision confidence between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

Gallupe found that there was no significant difference between groups supported and those not supported by a GDSS in terms of decision confidence. The raw data however, indicated that GDSS groups were less confident than non-GDSS groups. He hypothesized that consideration of more alternatives and more in-depth analysis of those alternatives meant that group members were less sure that they had made the “best” decision. His study anticipated that this effect would hold and GDSS groups would be less confident that non-GDSS groups. No research could be found that looked at decision confidence in a remote situation. Based on the face-to-face research, it was anticipated that the use of the GDSS would not be able to compensate for the lack of in-depth group analysis that could be expected with dispersed users. Hence there should be no difference in perceived decision confidence in remote decision making between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported.

H5: Satisfaction with Decision Making Process

H5a: In face-to-face meetings, groups supported by a GDSS will be less satisfied with the decision making process than those not supported by a GDSS.

H5b: In remote meetings, there will be no difference in satisfaction between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

Recent research into face-to-face GDSS supported meetings shows differences in group member satisfaction with the decision making process. Lewis and Steeb and Johnston found GDSS group members to be more satisfied than non GDSS group members. Applegate et al found GDSS supported groups were very satisfied with their use of a planning-oriented GDSS. Gallupe and Watson [40] however, found that use of a GDSS led to less satisfaction when compared to non-GDSS groups. Since the GDSS used is similar to the one used in the Watson research, it was anticipated that group member satisfaction would be less in GDSS supported groups. No research could be found that looked at satisfaction in GDSS-supported remote meetings. It was anticipated that the major factor affecting satisfaction would be group member dispersion and not GDSS use, hence, there should be no differences in satisfaction between GDSS and non-GDSS groups in the remote setting.

## Research Method

This study builds on previous GDSS research findings by considering the effectiveness of a Level

1 GDSS in a choice task context for face-to-face and remote group meetings. By manipulating group dispersion, the study attempts to determine the ability of a GDSS to improve decision making performance.

The method is a laboratory experiment with students as participants. While this approach limits our ability to generalize the findings to practical organizational use of the technology, it offers two important advantages in the early phase of GDSS/CMC research. First, in the laboratory setting we can carefully vary the characteristics of the decision task and measure their effects on the group decision process and outcomes, while controlling for external factors. These variables would be difficult to manipulate or measure in a field setting. Second, a large number of research participants are needed for adequate statistical power, making students an attractive subject pool. Finding a two-hour time commitment from 60 industry people proved infeasible. In order to manipulate group member dispersion and measure the effects of GDSS on group outcomes for a large number of groups, we chose to sacrifice generalizability of the findings for greater internal control.

## The Experimental Task

The case that created the task setting for this research was “The Canada Continuous Forms Company” case (a modified version of the Bonanza Business Forms Company case by Jarvenpaa and Dickson [23]). It describes a firm that sells continuous paper forms for businesses in three markets (small business, the hospital, and the financial institution market). During the previous three quarters, the profits of the company have been steadily declining while total sales dollars have been increasing. As described in the case, Canada’s management cannot determine the cause of the declining profits and have asked for help. The group's task is to determine the cause of the company's problem using a series of business reports. Group members are asked to rank the causes individually (from most likely to least likely) and then, as a group, reach a consensus as to the overall ranking of the possible causes of the problem (see Figure 1 for layout of the "decision form").

## Dependent Measures

The dependent variables were the decision performance and perceived reactions of group members for the decision making session.

Decision Performance. Three aspects of decision performance were examined: decision quality, decision speed and choice shift. Decision quality was measured by comparing how close the group's rankings were to those made by experts. Consistent with earlier studies into GDSS, a panel of three experts was used to establish the rankings for the best choice. The difference between the group's rankings and the expert's rankings constituted the measure of decision quality.

Decision speed was defined as the length of time it took the group to reach a consensus decision. The groups were informed that consensus was required in the decision task. Consensus was achieved when all group members agreed. Decision speed was measured by recording the time it took the group to agree and fill out the group decision form at the end of the case.

Choice shift was defined as the difference between individual rankings made by group members before discussion and the eventual consensus rankings. A numeric score was determined for each group member and for each group. Higher numeric scores indicated greater choice shift. This approach is similar to the one used by Kiesler et al.

Group members' perceptions. These measures consisted of self-report ratings of the group's decision confidence, and satisfaction with the decision process.

Group decision confidence was assessed in two ways: (1) a post-test, seven-point Likert scale was given to each group member to determine the individual's confidence in the decision; and (2) the entire group was asked during the post-experiment debriefing session, how much confidence they had in the decision (a seven-point Likert scale was also used). The intent in measuring group confidence using two methods was to obtain a group confidence measure that reflected the group as a whole. Previous research [e.g., 5] had used the individual measure aggregated to a group measure and found that the procedure was satisfactory. For this experiment, individual point values were summed and then divided by the number of group members to get the aggregated group confidence score. This was compared with the verbal statement of group confidence given in the debriefing session. It was found that the aggregated score correlated highly with the verbal score in all cases ( $r = 0.79$ ). The aggregated measure was used in the data analysis.

Group satisfaction was recorded using the same procedure as that for group decision confidence. First, individual group members were asked, in the post-test questionnaire, how satisfied they were with the decision making process, using a seven-point Likert scale. Individual values were summed and divided by the number of members in the group, to give an aggregated group satisfaction score. Second, the entire group was asked in the post-experiment debriefing session (and after they had filled out the post-test questionnaire) how satisfied they were as a group with the decision making process. The verbal measure was compared with the aggregated group measure to get a more reliable estimate of group satisfaction. Again, it was found that the two measures correlated highly in all cases $r = 0.81$ . The aggregated group measure was used in the data analysis.

## Setting and Subjects

Setting. The setting for this experiment was a “decision room” that was designed and set up to accommodate face-to-face group interaction (Figure 2), and two other rooms, each remote from the other, which were used for the dispersed meeting treatment.

The computer terminals were put on their own movable desks. This enabled them to be moved into position for those groups that had computer support, and moved out of the way for groups that had no computer support. Each computer terminal had a movable keyboard so that individuals could see the other group members as well as their own terminal screens. The only difference in the setting between groups that had GDSS support and those that did not was the placement of computer terminals (one per group member) to support group decision making. All groups in the remote treatment used the terminals to communicate through the computer.

The Computer-Mediated Communications Software. The CMC was a system called "Phone", a utility available for the VAX 11/780 that enables users to call up other users on the system and communicate interactively with them. Once the connection is made, each user's screen is divided into sections equalling the number of users connected together, so that each user can see the messages typed in by the others. The GDSS. The GDSS called “DECISION AID FOR GROUPS ONE” (DECAID1) was designed, coded, and tested by the researchers. It was developed rather than purchased because the available commercial GDSS software was either chauffeur-driven (not allowing each group member to interface with the system), too limited in scope (message storing only, without voting or preference ranking), or too hardware dependent (running only under specialized operating systems). The software was a modified version of the one used by Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson [13].

The GDSS software performed the basic functions of recording, storing, organizing, and displaying alternatives that were entered by group members, aggregating and displaying preference rankings that had been entered for those alternatives, and recording votes (either publicly or anonymously) for the various alternatives generated. These features have been identified as appropriate for supporting the communications needs of the group. As noted earlier, our interest was in studying the impacts of a Level 1 system on groups.

Subjects. 60 senior undergraduate business administration students took part in this experiment. These subjects had taken at least one course each in management science/decision analysis techniques, marketing, and management theory/organizational behavior, and all had exposure to case analysis techniques. Extensive pilot testing indicated that subjects could perform the task and that the task had face validity. Random assignment of individuals to groups closely simulated a practical situation where some members have worked together before but some may not have. In addition, the random placement of subjects into groups meant that individual differences, such as decision making ability, were spread among the groups. The subjects were randomly assigned to groups of 3, and each group was randomly assigned to one experimental situation.

A total of 20 groups were formed, but due to logistical problems only 18 groups were used (5 groups in each of the two GDSS treatments and 4 groups in each of the non-GDSS treatments). This yielded sufficient degrees of freedom for a statistical analysis of the data. In order to get subjects prepared for the experiment, all (in both GDSS and non-GDSS) were given an overview of the GDSS and CMC and encouraged to try it out before the actual experiment. All groups were exposed to the concepts of alternatives generation/organization, preference ranking, and voting, and all groups were given training in the use of the GDSS and CMC so that any unanticipated effects due to training would be constant across treatment conditions. Groups using GDSS received a brief refresher course at the beginning of their session.

Experimental Procedure. The following procedure was used for each of the groups in this experiment:

1. Subjects were asked to fill out a consent form and a demographic data form.

2. Subjects were asked to fill out a pre-test questionnaire that asked about attitudes towards computers and group decision making.

3. All groups were asked to follow the same general decision making steps:

1. Read and analyze the case individually,

2. Individually rank alternatives,

3. Initiate discussion about the case,

4. Generate alternatives,

5. Rank alternatives,

6. Resolve any conflict,

7. Vote on alternatives to reach consensus.

This placed some structure on the decision making session but allowed for free-flowing discussion. Groups that were supported by the GDSS were instructed to use whatever features of the system they thought might help the group decision making (all used at least one system feature). Groups supported by the CMC could use the system at any time to communicate with other group members. The agreed upon solution was written on the decision forms provided.

4. Individual group members were asked to fill out a post-test questionnaire that asked about confidence in the decision, and satisfaction with the process.

5. A debriefing session was given to each group to determine if the group members had any questions, comments, or suggestions regarding the decision making session. The group was asked to provide a rating of their decision confidence and satisfaction with the decision process.

## Impacts on Decision Performance

Decision Quality. The results indicate that use of a GDSS had no significant impact on decision quality in either the face-to-face or the remote setting. H1 was therefore rejected. In fact, no significant differences were found among any of the treatments.

The general finding that use of a GDSS does not improve decision quality is inconsistent with previously mentioned experimental studies conducted in face-to-face situations. These studies all reported higher quality decisions in GDSS supported groups. It may be that the decision task was not difficult enough for the GDSS to have an impact.

This finding however, is consistent with studies into computer-mediated messaging support which found that decision quality was not affected by face-to-face or dispersed group location. It appeared that the GDSS did not significantly aid the groups in the remote situation.

Decision Time. The time to reach a consensus decision was significantly affected by the use of the GDSS (GDSS groups took longer) and group member dispersion (remote groups took longer) (H2). This finding is consistent with that of the Steeb and Johnston study, which found that GDSS groups tended to take longer. It is also consistent with the Kiesler et al study which showed that dispersed groups took longer.

The GDSS groups and remote groups spent extra time working with the system and organizing the group process to work with the system (as reflected by comments such as: “Let me enter that alternative”, “I think I should change that alternative”, “Let’s vote on the alternatives now”, etc.).

Choice Shift. The only statistically significant interaction effect was found with choice shift. The main effects were not significant hence, H3 must be rejected. This interaction effect is interesting because it suggests that use of a GDSS tends to mediate the effect of dispersion of group members. The shift was less in the GDSS/remote and non-GDSS/face-to-face. Kiesler's research confirms the latter but not the former. A possible reason for this interaction effect is that the GDSS tends to even out participation in the group deliberations and this may be more effective in the remote situation.

## Impacts on Group Members's Attitudes

Analyses of variance for each of the two attitudinal variables (shown in Table 3) revealed no statistically significant interaction effects for the GDSS and dispersion variables but one interesting main effect.

Decision Confidence. The hypothesis that use of a GDSS in face-to-face decision settings would decrease confidence in the decision was not supported at the p < 0.05 level (H3a). This finding is consistent with the Steeb and Johnston and the Turoff and Hiltz studies that found that groups supported by a computer-based group decision aid had more confidence in the decision made. Hypothesis H3b, which stated that there would be no difference in confidence in the remote groups, was supported. Interestingly, the raw data results indicate that remote groups that used the GDSS were slightly more confident in the decision they had made compared to the other remote groups.

These results show that GDSS use or group dispersion does not affect confidence in the decision made. This result adds to the somewhat confusing results about decision confidence and GDSS use. As mentioned, some studies indicate increased confidence of GDSS supported groups. Other studies have yielded results that show confidence is reduced with GDSS use. This study shows no difference. Decision confidence is an important outcome variable for group decision making. One of the reasons for using groups is to encourage a number of individuals to participate in the decision making process and hence feel confident in the decision. Further study of the effects of GDSS technology on group confidence is needed.

Satisfaction with the Group Decision Making Process. Groups in the remote decision setting were significantly less satisfied than those in the face-to-face setting. This result confirms the work of other researchers who found that face-to-face meetings were more satisfying than computer conferences. Hypothesis H5a suggested that GDSS groups would be less satisfied than non-supported groups. This result was observed in the raw data but was not significant. Lack of user satisfaction in GDSS groups in both face-to-face and remote groups is disturbing. This result has been observed in some studies but not in others. More research is needed into the appropriate use of GDSS in both remote and face-to-face settings if this technology is to be used.

## Discussion

Use of a GDSS was found to increase the time it took groups to reach a decision yet had no impact on the decision quality of the groups. Dispersion of the group members made no difference in terms of decision quality but significantly increased the time for groups to reach a decision. The results confirm and extend knowledge of the effectiveness of GDSS in face-to-face and remote settings. Decision quality is not impaired by group member dispersion and may be aided by use of a GDSS.

This study again showed that differences do exist in the perceptions of group members when using computer technology to support group collaborative work. In particular, decision satisfaction was less in GDSS groups and in CMC groups when compared to non-computer supported groups. Ratings on these variables by the GDSS groups were not extremely low, in the sense that the average scores were on the positive side of a midpoint of 4 on a 7-point scale. Ideally, however, the goal of decision support systems should be to enhance user satisfaction and confidence, as well as to improve decision quality [25]. Positive sentiments following use of GDSS may be particularly important in organizational settings where group meetings are used to gain consensus and acceptance of ideas, rather than to gain better ideas per se.

Perhaps the most noteworthy finding of this study was the interaction effect of GDSS and group member dispersion on choice shift. It appeared that use of face-to-face GDSS tended to lessen the shift from individual values to group choice, but it had the opposite effect in the remote groups. A possible explanation for this result is that in face-to-face meetings the use of the GDSS had a “leveling” effect on the persuasive comments of other group members and hence less choice shift was observed. In the remote setting, use of the GDSS may have highlighted differing points-of-view (that is, acted as a persuasive media) that may not have been communicated using the CMC. This might have led group members to change their original rankings based on new information.

## Conclusions and Implications

Our research adds to the growing body of empirical literature which demonstrates that the use of GDSS technology should be fine-tuned to the task and setting for which it will be used. At this point, the precise reason for the positive and negative effects of the technology on groups is not altogether understood. In other words, how is the GDSS, interacting with the CMC, affecting group activities to yield greater choice shift? How is the

GDSS affecting group activities to negatively impact group member perceptions? Our observation was that the GDSS provided a different type of structured communication channel for the group members. The persuasive effect of this channel seemed to vary with group member dispersion. In the remote setting, the GDSS offered a “richer” mode of communication which enabled group members to structure their deliberation better. In the face-to-face setting the GDSS tended to foster more “democratic” participation and the persuasive impact seemed to be minimal. The GDSS did not force groups into a structure, but rather suggested opportunities for structuring group discussion. An avenue for further research is to be more precise in identifying the influence of GDSS characteristics, particularly suggested structure, on the group decision process in both face-to-face and remote settings.

The only interaction effect observed in this study was choice shift. Other interaction effects were not observed. Several explanations are possible here. A larger sample size, resulting in higher statistical power, may have resulted in significant interaction effects for some of the dependent variables. Another possibility is that the decision task was not difficult enough to yield the hypothesized impacts for the GDSS intervention. Every effort was made to validate the task. Further research might go beyond the ranking-choice task considered in this study. In particular, the question of whether GDSS can be helpful (for both face-to-face and remote groups) in “intelligence” and “design” tasks should be pursued.

All these findings must be interpreted with caution, given the limited scope and sampling of the study. More extended laboratory testing and field studies, involving a variety of GDSS/CMC designs over a longer period of time, with actual managers, and with larger groups than the current study, are required before firm conclusions regarding the impacts of these systems for specific task types can be truly understood.

Further study and replication of all of the findings of this research may be of interest. In addition, the results of this study raise the following questions:

\- What are the precise causes of the choice shift that was observed when group members used a GDSS in the face-to-face and remote settings?

\- How will extended experience with GDSS and CMC on the part of decision makers affect decision quality, speed, choice shift and user perceptions?

\- Can GDSS be useful to groups confronting highly emotional, or politically-laden tasks, particularly in remote group settings?

Finally, research might refine and enhance the approach of this study by examining different tasks with differing types of technology to support dispersed groups (GDSS and audio, for example), considering groups of varying size, precisely isolating the nature of GDSS impacts on group processes, and taking the methodology out of the laboratory and investigating the use of GDSS in actual organizational settings.
