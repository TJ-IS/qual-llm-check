# Information Systems Strategy Formulation

T.M. Waema and G. Walsham

Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge, Cambridge CB2 1RX, United Kingdom

Information systems in organisations can be viewed as social systems and the formulation of IS strategy as a social process. Alternative formulation models have been based on formal-rational or power-behavioural approaches, but here we propose a holistic analysis involving the context, content and process of strategy formulation. Some aspects of the methodology are illustrated using a case study of a UK building society.

Keywords: Information systems strategy, Social systems, Socio-political process, Wider context, Organisational context, Strategy formulation, Financial services.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) is recognised today as a major force influencing business performance. Consequently, there has been increasing concern in most organisations regarding its management. One of the important means of addressing this concern is the formulation of information systems (IS) strategies that are relevant to organisational needs and that will ensure successful implementation.

Information systems strategies can be considered as applications strategies that aim to align IS developments with business needs and seek competitive advantage from IT [7]. It has been noted in the literature [for example, 24] that the importance of IS strategy varies considerably between organisations and between sectors. There are significant differences in the perception of top-level management of the importance of IT in their organisation and many inherent difficulties in measuring that importance. The effectiveness of alternative approaches to the formulation of an IS strategy is perhaps even more difficult to assess. Even after using a series of approaches, many organisations are still not sure of the best way forward. Moreover, similar approaches to IS strategy formulation have been reported to produce different levels of effectiveness in different organisations [25,33]. This suggest that an IS strategy formulation approach needs to take into account the specific context of an organisation. It is therefore not surprising that researchers, such as Sullivan, have suggested a contingent approach to IS strategic planning [39]. These approaches have, however, lacked a theoretical basis and empirical evidence for describing organisational contexts.

In general, previous research has adopted limited perspectives that have been found lacking in explanatory power, particularly those that have taken the rather simplistic approach of studying

IS strategy formulation as a discrete activity at one point in time. We believe that it is imperative to treat strategy formulation as a continuous process in a constantly changing context. We therefore see the need for a broad and sound theoretical basis for the understanding of both context and process and believe that longitudinal interpretive case studies provide essential empirical evidence to test the validity of that approach.

This paper describes part of a research project on IS strategy formulation in UK financial service sector organisations. The main reason for choosing this sector is the high strategic importance of IT for financial organisations in coping with competitive pressure in the environment of these institutions. This is particularly so after the wave of deregulation since the early 1980s. In addition, narrowing the scope of the study to one sector allows the relationships between IS strategy formulation processes and the wider sectoral context to be explored more deeply.

## 2. Strategy formulation

We shall first trace the development of various models of strategy formulation, particularly the formal-rational and power-behavioural approaches. The major distinction between the two is in their perception of the nature of strategy formulation. The former considers strategy formulation to be mainly a formal process carried out with the help of analytical techniques; it largely ignores the context of the process. Power-behavioural approaches, in contrast, recognise the importance of behavioural contexts, such as the existence of coalitions and multiple goal structures, political differences between participants, and bargaining and negotiation in conflict situations [36].

The earliest contribution to strategy formulation came from attempts to formulate business strategies; the approaches were based on theories of decision-making. Business strategy formulation was treated as a special kind of problem-solving process for defining an organisation's business strategy [12]. The implicit view of problem-solving is then rational, and the strategy formulation models that have dominated the strategic management literature have concentrated on describing and prescribing techniques for carrying out the set of logical procedures that are seen to make up this formal process. These procedures can be listed as: identifying current strategy; analysing environments, resources and gaps; revealing and assessing strategic alternatives; and choosing and implementing carefully analysed and well-thought through alternatives $[3,4,9,26]$ .

The above approaches have theoretical underpinnings in traditional rational and boundedly rational decision-making process theories. Strategy formulation is seen as an outcome of an iterative, multi-level process, where decisions are the outcomes of rational or boundedly rational debates. Certain assumptions are made within this view, such as: goals are known and consistent; actors are analytically objective in carrying out logical activities; cause-effect relationships are fairly well understood; and enough information is available to tackle most issues effectively. These are unrepresentative of organisational reality and generally simplistic. One other major flaw has been noted: the over-emphasis on analytical strategy content at the expense of the contexts in which these strategies are formulated $[14,32]$ .

The limited view of a behavioural context in these early approaches came under severe criticism [8]. The assumptions underlying the approaches also came under attack. For example, securing information about alternative courses of action is extremely difficult as alternatives keep changing; calculation of the consequences of each alternative is also difficult if not impossible; and agreed values on goals may not exist. As a result, other approaches more amenable to organisational reality were advanced. Etzioni modified the early approaches to decision-making to fit his own view of decision activity which he called “incrementalism”. Both Etzioni’s “incrementalism” and Braybrooke and Lindblom’s [5] “muddling through” theories identified human value systems as an important contributory factor in environmental uncertainty; an important concept of decision-making. “Muddling through” for instance assumes that social systems and processes are complex phenomena and that it is impossible to predict exactly what results will be created by one’s actions or what difficulties will be encountered.

One of the important behavioural elements that rational approaches fail to consider is the diversity of interests and goals within organisations. In recognition of such political realities, Cyert and

March [6] and March [20] chose to include a coalition view of goal formation as part of their behavioural theory of the firm; this challenged the conventional assumptions made by the formal-rational view of organisational decision-making. Coalitions can be viewed as goal-oriented alliances among actors with different interests formed to mobilise joint resources to influence the outcome of a decision. Coalitions do not necessarily conform to the formal organisational structure, but are socially constructed groupings that emerge out of informal influence processes central to organisational politics. They evolve due to limited resources, interdependency of tasks, limited availability of information, differential but limited power, and differences and mutuality of interests [31,40]. Coalitions therefore form as a result of individuals and groups marshalling support from others whose interests would be enhanced, or at least not harmed, by giving their support. The main aim of a coalition is to reinforce or increase its power vis-à-vis that of other groups.

The consideration of coalition formation was a first step in integrating behavioural aspects into strategy formulation. Increasingly, psychological, social and political behavioural processes have been found to be important in influencing the effectiveness of strategy formulation. Social/political power behaviour has been found to be particularly closely representative of the reality of organisational decision-making. Within this power conception of strategy formulation, conflict is considered normal, or at least customary, when coalitions interact. The final decision in such a conflict situation will evolve out of a process of power mobilisation attempted by each party in support of its demands [29]. As Allison put it “action results from games among players who perceive quite different faces of an issue and who differ markedly in the actions they prefer” [2]. This is in complete contrast to the rational perspective that assumes a certain degree of consensus among decision makers; that strategy formulation can be understood by treating organisations as if they are centrally coordinated purposive individuals; and relies on objective normative analytical methods to derive what Mintzberg called “intended” strategy [28].

But power-behavioural approaches alone do not enable a comprehensive explanation of the effectiveness of strategy formulation in all contexts.

Some researchers have seen the need to combine these approaches with aspects of formal-rational methods in order to derive a fuller explanation. Quinn for example criticised the formal-rational, or what he called formal-analytical, approach to strategy formulation for its undue focus on measurable factors and its under-emphasis of the vital qualitative, organisational, and power-behavioural factors that underly strategic success in many instances [34,36]. However, he also criticised the early power-behavioural studies on strategy formulation [6,19,21,37,38] on the grounds that some were conducted in settings far removed from the realities of strategy formulation, while others ignored the ways in which strategic data analysis shaped and often determined crucial aspects of strategic decisions. He therefore advocated a combined approach to strategy formulation and used the term “logical incrementalism” for such an approach. This is described as a jointly analytical and behavioural process in which executives are described as and are recommended to “proceed flexibly and experimentally from broad concepts to specific commitments, making the latter concrete as late as possible” [35]. He saw logical incrementalism, not as a muddling process, but as “a purposeful, effective, proactive management technique for improving and integrating both the analytical and behavioural aspects of strategy formulation”. When interpreted using logical incrementalism, Quinn found that he could get a richer explanation of strategic change from his case study data.

The authors believe that strategy formulation in most organisations contains elements of both formal-rationality and power behaviours to differing levels, and that it depends on both the organisation's internal and external contexts. It therefore becomes necessary to include aspects of both perspectives, in order to describe more fully the process of strategy formulation. Indeed, one can go further, and consider that theories are needed for research on strategy formulation which include all the perspectives in a broader holistic analysis. A theoretical basis for the development of such an approach has been described by Pettigrew and illustrated by an in-depth longitudinal study in ICI [30]. Pettigrew describes the relationship between formal-rational approaches and power-behavioural aspects when stating that “strategy formulation and change processes in organisations may be understood in part as the outcome of processes of competition between...rationalities expressed through the language, priorities and values of technologists; of accounting and finance – the bottom line; or of the rather more diffuse perspectives adopted by specialist groups from planning, operational research, organisational development, or personnel".

Pettigrew describes a detailed approach to integrate the context, process and content of strategy formulation which avoids the use of “singular theories”, using both a vertical and a horizontal component of analysis. The vertical one refers to the interdependencies between different levels in the organisation and its context which can be explained by reference to a further level; for example, features of intra-organisational context and interest group behaviour linked to the impact of a changing socio-economic context. The horizontal or process-oriented component of analysis refers to the sequential interconnectedness between phenomena in historical, present, and future time. Pettigrew describes four elements: a theoretical and empirically connectable set of levels of analysis, a clear description of the processes under examination, a theory or model of man underlying the research, and, crucially, the way in which the contextual variables in the vertical analysis are linked to the processes. Although Pettigrew does not offer a theoretical method to analyse the linkages between contexts and processes, his approach, in our opinion, offers a more satisfactory basis for research on strategy formulation than earlier work.

We therefore view strategy formulation as made up of social and political processes embedded in a dynamic relationship with the organisation's internal and wider contexts. Formal-rational mechanisms used in these processes can be means by which individual actors and groups influence the perceptions of others and legitimise outcomes important to them. Outcomes are achieved through mobilisation and exploitation of available and controllable resources, which are aspects of context.

## 3. Information Systems

The positivists have long considered information systems as technical systems and only paid lip-service to “human” aspects. This view focusses on explicit economic, physical, or information processing features of the technology. Hirschheim [11] argues that this has prevailed because, historically, information systems resources have been more expensive than human resources, the self-selection in the system field has produced people who lack behavioural skills and concern for human values, and systems developers are therefore not rewarded for considering human values.

The conception that information systems are technical systems with social implications has been criticised as inadequate; for example, Land and Hirschheim consider that “information systems are not technical systems which have behavioural and social consequences, rather they are social systems which rely to an increasing extent on information technology for their function” [18]. The technological component therefore only forms part of an information system. The social context in which the technology is embedded is often as important, if not more important. Information systems are absorbed into a complex set of social relationships and can therefore rightly be considered as social systems where resource allocation is not neutral and resources can be selectively exploited to serve the interests of powerful and influential actors and groups.

From a formal-rational perspective, information systems are technical systems used to achieve rational objectives; increasing efficiency, reducing costs, saving time, and so on. Such results are sometimes achieved, but information systems may also be used as sources of power and vehicles for change. For instance, in the collection, evaluation, and utilisation of data, actors may disguise intentions and withhold, distort, or manipulate information. Further, goals that are espoused and information that is exchanged may be laden with symbolic overtones, making it difficult to assess its reliability [41].

Web models define the social context of an information system by incorporating the social relations between participants, the supporting infrastructural resources, and the history of commitments in and around the information system [17]. The integration of an information system into such a social context implies that it is likely to affect the existing social order. Keen considers that information systems redistribute data, and therefore redesign parts of the organisation, disrupt patterns of communication, and reallocate authority [13]. Information systems therefore have an effect on the distribution of power by virtue of their impact on information: a vital political resource. Building a database for example, is a political move; sometimes it is equivalent to a declaration of war. The systems designer needs to know who owns the data, who will share it, and what will be the perceived impact of redistribution on evaluation, influence and authority, and communication. Certainly, information systems will increase power and influence to those who can organise data to their advantage and also to those who have access to the technology and can understand its use. Thus it is important to understand organisational social and political contexts in which a system is to be utilised, as well as the system's technical features, in order to formulate a strategy for information systems.

Another illustration of the importance of organisational context is given by Kling [15]. He cites several studies, such as Albrecht's [1] study of information systems in juvenile courts and Greenberger et al.'s [10] study of the role of econometric models in developing U.S. fiscal policy, that emphasise the role of the socio-political context in influencing whether computing is adopted, which technologies will be selected, how they will be developed, and whom they shall serve. Most of the research in this area has been in systems development and implementation [e.g. 13,16,22,23] and very little on IS strategy formulation.

## 4. IS Strategy Formulation

These perspectives on strategy formulation and information systems can be synthesised into a research paradigm for investigating IS strategy formulation. The view of information systems as social systems emphasises the social dimensions and the importance of the context in the organisation. It is possible to integrate this with the socio-political process view to form a holistic description of IS strategy formulation in organisations.

Pettigrew's analytical approach can be described in terms of the contextual levels of analysis, the processes being investigated, the linkages between contexts and processes, and the theoretical perspective on human behaviour. In financial services sector organisations, three vertical levels of analysis can be identified:

\- the global and national level, consisting mainly of the political, social and economic environments;

\- the financial services sector level, principally made up of regulatory, technological, and competitive elements; and

\- the organisational level, made up of the contexts of corporate culture, organisational structure, people and their socio-political relationships, strategy, and technology.

Changes in a context at one level affect contexts at other levels, the processes of IS strategy formulation and the outcome. For instance, the U.K. Conservative Government's decision to put monetary control at the centre of its economic policy and its commitment to free market forces led to the removal of regulatory barriers in the early 1980s. The wave of deregulation that followed in the financial services sector in general and building societies in particular, coupled with technological advances, led to severe competition in the building societies' markets. This in turn led to changes in organisational level contexts in order to cope with the shifts. Many societies for instance changed their top leadership in order to try to foster an enterprise culture, and changed their organisational structure to reflect a marketing orientation, started diversifying into the deregulated markets, and used IT in a more strategic fashion.

The appropriate theoretical perspective on human behaviour in analysing IS strategy formulation is socio-political. The unit of analysis is the behaviour of key actors and groups in a particular IS strategy formulation process. But, as implied by our holistic approach, analysis is not restricted to this basic level. Human behaviour in IS strategy formulation processes cannot be explained only in terms of purposes or intentions without reference to how they interact with contexts at higher levels and the consequences of such interactions. Within this perspective, formal mechanisms and rational concepts used in IS strategy formulation are considered as ways of legitimising and communicating perceived interests.

Thus assumptions that we adopt are - IS strategy formulation is a socio-political process in which formal-rational mechanisms can form an important part;

\- Information systems and formal-rational mechanisms are aspects of organisational context. This context is not neutral; individual actors and groups can mobilise and exploit it to influence outcomes and to legitimise preferred decisions and actions;

\- IS strategy formulation processes have a dynamic and reciprocal causal relationship with the internal and wider contexts in which they are embedded.

## 5. Case Study

This example is part of longitudinal study of IS strategy formulation in a building society (Trent). Between February and May 1987, eight managers were interviewed, each for periods ranging from one to two hours. The deputy chief executive was interviewed twice in this period. Six of the managers plus another four were then interviewed in December 1987. In May 1988, four managers were interviewed for a third time. The managers were primarily senior executives, IS managers, and main IS users. Triangulation of the interview data was achieved by cross-interviewing and analyses of data from both primary and secondary sources, which included minutes of meetings, internal memos, the Society's monthly newspaper, the annual Directors' Report and Accounts, and the monthly Building Societies Gazette.

## 5.1. The Context of Trent Building Society

## The Wider Context of Building Societies

The operation of building societies, like other financial institutions, can be described as borrowing money in the short-term and lending it in the long-term, using interest rate differentials to cover overhead costs and provide the required profits. But unlike other financial institutions, building societies were restricted by law to lending for owner occupation, and for many years they dominated the house loan market. In addition, societies faced little difficulty in raising funds from the personal retail market, mainly due to the tax advantages they enjoyed over other financial institutions and the fact that investments with societies were perceived to be highly liquid and secure. The absence of inter-society price competition due to the Building Societies Association (BSA) rates cartel also provided a safe environment. But the situation changed in the early 1970s when banks and other financial institutions began to tap the retail savings market. The competition that ensued carried on into the 1980s, when it became even more severe following legal, political, socio-economic, competitive and technological changes in the immediate and more distant environment.

Competition in the personal savings market intensified when the new Conservative Government decided, in 1979, to fund the public sector borrowing requirement (PSBR) from personal savings through the National Savings Bank as part of its monetary policy. Competition increased further when the tax advantages which societies enjoyed over other institutions began to disappear. In February 1983, the Inland Revenue announced that investment profits for building societies were to be taxed at corporation tax rates (then 40%), whereas they had previously been subject to a lower rate tax (then 30%), with concessionary tax-free profits on gilts held for more than a year before disposal. The remaining advantages were stripped off when the Chancellor of the Exchequer, in the budget of 1984, announced that the special building society corporation tax rate of 40% was to be extended to the banks (then 52%) and that societies were no longer to treat profits on gilt trades as capital gains but as trading income in line with the banks. Banks thus became major competitors in the personal savings market. The BSA administered rate cartel had been abolished in 1983. This had, in turn, introduced inter-society price competition. The price of personal savings was later adversely affected by the Government's continued privatisation programme and an increased preference for unit trusts for personal investments.

By the early 80s, banks were also providing serious competition in the mortgage market while demand in the housing market had slowed. The banks' entry in this market had been triggered by the Government's decision to put monetary control at the centre-stage of its economic policy and to encourage free market competition. Following this, the Bank of England, in the summer of 1980, abandoned the 'corset' that constrained bank lending. This came at a time when most clearing banks were facing difficulties lending to Third World countries and when most of the capitalist economies were in recession. The combined effect forced and at the same time enabled banks to enter the mortgage market in a significant way. By 1982, they had increased their share of the market from less than 10% in 1980 to almost 40%. The security of the British mortgage market, compared to turbulent corporate and national lending, attracted further entrants. From 1985 onwards, other financial institutions, notably foreign ones, set up specialised mortgage markets that were funded from the cheaper wholesale money markets to which the building societies had little access. Both the clearing banks and these institutions could undercut societies in mortgage pricing. By the third quarter of 1987, the building society share of the house loan market had dwindled to 47%, from 86% in the first quarter of 1985.

Building societies had traditionally used IT to improve the quality of their services and to reduce overhead costs. But advances in IT, notably reduced hardware costs, increased software capability and the convergence of computing and communications technologies, have given rise to product and process innovations in financial markets. The consequences have been a significant reduction in entry barriers. Societies, which were heavily regulated, found themselves in difficulties in fighting infiltration into their markets. The larger societies began to put pressure on the Government to change the regulatory framework so that they could compete with some of their competitors' products. This culminated in the 1986 Building Societies Act which allowed societies to diversify into most housing, retail banking and investment services. It came into effect in January 1987 but societies still found this Act and the accompanying capital adequacy requirements very restrictive. Further lobbying resulted in major revisions to its provision in late 1987 and early 1988. Societies were given complete freedom in housing services and could offer most retail financial services.

This and other changes in the wider organisational contexts brought competitive pressure, and sometimes opportunities, in societies markets. This required a critical re-examination of intra-organisational contexts, particularly business strategies, available human resource skills, and appropriateness of the culture and structure to implement adopted business strategies. In an industry that uses IT to deliver its products and services, and where IT was becoming more of a basis for entry into new product market areas, information systems became of higher strategic value to business than before.

Internal Organisational Context of Trent Building Society

Trent Building Society is medium-sized, with over a billion pounds sterling worth of assets, over 250 branches and agencies, and over 400 employees. It has its headquarters in London and an administration centre outside London. Over 10% of the employees work in the Data Processing (DP) Department, located in the administration centre. By the end of 1987, the Society had had a remarkable performance record. From 1981 to 1987, its assets had multiplied about four times, operating costs had gone down from over 1.7% to 1.0% of mean total assets, advances on mortgages had multiplied about eight times, and the total number of employees had only increased by about 50 people.

A new Chief Executive (CE) was appointed in August 1981. He had been a Marketing General Manager (GM) in one of the top five building societies. At the time he was appointed, the Trent Building Society had stagnated for some time following a merger with a smaller society, and its operations were very costly. The new CE viewed his job as having to turn the Society into a more profitable and competitive institution. In September 1981, he made structural changes to create a functional management structure. The CE exploited the near crisis situation the Society was in to foster an enterprise culture and to pursue business strategies which were largely determined by himself alone. As a senior executive commented: "he did a lot of the thinking on his own. He wasn't a great one for communicating his ideas to other people...He must have had a good sense of purpose...". However, this "sense of purpose" was, according to most managers, biased towards his personal ambitions. Another senior executive thought that "he wanted to demonstrate to the outside world, as well as to the directors, that he could turn the Society round and make it much more profitable, reduce staff, reduce costs, become more efficient generally ... obviously with the ultimate view of being taken on by somebody else to do the same thing in a bigger organisation." He managed to centralise the three administration centres into one (1982–83), to decentralise both investment account and mortgage processing from the centralised mainframe system to on-line branch counter systems (1983–85), and to extend the Society's retail banking services by offering cheque accounts and introducing ATMs (1985–86). Attempts to merge with a larger society in 1986 fell through and he resigned in early 1987 to become the managing director of a larger financial institution. The failure of this merger was thought to have precipitated his resignation.

The board appointed a new CE on June 1 1987. He had been an operations GM in one of the top ten building societies. He immediately introduced a more participative management style. Strategic business thrusts were now formally determined in a Corporate Planning Team that consisted of the Management Committee and four line managers. In some of the initial stages of this formal process, up to 30 people from most levels of the Society were involved in conferences in which they had a chance to air their views. The new style, introduced at a time of new market opportunities and numerous problems in the Society, led to various individuals and groups expressing a variety of demands that had previously been suppressed. Some of the consequences were the use of structured development methods, user department-driven steering committees, establishment of a data recovery centre, and changing the management structure. In this, the DP Manager became an assistant GM in charge of computing. Although he still reported to the deputy CE, his duties had changed from managing day-to-day DP activities to strategically controlling the IS function.

## 5.2. IS Strategy Formulation

## 1981 to Mid-1987

IS strategy formulation was dominated by the CE during this period. At the business strategy formulation stage, neither the Board of Directors nor the Management Committee were consulted on the CE's strategies. The experience of a member of the Committee was that "in the Management Committee meetings, the CE tended to say most of the things he had decided on... He tended to make his own decisions and, as it were, tell everybody what we were doing... He was not prepared to discuss strategy with any one...The Board had no influence at all. They did what he wanted them to do".

The process of linking business strategy and IS plans was performed by the CE, largely alone. The linking can be said to have been achieved in the CE's mind and to have been shaped by his previous experience and a knowledge of actions of the leaders in the industry, partly through his interactions with them. His knowledge and ability to collect intelligence on other societies' actions was enhanced when he was elected to the councils of the BSA and of the International Union of Building Societies and Savings Associations in 1983 and 1984 respectively. But even before this, according to the Finance GM, "he seemed to be able to get all building society information from them, which was quite an advantage". He would normally improve on other societies' actions before implementing in his own Society. For instance, the decision to decentralise investment processing to branch front counters in 1983 had been influenced in this way. At this time, three of the top societies had implemented front office processing decentralisation. The society for which CE had previously worked was also decentralising its back office processing. He improved on this and modified the teleprocessing system to decentralise mortgage applications processing to the front branch counter. This addition was completed in early 1985.

Other stages of the process of determining IS strategy, such as evaluation of existing IS, selection of appropriate systems, and implementation of the chosen IT systems were primarily undertaken by the DP Department staff but with considerable involvement of top management. Even at the physical systems design stage, the CE would often provide guidelines on how he wanted the system developed. He had, for instance, himself drawn an outline for the computer screens for some IT applications!

This type of high level involvement lacked any standard approach and was aimed at ensuring that the Society's systems could react relatively quickly to changes in business needs. This was achieved to a large extent but at the same time, according to the Systems Development Manager, it was a major source of inflexibilities in the systems. It also reduced the IS staff into pressured and routinised workers leading to resentment and low motivation, with adverse social and psychological consequences.

## Mid-1987 Onwards

The departure of the previous CE changed the nature of formulating IS strategy. The deputy CE initially regained direction and control of the IS function. He identified the broad IS needs and opportunities, acted as the main link between IS developments and business needs and had considerable influence over the choice and implementation of IT systems. From the CE's viewpoint, "the deputy CE, a member of the management committee, has his finger very much on the pulse of IT. The deputy CE, having come up via that route and got to the top, is a very good computer technician. Our strategy ahead, in as much as IT is concerned, is affected by the input the deputy CE has...So IT strategy is determined partly by the deputy CE, partly by me – virtually nobody else".

But as the new participative style began to take effect, particularly with the establishment of a formal corporate planning process, input to IS strategy was widened to other members of line and senior management. For example the new CE cited that “an input to IT strategy comes from the DP Manager rather than him being given a list of tasks to do”. He was, for example, expected to draw up the IS plans that would support the Society’s objectives and business strategies emerging out of the planning process.

The new management style also enabled other aspects of IS strategy formulation to change. The Systems Development Manager seized the opportunity to change the previous approach to systems analysis and design. He proposed, and top management approved, the use of structured development methods and the participation of users in steering committees. The planning and implementation of strategic IS projects was heavily influenced by the new user department-driven steering committees. These, chaired by a senior user manager, established business and user requirements for major IS projects. In conjunction with DP analysts, the steering committee proposed the various implementation options. The final selection of the IT system still rested with top management. After the selection, the steering committee and the DP analysts defined the required systems in greater detail, before proceeding to conceptual and physical systems design.

## 6. Preliminary conclusions

A snapshot of IS strategy formulation would have provided very little understanding of its complex and dynamic nature. Our study has shown the importance of both internal and external organisational contexts and their linkages in understanding IS strategy formulation.

During the autocratic era of the former CE, IS strategy was largely an informal and incremental process dominated and driven by him. He exploited his knowledge and expertise, his access and control of information, his personal characteristics, and his position of formal authority to formulate and implement strategic IS plans. These strategies benefited the Society but were also well matched to his personal interests.

The linkages between the wider and internal organisational contexts, and between them and IS strategy formulation, were also dominated by the CE. The interpretive processes involved drew upon his previous experience, his cognitive maps and personal interests. The linkages after mid-1987 were more complicated, because they involved the perceptions, interpretations, and power-oriented behaviours of a group of actors in a changing corporate culture.

The use of information systems as sources of power can be illustrated by the processing decentralisation project. On the one hand, the project can be viewed as having decentralised power from the administration centre to the branches; the mortgages line manager lost the deeds department and other aspects of the mortgage function after decentralisation, while branch managers gained power to make autonomous decisions using the low level management information that was available from the teleprocessing system. On the other hand, however, the CE used information systems to centralise his power; he used a PC to access the mainframe database and controlled both the activities of the DP department and the branches. He also had a PC remotely linked to the management database in the finance division. He could use the finance model to simulate ‘what if’ situations and make strategic pricing decisions when competitive pressures had forced most societies to concentrate on such policies. Therefore, although information systems were used to decentralise power in some respects, they were also used to further vest power in the CE. One can argue that information systems were used to enhance the CE's autocratic decision-making style. The charisma and drive of the former CE, his autocratic decision-making style, and the scant communication of business strategies ensured that strong coalitions could not form to bargain or negotiate for their interests. The rest of the management and staff either went along with the assertive objectives of the CE or merely did their work out of self motivation. It is possible that the simple power relationships exhibited here are typical of small organisations whose major objective is growth and which are dominated by a powerful CE; what Mintzberg called the ‘entrepreneurial’ mode of planning [27].

The simple power distribution changed, however, following the change in leadership style. This, and to some extent the change in the external environment, were responsible for the changes in IS strategy formulation. The new CE brought with him a management style characterised by increased participation in decision-making, more delegation of responsibilities and more communication. There was now a medium through which individuals and groups could make their demands. One result of this was increased formalisation.

From the perspective of IS function and user department personnel, formalisation, such as structured systems methodologies and the use of steering committees, was a means to reduce the control of senior management. The expressed rationales (to ensure that systems met user requirements, to remove the inflexibilities in the systems developed, and to ensure that systems were flexible enough to respond to changes in business environment) were true at one level but also were means to justify the interests of the IS function and user department personnel. From the perspective of senior management, the introduction of formal and participative corporate planning systems by the CE, and the approval of formalised mechanisms initiated by IS and user personnel, was seen as having been needed to counter the previous management style and the recent changes in the financial services environment following regulatory and competitive changes.

The results from the case study are encouraging in terms of understanding IS strategy formulation. Current work is concentrating on tracing one or more specific strategic decisions in depth over a period of time. In addition, the links between context and process are being developed further and tested empirically in the case study.
