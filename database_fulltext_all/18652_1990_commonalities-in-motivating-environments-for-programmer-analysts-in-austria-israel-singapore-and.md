# Commonalities in Motivating Environments for Programmer/Analysts in Austria, Israel, Singapore, and the U.S.A.

J. Daniel Couger

College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

Heimo Adelsberger

Vienna University of Economics and Business Administration, 1090-Vienna, Austria

Israel Borovits and Moshe Zviran
Tel Aviv University, 69 978 Tel-Aviv, Israel

Juzar Motiwalla

Singapore Institute for Systems Science, Singapore

A comparison was made of perceptions of programmer/analysts on factors relating to motivation and goal setting/feedback in Austria, Israel, Singapore, and the United States of America. The JDS/DP, a modification of the Job Diagnostic Survey instrument, was used to collect data in these locations. The results indicated significant commonalities in the four populations. Not only were individual characteristics of growth need and social-need similar, but their perception of job-related variables were also quite similar. With this information, I.S. managers in the four countries can use proven universal techniques, such as job characteristics theory, for improving motivation of programmer/analysts.

Keywords: Motivation, IS Management, Productivity Improvement, Cultural Differences, Job Diagnostic Survey.

## Introduction

Despite significant cultural differences, the countries of Singapore, Israel, and Austria have a great deal in common. All have small populations (less than 3.5 million persons) but compete quite satisfactorily in international markets. All have high levels of technical expertise in the computer field. Most require English language proficiency of computer science university students at an early age, enabling computer personnel to use English manuals/books for all their training and application. Each has few natural resources and knows it must rely more on the intellectual resources of the people than the natural resources of the land; thus they strive to obtain a greater share of the international software computer market.

To facilitate market penetration, officials in each country decided that it would be useful to determine the level of motivation of software personnel and to compare such environments with that of the U.S.A. Research organizations at the national level in each country decided to use the JDS/DP for these comparisons; this is a modification of the Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) instrument [4]. The Couger-Zawacki data base was the basis of comparison.

In addition to the institutional goals, the researchers had another goal in conducting the surveys: to test the hypothesis that the computer field attracts persons with similar characteristics, irrespective of nationality or cultural background. JDS/DP research had been conducted in international divisions of American firms, such as IBM and GE, and the findings revealed little difference in characteristics and perceptions between personnel. However, it could not be determined if those similarities resulted from a corporate culture; i.e., by their use of standardized approaches for employee selection and culling.

## Background: The Survey Methodology

The JDS is based on the job characteristics model theory of motivation [7,3]. The essence of this theory is that to ensure motivation and productivity, an individual's need for growth must be matched by the degree of richness of the assigned job. Individual Growth Need Strength (GNS) is defined as the degree of an individual's need for challenge, moving beyond his/her present level of knowledge and ability, and being stretched. The motivating potential of a job is determined by the degree of richness of five core job dimensions: skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback from the job. The job's MPS (Motivating Potential Score) is computed from the survey responses on the core job dimensions.

When MPS is matched to individual growth need, motivation can be expected to improve. If an imbalance occurs, motivation will not reach its potential. Fig. 1 depicts the job characteristics model of motivation.

The reliability and validity of the JDS/DP has been substantiated [2]. The survey instrument obtains perceptions on 26 variables related to the work environment. Over the past 10 years, the JDS/DP has been used to develop a data base of motivation characteristics and perceptions for more than 8,000 U.S. computer personnel. Motivation norms were developed for 19 job types within the computer field, from data entry to vice president. (This is a very rich data base. Analysis of the data has resulted in two books, a monograph, and more than 30 papers.)

The major difference of the JDS/DP over the original JDS is the addition of goal-related variables. A second difference is the addition of a section to obtain rankings of key motivational factors, as defined by Herzberg [6].

Analysis of the survey results was made by a special program to compute and analyze data. The SPSS package was also used for analysis of demographics. These analytical tools compared the four populations against the 26 survey variables. Definitions of the variables are provided in Appendix A.

## Survey Participants

The participating organizations were selected from both government and private industry. Eighteen Austrian, 24 Israeli and 32 Singaporean organizations participated. Sample sizes were: Austria (600), Israel (550), and Singapore (1,100).

Insuring representatives of survey participants was the responsibility of a co-researcher in the sponsoring organization. In Austria, this was the Vienna University of Economics and Business Administration. In Israel, they were the Marcel and Annie Adams Institute for Business Management Information Systems and the Israel Institute of Business Research. In Singapore, the sponsor was the National Computer Board.

Demographics are shown in Appendix B. Not surprisingly, a large percent of the survey population is below the age of 41, ranging from 78 percent in the U.S.A. to 98 percent in Singapore. The computer field is young. Similarly, experience levels are low relative to other occupations. The gender difference is more characteristic of the culture than any other factor. Females comprise a high percent of all technical fields in Singapore and a low percent of technical fields in Austria. The education category is difficult to assess, because the amount of schooling varies a great deal for termination levels. For example, Austrians have one more year of pre-college education than Americans and Singaporeans. On the other hand, Singaporean pre-college education is much richer (more intensive) than that of Austria and the U.S.A. The demographics exhibit more similarities than dissimilarities.

## Survey Results

The analysis here will concentrate on the job type which comprises the majority of computer professionals, that of programmer/analyst.

The results substantiate job characteristics model theory as the appropriate motivation model for the programmer/analysts (P/As) involved in our surveys. As shown in Table 1, the work itself is ranked first in importance for all four countries. Other rankings for survey participants in all four countries were similar.

Survey respondents in the four countries are similar in a second respect – their need for growth is high. As shown in Table 2, all are above 5.50 on the scale of seven. (Significant differences are indicated in this and subsequent tables with a plus or minus, significant at the p < 0.01 level.) P/A GNS is quite high compared to the more than 600 U.S.A. occupations studied by Hackman and Oldham (1980).

A third factor of similarity is Social Need Strength (SNS). This is a measure of a person's need for interaction with other people. SNS for survey respondents is quite low in all four countries, below five on the scale of seven. Although Singaporean and Austrian respondents have somewhat higher SNS, it's still below five on a scale of seven.

Responses on the goal-related variables are also similar for the four groups of respondents. Table 3 shows that goals are clear and acceptable. The responses are well above five for all countries. Respondents would like to have more involvement in setting goals, however. Except for the Austrians, responses hover around the 4.0 level. ## Conclusions

Analysis of the survey data reveals that the populations in the four countries exhibit significant similarities, both in characteristics and perceptions. Programmer/analysts in the survey groups have a high need for growth and a low need for social interaction. Their perceptions on goal-related variables are quite similar. In addition, there was a high degree of consistency among the four groups in their ranking of motivational factors.

Considerable effort was expended to insure that the survey populations were representative of the I.S. community in the four countries. In addition, a presentation of results was made in each country by the U.S.A. project director and the country's principal researcher to a meeting of I.S. managers. The consensus in these meetings was that the survey was representative.

The research findings are apparently of value to I.S. managers in all four countries, both for selecting personnel and for matching jobs to individuals.

The high growth need is a positive characteristic that should continue to receive emphasis in employee selection. The rapid turnover of knowledge in the computer field demands personnel who are not threatened by the continuous need to re-tool. Therefore, I.S. managers need to continue selecting high-GNS type of personnel for the P/A job. Concomitantly, I.S. managers need to reevaluate programmer/analyst jobs on a regular basis to ensure that the core job dimensions are maintained at a level consistent with the high need for challenge of their P/As.

The low social need is a positive characteristic only for the entry level job in programming, a job that is done independently for the most part. People who work well alone find the programmer job appealing. When these people are promoted to the P/A job, however, there is an increased requirement to interact with clients, the user of their systems. User requirements must be comprehensively defined to prepare proper specifications for programs. Managers need to help their low SNS personnel acquire the skills to interact effectively to insure that systems meet the needs of clients. Although the solution would appear to be selection of high SNS personnel, the nature of the work does not lend itself to this solution. While meetings with users are important, they represent a small percent of the time of the P/A activity. Design and programming occupy the majority of the time and are activities that are essentially done alone. Therefore, a more appropriate solution is the training of P/As in behavioral skills to enable them to be effective communicators.

Computers are increasingly important to compete more effectively in international markets. Improving productivity of a scarce resource, experienced computer personnel, is necessary to capitalize on the capabilities of computers. Now that I.S. managers know that the work itself is the top motivator for P/As, they can be assured that efforts to improve the MPS/GNS match will result in high payoff. Now that they know that P/As are very similar in characteristics and perceptions, they do not have to develop motivation techniques tailored to the needs of their culture, but can rely on proven universal techniques, such as the job characteristics model of motivation.

It is inappropriate to extrapolate these results to indicate their applicability to other nationalities and cultures. However, the results indicate that expansion of this research to other countries appears worthwhile.

## Appendix A: Definition of Survey Variables

1. Key Job Dimensions: Objective characteristics of the job itself.

A. Skill Variety: The degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities in carrying out the work, which involve the use of a number of different skills and talents of the employee.

B. Task Identity: The degree to which the job requires the completion of a “whole” and identifiable piece of work – i.e., doing a job from beginning to end with a visible outcome.

C. Task Significance: The degree to which the job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people – whether in the immediate organization or in the external environment.

D. Autonomy: The degree to which the job provides substantial freedom, independence, and discretion to the employee in scheduling his/her work and in determining the procedures to be used in carrying it out.

E. Feedback from the Job Itself: The degree to which carrying out the work activities required by the job results in the employee obtaining information about the effectiveness of his or her performance.

2. Satisfaction Measures: The private, affective reactions or feelings an employee gets from working on his job.

A. General Satisfaction: An overall measure of the degree to which the employee is satisfied and happy in his or work.

B. Specific Satisfaction: These scales tap several specific aspects of the employee's job satisfaction.

B1. Pay satisfaction

B2. Supervisory satisfaction

B3. Satisfaction with co-workers

3. Social Need Strength: This is a measure of the degree to which the employee needs to interaction with other employees.

4. Goal Clarity and Accomplishment: These scales measure the degree to which employees understand and accept organizational goals. Further, it taps into the employees' feelings about goal setting participation, goal difficulty, and feedback on goal accomplishment.

A. Goal Clarity. How clear and specific the goals are for the job or the person. The person has a clear sense of priorities on his/her goals.

B. Goal Difficulty. The extent that goals or work objectives require a great deal of effort to complete.

C. Goal Acceptance. The extent that the person is willing to accept organizational goals.

D. Goal Setting Participation: The feeling of having involvement in the goal setting process – of having an impact.

5. Individual Growth Need Strength: This scale measures the individual's need for personal accomplishment and for learning and developing beyond his/her present level of knowledge and skills.

6. Motivating Potential Score: A score reflecting the potential of a job for eliciting positive internal work motivation on the part of employees.
