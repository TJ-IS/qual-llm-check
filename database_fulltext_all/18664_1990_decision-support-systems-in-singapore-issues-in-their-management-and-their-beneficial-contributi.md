# Decision Support Systems in Singapore: Issues in their Management and their Beneficial Contributions

K.S. Raman and C.K. Phoon \*

Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Kent Ridge, Singapore 0511

A survey of 146 organizations in the key industry sectors of Singapore indicates a trend towards the use of computer-based Decision Support Systems (DSS) to support managers in decision making. The organizations consider four issues in the management of DSS critical to their success: (a) identification of managers' information and decision support needs, (b) selling the idea to top management, (c) effective planning and design of the support system, and (d) systems integration. The significant beneficial contributions of DSS: (a) are improved quality of information for decision making, (b) increased managerial productivity, and (c) support for achievement of competitive advantage. Cross-industry analysis of the data shows a high degree of consensus among industries on the ranking of critical management issues for the success of DSS and significant beneficial contributions of DSS.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems (DSS), Management Information Systems (MIS), Benefits of DSS, Managing DSS.

## 1. Introduction

The process of management and decision making in organizations has been a topic of continuing research the USA and Europe. One of the main themes of the research has been the impact of new development in science and technology on management and decision making in organizations. The impact of computer systems on management and organizations in the USA has been discussed by several authors. The most notable are Leavitt and Whistler [8], Simon [13], and Whistler [17] who focus on the beneficial potential of computers, and Ackoff [1] and Dearden [4] who are highly critical of computer-based systems for supporting the management process.

Computer-based Decision Support Systems (DSS) in the 1970s [5,7,2] addressed some of the problems of computer support for managerial decision making. Since the publication of the Gorry-Scott Morton paper [5], more than 20 books and 200 papers covering the conceptual and application aspects of DSS have been published. These address the issues and problems relating to the design, development, and use of DSS, mainly in organizations in the USA and to a lesser extent in Europe.

In Singapore, government departments, branches of multinationals, and large Singaporean companies have been using computers for data processing since the early 1960s. In the period 1980–86, the use of computers by organizations in Singapore increased more than ten-fold, from 256 mini and mainframe computers in 1980 to more than 3,000 in 1986 [9], and computers are being used in sophisticated applications.

Although Singapore and other newly industrializing economies (NIEs) in the Pacific Rim have made rapid progress in the use of computers in organizations since 1980 [3], very little has been reported on the issues and problems relating to the use of computers, especially for sophisticated business applications and management decision support. This research is an effort to fill this gap.

## 2. Objectives and Scope

The main objectives of this study are to examine and ascertain, on an overall and cross-industry basis in organizations in Singapore:

\- Managerial perception of DSS.

\- The present status of use of computer-based DSS to support managers and issues in their management,

the impact of DSS on managers and the beneficial contributions made by DSS to organizations, and

\- the factors that encourage or inhibit the use of DSS by managers.

It is to be noted that the emphasis of the study is to collect data on some selected aspects of the use of DSS, and analyze and interpret the data. The study does not aim to prove or disprove any hypotheses on use of DSS in Singapore. The scope of the study is limited to medium to large organizations in nine major industry sectors: (a) Banking and Insurance, (b) Communications and Transportation, (c) Government Departments and Statutory Boards, (d) Hotel and Property, (e) Manufacturing, (f) Oil and Gas, (g) Retail, (h) Shipbuilding and Repairs, and (i) Trade and Distribution. The study does not cover small enterprises.

3. Taxonomy of Information Work and Information Systems

## 3.1. Taxonomy of Information Work

Computers are used in organizations to support information work. Information work may be classified into Type I and Type 2 [15]. Type 1 essentially covers all clerical and front-end operations performed by clerical staff in organizations. Its characteristics are: (a) high volume of transactions, (b) low cost or value per unit transaction, (c) well-defined or well-structured procedures, and (d) results can be measured in terms of activity levels. Type 2 work consists of supervision, management control, problem finding, problem solving and planning activities performed by supervisors and managers at various organizational levels. Its characteristics are: (a) low volume of transactions, (b) high cost or value per unit transaction, (c) procedures are ill-structured and in some cases there may be no procedures at all, and (d) results are measured in terms of goals achieved [15].

Both Type 1 and Type 2 work may involve predominantly numeric, predominantly narrative information, or a combination of the two. In Type 1 work, the narrations are simple statements or instructions; e.g., packing and delivery instructions on purchase orders and making/ processing instructions on product technical specifications. In Type 2 work, the narrations are usually models, rules of thumb, and heuristics. Some examples are: accounting models, processing applications for loans, and career planning and promotion decisions for senior appointments.

## 3.2. Taxonomy of Information Systems

The characteristics and design features of computer-based systems depend to a large extent on the type of information work they are required to support. On this basis, information systems may be classified into Transaction Processing Systems, Management Information Systems (MIS), Office Automation Systems, Decision Support Systems (DSS), Expert Systems and AI Systems, and integrated Decision Support and Expert Systems. ## 3.3. Definition of DSS

There are several definitions and interpretations of the term DSS. The most widely used definition is: DSS are interactive computer-based systems that help decision makers utilize data and models to solve unstructured problems [14]. For purposes of this study, this definition of DSS was modified as: DSS are any computer-based systems that are used to support managers in their decision making in planning, coordination, control, organizing, forecasting, budgeting, administration, and general management.

These definitions of DSS seem to differ from the taxonomy of Exhibit 1. The narrative information inputs do not appear explicitly in these definitions. In the DSS as defined by Sprague [14], the narrative information are in the decision maker's knowledge base which is external to the system. The information may be in the form of rules, heuristics or knowledge stored in a chalkboard, scratch pad, notebook or in the head of the decision maker. This definition and explanation of DSS clearly excludes integrated DSS and Expert Systems [16] and business expert systems such as GURU [6] which offer the capability to store and use rules and heuristics as an integral part of DSS.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Designing the Survey Instrument

Two approaches were considered for collecting data and soliciting views on the use of DSS:

personal interviews and mail survey. Mail survey was preferred in view of the flexibility in schedule and economy in use of managers' (respondents') time, and telephone follow-ups were conducted to encourage a high level of response. A success rate of 50 percent was targeted.

The survey instrument was designed to be simple and easy to use. The instrument consisted of open-ended and pre-coded questions. Care was taken to ensure proper question content, sequence, and wording so as to avoid ambiguity and bias in interpretation of the questions. In addition, the key technical terms were defined in the survey. Sensitive questions on the organization and people were avoided.

As the study would cover all the major industry sectors of Singapore, it was considered desirable to design the survey instrument to suit the characteristics of the sectors. Three different survey instruments were designed, one each for public sector organizations, private sector organizations, and private sector service industries. Complete descriptions of these instruments and samples are available in the detailed report on the study [10].

## 4.2. Selecting the Organizations

A sampling process was used to select organizations in Singapore using computers to participate in this study. The process identified major Singaporean organizations, branches/operating units of multinationals in selected industry sectors, government departments/ministries, and statutory boards. The organizations include all ministries except the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the ministry of Law, and all major statutory boards, the leading multinationals, the companies in the Business Times Top 100 List, the companies in the Asian Finance Top 40 List, and companies scheduled for listing in the Stock Exchange of Singapore Dealing and Automated Quotation Systems (SEADAQ). Sales turnover was also a criterion in selecting companies from the private sector. The sample includes companies in the turnover ranges less than S\$20 million, \* S\$21–50 million, S\$51–100 million, S\$101–500 million and greater than S\$500 million. Altogether 211 organizations were selected for the study.

## 4.3. Analyzing the Data

The survey returns were carefully checked for clarity and completeness, and the respondents were contacted to clarify ambiguous statements and/or provide missing data. Out of the 152 responses received, six (6) were rejected after checking resulting in a net of 146 returns. Data from these 146 checked returns were analyzed using the PC-SAS package for mean values, distributions and percentages. The analysis was carried out at the overall level and specific industry sector level.

## 5. Industry Profile of Respondents

The survey instrument was sent to a sample of 211 organizations. The instrument requested data on certain items relating to the use of Information Technology in the organization and the importance of IT to the organization. ## 6. It Profile of Respondents

## 6.1. Investment in IT

In this study, investments in IT are classified into three types: (a) IT capital investment comprising initial capital outlay on computer and communication hardware, software, and facilities, (b) IT on-going investment comprising hardware maintenance charges, software licensing fee, backup and security costs, and IT staff costs, and (c) IT reinvestment comprising enhancements to existing hardware, acquisition of new hardware, software, and development tools. Analysis of the returns shows that IT capital investments ranged from over S\$50 million to less than S\$1 million. One-hundred-and-ten organizations (72.5%) made a capital investment of less than S\$5 million in IT. The distribution is: more than S\$50 million, 6 organizations (3.9%); S\$10–49 million, 22 organizations (14.3%); S\$5–10 million, 14 organizations (9.3%), S\$1–4 million, 48 organizations (32%); and less than S\$1 million; 62 organizations (40.7%).

## 6.2. IT Human Resource

The survey requested information on the number of IT staff employed by the organization. The term IT staff covers systems analysts, analyst programmers, and people in various layers of managing IT in the organization. Although the returns show an average of 20.9 IT staff per organization over the sample, 86 organizations (56.6%) have less than 10 IT staff and 58 organizations (40%) have 5 staff or less. The percentage of organizations with 20 or more IT staff drops appreciably. Six organizations (4%) have more than 100 IT staff and 10 organizations (6.5%) employ 50–100 IT staff; no organization has an IT staff strength of 200 or more.

## 6.3. Investments in IT in Relation to Sales

Investment in IT as a percentage of sales turnover is a widely-used index of the importance organizations attach to IT. This index for specific industries has also been used to evaluate an organization's application and use of IT in relation to the industry average. In the survey, the respondents were asked to provide sales turnover and IT investments in certain ranges. A study of these figures shows that organizations with sales exceeding S\$500 million typically have IT capital investments in ranges S\$10–50 except for three organizations which have invested more than S\$50 million. Organizations with sales in the range S\$20–500 million typically have IT capital investments in the range S\$1–5 million, and organizations with sales of less than S\$20 million invest less than S\$1 million in IT.

Analysis of responses to questions on use of information system for decision making shows that most respondents, 97.7%, rely on regular reports for decision making, and 50.8% use ad hoc reports. This pattern of information system use suggests that transaction processing systems and management information systems which support the mainstream business functions form the foundations of DSS in most of the responding organizations. ## 6.7. Trends in Information Architecture

Centralized data processing currently dominates the corporate information processing scene as 75.6% of respondents have centralized facilities. Decentralized approach to information processing has been adopted by the remaining 24.4% of respondents. Looking into the future, 23.6% of the organizations are presently expanding their central facilities and a further 60.4% plan to do so over the next two years. These findings indicate a general preference for centralized processing as an effective means of cost and management control of the information processing function.

## 6.8. The End-User Environment

The responding organizations seem to be aware of the importance of End-User Computing (EUC) and Office Automation (OA) technologies to improve information availability to decision makers. Forty-nine percent of the respondents use EUC and OA, and over the next two years about 34% of the respondents have plans to implement these technologies. At present, only 8.7% of organizations use Local Area Network technology (LAN); over the next two years, 42.5% of the respondents plan to have LAN.

## 7. DSS Profile of Respondents

## 7.1. Users' Perception of DSS

A majority of respondents characterized their understanding of DSS primarily as a tool to access information for their decision making.

## 7.2. DSS Applications

Prior to the availability of DSS technology in Singapore, users relied mostly on regular reports generated by the organization's information system, which is in agreement with the findings of an earlier pilot study [12].

The number of DSS applications is used as an index to gauge the extent of DSS usage in organizations. The respondents' returns show that on an average each organization has about 13 DSS applications. The breakdown by industry sector is shown in Exhibit 5. This table shows that Banking and Insurance sector leads the application of DSS technology with an average of 20 DSS applications per organization, followed by Oil and Gas with an average of 18 applications per organization. Government Departments and Statutory Boards have over 400 DSS applications making them the largest users of DSS technology in terms of number of applications.

An analysis of the functional areas of application of DSS technology by industry sector shows that Financial Accounting tops the list in all sectors. The other functional areas of DSS application common to all sectors are Planning and Budgeting, Personnel, and Administration. Alter [2], based on a study of application of DSS in 56 large organizations in the US, classified DSS applications broadly into Data Oriented and Model Oriented systems. Within these broad classifications Alter identified three types of Data Oriented DSS and four types of Model Oriented DSS. In Alter's taxonomy, a majority of the DSS applications in Singapore are data-oriented, data-analysis type, and model-oriented, accounting-model type.

## 7.4. Criteria for Selecting DSS Software

Regarding the criteria for selecting DSS software and vendor, the respondents rank matched functionality, defined as match between the desired functions and those offered by the product, the most important criterion with a score of 4.64 on a 5-point scale, see Exhibit 7.

Model management systems ranked low with a score of 3.86 on a 5-point scale; price of the software was ranked second-lowest, with a score of 3.83.

## 8. Issues in the Management of DSS

The survey instrument listed eleven issues in the management of DSS and asked the respondents to rate them in importance on a five-point scale. The results are shown in Exhibit 8. The top four issues address the development life cycle of a DSS, which is indicative of the increasing awareness and use of DSS among the respondents. It is interesting to note that cost justification is ranked fourth by the respondents. This is in agreement with the perception that DSS is a value-added system and not a cost reduction system, and that the values are often intangible.

The top four issues are now discussed in some detail.

## 8.1. Identifying Users' Information Needs

Identifying users' information needs was ranked the most important issue by the respondents with a mean score of 4.40. On a 5-point scale, over $50\%$ of the respondents rated this issue a full 5 points, and $88.4\%$ of respondents gave this a rating of 4 or more. Analysis with reference to private sector organizations by sales shows that large, medium, and small organizations give about the same rating to this issue. This indicates that identifying users' information needs is an issue of the highest importance in all organizations, irrespective of size.

## 8.2. Selling the Idea to Senior Management

The respondents are of the view that selling the DSS idea to top management is a very important issue in managing DSS. This is considered essential for purposes of committing adequate resources for setting up DSS facilities and creating an organizational environment conducive for managers to use the DSS facilities.

## 8.3. Standards and Integration

DSS draw data from a variety of internal and external sources. The fact that standards and integration is ranked third by the respondents indicates the awareness on the part of MIS managers the inherent pitfalls in unplanned, laissez-faire development of information systems, databases, and personal computers. The issue of standards and integration includes hardware standards, operation system standards, application and database development standards, personal computers, and networking of computers.

## 8.4. Effective Planning, Design, and Implementation

The ranking for this issue was the same as the ranking for standards and integration. Effective planning and design are essential to achieve good standards and integration. Results of the study show that MIS managers are aware of this dependence. As organizations rely more on computers for information and decision making, they are paying more attention to overall planning and design of what may be called an information utility.

## 9. Major benefits of DSS

The survey instrument listed ten potential benefits that can be derived from DSS technology and asked the respondents to rank them on a 5-point scale based on their experience. The results are tabulated in Exhibit 9. The top four benefits are all related to the rather subjective, value added type of benefits. Out of nine potential benefits, improving administrative productivity which may be perceived to have a tangible benefit was ranked fifth with a score of 4.01 out of 5. The top four benefits are briefly discussed here.

## 9.1. Better Quality Information

Better quality of information for decision making received the highest average score of 4.52. A further analysis of responses shows that organizations in the more advanced stages of using DSS see greater benefits of better quality information. Another interesting finding is that organizations which do not yet have experience with DSS give a high rating for quality of information. The high ratings are mainly attributable to the interactive user interface, non-procedural data analysis languages and colour graphics features of DSS which provide direct access and control over information to decision makers.

## 9.2. Improving Managerial Productivity

Improved managerial productivity due to the use of DSS ranks second, with an average score of 4.07. It may be argued that a manager does not produce anything and there can be no such concept as managerial productivity. In the context of this study, improved managerial productivity is viewed as a function of the time spent in retrieving information, generating value added information and problem finding in the intelligence phase, and developing alternative solutions in the design phase of decision making [13]. A good DSS reduces the time required in the intelligence, design, and choice phases of decision making and thereby improves managerial productivity.

## 9.3. Achieving Competitive Advantage

Porter and Millar [11] show how and why information technology changes the way organizations operate internally as well as the relationships among competitors. The respondents of this study ranked the competitive advantage benefit of DSS third, with an average score of 4.06 on a 5-point scale. This could be due to the nature of competition and market in the Singapore environment. Another reason could be that information systems which integrate internal and external data and provide value-added information for decision making are relatively recent in Singapore. The Banking and Finance industry which operates in a highly competitive environment and has mature and sophisticated information systems rank competitive advantage benefit very high with an average score of 4.33, marginally below better quality information ranked first with an average score of 4.35 (see Section 10.2, Exhibit 11).

## 9.4. Achieving Business Excellence

The respondents ranked this benefit fourth with an average score of 4.05 on a 5-point scale. Business excellence is a part of competitive strategy among organizations which offer the same range of products and services, and refers to their qualitative differentiation. Not surprisingly, in the case of public sector organizations and government departments where there is no competition, business excellence is ranked well above competitive advantage (see Section 10.2, Exhibit 11).

## 10.2. Beneficial Contributions of DSS

Exhibit 11 is a tabulation of the rankings of the beneficial contributions of DSS by industry sector. It is interesting to note that Better Quality Information was ranked first by all the industry sectors and there was a high degree of consensus over the next three beneficial contributions. Again, the Retail sector which ranks Business Excellence and Systems Integration third with a score of 4.0 is an exception. Government Departments and Statutory Boards rank Business Excellence second with a score of 4.09 and give a lower ranking to Achieving Competitive Advantage.

## 11. Summary

This study indicates that while DSS is still an emerging technology in Singapore, it is rapidly becoming an institutional reality, especially in those industry sectors with mature information system departments. Organizations rely on the information base built by the transaction processing and management information systems for DSS. The key management issues are determining users information needs, selling the DSS idea to top management, standards and integration, and planning for DSS. The key benefits derived from DSS are better quality information, improved managerial productivity, and support for achieving competitive advantage and business excellence. Cross-industry analysis of returns shows a high level of consensus in the first four rankings of the issues and benefits among the industries. The Retail sector is an exception which is indicative of the special problems and challenges in the application of information systems in this sector.
