# Managing J. Pierrepont Finch: Should he be given a PC? $^{1,2}$

R. Mark Isaac

Department of Economics, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

David E. Pingry

Department of Management Information Systems, Department of Economics, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

An unprecedented phenomenon is transforming some of the fundamental relationships of our society: the wide distribution of cheap, general purpose information processing capacity in the form of microcomputers or personal computers. Although general purpose productive capacity has been a major factor in the past (e.g. through the use of railroads and automobiles), the new revolution is aimed directly at the heart of hierarchical organizations such as corporations. One aspect of the potentially revolutionary impact of PCs, the wide and rapid distribution of this general information processing capacity to end-users, can affect manager–employee and employee–employee relationships. A model of “office politics” is presented which may partially explain both the rapid diffusion of personal computers to lower and middle management and the concurrent, and surprising decline in white collar productivity.

Keywords: End-user computing, Productivity, Principal-agent theory, Office politics, Personal computers

## 1. Overview

When questions about the rate and impact of the diffusion of new inventions or innovations are considered in hindsight, a normal question is: Why did it take so long for the invention to diffuse? Our question is from the opposite perspective. From our casual observation, we believe that there is evidence that personal computers (PCs) and assorted software have been rapidly deployed. Lower and middle level managers often acquired them on their own volition. There are stories from the early days of the PC of companies whose senior management grossly underestimated the number of PCs being used by middle and lower level management. This was partly because managers were using any discretionary funds they could divert, and sometimes their own personal funds, to acquire PCs for themselves and their units. This became a major concern of the centralized, usually mainframe oriented, computer centers and information systems departments, which often played the more traditional role of resistance. Al-

At first glance, this rapid diffusion of what is an amazing and potentially productive technological innovation would seem to be a uniformly good thing, especially when compared to the frequent stories about tradition-bound employees who refuse to go along with new products or techniques. But this is not necessarily the case. PCs and associated software plus user learning and development time cost money. In principle, there is an optimal diffusion of PCs in a firm, and we will argue that firms need to worry both about the numbers and uses of these machines. Further complicating the situation is the fact that the PC is a general use technology whose usage is relatively costly to monitor in a stand-alone mode. Such observations seem even more important in light of the recent concern about the decline of white collar productivity in the face of a large and expanding investment in PCs.

Concern about the decline in white collar productivity has reached the popular media. For example, Evans [6] reported that in the 1980's almost 45% of capital spending was on information technology. Roughly 40% of that 45% (or 18% of capital spending) was on end-user computing. Further, Evans reported that during the same time period, white collar productivity was declining at the rate of 1% per year. Bowen [3] reported that "U.S. business has spent hundreds of billions of dollars on them (PCs), but white-collar productivity is no higher than it was in the late Sixties."

Here we present a model which attempts to reconcile the apparent contradiction between the rapid deployment of white collar capital and declining white collar productivity. In the context of this model it is possible to argue that the rapid deployment was due to the personal gain (promotions, perks, etc) that individuals could capture by improving perceived job performance through the use of the PC (this may have been further encouraged by uncooperative information systems [IS] departments). Further, it appears that most firms allowed this to happen, either on purpose or through neglect. They apparently believed, perhaps prematurely, that there would be general productivity gains. This led to decentralization of control over computer hardware. As a result, employees were placed in a dilemma. Even if their natural instinct were to utilize the computing capacity to improve the overall productivity, in order to enhance (or protect) their career they felt compelled to devote a substantial portion of the computing capacity and their time to what we call “redistributive activities” or “office politics.” Thus, a major portion of the new capital, and a large portion of employees’ time, was devoted to “nonproductive” purposes, leading to a decline in white collar productivity.

The basis of our model (following that of Williamson [21] and others) is the view that the firm is not simply a technological black box but a collection of individuals and equipment organized internally for command and control and organized externally to interact in markets. Within this view, a popular framework for analyzing the manager–employee relationships inside the firm has been the principal-agent model. This grapples with a relationship that permeates our economic life: what remuneration incentives should be offered to an agent (employee) in order to motivate that person to act on the behalf of a principal (manager) when the agent has asymmetric information about the agent's ability to do so? This relationship has been explored for both the single agent (see [8], [18], and [19]) and the multiple agent (see [5], [10], and [13]) cases.

At first glance, the problem of controlling the use of PCs might seem to be simple reinterpretation of the standard model. We believe, however, that the direct application of the principal-agent models, as currently formulated, to the manager-employee relationship in a hierarchy is inappropriate. The models do not adequately capture the role of manager-employee and employee-employee gaming in an environment where promotions and bonuses are used.

Here we present a model that expands the principal-agent approach to include office politics. We use this model to look at the manipulation of information or information processing capacity by a manager in charge of employees who may choose to use these resources for improving their relative position within the firm. We show, under some conditions, that if a firm provides information or processing capacity to its employees it will give its employees incentive to engage in office politics.

Further, we will argue that this result has important implications about the recent, rapid proliferation of PCs and the concurrent decrease in productivity.

It should be noted that there are many other issues associated with the wide distribution of PCs that we will not address. Many of these have been discussed in the management of end-user computing. They include: effects on organizational form and structure, duplication and redundancy, lack of design and documentation standards, etc. For a discussion of some of these issues see $[1]$ , $[7]$ , $[14]$ , and $[17]$ .

It should also be emphasized that our purpose is not to use models to answer empirical questions. Our interest in the subject began with some seemingly anomalous observations. We believe the model is a logical next step, raising questions that could provide a framework for more extensive empirical or experimental investigations.

## 2. An Alternative Manager Employee Environment

The typical model in the principal-agent literature has involved a strategic choice by the agent to provide some level of resource (effort) to the principal. In the context of a firm, agents (employees) have disutility from providing effort, which is a productive factor in the profit function of the firm, and the principal (manager) does not know how much effort the employee can provide. In a technical sense, this literature has focused on “optimal” or “incentive compatible” contracting institutions between the principal and the agent (or agents). It is not too much of a simplification to describe the standard model as addressing the problem of inducing effort out of a lazy employee (or employees), given that it is impossible to predict how hard they can work.

Although the principal-agent models have been modified in many ways (many agents, multiple periods, communication, risk attitudes, etc.), we argue that the basic behavior of the agent has been inappropriate for the manager–employee relationship. In particular, the principal-agent models are generally missing two important elements: (1) competition among the employees for the promotions, perks, etc., and

(2) the possibility of employees devoting resources to this competition in the form of office politics, a behavior that is difficult to distinguish from productive behavior.

The usual interpretation of the asymmetric information assumption of the principal-agent model in the context of the manager–employee relationship is that the manager cannot tell shirking from productive work. This interpretation of asymmetric information limits the behavior of the agent (employee) to the strategies of shirking or not. This may be appropriate for laborers hired out of a pool or low responsibility assembly-line workers; however, neither the problem of monitoring effort nor the problem of revealing asymmetric information seem to be of much interest for these jobs. The tasks and the workers' ability to perform the tasks are well known and the competition for the jobs is high. As Williamson [21] points out, large numbers will discipline opportunistic behavior.

For the higher level employee, however, the problem of asymmetric information is likely to be somewhat more involved. Each employee may be doing a similar but different job (making reports, forecasting, managing different projects, hiring, etc.). The manager cannot be perfectly informed about all these functions. The manager's asymmetric information problem is that it is costly to determine if the type and level of activity of the employee is productive for the firm.

The manager's problem is further complicated by the fact that higher level employees are typically in a continuing contractual relationship (the internal labor market) with the firm: this includes possibilities for promotion, bonus, perks, etc. A cooperative-competitive environment creates incentives for the employee to devote time and resources to competition with other employees. This interaction is not captured in the principal-agent model. Thus, the re-interpretation of the assumption of asymmetric information has the effect of making the current structure of the principal-agent model inadequate for the same environment.

The political behavior may be opportunistic, purely for personal gain with no productive benefits for the firm. J. Pierrepont Finch, the source of our title, is known for devoting all of his time to the most non-productive kind of political activity. It also may be the result of individuals trying to do the “best job,” from their understanding of it (e.g., the person who produces a report with no typos but who took three days to find the last three). The problem, which is their manager's problem – not theirs, is that their understanding of the objective of their job may be faulty. They overperform in minor ways and reduce profitability. Although, they may have the best of intentions, this has a potentially negative impact on profit.

Consider the following example: A white collar worker is assigned the task of producing a quarterly report. She is obviously busy on this project, and her effort results in the best quarterly report yet, but she has used her time to refine the report beyond the necessary quality. This time is lost to the next project, which might have been more beneficial to the firm.

## 3. The Model

Two recent papers have modelled aspects of the issues addressed here. Nalebuff and Stiglitz [16] examined the “tournament” between two employees competing for a bonus, given that their choice was between effort and shirking. They examined the relationship between the payoff to the tournament and the amount of equilibrium effort chosen by the employees.

Milgrom and Roberts [15] addressed the issue of office politics. They examined whether the granting of a promotion should be based on job performance or credentials (such as jobs held, awards, schooling, special training, etc.). They assumed that the manager could measure these two issues, but could not distinguish between effort on the current job and credential building. They also assumed that effort was related to current job performance and credentials affected performance on the new job. Thus, the form of the policy for promotion and/or bonuses for performance resulted in different allocations between effort and credential building.

We assume that three types of employee activities are possible: “shirking” (or “leisure”), “productive effort,” and “office politics” (or redistributive effort). The first activity is never productive for the firm, the second is productive, and the third may be. We also assume that the manager cannot costlessly distinguish between the three activities, although it is easier to distinguish shirking. In fact, we assume that allocating more time to shirking is observable to the point that it will decrease the employees probability of being viewed as productive. The allocation of more time to purely productive or redistributive activities will increase the probability of the employee being viewed as productive.

We assume that a manager manipulates the incentives of interemployee gaming utilizing the promotion process, the control of information flows, and the deployment of processing capacity in the firm. The manager uses performance on the current job as a signal for choosing among employees for promotion. The management values the signalling process to the extent that it facilitates choosing “the best bet” for a higher level job. This is different from the Milgrom and Roberts approach, which assumes that performance is related to the current job, and that credentials are related to the next job. Their office politics could be viewed more as an investment in education.

For the typical employee the incentives are different. Mr. Jones is not interested in who “is the best bet” for the promotion, Mr. Jones is interested in whether or not he is promoted. Consider the following extreme example:

Two employees, Ms. Cap and Mr. Small, are equally qualified. If management was sure of this, there would be no further value to any process of choosing between them: flipping a coin would be fine. Ms. Cap and Mr. Small, however, have an incentive to engage in activities to make them appear most qualified.

The option of office politics leads to a fundamental problem. The firms value employee performance information in light of their global objective of profit (Vf). Employees provide the information based upon their private valuation (Ve); this consists, over and above returns from the current job, of benefits (or losses) from the continuing relationship between the principal (senior management) and the multiple agents (employees). When Ve > Vf, the employee has an incentive to engage in information signalling activity which is potentially greater than its demand for this information by the firm. We call this the J. Pierrepont Finch Paradox.

The implication of this is that managers interested in the bottom line of their firm must not simply be interested in “effort” as opposed to “shirking”, but also must be interested in the composition of apparently productive activity.

The form and expected returns of the game between the employees depend on the information that each employee has about other employees and the manager. Managers can utilize control over information flows in their organizations to make office politics more or less attractive.

We assume that manager's control is of two types: (i) To encourage information flows if they are at a level less than optimal for the manager. (ii) To discourage information flows if they are at a level greater than optimal for the manager.

There are many examples of both types of controls in existing organizations: employee orientation, information bulletins, and distribution of company data are all examples of the first type of control; office partitions, computer access codes, confidential documents, and employee conduct rules (e.g. “Do not talk about your salary”) illustrate the second type. In both cases the manager incurs cost to alter the flow of information.

In both cases the control can be applied to all employees or limited to a subset of the employees. In our model we will assume that the manager has the following option: take actions to make certain company data (e.g., employee salaries, future strategies, sales forecasts, etc.) available to all employees or, take action to inhibit its availability.

The manager's problem is to control information in order to direct the employees' resources towards productive activities. This, of course, requires that the manager knows something about the relationship among the policy variables, the structure of the office politics game, and the productive and office politics activity allocation by employees.

The problem facing the manager is very similar to a problem in the field of economics of information: when should a government provide public information? There are two arguments in favor of government provided public information. One states that governments should provide public information, or too little information will be provided. If information is produced by one party, it often can be deduced by others from the actions of producer (e.g., drilling for oil reveals a favorable seismic test result). This provides an incentive to potential producers: wait for a free-ride. As a result, less information is produced than if the producer could obtain full value.

Secondly, some information can be kept private long enough to produce private value. Therefore, all individuals seeking private benefit produce the same information, such as collecting information about horses at a race track. The way to stop this wasteful over-production, it is argued, is to have the government provide it (see $[9]$ for a literature survey). Unfortunately, in this scenario the simple solution “provide the information publicly” does not necessarily solve the problem. In fact it can make matters worse. Isaac ([11] and [12]) demonstrated that, barring exceptional conditions, public information may not be a solution to over investment in private information; e.g., publishing the Racing Form does not necessarily lead to less production of private information, it changes the information sought.

Thus provision of information or processing capacity within the firm will not necessarily reduce office politics. In fact, it may increase the incentives for such activities by increasing the private benefits or lowering the employee's private costs.

For a representation of the J. Pierrepont Finch Paradox, consider the normal form of a simple two-employee and manager game. The two employees are the top candidates for a promotion. The promotion will be given to the employee deemed most productive. Suppose both have been judged to be “equally qualified,” but over the next three months either one or both can engage in information signalling or office politics: diverting an additional half hour of their time from other activities to enhance market files requested by upper management. The enhancement is somewhat redundant and costly in unnecessary use of resources or lost productive effort both to the employee and the firm. But, it may not be so obviously wasteful to senior management: after all, they requested the files.

The formulation of the game is clearly not the most general representation of the principal-agent - tournament environment. Our proffered formulation is aimed at asking the following question: Given the payoff to the tournament (value of promotion), can an increase in information availability or distribution of PCs increase the equilibrium amount of office politics? This specific model does not deal with the more general problem of selecting the optimal contract between the manager and the employees - certainly an interesting problem to pursue.

On the other hand, if one employee engages in office politics while the other one does not, then the one engaging in office politics increases his/her probability of promotion relative to the other employee. Also, the value of the promotion is fixed and the promotion is always given with a probability of one.

From the point of view of the two employees, however, the incentives are different. For this example, the value of the extra work as a means to promotion yields a classic dilemma (known as the Prisoners' Dilemma by game theorists) for the employee. If these inequalities are reversed, it is a dominant strategy for the employees not to work extra.

## 4. PC or Not?

The incredibly quick emergence of the decentralized computing environment in the form of personal computers has sent shock-waves through the organizational structure of many firms. The interests of the old IS department and the grass roots proliferation of PCs throughout the organization has forced firms to deal with issues of efficiency and control. Many firms have moved to support decentralization, hoping to control its costs and capture its benefits. Some have moved to quash it and force their departments into the more controlled large mainframe environment. Most firms have adopted a mixed strategy, incurring the costs and benefits of both.

Historically the role of management has been to give the employee resources and information to do the job and monitor performance. It has been implicitly assumed that these resources were made available on a “need to know” or “need to produce” basis. For the most part, it is very difficult for the employee to divert special purpose resources or information to other activities (except through theft or leisure).

The case of general information processing capacity is somewhat different: it is more flexible and it has a relatively low cost of diversion to private uses. This would argue for increased monitoring, but it would be very expensive in a decentralized environment (an important argument for the mainframe/terminal or network environment). The provision of a large amount of information storage and processing capacity in the hands of the individual employee raises the possibility that it may be used in the employees' interest (at low personal cost). In particular, this capacity might be used to improve the individuals relative position in the firm.

Thus even if a firm can monitor and/or control shirking there is a wide variety of activities that are redistributive rather than productive. These activities are likely to be more costly to distinguish from purely productive activities than shirking.

An example of a firm in Tucson illustrates this point. It has a rule under which an employee can be fired for using a PC for recreation (playing games). This is not too difficult to monitor. But an employee might use a fancy graphics package to make unnecessarily “higher quality” reports for the next meeting (at least in the report presentation): the employee may be doing this for career advancement (or protection). Of course, we are not imputing unethical behavior to the employees: almost every firm compares employees to decide whom to promote.

Consider another example: An employee develops a PC software system which improves his productivity. Now the employee does not reveal the existence of this software to higher management and gains promotion for “unique management skills.” Performance in his area reverts to the old levels of performance after his promotion. The employee has improved his relative position in the company but has provided no long-run benefits to the firm. Clearly the likelihood this occurs will depend on the compensation and evaluation policies of the firm.

The behavior of the employees can certainly be manipulated by controlling promotion and compensation. However, controlling the availability of information and information processing capacity is an important management option.

Providing employees with PCs can alter the net payoffs to the redistributive activity in two distinct ways. First, the processing capability can lower the personal cost of intra-firm gaming by using available information. Secondly, provision of the PC often means a concurrent provision of (or access to) the organization's data base. That is, PCs augment the information available. The existence of a new data source can, in-and-of-itself, alter the redistributive game so as to make the gaming worthwhile.

Again consider the example. Let us assume initially for Ms. Cap that Pc( $^{-}$ W, w)\*Ve > Ve/2 - Ce and Ve/2 > Pc(W, $^{-}$ w)\*Ve - Ce. Analogous inequalities are assumed for Mr. Small. Each employee has a dominant strategy not to do extra work. The gain in expected payoffs are outweighed by the costs, regardless of what the other one does.

The introduction of PCs and associated software and data bases could impact this equilibrium. First, viewed as a technological innovation in data processing, PCs lower Ce. It is unambiguous that, if Ce is lowered enough, the inequalities would reverse, making extra work a dominant strategy.

Secondly, the introduction of PCs and associated information could change the value of extra work by changing the probability calculations. Making information generally available could increase or decrease the expected benefits of extra work (from the public goods standpoint). Assume that the costs and/or benefits are changed for the employees so that the inequalities are reversed. Then each employee has the dominant strategy to perform extra work, although both employees would be better off (in terms of expected value) by not doing so!

Given our assumptions, both the private cost reduction for extra work and the increased information availability could lead to increased extra nonproductive work and, thus, to lower profit for the firm. Thus, the cost reduction and the data augmentation effects of decentralized computing can have “unexpected” costs if the systems are diverted to redistributive aspects of employee gaming.

Of course, the reduction in productivity is not the only possible outcome. The system can also increase productivity and improve leisure time (games). If, for example, the information provided by the PCs is sufficient to supplant information gained from extra work (where the word sufficient is used in the sense of Blackwell [2] and De Groot [4]), then the provision of the PCs will eliminate incentives for extra work. Thus, the productivity increases may dominate. In fact, it is also possible that the firm can get extra work and increased productivity. In this case, Vf - 2Cf > 0. This could be viewed as a situation where the firm can “afford” to run an internal competition among employees and still capture net productivity gains or where the generally available information lowers the return from extra work, since all employees now have the information.

The formulation of the game here suggests another dimension of management control. It is likely that cost of the extra work to the firm is a function of the number of employees who can potentially gain from extra work. We have assumed the manager could give the PCs or information to all or none of the employees (i.e., no favorites). However, the PCs could be limited to a subset k of the employees where k < n. This might enable the firm to capture the benefits of extra work at lower cost.

There is some evidence that firms engage in this sort of management. For example, in some firms certain people are known to be “on the fast track.” That could be interpreted as “in the game.” It might also explain an individual’s desire to have a PC, whether they use them or not: not having a PC is an announcement that they are “not in the game.”

The final dimension of management control suggested by the analysis is the level of promotions, perks, etc. PCs have been introduced into an incentive environment that was not designed with them in mind. Given that the PCs have raised the net returns from office politics, it may make sense to lower the expected net returns by reducing the expected value of promotions, bonuses, perks, etc. Even simple policies aimed at commenting on the quality of the analysis rather than the graphics or presentation could have substantial effect on the level of office politics.

## 5. Conclusions and Observations

Here we have used an expanded notion of the principal-agent relationship to analyze a firm's decision on the deployment of microcomputers. It was shown that there are circumstances where the manager encourages redistribution activities of the employee by the deployment of PCs. There are also circumstances in which the deployment of PCs and/or wider distribution of the firm's data bases will increase the productivity of the employees, as measured by the bottom line of the firm.

Casual evidence (declining white collar productivity) is strong in support of the argument that stand alone PCs and wider distribution of data has led to extra unproductive but possibly self serving work. In the context of our model, this decline is consistent with the rapid deployment of PCs. Both are motivated by the employees' incentive to "look good."

Trends in the marketplace and the popular media also seem to recognize these observations. Several new products are designed to bring decentralized hardware under control. Wessel [20] reported on work stations without disks or other local permanent data store. Other products include memory resident software that logs the use of PCs. Networked PCs can also be used to reassert control over decentralized capacity. These products monitor the employee. Our analysis suggests that the number of employees with access to data and technology are also important management tools for controlling the decentralized technology.

As discussed previously, Evans says that the first phase of investment in PCs is “characterized by opportunistic use of the technology. Individuals make discrete use of the technology to automate the tasks associated with their jobs.” In the new phases of investment the buzzwords now are integration and networks. It is usually argued that synergistic benefits can be captured by linking systems and redefining work. This is probably true. However, we might argue that one of the major benefits that can be captured is cheaper control over the decentralized computing capacity. If this is true, there may be a large increase in white collar productivity as systems are integrated.

If one takes a broad view of the history of the deployment and use of the PC, the decline in productivity may not be all bad. The personal incentives probably result in its rapid deployment, which may have taken a long time if the central IS groups had controlled the process. Now the gains from linkage and control may be large. One can contrast this to the telephone, where there is no incentive to have a phone until others have them.
