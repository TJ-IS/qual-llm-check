# Selecting a requirement determination methodology-contingency approach revisited

Bijan Fazlollahi

School of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

Mohan R. Tanniru

School of Management, Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY 13244, USA

Previous research proposed a contingency approach to selecting a particular requirement determination methodology because of the uncertainty that exists in defining application/organizational information requirements. Recent research has shown that organizations use information to reduce uncertainty, to resolve equivocality, or both. Since the nature of information used to reduce uncertainty is different from that used to resolve equivocality, it is necessary to decide which is needed before starting to design the future system. This paper presents a three step approach to selecting a requirement determination methodology. The first step calls for assessment of the degree of uncertainty and equivocality present in the application. Based on this, an appropriate information acquisition strategy is identified. The third step calls for the selection of RD methodologies that are effective in acquiring the needed information. This approach is illustrated with a set of examples.

Keywords: Requirement determination, Systems analysis, Uncertainty, Equivocality, Information acquisition, Contingency approach

## 1. Introduction

The requirement analysis phase in MIS planning translates the user needs/objectives (derived from the strategic planning phase) to specific information system (IS) proposals. These are evaluated in the resource allocation phase to arrive at an acceptable 'system portfolio' [6]. The requirement analysis phase can be broken into two steps: requirement determination and requirement evaluation. The determination step translates the needs/objectives into specific IS requirements, while the evaluation step develops a specific IS proposal that meets these requirements within a socioeconomic context.

A number of strategies have been proposed, and some extensively used, to determine information requirements. These include techniques such as: Strategic Set Transformation [17], Business Systems Planning [14], Critical Success Factors [22], Ends and Means Analysis [25], Decision Analysis [1], Data Flow Diagram [7], and Warnier-Orr Diagrams [21]. However, there are generally very few guidelines on when and under what circumstances a certain strategy should be employed.

Davis [5] uses the uncertainty present in the requirements determination process to select one of these strategies. He attributes this uncertainty to four components; the utilizing system (the object system that receives information from the application), the application system, users and analysts, and shows that uncertainty in any of these components can affect the analyst's ability to derive a set of usable requirements (see Figure 1). He proposes a contingency approach that first assesses the overall uncertainty associated with deriving information requirements to satisfy a user need and then selects one of four broad categories of requirement determination strategies: asking (the user), deriving from an existing system, synthesizing the characteristics of the utilizing system, and discovering through experimentation. On the uncertainty reduction scale, 'asking the user' is considered appropriate when uncertainty is low, while 'discovering through experimentation' is considered suitable when uncertainty is high.

Organizations, however, have been assumed to process information to reduce uncertainty and to resolve equivocality [26]. While uncertainty is defined in terms of the difference between the information that is needed to make decisions and the information an organization already has [11], equivocality implies the existence of multiple and conflicting interpretations about an organizational situation [4]. Uncertainty can be reduced by asking questions about variables that influence the decision making process. However, in equivocal situations, it is difficult to ask any questions, as the organization is not aware of the variables that may affect the decision making process. Daft and Lengle [2] suggest that richer, and not necessarily more, information is needed to resolve equivocal situations, while more quantitative information is needed to handle uncertain situations.

The ‘uncertainty’ among the components (users, analysts, utilizing system, application system) actually includes factors that can cause equivocality, uncertainty or both. For example, an incomplete statement of ‘user need’ may call for an extensive interaction between the user and an analyst. An equivocal situation can arise if either the user, analyst, or both lack experience in defining the ‘user need’ clearly and completely. However, if a user need is well stated but the information requirements to satisfy this need is incomplete, then the RD methodology chosen has to reduce this gap (uncertainty). While both cases need information, the methodology used to gather information in the former has to be more ‘user intensive,’ calling for richer human-interaction, whereas the other is more ‘procedure intensive,’ calling for a detailed process analysis. The objective of this paper is to propose a revised contingency approach that selects an RD methodology based on the uncertainty and equivocality in requirement determination.

## 2. Equivocality and Uncertainty in Organizational Processes

Uncertainty may be reduced by the collection of more data; equivocality is reduced by using consensus seeking procedures. There are three sources of organizational uncertainty and equivocality: technology, interdepartmental relations (linkage and coordination among departments), and the environment (events and problems external to the organization).

## 2.1. Technology

The technology component corresponds to identifying the tools and techniques necessary to transform a task's input into outputs. This transformation process becomes complex if it has to deal with a variety of events whose occurrence is difficult to establish ahead of time, or if the task is unstructured or semistructured and is difficult to analyze. The former is said to have high task uncertainty, while the latter situation has to deal with high equivocality.

An invoicing system that charges customers according to customer type (household, business, etc.), product type, customer credit rating, sale period etc., may face high uncertainty if it does not have all the information it needs to prepare the invoice. Here gathering all relevant information is necessary to complete the task. On the other hand, the task of hiring a top executive who is likely to succeed in an organization is primarily one of high equivocality, as the variables that are relevant to choosing a successful candidate are difficult to identify. In this case, information gathering should focus first on identifying the relevant variables that impact the decision making process.

An example of low uncertainty and equivocality is the standard billing task that charges customers a fixed price and tax for products shipped, while the customer loan authorization task in a bank can be of high equivocality (an interpretation of a customer's past financial activity is often ambiguous) and high uncertainty (customers can bring in varied background which affects their payment profile) (See Figure 2a).

## 2.2. Interdepartmental Relations

A task depends to a varying degree on tasks performed by other organizational units, and this relationship is either well understood but changes with time, or ill-defined due to a lack of explicit relationship between the goals of each of these units. The former is of high dependency, while the latter is one of high differentiation. While information to interface high dependency tasks, whose dependencies change with time, has to address uncertainty, the information to bridge highly differentiated tasks, whose goals are different or perceived to be different, need to address equivocality.

The case of ‘sale and delivery of goods to a customer’ generally calls for a significant interaction among various units in an organization and uncertainty arises if the way a customer order is handled changes frequently. However, an organization that is establishing a policy to handle ‘return of sold merchandise’ may find that different units (such as finance, sales, and manufacturing) view the impact of such a policy on their operations differently. Lack of a clear resolution of these multiple goals can result in high equivocality.

The task of ‘updating financial ledgers’ is typically one of low dependency and differentiation, because the nature of dependency between accounting and other units that generate financial transactions is pre-established, well defined and relatively stable. On the other hand, ‘job shop scheduling’ task can be of high dependency and differentiation if the complexity and completion time associated with each job varies significantly (see Figure 2b).

## 2.3. Environment

Any decisions may influence the environment external to the organization or be influenced by the dynamics of the environment or both. If an organization intervenes extensively with a volatile environment, the impact of intervention is highly uncertain. On the other hand, if one is unable to express the causal relationship between the environmental and decision variables, then an organization is faced with high equivocality.

A firm marketing a product in a growing metropolitan area, in which the consumer profile is constantly changing and new competitors are frequently entering, is faced with high uncertainty in determining the impact of its intrusiveness on profitability. On the other hand, a firm that is evaluating a new promotion policy to increase its market share may have difficulty in predicting the impact of actions taken by its competitors, industry, customers, etc. on its success: here the firm is faced with high equivocality.

The impact of a wholesale distributor locating a new warehouse may be relatively easy to establish. Typically, it impacts in a predictable manner: the tax base, labor force, distribution channels, and other changes in environmental variables have a predictable affect on the cost structure of the wholesale distributor. On the other hand, a firm that is introducing a new product into a new market faces high uncertainty in its ability to evaluate the impact of its intrusiveness (through active sales promotions, etc.) and equivocality in predicting the impact of market forces on profitability (see Figure 2c).

## 3. Measurement of Uncertainty and Equivocality

Definitions of uncertainty are numerous, including those from information theory, decision theory and organizational behavior. Likewise the concept of equivocality is defined widely in organizational behavior literature. However, there are no agreed-upon measures to establish the degree of uncertainty or equivocality that may exist in a given situation.

Most of the proposed measures of uncertainty are based on information provided by the user group: e.g. from interview or questionnaire. These are therefore based on the individual perception of uncertainty faced in the decision situation; many use ‘Lickert-type’ scale to tabulate these perceptions. Duncan [10] establishes the degree of uncertainty by tabulating a user’s perception of the impact of various environmental factors on the decision, the consequences of the decision, and the ability with which s/he can assign probabilities to these factors’ effect on the decision performance measures. Milliken [20] classifies uncertainty in terms of state (those factors in the environment that affect a decision), effect (the nature of impact of these factors on the decision outcome), and response (the possible consequences of choosing a particular response). Such a classification allows for a better identification of the uncertainty that actually exists in a given situation and, in turn, the targeting techniques to measure this uncertainty. Daft, Lengl and Trevino [3] use several decision makers’ classification of organizational incidents to measure their perception of equivocality.

Measures to evaluate the extent of uncertainty that exists in a given situation are not very successful; this has been attributed to not recognizing the differences in the types of uncertainty faced by a decision making unit $[8,9,15]$ . For example, knowing the types of environmental factors that affect a decision, but not knowing what values they might take at the time of the decision is not the same as not knowing what types of environmental factors affect a given decision situation. The former is caused by lack of sufficient information (informational uncertainty) to estimate the values of certain environmental factors, while the latter is caused by lack of sufficient knowledge (knowledge uncertainty) about the relevancy of the factors themselves on the decision making process. In other words, informational uncertainty can be corrected by gathering more information, while knowledge uncertainty requires a synthesis of ‘richer’ information and these two dimensions correspond to the uncertainty and equivocality dimensions.

Here we propose a line of questioning that allows us to classify a situation as one of high/low uncertainty and high/low equivocality, as shown in Table 1. The questions are derived from the basic definition of uncertainty and equivocality, as proposed by Daft and Lengl [2], environmental factors, as in the research by Duncan [10], and the interdependency in organizational decisions, as defined by Thomson [23].

## 4.1. User Need Identification

UNI must be specified in measurable terms so that any proposed solution can be evaluated. For example, a user need such as ‘reduce customer complaints with regard to back-order delays’ can be expressed as: ‘improve timeliness of back order processing’ or ‘improve the inventory management’. The former requirement has a much narrower focus than the latter; this may result in different performance measures.

It is possible to express a single user need as a union of several other needs and vice versa. For example, a need such as: ‘improve customer service’ can be satisfied if needs such as ‘better customer order monitoring’ and ‘faster shipment’ are all satisfied. Also, satisfying a single user need can satisfy multiple user needs.

Role of technology: In general, if the user need is clear but information that is used to make this decision is uncertain and changes over time, then one needs to continuously gather and synthesize new information that may be relevant and useful. However, when the user need is highly equivocal then one has to agree first on what constitutes the satisfaction of a user need before embarking on obtaining the needed information.

Role of inter-departmental relations: Defining a user need may require interaction with users from multiple departments. Then, the strength of user interdependency and perception of the system affects the needs. The definition of a ‘perpetual inventory system’ may be clear to multiple users, but each may see the role differently. For example, a sales unit may want a 2 second access to the inventory file, the purchasing unit needs exception reports on out-of-stock items, and the order follow-up unit needs back-order reports on items out-of-stock. These multiple interdependent user needs have to be considered explicitly. However, if the users see the role of a system differently, then one must reconcile the multiple views and resolve any equivocality.

Role of environment: If the user need calls for an active intrusion into the organizational environment, then it is crucial to establish the level of impact desired as a part of the user need. For example, if a user wants to influence the vendor performance then it is necessary to agree on the extent to which environmental intervention is desired and accepted. Since such intrusion may have a significant impact on organizational visibility, it is necessary that any uncertainty with respect to these user needs be reduced. However, if the role of environment is unanalyzable then multiple views have to be gathered and synthesized. See Table 2 for examples.

## 4.2. Application Change Determination

Once user needs are identified, they should be related to the current or proposed system to determine any possible changes to the application environment. One should identify the features, if any, of the current system to be changed and the new features that must be added to meet the user needs. This 'Application Change Determination (ACD)' step identifies various processes, flows/files, persons, and procedures, or their combination, that will be affected in meeting the user needs.

## 4.3. Information Requirement Determination

The next step is to translate the application changes into information requirements in order to generate alternatives. Not all application changes may require information system solutions. In cases where the proposed changes in the application can be met by specific information system tasks such as data processing, data management, and data presentation, then these tasks form the core of the third step: Information Requirement Determination (IRD).

Role of technology: Understanding the nature of information processing tasks that are necessary to support the application changes is the primary objective here. If determining the information requirements is equivocal, then a resolution is needed first as to what constitutes relevant information before defining any information requirements.

Role of inter-departmental relations: IRD often needs interaction with various agents of the utilizing system. For example, a system that calls for an integration of order processing, inventory control, and accounts receivable activities to 'monitor customer orders' requires interaction with multiple departments and user personnel by identifying the role of each of these units. It is possible, however, that individuals have a different view of the need for "accurate and timely information", thus causing equivocality. These multiple views have to be reconciled before arriving at a decision on the information requirements.

Role of environment: Sometimes determining information requirements may call for an elaborate analysis of the environmental data. For example, an application that calls for an immediate update of customer accounts for deposits and withdrawals using ATMs will require collecting significant information on customer's financial transaction environment. Such an environmental intrusion, unless analyzed carefully, can have uncertain impact on the user needs. However, if the impact on the environment is equivocal then one needs to evaluate this impact carefully from multiple perspectives before defining the information system requirements of such a network. Daft and Lengle identified several information acquisition strategies (they referred to these as design strategies) for reducing uncertainty and resolving equivocality; these are, in decreasing order of richness: group meetings, integrators (of boundary spanning activity), direct contact, planning, special reports, formal information systems, and rules and regulations. While group meetings provide a forum for free, face-to-face exchange of ideas, integrators provide a more formal mechanism for idea communication. Direct contact refers to one-on-one interviewing, while 'planning' implies a combination of both an informal discussion at the beginning and close monitoring and reporting of activities towards the end. Special reports provide some specific, well defined information, while information systems provide periodic data on various activities. The rules and regulations typically provide information on well understood phenomena.

## 6.1. Hospital Records Maintenance System

A university hospital initiated a study of its record keeping system. Patient records were found to be taking too much space and retrieval was cumbersome. Further investigation lead to the conclusion that patient file explosion was due to a lack of policy on removal of patient records from the master file. The user need was, thus, to identify which patient records should be kept in the system, and for how long.

This user need impacted multiple users. The legal arm of the hospital needed patient information to meet government regulations, the auditing unit needed past information on patients for credit evaluation, the hospital associations needed information to conduct health related surveys, and the drug prescription information on a patient was used by other hospitals who served the patient later. The interdepartmental dimension of the UNI, hence, was of high equivocality and low uncertainty. A form of brain storming was used to reach a consensus on a ‘retention’ policy for maintaining the patient file. Data flow analysis was used to analyze the application changes. Ends and means analysis and process analysis were used to determine the specific information requirements.

## 6.2. Distribution of Order Processing Activities

An office supply company was interested in distributing some of its order processing activities (order preparation, order filling, inventory management, and billing) to its warehouse locations in order to improve customer service. User Requirement Determination was shown to be of low uncertainty and low equivocality on all dimensions. Additional investigation showed that the application changes could be extracted with little uncertainty or equivocality. However, the information requirements determination step had varying degrees of uncertainty and equivocality.

Several alternative architectures were available for networking. Their diversity (dial-up vs lease, star vs ring, direct vs multi-drop, etc.) and varied information needs made this task highly uncertain. Thus, the technical and environmental components of the information requirements were of high uncertainty and low equivocality. Direct contact was used to obtain the needs, i.e. what activities were candidates for distribution. The processes to be distributed were identified using process and data flow analysis. Normative modeling and simulation methodologies were used along with process analysis to extract information needs and generate feasible alternatives. Socio-technical analysis was then performed and a weighted-CSF approach (weighing the degree to which each of the alternatives met the critical success factors) was used to arrive at a consensus. ## 7. Conclusions

This paper treats the requirement determination of an MIS planning framework as an organizational process and identifies their sources of uncertainty and equivocality. By sub-classifying the requirement determination process into user need identification, application change determination, and information requirement determination, the sources of ambiguity or uncertainty can be isolated and appropriate information acquisition strategies identified. Using the relationship between the information acquisition strategies and requirement determination methodologies, an appropriate RD methodology or methodologies can be selected to translate a user need into information requirements.

Project teams are often formed to implement methodologies chosen to determine information requirements. By knowing the particular information acquisition strategy employed, one can determine the project team characteristics and, possibly, change the team composition as the strategy varies. The project team (the number of individuals involved and their skill level), the team's organization (how they communicate and coordinate their activities), and the extent of user or team member involvement in various project related activities can be derived from the RD methodology selected. In addition, the role of various members in the team will change as the RD methodologies vary during the requirement determination phase.

Another extension to this approach is to study the role of equivocality and uncertainty in other phases of the development life cycle and evaluate the type of appropriate methodologies. This should provide guidance for structuring project activities over the entire project life cycle and manage it effectively. There has already been some research into the use of these dimensions in system implementation [16]. If the methodologies appropriate in various phases of the life cycle are different, it would be interesting to see what impact this would have on project costs and time estimate, project personnel performance evaluation, and the degree of feasible personnel substitution.
