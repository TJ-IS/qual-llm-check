# Usability of knowledge-based systems Beyond technical feasibility

Abdulla Hasan Abdul-Gader

Department of Accounting and Management Information Systems, College of Industrial Management, King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, Dhahran 31261, Saudi Arabia

Designing a usable computer-based information system is a challenging task. Information systems are costly and require a great organizational commitment. Knowledge-based systems are no exception. To be able to extract domain expertise and encode it in a computer system is not all there is to do. This paper advocates a user-centered view in knowledge-based systems design and development. The ultimate user's task and the level of representation suitable for it must be incorporated and accounted for in designing such expert systems. This therefore calls for a shift from "efficiency" to "adequacy" of the problem solution, a shift of concern from the requirements of the "computer" to those of "user". Our findings are based on two implementation experiments of a seismic interpretation knowledge-based system.

Keywords: Knowledge-based systems, Expert systems, Artificial Intelligence applications, Seismic interpretation, Systems design, Human-computer interaction, Usability.

## 1. Introduction

A knowledge-based system is a computer representation that uses knowledge and problem-solving paradigms on a skill level comparable to those of human experts. Knowledge-based systems, also known as expert systems, are the most visible applications of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) discipline [15]. Compared to its other branches, such as voice and pattern recognition, expert systems by far are the main contributors in moving AI from research laboratories to manufacturing floors and other real world applications.

Recent applications of knowledge-based systems have demonstrated their potential to mimic high-level human performance. Knowledge-based applications have been developed not only in the scientific and engineering sectors but also in educational and managerial tasks $[4,5]$ . Plant scheduling, underwriting claims, and long-term financial strategy recommendations are some of the commercial applications of expert systems technology. In the United States of America, the expert systems market is growing at almost 30% a year and will soon be a 2.0 billion dollars industry $[10]$ . The advent of knowledge-based systems might be attributed to their inherent capacity to exploit and retain the domain expertise gained over time.

The technical feasibility of knowledge-based systems is fairly established for many task domains because of the availability of more powerful development shells, more processing power of computers, and the latest advancements in knowledge acquisition and representation technologies. Successful implementation of computer-based systems, including knowledge-based systems, require more than technical feasibility. Like any other computer information system, the availability of knowledge-based systems does not necessary mean realization of their full potential. These systems may or may not be used, and even if they are used, productivity enhancement may or may not be attained.

To ensure successful implementation of knowledge-based systems, the management and development team (i.e., knowledge engineers) must establish technical and human feasibility. This entails careful consideration of hardware and software, well planned integration of the knowledge-based system with current other computer-based technology, and awareness of social issues generated by the introduction of the technology. This socio-technical approach looks to technology as a social change that affects employees and the quality of their work. It offers a framework to study employees' acceptance of or resistance to technological change; this is the key factor in the success or failure of systems.

The AI literature provides undue emphasis on the technical aspects of expert systems development, ignoring usability and human issues. Two issues seem to dominate the discussion of expert systems construction [6,7,14]: how to acquire experts' Knowledge, and how to provide for its computer representation.

The major objective of this paper is to bridge the gap between technical considerations and human factors. Based on the author's experience in designing a knowledge-based system, implications of their design and implementation are drawn. Special attention is devoted to discussing threats that may jeopardize usability of knowledge-based systems and, hence, their effectiveness. Specific Knowledge engineering design strategies are also presented.

## 2. Seismic interpreter

The Seismic Interpreter is a microcomputer-based prototype knowledge-based system developed at a major American oil company to capture geophysicist's expertise in seismic interpretation [1]. This is an essential component of the oil exploration process. It entails analyzing a two-dimensional map of a vertical section of the earth (the oil prospect) showing sound wave reflections across many layers, called a seismic section. To produce the section, an appropriate sound generator is used to generate sound waves; then the reflected signals are recorded on magnetic tapes through special receiving instruments called geophones. Very involved mathematical algorithms are used to process these tapes in order to output the seismic section. The system is built and tested as an initial step in developing a more comprehensive knowledge-based system for oil exploration. Such a general system must incorporate all aspects of the exploration process: geological, economical, and geophysical analyses.

Two senior geophysicists who are considered experts in seismic interpretation participated in the project. To insure complete coverage of the subject, two experts were believed to be better than one, especially if the knowledge engineer is not familiar with the domain $[3,8]$ .

When a reasonably acceptable prototype of the seismic interpretation had been developed, two experiments were devised to test user acceptance of the system. In each, a user is asked to assess performance. Two geophysicists, not the project experts but working in the same company, were asked to instructively probe the system. One of these is a senior geophysicist, whereas the other is a new graduate in a training program. The participants were instructed to challenge the system and test every aspect of it. Their candid feedback has helped in formulating the design guidelines. It is important to note that these experiments were intended to illustrate overall design weakness rather than just to certify that the system is correctly interpreting seismic sections. Indeed these two concepts are not the same, since even if the system satisfactorily models seismic interpretation expertise, there still remains the question: "Will the system be used for seismic interpretation?".

## 3. Expert system design

Building knowledge-based system is not a trivial job. Past work has shown that the time required to build an expert-level prototype has ranged from six months to two years [12,13]. Despite the technological advancements, both in software and hardware, knowledge engineering is still a major challenge. Designing knowledge-based systems comprises a mixture of obtaining the expert's knowledge, a process called knowledge acquisition, and implementing the system. Knowledge acquisition and Knowledge representation (the computer model of domain knowledge) are often termed knowledge engineering. Knowledge engineers are responsible for extracting knowledge from domain experts and designing for its representation in a computer system. A good understanding of the nature of knowledge is important.

Abstractly, domain knowledge consists of three components, namely: descriptions, relationships, and procedures. First, the properties and attributes of the domain objects and classes are described in order to differentiate and classify them. Then the interdependencies and associations among these objects and classes are established and stored as a special type of description (relationship). The last component of knowledge is procedural knowledge; an expert develops effective and efficient search procedures to navigate the possible paths from one state to another closer to the goal.

A major impediment to the development of a knowledge-based system is aiding the expert in describing his knowledge. The knowledge acquisition process is often called the bottleneck that delays the knowledge-based systems development. A time-consuming series of incremental interviewing, building, and testing cycles are necessary requirement to reach performance comparable to that of an expert. On one hand, there is a cultural gap between the domain experts and the builders of knowledge-based systems. Each has his own jargons and terminology. On the other hand, and at a more serious level, experts may not be fully aware of their own mental processes and as a result, cannot express them easily [2]. In spite of the importance of the knowledge elicitation process, little has been reported about specific knowledge acquisition techniques [8,9]. Knowledge representation usually gets the most attention in the literature.

To represent and store the extracted knowledge in a computer system, many approaches have been implemented [11]. The most popular approach is the production system, where knowledge is represented in the form of IF THEN rules. Another approach is frames. Conceptually, a frame is akin to a preprinted form containing blanks known as slots. They hold attributes of the object described by the frame. Slots may also contain pointers to other frames or procedures explaining how the slots should be filled.

Knowledge-based systems consist of three major components:

(1) Knowledge base: a reservoir of domain facts and rules that symbolize expert knowledge. Based on the knowledge scheme, descriptions of domain objects and their relationships are encoded in the knowledge base.

(2) Inference engine: the algorithms used to derive inferences and to control the reasoning process. Procedural knowledge is the major component of the inference engine.

(3) User interface: provisions for user interaction with the system (e.g., graphical display of the reasoning process).

As shown in Figure 1, a comprehensive design model is proposed. Three components are isolated and explicitly discussed: users, task, and representation. All three components are highly interrelated and must be viewed that way. A vital ingredient of a successful implementation of expert systems is the ability of knowledge engineers to explicitly treat this interrelatedness issue.

A common tendency in expert systems development is to concentrate on the domain task under investigation; this involves the process of understanding how experts behave when performing the domain task. Acquiring domain knowledge from an expert and later adopting a representation scheme for implementing it are often thought of as the only components of knowledge engineering. The emphasis on dealing with the domain expert rather than the ultimate users of expert systems and on the domain task rather than the day-to-day task of the ultimate user are the major threats to a good design.

In the development of the Seismic Interpreter, we focussed on the seismic interpretation failing to identify the potential system users. In other words, we dealt with the geophysicists as domain experts, articulating “how they are interpreting the seismic section” and not “how the final product (i.e., the Seismic Interpreter) will fit in their normal way of seismic interpretation”. User class and user routine tasks must both be considered in expert system design.

There are different classes of information systems users. User classes range from entry-level domain expert to senior-level domain expert. In some applications, systems users might have no domain knowledge (e.g., in the yet-to-be geophysicist class). Depending on the user class, the system developer must formulate the real goals of the system at the initial stages of the development cycle. Three classes of goals might be tackled:

(1) To help users in solving a problem (high level users),

(2) To train users in solving a problem (trainees),

(3) To help users to understand a problem (low level users).

Different goals require different designs and different representations. As we move down this list, the need for higher emphasis on representation increases. Representation involves the system's ability to represent and clarify the problem situation to the user. Explaining an inference might be considered a form of representation if it provides for more than just a rule tracing capability. An ideal representation should be equivalent to an articulate domain expert who can answer low level why and how questions.

If the developer's goal is to build a knowledge-based system that offers assistance in solving a problem, representation is of minor concern. The opposite situation will occur as users demand to understand the problem, since more than surface rules tracing will be needed. In the first trial experiment of the Seismic Interpreter, a senior geophysicist was asked to use the system to analyze seismic sections of an oil prospect. His first reaction was appreciation of the reasoning mechanism. But he also pointed out the Seismic Interpreter's inadequacy in explaining its decisions and recommendations. This problem surfaced more prominently when a trainee geophysicist used the system. After a few minutes, the trainee became confused and started losing interest in continuing. Because of “the system complexity”, as described by the trainee, the Seismic Interpreter decisions were convoluted and groundless.

Based on these observations, the following five implications are formulated:

1: Users want justification and comprehension. They need to know “where do the answers come from?”. System designers must observe this fact and design for system users.

2: Rules that are adequate to produce answers and solutions are not necessarily adequate to explain them. The system must represent the problem situation to the user. Thus, rule sequence or rule tracing per se is not a real explanation.

Overcoming the representation problem is difficult but it can be solved. One way is to expand the role of knowledge acquisition phase to allow the designers to elicit potential explanation requests for different users. Theory-based knowledge acquisition techniques such as the Coherence Method is a step in the right direction [1].

Another important consideration in expert system design is the users' task. Users will not use the system unless it fits smoothly into their work:

\- The system needs to be consistent with their existing information systems.

\- Access to the system must be effortless (e.g., requiring no special login) and physically convenient (e.g., does not require the user to leave the office for a distant location).

\- Users must have time to learn and use the system.

\- Users must feel that the system is there to help them not to replace them.

3: The system must be smoothly integrated into the user's task.

Hiding the system in an existing instrument is one way to deal with these problems. Expert systems can be designed to be part of a conventional software application. These are callable modules that can be imported into applications. An insurance company, for example, uses computer applications that call an expert system for underwriting insurance claim decisions. PUFF [6] is also another example of such implementation and design strategy.

In addition to integrating expert systems into users' routine task, these systems must, whenever feasible, utilize existing data. Most knowledge-based system deal with information that is already available in some computer system. Manual reentry of data is error-prone and costly and will not be tolerated by users. Operational systems must access existing data automatically. This may mean escaping from the AI environment into the hostile, idiosyncratic data processing environment.

4: The system must be smoothly integrated into the existing data bases.

5: Designers and management must be aware of the costs and dangers of incorporating knowledge-based systems with existing information systems.

Oil exploration data analysis is highly automated. Seismic sections, for example, are generated by computer systems and based on data recorded directly on computer tapes. An ambitious objective for the Seismic Interpreter is to be able to read and interpret these sections with minimum human intervention.

## 4. Conclusion

Making computer-based information system usable is an enormously important design consideration. Information systems are costly and require a great organizational commitment. Knowledge-based systems are no exception. To be able to extract domain expertise and encode it in a computer system is not all there is to do. This paper tried to promote a user-centered view in knowledge-based systems design. The ultimate user routine tasks and the level of representation suitable for them must be incorporated and accounted for in designing expert systems. This paper advocates a shift from the “efficiency” to the “adequacy” of the problem solution, a shift of concern from the “computer” to the “user”. These findings were based on two implementation experiments of a real knowledge-based system.
