# A comparison of perceptions about information center success

Robert L. Leitheiser

School of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin-Whitewater, Whitewater, WI 53190, USA

James C. Wetherbe

School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

Information centers (ICs) are a popular way for firms to facilitate and coordinate end-user computing. A continuing role for ICs in organizations depends on how successfully they meet their goals. This paper explores perceptions of IC success held by IC managers and MIS executives. Data was collected using a mail survey and then analyzed to produce both specific item scores and aggregate measures for realized success, major problems, and critical success factors. Comparisons were done to determine if MIS executives viewed IC success in the same way as IC managers. The results suggest that while some differences exist, on the whole there is agreement about what an IC must do to be successful.

Keywords: End-user computing, Management of MIS, Critical success factors, Information centers.

## 1. Introduction

An Information Center (IC) is an organizational unit established to facilitate and coordinate end-user computing. The IC Is usually part of the MIS function and supports end-users by offering consulting, training, troubleshooting, application development, data extraction, and other services $[12]$ . Some observers believe that ICs are only the first stage of end-user support and that after a period of time firms will move to a more distributed arrangement $[10]$ .

One indicator of the need for such a change would be the perception that ICs are not successfully meeting their support objectives. The purpose of this paper is to determine if ICs are considered to be successful by their managers and by the head of the MIS function. The two perspectives are compared for differences that could suggest a lack of direction, poor communication, or a more fundamental problem with the IC approach to end-user support. Specifically, the study was designed to address these questions:

## 2. Prior research

Information centers were often established as a response to rapidly growing end-user computing activities. The characteristics of ICs were explored in field surveys by Sumner [11] and Wetherbe and Leitheiser [7,12]. Both studies produced descriptions of centers that included services offered, software supported, types of users, size and age of center, etc. Especially important for the purposes of this paper, is that they also asked IC managers about their perceptions of success. Sumner interviewed IC managers at 13 companies and from these interviews identified 3 success factors for each IC. Unfortunately, no attempt was made to combine or otherwise process the factors so their usefulness for other firms is limited.

Wetherbe and Leitheiser conducted interviews with the managers of 25 ICs. Respondents were asked to list their most important successes, most serious problems, and the factors most critical to IC success. The managers' responses were combined and aggregated to create lists that represented the entire sample. The three lists were ordered in terms of importance by using number of times they had been mentioned. There was also an attempt to identify relationships among these factors, but the analysis was limited by the study's methodology.

Based in part, on the critical success factors identified in the two previous studies, Magal, Carr, and Watson designed a more rigorous study to measure IC managers' perceptions about their Critical Success Factors (CSFs). A questionnaire was mailed to 1490 IC managers of which three hundred and eleven returned usable responses. The authors performed a factor analysis to reduce the data to a smaller number of composite factors. These composite factors were used to investigate how CSF importance depended on the stage of IC maturity [8], and whether CSF importance was related to IC age, IC size, size of user group, or type of hardware supported [9]. The authors found little evidence that CSF importance varied due to these factors. Apparently, the perceptions of importance of the CSFs by IC managers is relatively constant.

The IC manager's perception of success may not represent the views of the rest of the organization. Brancheau, Vogal, and Wetherbe [2] asked representative end-users in five companies to describe various aspects of the ICs that they utilized. One of the interview questions attempted to identify CSFs for the IC. The most frequently mentioned CSF category concerned the quality of the IC staff. Other CSF categories were: availability of services, training, appropriate equipment, research, and management support. The responses suggest some agreement with the IC managers' CSFs, but since a standard measure was not used, and a matched group of IC managers was not surveyed, a stronger statement about agreement can not be made.

An important perspective that has not been investigated is that of the MIS executive. Given that an IC is usually part of the MIS function, the perceptions of the MIS executive are critical to its continuing operation. This study examines the perspectives of IC managers and their MIS executives. An attempt is made to determine if those perspectives on success are the same, and if not, to identify the differences between them.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. The questionnaire

In order to address the research questions, a study was designed to measure perceptions of IC success held by IC managers and MIS executives. The overall strategy was to use a mail questionnaire to collect data about IC success from both management perspectives. The items for the questionnaire were derived from three open ended questions used in a previous study $[12]$ .

1. What have been some important successes you have realized with your IC?

2. What are the major problems that you are currently facing that concern your IC?

3. What are the few things that you absolutely have to do right in managing your IC to be considered a success by your organization?

The critical success factors that were produced from question No. 3 were also used as part of the study by Magal, Carr and Watson.

The questionnaire itself consists of a demographic page and one page each for rating successes, problems, and CSFs. Respondents were asked to provide a rating for both the level of importance and the level of success. Items were placed in a randomized order.

## 3.2. Sample

The sample for the study was a group of 60 Midwestern organizations. One IC manager questionnaire and one MIS executive questionnaire was sent to each firm. Several reminders were given to help improve the response rate.

Forty IC managers and thirty-three MIS executives returned usable questionnaires. It was decided to only use matched IC manager/MIS executive pairs of questionnaires so that differences among firms would be minimized. This restriction left 28 pairs of questionnaires that became the basis of the analysis (a 48% response rate). The basic characteristics of the firms and their ICs are given in Table 1.

## 3.3. Analysis

The analysis consisted of two parts. First, the responses for all the items were averaged to produce an ordered list of successes, problems, and CSFs. Second, a multidimensional scaling analysis was performed to reduce the items to a smaller set of composite factors, and to investigate the structure that exists within the items. The reduced set of variables was used in further analyses.

Also presented in Table 2 are the mean ratings for the perceived degree of success achieved for each type of success. A strong correlation between the levels of success perceived by IC managers and MIS executives (r = 0.89) suggests that there is agreement on how successful the IC has been. A moderately strong relationship between importance and success is suggested by the correlations between the two measures for each management level (IC: r = 0.79 and Exec: r = 0.69).

Another dimension of IC success concerns the existence of major problems. A list of problems and associated mean ratings are given in Table 3. IC managers rated the problem of inadequate controls on user development as most important. This was followed by the problem of not having enough IC capacity to meet demand for services. MIS executives also rated these two problems as most important (but in the opposite order). Both problems were mentioned by a large number of respondents in the Leitheiser and Wetherbe study [7].

Overall, the agreement between IC managers and MIS executives over relative importance of problems is much less than for realized successes $r = 0.43$ . Specifically, the problems of incompatible hardware, obtaining top management support, absence of top management use of IC services, and lack of publicity were seen as more serious by IC managers. MIS executives, on the other hand, are more concerned with software procurement, user expectation, and data administration problems.

A negative relationship exists between the importance of a problem and the degree of success with it (IC: r = -0.38 and Exec: r = -0.59). MIS executives in particular associate low levels of success with the more important problems. Overall agreement on the level of success, however, appears to be quit high (r = 0.93).

The final dimension of success concerns CSFs (see Table 4). Both IC managers and MIS executives considered the development of a competent IC staff and effective end-user training to be the most important factors in determining IC success. Recall that the related success items (Table 2) were also rated most important. The relative importance placed on all CSF items by IC managers and MIS executives were highly correlated (r = 0.84) which suggests a similarity of perceptions by the two groups.

IC managers apparently put effort where their perceptions of importance were, because both management groups rated the IC as being very successful in developing a competent staff. Interestingly, the level of perceived success for end-user training was markedly different for IC managers (4.9) and MIS executives (5.5). An examination of the relationship between the importance of CSFs and their degree of achievement found it to be strong for both IC managers (r = 0.93) and MIS executives (r = 0.96).

## 4.2. Structural analysis

To further examine the underlying phenomenon, individual items were grouped together into composite successes, problems, and CSFs. These composites are derived empirically and represent the underlying structure of the data. Magal, Carr, and Watson used factor analysis to group their CSF items into 5 composite factors: (1) commitment to the IC concept, (2) quality of IC support services, (3) facilitation of end-user computing, (4) role clarity, and (5) coordination of end-user computing. Another means of grouping items is Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) [4,6]. The latter approach is more appropriate for the current study because of the small sample size.

All of the MDS solutions were developed using four dimensional configurations. This level of dimensionality was selected because it (1) met goodness of fit criteria and (2) it produced interpretable clusters of items [6]. In order to present the solutions in an economical and easily interpretable way, the clusters are presented in their two-dimensional configuration (see Appendix 2).

## 5. Concluding remarks

## 5.1. Summary

The purpose of this paper was to investigate differences in perceptions concerning IC success. In particular, the perceptions of IC managers and MIS executives were measured and analyzed. Four research questions were proposed and addressed.

The first question was concerned with determining the most important criteria for assessing IC success. The ordered lists of realized successes, major problems, and CSFs provide some guidance for these criteria. The ability to develop a competent IC staff and to deliver effective end-user training are the two most important criteria suggested by both the realized success and CSF results. The most serious problems facing ICs in the opinion of the IC manages and MIS executives were inadequate controls for user development and demand for services that exceeds capacity. The ability to solve these problems presents another criterion for success.

The second question focussed on whether there were differences between the perceptions of success for IC managers and MIS executives. Correlations between item means for IC managers and MIS executives suggested that perspectives on realized successes and CSFs were very similar but that differences existed with regard to major problems.

A structural analysis of the item responses found similar composite groups for the success and CSF data but important differences in the problem data. Specifically, the IC manager groups focussed more on internal management problems while the MIS executives were more concerned with broader, corporate-wide problems. Tests on the differences between importance composite groups for the two management levels resulted in only one significant difference. It is possible that the underlying reasons for concern about the problems differ but that these reasons result in similar ratings of importance.

The third question concerned perceptions of level of success. Correlations between IC manager and MIS executive mean ratings for level of success were relatively high suggesting no difference in perspective. Using the IC manager importance clusters, composite success level scores were compared for IC managers and MIS executives. Only the difference in perceived success with eliminating outside timesharing achieved statistical significance. Not much weight should be placed on this result because it was only one of two positive results that came from three sets of paired comparison tests.

The fourth question dealt with whether there were relationships between importance and levels of success for successes, problems, and CSFs. Positive correlations of varying degrees of strength were found for successes and CSFs, while negative correlations were the norm for problems. This means that the more important a problem was, the more likely the respondent would rate success with the problem low. Important problems have not yet been solved.

## 5.2. Implications for managers

The identification of important criteria for determining IC success has its applications in management. The CSF results can be added to those of other studies to create a fairly stable picture of the CSFs for ICs. These factors appear to be relatively stable across organizations with various characteristics, across studies with different methodologies, and across different groups including IC managers, MIS executives, and end-users. This study also provided criteria for success based on realizing specific successes in solving important problems. A management strategy that addresses these three types of success criteria should have a good chance of producing an effective IC.

The study did not find glaring differences between perceptions of success held by IC managers and MIS executives. While some differences in the structure and rankings of problems surfaced, those differences are not enough to create concern about a lack of communication or understanding between the two levels of management. If end-user support moves on to a next, more decentralized stage, it will not be due to a lack of perceived success for information centers.

## 5.3. Implications for researchers

The contributions of this study to research include (1) building on prior research findings, and (2) indicating possible relationships that might be explored in further research.

This study builds on earlier studies of perceptions of IC success. It uses items developed in a previous study and produces results that can be compared with two other studies. This kind of cumulative research helps further our understanding of important MIS phenomena.

The second research contribution is to suggest directions for further research. There appears to be little to be gained in examining other perspectives of IC success. A better use of research effort would be to determine the factors that lead to successes and problems. For example, does the negative relationship between problem importance come about because important problems are hard to solve, or because problems that are solved become unimportant? What management actions lead to effective end-user training or to building a competent IC staff? Eventually, the goal of such research would be to produce an empirically based causal model that would explain and predict the success of ICs.

ICs have become very popular in the last decade as a means for facilitating and coordinating end-user computing. While there may be other means for performing these functions, as long as ICs are perceived as being successful they will have a role to play in business organizations.
