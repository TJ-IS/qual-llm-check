# An information system profile of U.S. companies

Eldon Y. Li and John C. Rogers

School of Business, California Polytechnic State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

The information system (IS) characteristics of U.S. companies were surveyed to produce an overall IS profile. This profile was examined for statistical differences as a function of size of company and stage of IS sophistication. In general, most firms surveyed are in the so-called integration or data administration stage. There were apparently no significant differences found in EDP/MIS expenditures, computing hardware expenditures, and MIS-supported activities, due to the organization's stage of IS sophistication. The size of company is found to have no significant effect on the stage of IS sophistication. Most companies have formal IS planning and control systems and some form of distributed data processing (DDP). The majority of the companies use software for conventional programming and database management purposes with very little emphasis on their use in decision modeling and statistical analysis. Quite surprisingly, IS is still being used primarily for operational and management controls rather than for strategic planning.

Keywords: IS profile, IS sophistication, Stage models, IS expenditures, Stage benchmarks, IS planning.

## 1. Introduction

Since the late 1970s, information technology has advanced rapidly. The costs of computers have dropped dramatically, yet their power has increased enormously. These changes have occurred not only with mainframe computers but also with microcomputers. And while large companies today can upgrade their mainframe computer power without large expenses, small business can generally acquire their microcomputers without financial difficulty. With the advent of user-friendly spreadsheet and database software, users in today's organizations, regardless of their size, are developing their own applications and becoming relatively independent of the application services provided by the electronic data processing or management information system (EDP/MIS) department. Consequently, more people are using computers to process their information. The end effect is that more information is being processed by today's companies and more total dollars is being spent on the IS functions. According to a recent survey by Datamation [9], an average service company in the U.S. will spend 2% of its revenues on IS while manufacturing

This huge investment indicates the importance of information technology in today's corporate America. However, not all firms are equally sophisticated in their use of information technology. In general, the sophistication of an organization's IS seems to move along stages.

According to Nolan's model [17,18], the IS of an average company evolves along six stages, namely, initiation, contagion, control, integration, data administration, and maturity. As King and Kraemer [12] stated, the popularity of the stage model “is probably due to its bold approach to dealing with a phenomenon of great complexity in a straightforward and clever manner.” Many empirical studies have either tested or adopted the stage model as a surrogate measure of IS sophistication [2,3,5,6,8,14]. However, all the existing empirical studies pertaining to the model have invariably focused on the stage representation of IS sophistication. None has reported the IS profile of the companies they have surveyed; nor have they explored or examined differences in the IS profile by size of company and stage of IS sophistication. By “IS profile” we mean the specific characteristics of the IS organization and its functions. This profile gives the IS management an overall picture of the existing IS and provides the foundation for planning effective IS strategies.

## 3. The level of IS sophistication

IS sophistication is defined in this study as the overall status of an organization's IS within its growth process. In order to reflect its level, a composite score is derived based on an equally-weighted linear combination of five of the benchmarks: DP expenditure benchmark was consistently found to have no discriminating power between IS growth stages $[4,8,13]$ , therefore, it was excluded. The composite score, which was rounded off to an integer, then serves as the indicator of the level of IS sophistication for each organization.

## 4. Research methodology

Based on the elements of the IS profile, a two-part questionnaire (see Appendix) was designed. The first contains seven questions of which the first five were adapted from Nolan's second-level benchmarks. The sixth question regarding the structure of IS resources was adapted from Nolan, Gibson and Nolan [7], and Kanter [11]. The last question contains eleven nominal-scale items; the first three were adapted from Nolan and Gibson and Nolan while the rest were identified by the authors. Nolan's technology benchmark is measured by three elements: data processing approach, mode of data processing, and computing hardware expenditures. These are included in the second section of the questionnaire in which respondents were asked to supply the percentage of IS usage, expenditures, effort, or activities in eight different profile categories: (1) EDP/MIS expenditures, (2) company-wide software usage, (3) computing hardware expenditures, (4) data processing approach, (5) system effort, (6) development effort, (7) mode of data processing, and (8) MIS-supported activities. At the end of the questionnaire, some basic demographic questions (such as sex, age, marital status, level of eduction, and job title of the respondent, industry type, annual company sales, number of company employees, annual EDP/MIS budget, and years of EDP/MIS experience) were also included. The questionnaire was pretested and refined into its final form.

The final questionnaire, with a cover letter assuring the respondent's confidentiality, was sent to the IS executives of 1000 companies randomly selected from a recent version of the Standard and Poor's directory. One hundred and thirty three questionnaires (or $13.3\%$ ) were completed and returned. Among them, 10 could not be used due to excessive missing values, leaving 123 $(12.3\%)$ usable. This response rate was expected, because it is common in many surveys of business organizations [16]. ## 5. Analysis and results

The overall IS profile of the surveyed companies is developed by examining the profile elements reported by the respondents. Each of the elements is further evaluated for significant differences by company size and stage of IS sophistication.

## 5.1.IS profile

More than half (54%) of the companies surveyed have indicated that their IS expenditures are less than the rate of sales growth. The majority (87%) have application portfolios centered on integrated database applications (stage 3 and higher). Many companies (38%) still have IS organizations located at the operational management level. Most companies (80%) have formal IS planning and control systems while only a few have implemented strategic planning and control for data resource. It is delightful to see that many (45%) of them have accepted the quality and utility of IS as a joint responsibility of user and IS staff. Furthermore, the majority of the companies (84%) have some form of DDP structure (stage 3 and higher) and many of them (71%) are still running 50% or more of their applications in batch mode (at or below stage 3 of technology benchmark).

Almost all (98%) of the companies have separate IS departments with 97% having independent IS budgets. More than half (59%) have a 3–5 year strategic IS plan. Many have strong support for end-user computing (73%) and utilized local area networks (67%), wide area networks (62%), and information centers (54%). However, there is very little use of computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools (24%) and teleconferencing (24%) taking place, except in the large companies which accounted for most of this usage. Application systems prototyping (46%) and the existence of an integrated electronic office environment (44%) were reported by less than half of the companies and, again, the large companies represent the majority of this usage.

## 5.2. IS profile by company size

The IS profile was examined for statistical significant differences (at the 0.05 level) as a function of company size. The companies were grouped by size, defined in terms of annual sales dollars. Cross-tabulation and analysis of variance were run on all the elements of IS profile reported in the Sections A and B of the questionnaire. Somewhat surprisingly, there were only a few differences in the IS profile of companies that were related to company size. As can be seen from Table 4, large companies appear to have strategic 3 to 5 year plans for their ISs more often than small companies. Similar results are found with respect to local or wide area networks, information centers, and teleconferencing activities. They seem to use more state-of-the-art CASE tools than small companies.

On the other hand, small companies tend to (1) use less statistical software, (2) spend more money on computing services and less on telecommunication, and (3) devote more development effort on programming and less on analysis than large companies (see Table 5). Furthermore, on-line batch processing decreases with the size of the firm, remote job entry increases with the size of the firm, and off-line batch processing is used very little by small companies. Finally, small companies tend to spend more money on minicomputers and microcomputers while medium to very large companies spend more on mainframe or supercomputers.

## 5.3. IS profile by stage of IS sophistication

The IS profile was also examined for statistical significant differences (at the 0.05 level) as a function of stage of IS sophistication. The companies were grouped by the composite stage identification. No firms were in stage 1 and most (76%) were found to be in stages 4 and 5. The size of company was found to have no significant relationship with IS sophistication.

Cross-tabulation and analysis of variance were once again run on all IS profile elements. As one might expect, there were a number of differences when related to stage of IS sophistication. First, as can be seen in Tables 6 and 7, Nolan's four second-level benchmarks and the technology benchmark were found to relate significantly (at the 0.001 level) to the composite stage identification indicating the validity of the equally-weighted linear combination approach adopted by this study. The correlations in sequence were 0.63, 0.53, 0.74, 0.75 and 0.41.

Companies that reach at least the integration stage (stage 4 or higher) seem more likely to have a strategic 3 to 5 year IS plan. They also tend to support end-user computing and develop applications through prototyping. With regard to company-wide software usage, conventional programming is used more in stages 2 and 3, decision modeling increases as the firm becomes more sophisticated, and data management is most used in stages 4 and 5. The more sophisticated organizations appear to process data using shared databases while the less sophisticated firms use individual files. The mode of data processing also differs as the more sophisticated firms tend to be oriented towards on-line interactive while the less sophisticated organizations process more of their data by off-line batch. There were no significant differences in IS expenditures, computing hardware expenditures, and MIS-supported activities. However, the more sophisticated firms spend more system effort on development. Design effort seems to increase with stage of IS sophistication while programming and debugging effort seem to decrease.

## 6. Conclusions and implications

The IS profile developed in this study is only representative of the companies that responded. However, our sample includes firms with varying industry types, annual company sales, number of company employees, annual EDP/MIS budgets, number of EDP/MIS employees, years of company's EDP/MIS experience. Therefore, we believe that the sample should be fairly representative and adequate for exploring IS profiles.

Our overall IS profile of U.S. companies shows that most of them have their application portfolios centered on integrated database applications. Most have formal IS planning and control systems and some form of DDP structure. Batch processing is still the major mode of operation among these companies. Most companies have an independent IS department and budget and strongly support end-user computing. Many of them have local or wide area networks as well as information centers. However, not many of them have teleconferencing or advanced CASE tools. The majority of the companies use software for conventional programming and database management with very little emphasis on decision modeling and statistical analysis. These companies process their data using both shared data bases and individual files through on-line interactive, off-line batch, or on-line batch mode. The companies spend the major portion of their IS budget on computing and personnel and very little on training: these expenditures, most go to hardware and other related equipments. Computing hardware is concentrated in mainframe computers while maintaining some mini- and micro-computers. The system effort is split among development, maintenance, and operations, with the development effort being primarily in programming, design, and analysis. Finally, regardless of company size, IS is being used primarily for operational and management controls rather than for strategic planning.

On the other hand, small companies process their data least in off-line batch mode and they own a higher percentage of mini or micro computers.

When the general IS profile is examined by IS sophistication stage, a somewhat different picture is seen. The more sophisticated businesses tend to:

(1) have teleconferencing activities, a strategic IS plan, office automation (OA), and information centers,

(2) support end-user computing,

(3) develop applications by prototyping,

(4) use less conventional programming,

(5) use more decision modeling, database management, and expert system shell,

(6) use less individual files and more shared data bases,

(7) put forth more effort in system development,

(8) spend more development effort on system design, yet, less on programming and debugging,

(9) use less off-line batch and more on-line interactive processes.

In addition to developing the IS profile, this study reconfirms the finding that IS expenditure is not at all indicative of an organization's level of IS sophistication. The majority of the companies have their IS expenditures less than the rate of sales growth regardless of their size and IS sophistication. It also reveals that the levels of Nolan's benchmarks (technology, application portfolio, IS organization, IS planning and control, and user awareness) are significantly related to the composite level of IS sophistication at the 0.001 level. However, such relationships do not imply that each individual benchmark is always indicative of “the overall status of an organization’s IS within its growth process”, or vice versa. One must examine all of them to determine the level of IS sophistication.

It is clear that the IS profile of U.S. companies will change over time. However, the changes should have little to do with the size of the company but more with the level of IS sophistication. There is little doubt that, in the future, companies of all sizes will have access to high-powered computing, as microcomputers and minicomputers become nearly as powerful as mainframes. There has, of course, been debate on the advantages of microcomputers over mainframes; however, companies of all sizes will probably increase their use of microcomputers while maintaining a mainframe or mini. As ISs evolve, future profiles will probably have increased software usage in the areas of decision modeling and statistical analysis. Data processing approaches will probably show an increase in on-line interactive usage of shared data bases. IS expenditures will remain steady, with an increase in the percentage allocated to telecommunications. As technology advances, more system effort will be diverted to development from operations, with more development effort taking place in planning, analysis, and design.

The IS profile may play an important role in an organization's strategic and tactical IS planning process. By developing a company's IS profile, one is virtually performing an IS audit, which gives the company an indication of its strengths (or weaknesses) for competitiveness. The company will have an understanding of the necessary improvements that should be made to its IS profile in order to gain or maintain its competitive edge. The length of time of such an improvement may take several months or even years, making the adjustment of a company's IS profile a medium-term to long-term issue. Therefore, the IS profile is important to a company in formulating its IS plans. Given today's competitive global market, more and more companies in all areas of business have begun to use information technology as a competitive weapon by linking ISs with business plans $[1,10,19,20]$ . It seems that any company which wants to stay competitive will have to become more sophisticated in its use of information technology $[15]$ . The IS profile of a company may be a useful planning tool for that company in this regard.
