# Managing new MIS professionals

Ephraim R. McLean and Stanley J. Smits
Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

John R. Tanner

University of Southwestern Louisiana, Lafayette, LA 70504, USA

Job search and career planning in the MIS field have received relatively little attention among academic researchers; and yet they are of significant concern to practitioners who are faced with the on-going task of attracting, retaining, and motivating their MIS professional staff. This paper presents the results of a recent study of university graduates who are about to enter the MIS field and combines these findings with work that has been done in other disciplines to lead to some recommendations for managers who are responsible for recruiting and managing new MIS professionals.

Keywords: Career paths, Career planning, Employee retention, Job search, Personnel, Realistic job previews, Recruitment, Turnover.

## 1. Introduction

The transformation from an industrial to an information society has created a seemingly insatiable demand for workers with knowledge and skills in the field of management information systems (MIS). In turn, large numbers of college students have chosen this career option, thereby creating a demand for additional curriculum offerings in MIS. The rapid growth of MIS as a discipline and the lack of longitudinal career research prompted us to initiate an MIS Career Patterns Study in 1986. The first phase of the study has produced some information that may help managers reduce the amount of employment turnover typical of MIS personnel during the early years of their careers.

MIS students graduate from college and enter employment in the information systems field with a reasonably stable but highly idealized set of preferences and expectations. On the positive side, they have a strong desire to contribute to the success of their employing organizations and the apparent work traits needed to ensure success. Why then is industry experiencing such high turnover rates among entry-level MIS personnel? Why do they have a lack of commitment to the organizations that employ them?

With the exception of the work of Couger and Zawacki [3,4,19] and a few others, this human dimension of MIS has received only modest attention from researchers. However, there is a large body of potentially useful related research from other disciplines. In this paper, we will bring together Solberg's Job Search and Choice Model [12,15], the use of realistic job previews [2,10,18], research regarding commitment and turnover [8,9], and our own recent research findings [14] to provide some practical guidelines to those responsible for managing new MIS professionals as they enter the work force.

## 2. The model

To assist in the understanding of the process by which a new employee becomes committed to an organization – or, conversely, is dissatisfied and leaves – we propose a model of the major steps in this process. It is shown graphically in Figure 1. A key first step in this model is a recognition of the central role played by job expectations. Mowday, Porter, and Steers summarized the importance of employee expectations as follows: “Several studies have found that employees who enter organizations with more realistic expectations are less likely to leave voluntarily than employees whose expectations are unrealistic.... Several studies have found a positive relationship between commitment and the extent to which employees who had been on the job for some time believed their expectations had been realized.” [9]

![](/api/attachments/A72XHNAE/fulltext/images/321ad240a4aca1d21b6a0079ddbbec97deb3dad65fb4d9fdc0826e5ad3fe7616.jpg)

Solberg's pioneering Job Search and Choice Model [12,15], contains four phases: Identifying an Ideal Occupation, Planning the Job Search, the Job Search and Choice, and the Decision Confirmation and Commitment. In the first phase, people organize their preferences to the point that they are able to identify an ideal occupation. They subsequently attempt to achieve this ideal by engaging in job search activity to find a position that fits their preferences. As the job search and choice activity progresses, job seekers often modify their aspirations and criteria. Therefore, one of the tasks for MIS majors involved in making the transition from academic life to the world of work is to modify and stabilize their preferences to the point that they can make a specific job choice from among the many alternatives available. When this occurs, the last phase of decision confirmation and commitment can be realized.

Given the number of alternatives, it seems doubtful that many MIS majors make an extensive comparison of job opportunities prior to making a job choice. Perhaps the decision is made more on the basis of the exhaustion of their time, energy, and resources than on having optimized their career search. Or, as Zawacki et al. [19] suggest, they may be “pressured into their career choices by the wishes of significant others in their lives.” Couple these reasons for a decision with the lack of explicit MIS career tracks in many organizations [6] and it seems reasonable to assume that, for many MIS majors, their initial job choices are very tenuous and subject to change. The resulting iterative job search and choice process, which appears typical of many early MIS professionals, is shown in Figure 2.

Perhaps the high turnover rates among new MIS professionals reported in the literature $[1,5,7]$ result, in part, from repeated attempts to find

According to this model, the challenge facing managers of entry-level MIS personnel is clear. They must preserve the positive motivational forces in the new employee's idealized aspirations, expectations, and self-perceptions, while simultaneously introducing enough reality so that these expectations have a reasonable chance of being met. If they are able to do so, the result is a motivated, committed employee with a reduced probability of turnover.

## 3. The study

## 3.1. Data collection

In February 1987, twenty-two colleges and universities were invited to participate in an extensive field test of a questionnaire designed to create a national database for longitudinal research dealing with career patterns in MIS. Sixteen institutions (73%) agreed to participate. Students who were in MIS and within six months of graduation were asked to volunteer to complete a three-page questionnaire. Responses were received from 261 MIS majors at the bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degree levels.

The questionnaire had been pilot tested and revised prior to distribution. It requested information about the students' personal characteristics and family backgrounds, their immediate and long-range MIS career objectives, their certainty regarding MIS as a career choice, their academic and vocational histories, and their progress to date in finding MIS employment. Also, they were asked about the importance to them of various MIS job characteristics (e.g., job security, opportunity for promotion, performance feedback, etc.) and about the work-related personal characteristics that they saw themselves bringing to the job (e.g., industriousness, adaptability, punctuality, etc.).

The 261 completed questionnaires were then analyzed using the SPSS-X statistical package. Procedures used included t tests, ANOVA, and factor analysis.

## 3.2. Results

One of the key results was the analysis of the responses to the Job Preference Inventory (JPI) questions. Using factor analysis, the top rated 15 JPI items produced three factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, i.e., 5.24, 1.39, and 1.31. The variables and loadings for each factor, and the labels assigned to each factor, are shown in Table 1. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for JPI factors 1, 2, and 3 were 0.73, 0.80, and 0.60 respectively, and 0.84 for the total 15 highest rated items. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that the responses to the JPI items are relatively reliable and consistent.

The respondents were grouped for selected demographic, academic, vocational, and programmatic characteristics (independent variables); and their JPI scores (dependent variables) were analyzed for statistical significance. None of the JPI score differences was significant at the 0.05 level of confidence, thereby suggesting a remarkable consistency regarding the attributes of their preferred MIS positions.

## 3.3. Discussion

An examination of these factors suggest that MIS graduates enter the world of work with a strong preference for jobs where they are treated and evaluated fairly; have state-of-the-art equipment, promotional opportunities, and competitive benefits; and where the work itself provides a sense of accomplishment, task variety, and challenge. This last finding is particularly important for it is an aspect of MIS work that is not always found in other fields of endeavor. It also confirms the recent work of Couger, who states: “the number one motivating factor for IS personnel is the work itself [emphases added].”

He goes on to say that “in 1978, our research revealed that analysts and programmers had a low need for social interaction.... In fact, ...their social needs were lower than any of the 500 occupations measured by [Richard] Hackman and [Greg] Oldham.... My continuing research on other job types within the computer field shows the social need to be equally low for all professional computer job types.”

Somewhat surprisingly, this finding was not confirmed by our data. The respondents in our study reported a strong preference for jobs capable of providing them with opportunities to develop professional friendships and to have close contact with users. In addition, these new MIS professionals see themselves as having a number of desirable personal characteristics and work traits, such as strong performance orientation, confidence, punctuality, and industriousness. Therefore, in most cases they have achieved the first phase in Solberg's Job Search and Choice Model, i.e., they have organized their preferences sufficiently to be able to identify an ideal occupation.

## 4. Implications for management

Drawing on these findings and the Job Search and Choice Model, there are at least four ways in which management can make the initial experience of entry-level MIS professionals a more positive one and thereby increase the chances of job retention. These are to provide realistic job previews, to engage in comprehensive career planning, to promote interpersonal bonding, and to engender organizational commitment.

## 4.1. Realistic job previews

Personnel recruiters in fields where the number of open positions far exceeds the supply of applicants often exaggerate the opportunities and benefits available in order to induce new graduates to accept positions in their organizations. When this is done, it only adds to an already unrealistic set of expectations on the part of the MIS job applicant; and the disappointment which follows is predictable. In the long run, it is far better to provide prospective employees with accurate and realistic information about the job and their opportunities for advancement. This may result in the need to invest more heavily in other parts of the recruitment effort or in the loss of some candidates, but it greatly increases the likelihood of being able to meet the new MIS employees expectations once they are on the job.

The literature on personnel management $[2,10,18]$ stresses the importance of providing these realistic job previews (i.e., accurate and complete information). This becomes even more critical in a field like MIS where the idealism and job expectations of the recent graduates are already quite high, and overselling of a job can only make matters worse.

Some may argue that providing an accurate picture of the job will merely drive applicants away. However, research has found this to be only partially true. Premack and Wanous [13] concluded from a meta-analysis of previous realistic job preview (RJP) experiments that “RJPs tend to lower initial job expectations, while increasing self-selection, organizational commitment, job satisfaction, performance, and job survival.” Suszko and Breaugh [16] conducted a field experiment using RJPs with applicants randomly assigned to the experimental group. While the RJP recipients were more likely to turn down the job offer, the retention rate for those who did accept employment was substantially higher, leading to a net gain of 31 percent.

## 4.2. Career planning

An important way to shape expectations and increase motivation for entering MIS professionals is to develop a career plan. The steps built into the plan become the expected work accomplishments and self-development activities at each stage. However, the career plan should be flexible enough to provide alternative paths at various stages of development in order to accommodate changing individual preferences and the dynamic nature of the MIS field. Periodic manager-subordinate reviews of the plan provide a basis for assessing progress to date, making decisions regarding alternative career paths, and for setting mutually agreed upon objectives for the future. Career planning sets a baseline for growth-oriented performance appraisals.

Unfortunately, it appears that careful career planning for MIS personnel is not very common in practice [6]. Perhaps this is because managers of MIS personnel do not feel comfortable with the career planning, counseling, and coaching role. If this is the case, managers can work with the organization's human resource development specialists to develop career plans for their subordinates and to familiarize themselves with the processes involved. In addition, the manager could consult some of the excellent career models available in the literature [17]. By whatever means it is accomplished, there should be the development of clear but flexible career plans for entering MIS personnel; and these plans should be used to shape expectations, set immediate and long-range objectives, and reinforce job motivation and organizational commitment.

## 4.3. Interpersonal bonding

Our research suggests that today's entry-level MIS professional has much stronger social needs than previous research has indicated. If true, this is quite fortunate for two reasons. First, these new professionals will fit well with the trend toward greater involvement by MIS personnel with other organizational units, often through serving on multidisciplinary project teams. In fact, the whole area of end-user computing demands such close personal interaction. Secondly, it provides a potent mechanism for building organizational commitment on the part of these new MIS employees.

If bonding is to take place between a person and the employing organization, it is commonly based on interpersonal relationships. Managers can facilitate such bonding by:

\- Engaging in open, frequent communication with subordinates;

\- Using team assignments and team rewards;

\- Engaging in group supervision and team-building;

\- Encouraging work-related social activities; and/or

\- Encouraging involvement in professional and trade association groups.

Motivational theorists have long maintained that people have a need to belong that must be satisfied before people pursue higher order needs. Therefore, once new MIS professionals begin feeling a sense of belonging to the organization of which they are a part, the likelihood of turnover will decrease dramatically.

## 4.4. Organizational commitment

Mowday, Porter, and Steers contend that organizational commitment is characterized by “(a) a strong belief in and acceptance of the organization’s goals and values; (b) a willingness to exert considerable effort on behalf of the organization; and (c) a strong desire to maintain membership in the organization.” [7] The process of building organizational commitment, therefore, involves integrating the goals of the individual with those of the organization. Theoretically, complete congruence between the goals of the individual and those of the organization are served simultaneously. However, commitment is quite different from compliance.

The first few months of employment are crucial to the development of commitment. This is the time that the newly employed individual attempts to verify the degree to which the job meets his. or her pre-entry expectations and preferences. If the initial commitment is high, and if the initial work experience creates a feeling of responsibility, there is an increased likelihood that lasting commitment will evolve. Mowday et al. point out that “the likelihood of developing a self-reinforcing cycle of commitment...is largely dependent on the opportunity to engage in behaviors that are committing.... Such task characteristics as autonomy, challenge, and significance may increase the behavioral involvement of employees in their job and thus increase their felt responsibility.... Felt responsibility generally increase...when...employees depend upon each other in the performance of their jobs.”

To accomplish this, the new MIS professional should be given meaningful team assignments where task interdependence is a central component. As he or she matures on the job, organizational commitment can be further enhanced by increased two-way sharing of goals and objectives, by shared visions of the future, and by increased participation in decision making.

## 5. Conclusion

New MIS professionals enter the workplace with high expectations. To retain these new employees, managers must respond in a manner that maintains their enthusiasm and, at the same time, focuses their energies on the attainment of organizational goals.

First, they need to be realistic in the manner in which jobs are presented to potential employees in the first place. Exaggerated job descriptions can only lead to disappointment and eventual turnover. Next, career planning plays an important role in guiding and reinforcing the activities of the young professional. Not only are career paths delineated, but objectives are also defined, leading to a clearer understanding on the part of the employee of what is expected of him or her. Finally, through a variety of interpersonal activities, such as team participation and involvement in group efforts, organizational commitment is established and strengthened. And, in all of this, it is important to remember the satisfaction and sense of accomplishment that is an intrinsic part of much of the work in the MIS field. Care should be taken not to do anything that would detract from this powerful motivating force. If these activities can be successfully pursued, it should result in a long-term, mutually beneficial association between the individual and the organization.
