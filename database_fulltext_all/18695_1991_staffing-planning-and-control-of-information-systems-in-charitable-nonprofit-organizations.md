# Staffing, planning, and control of information systems in charitable nonprofit organizations

James B. Pick \*

Graduate School of Management, University of California, Riverside, CA 92521, USA

Staffing, planning, and management control of information systems in the private not-for-profit (or nonprofit) sector are studied, based on a survey of symphony organizations in the United States and Canada. A conceptual model of information systems for charitable nonprofits is presented which emphasizes staffing, planning, and control. Empirical results for symphony orchestras reveal low IS funding, weaknesses in IS staffing, heavy dependence on outside expertise, low levels of IS training, inadequate IS planning and control, and moderate IS satisfaction. The model is tested empirically and found to be largely valid. A model modification is suggested in assessing the factor of IS satisfaction.

Keywords: IS planning, IS control, IS staffing, nonprofit organizations, IS problems, IS theory, Computer training, symphony orchestras.

## Introduction

This study analyzes computer-based information systems in charitable nonprofit organizations, in particular examining symphony orchestras. A nonprofit is defined as an organization that prohibits the distribution of profit to owners or managers. Profit may be earned, but is re-allocated back into the organization, rather than benefitting the organization's ownership and management. One important result of nonprofit status is that financial rewards are separated from organizational performance [33]. A charitable nonprofit is defined as a nonprofit organization engaged in arts, religious, scientific, philanthropic, or educational activities. A charitable nonprofit is distinguished from a mutual benefit nonprofit, such as a labor union, social club, or business association [33].

Nonprofit organizations constitute a major portion of the U.S. economy. For instance, in 1985, there were 887,000 nonprofits in the United States, of which 336,000 were fully tax-exempt [33]. Nonprofits contributed 4.4 percent of national income in 1985 and accounted for 5 to 8 percent of the national labor force, as well as another 5 percent of the labor force if volunteer workers are included [33]. Yet, in spite of nonprofits' economic contribution, relatively few academic studies have explored information systems in these organizations.

There are several justifications for the present study. First, since preliminary studies have tentatively indicated that charitable nonprofits differ in their information systems from private companies $[27,32]$ , it is important to systematically study these organizations in more depth; i.e. by means of a nationwide survey study, in order to validate unusual and distinctive features identified in pilot studies. To achieve this objective, symphony orchestras (also referred to in this paper as symphonies) are selected as a representative type of charitable nonprofit, since a systematic study across all types would be too large a project to undertake. Second, a systematic national survey of symphonies' ISs serves to establish a baseline of empirical results, which, in the future, can be analyzed and compared longitudinally for growth and change trends. Third, the study is justified because it can motivate the development of aspects of MIS theory to encompass these types of organizations.

The present study has the following specific goals: to present a theoretical model of MIS in charitable nonprofits; to analyze empirically the staffing, planning, and management control aspects of information systems in symphonies as an example of charitable nonprofits; to compare the analytical results with prior literature to determine what is different about IS staffing, planning and management control in the nonprofit versus the for-profit sectors; and to test the theoretical model and modify it, if necessary, based on the empirical findings. The final goal is to examine the implications of these results for MIS research and theory, as well as the practical needs of symphony managers.

Because of the lack of academic studies of nonprofits' IS, most relevant prior empirical research has concerned information systems in small for-profit firms [10,11,25,30,31]. That research emphasized determinants of success for IS in small business. For instance, Raymond [31] queried controllers in 464 Canadian businesses with an average size of 80 employees. He used as a measure of IS success factor loadings extracted from 13 user satisfaction questions. Determinants that increased success were (1) in-house IS operations (versus reliance on outside service bureaus), (2) on-line versus batch applications, (3) large number of administrative applications, (4) high organizational level for IS, and, to a lesser extent, (5) development of applications inside the firm, and (6) less EDP experience in the firm. Using different methodology, Delone [11] conducted a questionnaire study of IS success in 93 manufacturing firms of less than \$30 million in sales and less than 300 employees in Los Angeles. He measured success by the frequency of, and time spent utilizing computer reports, and a computer application success score. Results demonstrated the important determinants of IS success to be top management knowledge of and involvement in computerization, in-house computer use, and, to a lesser extent, level of IS planning and control. These studies agree on the importance of a high level location of IS and of in-house computer use for IS success.

## A theoretical model for MIS in charitable non-profit organizations

This section presents a conceptual model for information systems in charitable nonprofits, focusing on IS staffing, planning, and control. In the next section, the survey study's descriptive results are given, followed by a section which tests the model empirically and modifies it in view of the empirical results. The tests compare the levels of model factors, and evaluate the strength and direction of hypothesized associations between factors.

The theoretical discussion concentrates on charitable nonprofits, although some comparisons are made to profit-making organizations. Several past theoretical models of HIS are relevant to the present study, but none includes the distinction of nonprofit versus for-profit statuses. These include stage theory $[15,26]$ , which outlines the evolutionary development of IS in organizations, and Leifer's model $[22]$ , which presents a typology that matches ISs with appropriate organizational structures. Both of these MIS models have limited application to the present study. Stage theory has the problems of being a descriptive, evolutionist model, which does not include pathways and mechanisms of change $[19]$ . Although it incorporates the factors of IS staffing and IS control, it lacks applicability to the nonprofit sector, and, in fact, was developed mainly based on observations of the business world [15,26]. Leifer's model consists of a typology, which outlines appropriate fits between computer resource types and organizational structures. Although as an MIS model it is especially sensitive to organizational differences, it devotes little attention to IS staffing, planning, and control. It looks at generic organizations, and does not give specific attention to, nor refer to examples of nonprofits. Because of the lack of attention of these and other MIS models to the charitable nonprofit sector, a theoretical model is introduced here that concentrates on charitable nonprofits. The model draws some ideas from a pilot interview study of five symphonies [27]. $^{1}$

The model emphasizes the following seven major factors:

1. IS funding intensity. This variable is the percent of the organization' budget allocated for IS purposes. IS funding intensity reveals the budgetary emphasis that top management places on IS. It reflects the importance of IS to the organization as a whole.

2. Computer hardware and software power and complexity. This factor is the power, complexity, and sophistication of the institution's computer hardware and software.

3. Computer staffing. This variable reflects the user support capabilities and technical competence of computer staff. Staff is taken broadly to include professional IS personnel, salaried non-professional IS personnel, outside consultants, vendor support personnel, and volunteers. Hence, persons giving technical support and assistance in IS (termed TSAs) are assumed to reside only in part with formally trained and designated IS professionals.

4. IS training. This item is the extent of IS training provided by the organization to its employees. It may consist of in-house or outside training.

5. IS planning. This variable is the organization's short term and long term IS planning, as well as its computer capacity planning.

6. IS control. This item consists of management controls that are applied to information systems. It includes systems and programming controls such as documentation and backup, organizational controls such as a steering committee and policies to control the proliferation of pcs, and financial controls such as IS cost/benefit analysis.

7. Organizational satisfaction with IS. This factor constitutes the organization's satisfaction with the uses and benefits of its information systems. IS satisfaction may be measured by subjective satisfaction of management and users, or by objective measures such as computer utilization.

These factors constitute only a part of the information system $[2,9]$ . They represent a simplified model, which is appropriate to examining and understanding information systems in charitable nonprofits. The model emphasizes IS funding, staffing, planning, and control, which are important in all organizations but especially charitable nonprofits $[27,28]$ . The model postulates major differences between organizations in the charitable nonprofit versus profit-making sectors. Controlling for organizational size, nonprofits are assumed to have reduced IS funding intensity, less powerful hardware and software, and reduced levels of IS staffing, training, planning, control, and satisfaction. MIS in nonprofits, compared to equivalently sized for-profits, is assumed to be more informal, unstructured, and uncontrolled. MIS is assumed to be perceived as a less important organizational success factor and to receive fewer resources.

With the exception of IS funding intensity, these factors are postulated to vary along a continuum by organizational size, $^{2}$ with reduced IS capabilities, control, and satisfaction for smaller organizations (see Table 1). It is important to note that the largest of business organizations surpass in size the largest charitable nonprofits. For example, the largest symphony in the present sample had an annual budget in the fiscal year prior to 1987 of \$50 million, compared to the budget of the largest Fortune 500 business in 1986 of \$102.8 billion [14].

IS funding intensity is assumed to be generally more constrained in charitable nonprofits than in profit-making enterprises, and not to vary with organizational size. One possible reason for nonprofits' reduced funding intensity is the presumed lower levels of knowledge and appreciation of computing in their top managements, leading to reduced budgetary emphasis on IS. Another reason is that charitable nonprofits have a multiplicity of goals $[24]$ , which may lessen the budgetary focus on IS. The reasons do not appear to depend on the size of nonprofit organizations.

The model postulates that computer hardware in charitable nonprofits is generally micro-based, with larger nonprofits having more hard-disk and network oriented hardware. For-profit firms are hypothesized to have generally more sophisticated and powerful hardware than nonprofits, controlling for organizational size. Likewise, the model assumes that equivalently sized for-profits are more advanced than nonprofits in computing software.

The model postulates nonprofits to be weak in IS planning and control. This is plausible, since their personnel lack IS training and experience. Hence, their managers have less appreciation of, and place less emphasis on IS planning and control. For-profits, with more computer knowledgeable managers and better trained computing staff, have higher levels of IS planning and control.

In the area of IS staffing, charitable nonprofits are hypothesized to depend less on salaried, full-time professionals in IS and more on salaried, non-IS-professional employees who are not formally designated as IS employees (termed “non-IS staff”). For larger nonprofits, some in-house professional IS staff is present. By contrast, businesses are hypothesized to have generally greater professionalization in IS personnel. This is due to

The reduced IS funding, less expert IS staffing, and reduced levels of IS planning and control in charitable nonprofits is postulated to lead to lower IS satisfaction than in for-profits. In summary, the model's seven factors are hypothesized to vary by sector as well as along size continua, with the for-profit sector and larger organizations having more advanced computer technology, greater IS planning and control, more professionalized IS staffing, and higher levels of satisfaction with computing. IS funding intensity is hypothesized to vary by sector, but not by organizational size.

A more detailed view of the model of information systems in charitable nonprofits shows the linkages among the seven factors (see Figure 1). The model is fundamentally based on the reduced funding intensity for IS. Low funding intensity results from the budgetary decision-making process, in which IS receives relatively low priority. Top management perceives that goals other than IS are more important, such as fundraising, providing institutional services, physical operations, etc. Given the strong financial pressures that face charitable nonprofits, IS ends up with low levels of funding.

Reduced IS funding intensity is hypothesized to lead to acquisition of relatively inexpensive and “simple” hardware and software. Another assumed effect is increased management proclivity to assign non-IS staff to perform computing tasks. This re-direction of some non-IS staff is a cost-saving solution, since already present non-IS staff can be relatively easily and economically re-assigned into new computing job roles, while the hiring of outside IS professionals is more costly and difficult. In the model, IS funding constraints also lead to lack of IS training, since most computer training is expensive for nonprofits.

The non-professional IS staffing and reduced IS training levels lead in turn to lower levels of IS planning and control. This is because effective IS planning and control require appropriate education and experience. For instance, effective computer capacity planning requires specific education and training in capacity planning techniques. The deficits in training, planning, and control lead to IS operational or systems development problems, which in turn reduce organizational satisfaction with IS.

To summarize the model of IS in charitable nonprofits, constraints on IS funding leads to an understaffed and undercontrolled, albeit technologically-simpler IS environment. Lack of staff and IS control cause more IS problems to occur, reducing organizational satisfaction. An example is a very small symphony which has a tiny IS budget and hence cannot afford IS staff or training. Consequently, there is poor IS planning and control, lowering organizational satisfaction with IS.

## The 1987 national symphony survey

The 1987 National Symphony Survey examined patterns of symphony computer utilization based on respondents from the full population of symphonies located in the United States and Canada. The survey covered the areas of information systems equipment, software applications, organization of IS, systems analysis and design, and IS staffing, management, and control [28]. This section describes the survey findings, focusing on the latter three areas.

This survey was more comprehensive than most previous surveys on computer use in nonprofits. For instance, the National Surveys of Computer Use in 1985 and 1986, performed by the American Symphony Orchestra League (ASOL), were restricted mainly to the areas of computer equipment and software applications [3,4]. $^{3}$ An Arthur Young survey in 1983 of information systems in art museums emphasized IS management, budgets, hardware, and software applications in 54 middle-sized museums [34]. However, its limited scope, small sample size, and practitioner emphasis restricts its usefulness in an academic context.

The present survey is based on a 37 item questionnaire, mailed to the entire ASOL list of 854 U.S. and Canadian symphonies in June, 1987. Questions were developed based on the results of a prior interview survey [27] and MIS research studies of the private sector [17,20,21,30,31], as well as on theoretical considerations. Earlier versions of the questionnaire were field-tested on several symphonies of different sizes. The final questionnaire was mailed to the symphony executive manager or director, to be forwarded to the person in charge of computing. Responses were mostly returned within one month, although a few arrived after three months. A total of 209 usable questionnaire forms were returned, yielding a response rate of 24.5 percent. If a returned questionnaire had a high overall proportion of missing responses, the case was rejected from the analysis. Each respondent was categorized as a computer user, non-user, or undetermined, based on questionnaire items on the presence of computers and computer funding. ## Descriptive results

The descriptive findings are presented in some detail, because the basic features of information systems in charitable nonprofits are little known on a nationwide basis. This section is divided into four parts: general features, staffing, planning, and control. Results refer to the user/accepted sample, unless otherwise noted.

## General features

Symphonies showed large variation in size, and were funded from a variety of non-governmental sources. The organizations' median annual budget was \$200,000 for computer users, compared to \$54,075 for non-users. Symphony budgets in the user sample ranged from \$1,500 to \$25,000,000, with twenty orchestras over \$2 million. The budgets were 93 percent funded from nongovernmental sources. The sample was further divided into three size groups, based on annual budget size. These were: small size (\$0–194,999), medium size (\$195,000–749,999), and large size (more than \$750,000). The three groups had 62, 33, and 32 cases respectively. The mean budget sizes for the groups were \$79,775, \$552,380, and \$5,212,270. In crosstabulations discussed later, the medium and large size groups are aggregated together to achieve sufficient sample size and called the medium/large size group.

Computer budgets consisted of salaries for in-house computer personnel, computer hardware, software, supplies, consulting services, and service bureaus. The mean computer budget was \$21,739, with a median of \$900 (N = 119). The ratio of computer budget to organizational budget, referred to as computer funding intensity, had a mean value of 1.1 percent, with a median of 0.32 percent. This is considerably less than the average level in businesses. For instance, one study estimated computer funding intensity in 1988, averaged for large businesses in twelve major business sectors, at 2.3 percent [14]. The symphonies' lower intensity reflects the lack of "profits" and the multiple goals of charitable nonprofits.

The foremost funding source for computing budgets was operating funds, followed by individual and corporate/foundation funds, with government supplying only 4 percent of funding. The lack of governmental funding is surprising given the fragile financial status of many orchestras [23].

On the whole, the micro was the mainstay of computing in symphonies. Based on the full user sample, there was an average of 2.2 micros, 0.29 word processors, and 0.14 minis per symphony. Micros were about equally split between diskette-based and hard-disk-based. A symphony's total number of computers was strongly associated with its budget size. A low number of machines (0 or 1) was more typical of small-budget orchestras, while 5 or more machines characterized thirteen very-large-budget orchestras. Minis were characteristic of large symphonies. At the high end of computer use, nine larger orchestras had three or more hard disk micros; three very large orchestras had 2 or more minis, while none had mainframes.

Software tended to be “off the shelf” and micro-based. Results showed that software was commercial packages (60 percent averaged across applications) or modified commercial packages (29 percent), rather than programs developed in-house (11 percent). The five most important software applications, in order of prevalence, were word processing, mailing lists and maintenance, contribution processing, contribution history, and payroll and tax, all present for more than seventy percent of cases. These applications tended to be micro-based and relatively simple.

Outside personnel include computer consultants, contract programmers, vendor personnel, and non-IS volunteers.

Non-IS employees who perform computer tasks were about twice as numerous as IS employees. A mean of 3.2 non-IS employees spent 25–75 percent of their time on computer tasks, versus a mean of 0.9 for such employees spending 75 percent or more of their time on computer tasks. The importance of non-IS staff has not been noted in private sector studies of small business. It is likely that this effect is due to the high relative expense of IS personnel in the charitable nonprofit versus the private sector; to the presence of volunteers, nearly all of whom are non-IS, performing IS tasks; and to the increasing level of computer skills in society as a whole, which makes is easier for management to redirect an existing staff member's work into computing tasks.

For the thirty symphonies that paid any salary to in-house IS staff (including part-timers), the mean IS staff payroll amount paid was \$22,569, with a range from \$1,000 to \$160,000. Table 6 compares the salaries for full-time staff of symphonies with comparable salaries from the private sector taken from the 1987 Datamation data processing salary survey [7]. Symphony salaries were, on the average, 64 percent those of corporations. It is likely that salary discrepancies are also present for other technical/professional employees in symphonies, such as accountants. In computing, the lack of professional background may lie at the root of problems in systems documentation, planning, and control. Comparing symphony categories with Datamation's category of “education”, symphony salaries still compare poorly, averaging 68 percent of those for educational nonprofit institutions. Hence, symphonies offer poor IS salaries, even within the charitable non-profit sector.

Based on the results of the pilot survey and a study by Lee [21], the present survey was designed to gather detailed information on two special personnel types. The first is called technical support and assistance person (TSA). A TSA is defined as a person who gives substantial technical support and assistance to people on the symphony staff regarding development and operation of the symphony's computer systems. A TSA may be on or off-staff, and a symphony may have more than one TSA. The other personnel type is the lead technical support and assistance person (LTSA), defined as the TSA who best fulfills the role of technical support and assistance. These categories were included in order to differentiate the key computer employees or consultants from more ordinary ones. An LTSA closely resembles the "lead user" concept of Lee. Lee studied PC usage patterns in twelve organizations, eleven of them medium or large sized companies. He defined lead user as a PC user who serves as an informal consultant as identified by at least two coworkers. He found that lead users spent more time on PCs for a greater variety of applications than ordinary users, that lead users were more likely to be college graduates, and that lead users were crucial in the organizational diffusion of PC usage.

As seen in Table 7, TSAs are present for sixty one percent of symphonies using computers. There was an average of 2.0 TSAs per symphony, with about a third on the staff and two thirds outside the staff. By contrast, all of Lee's lead users were on-staff. For outside TSAs, the most prevalent categories of TSA were consultant and "other", followed by outside vendor. These results reveal a very large dependency on outside employees as key IS personnel, especially in light of their potential lack of time availability and difficulty in carrying out security/control responsibilities. TSAs on the staff were about evenly divided between IS and non-IS. Thus half of the key on-staff computer personnel lack the professionalism associated with the formal IS designation. Thirty one percent of organizations reporting TSAs had a single TSA, 28 percent had two TSAs, and 18 percent had three or more. Multiple TSAs were characteristic of larger symphonies. For example, 73 percent of large symphonies had 2 or more TSAs, versus only 35 percent for small symphonies.

For example, LTSA's age and education are significantly associated in crosstabulation, while education and computer experience are also strongly associated. About half of symphonies had instituted computer training of employees. However, training was more prevalent in large-sized symphonies (68 percent had instituted training) and medium-sized ones (62 percent), compared to small sized ones (26 percent). This is not surprising since small orchestras' computer training is constrained by very small IS budgets, lack of interest from vendors, and a lack of IS staff to offer training in-house. For the group of small symphonies, the average annual IS budget, including IS salaries, hardware, software, supplies, consulting, and service bureau fees, was only \$1,421. By comparison, for large symphonies, the mean annual IS budget was \$91,363. In addition, small symphonies were handicapped in offering training given by their own IS staff, since they have, on average, only 0.05 full-time in-house IS staff and only 0.12 outside IS consultants.

Employees were asked to rank six well-known computer training methods from 1 (prefer most) to 6 (prefer least). Employee-supervised training (mean rank score of 1.6) leads in popularity, probably due to its low cost and ease in organizing and conducting. It was followed by consultant supervised training (2.1), vendor-supervised training (2.3), college courses (3.0), video training (4.4), and conventions or meetings (4.5). However, except for the relatively few large symphonies having paid and professional IS staff, employee-supervised training offers limited potential for updating IS knowledge to state-of-the-art levels, since the employees giving the training are themselves in need of training, relative to accepted levels in the business world [32].

## IS planning

Symphonies lacked computer planning. Although most symphonies had a written general plan, only nine percent indicated presence of a written computer plan. By comparison, small manufacturing firms in Los Angeles had substantially more IS planning [11]. $^{5}$ A large differential in IS planning was apparent by symphony size. The prevalence of computer planning ranged from 2 percent for small symphonies to 12 percent for medium ones to 19 percent for large ones. For smaller symphonies, reduced IS budgets and staff discourage computer planning. However, even for large symphonies, four out of five lacked formal IS planning.

Computer capacity planning was present for 55 percent of respondents. $^{6}$ As shown in Table 8, expert opinion and simple trending were the most common methods of capacity planning. Use of these simple methods is understandable in the present study, since they require neither formal training nor software, other than a spreadsheet at the most. By contrast, capacity planning methods were much more advanced in a computer capacity planning study of Fortune 1000 firms in 1985 – 89 percent of respondents utilized visual trending, 79 percent employed user projections (a type of expert opinion), and 58 percent utilized time series regression [20]. For symphonies, the relatively low level of capacity planning is unfortunate, since it is essential in order to cope with the rapid performance and generational changes in micros, which typically constitute symphony hardware.

The low levels of computer planning appear plausible. First, there is lack of training in computer planning methodologies. Second, work pressures among orchestra computer staff may cause computer planning to be expendable. Third, the multiple goals of nonprofit organizations $[24]$ may increase the difficulty in all planning, including computer planning. ## IS control

Survey results reveal a general lack of management control of information systems, and, at the same time, a high level of concern for problems of lack of control. This was true regardless of size of symphony. In every respect, the smaller the symphony, the more inadequate the controls and the weaker the concern about them. As seen in Table 9, only 12 percent had an IS steering committee and only a quarter of respondents performed cost/benefit analysis for their last computer acquisition. Likewise, the average extent of documentation for systems design and programs was rated as very slight. Respondents rating themselves on their management control of the successful implementation of micro and mini software indicated an average level of “some” control. The low overall extent of management control of IS contrasts with results for small manufacturing firms, which revealed strong IS controls $^{7}$ [11].

Concern was expressed regarding many areas of IS control. This was especially strong for documentation, back-up, and disaster planning. For instance, the average level of dissatisfaction with documentation was rated “somewhat”. On a scale of 1 (major concern) to 5 (no concern), concern for regular backup was rated 2.1 (N = 121) while concern for disaster planning was rated 2.7 (N = 118) and physical security was rated 3.3 (N = 119).

In all instances, there was much more concern in large versus small orchestras. The high level of concern is an appropriate response to the real dangers posed by lack of IS control. The greater concern about control in large versus small symphonies stems from their greater overall dependence on IS and from the enhanced ability of their more IS-knowledgeable staff to perceive current inadequacies.

## Discussion of empirical findings

Several findings in the current study require special emphasis. First, the present study points to inadequate funding for symphony computing. Not only are salaries at two thirds of the comparable business-world levels, but median amounts spent on hardware of \$2,812 (N = 58), on software of \$625 (N = 49), and on supplies of \$260 (N = 83) are quite low relative to the median organizational budget of \$200,000 (N = 126). The low amount of IS spending is certainly a factor provoking management's concern about lack of IS funding. It is likely that computer funding is not given a high enough priority in symphony budget proposals – the result perhaps of a low level of knowledge of computing's tangible and intangible benefits by general management and by the budgetary approval entity, which is generally a board of directors. Also, symphonies may not be adequately tapping potential funding sources. For example, the 12 percent portion of IS funding from foundation and corporate grants could in many cases be increased if symphonies were to become aware of all possible funding sources.

## A test of the model of IS for charitable nonprofits

The descriptive results from the survey correspond in large part to the theoretical model presented earlier in the paper. To systematically test the model for the effects of organizational size and the associations between model factors, the model factors were operationalized as follows.

1. IS funding intensity. Ratio of annual computing budget to total organizational budget. Computing budget consists of the salaries for in-house IS staff, hardware, software, computer supplies, computer consultants, computer service bureaus, and other computer expenditures.

2a. Computer hardware power and complexity. The ratio of the number of diskette-based micros plus the number of word processors to the total number of computer and word processor systems.

2b. Computer software power and complexity. A indicator ratio showing the presence or absence of the eight leading software applications (word processing, mailing lists, contribution processing, contribution history, payroll and tax, budgeting, solicitations, and orchestra personnel). Specifically, the ratio is the sum of eight indicators, where 1 or 0 indicates presence or absence of the application, divided by 8. For instance, presence of only word processing and budgeting implies a ratio of 0.25.

3a. In-house professionalism of IS staff. Presence or absence of in-house, full-time professional IS staff. IS staff includes in-house and outside, as well as full time and part time personnel.

3b. Presence of technical support and assistance person (TSA). Presence or absence of TSA(s).

4. IS training. Presence or absence of computer training.

5. IS planning. Presence or absence of computer capacity planning.

6. IS control. An index of IS control equal to the average of four responses, extent of documentation of systems design, extent of documentation of programs, concern for physical security of computer locations, and concern for backup of storage media. All responses are on a five point Likert scale from 1 (no control) to 5 (very much control).

7. Organizational satisfaction with IS. Levels of organizational satisfaction were based on two survey questions that asked, “to what extent do you feel computerizing has met the organization’s expectations?” (i.e. “IS satisfaction”) and “what is the effect of computerization on morale?” (i.e. referred to as “IS morale improvement”. Response categories were grouped into high satisfaction (response 1 on a 5 point Likert scale) and medium/low satisfaction (responses 2–5 on a 5 point Likert scale).

## Results

The hypothesized levels of, and institutional size effects on the model factors are largely confirmed. The means of the factors are shown to have statistically significant differences, as measured by t tests and crosstabulation. The factor means vary in the expected directions, with the one exception of IS satisfaction $^{8}$ (see Table 10). IS satisfaction does not vary perceptibly by size, contrary to hypothesis. The lack of size effect for IS satisfaction may be the result of reduced expectations in smaller organizations, which implies that lower levels of IS success are balanced by lower expectations. The higher than expected mean levels for both IS satisfaction and IS morale improvement may be due to biases of respondents' self-reporting.

The model's pathways were tested by examining two-way crosstabulations for each pair of model factors with hypothesized linkages (Agresti, 1984). Based on these empirical results, it is suggested the theoretical model be modified to include more comprehensive satisfaction measures. Organizational satisfaction needs to be examined more broadly, perhaps basing it on a multidimensional panel of responses [see 31], by surveying more layers of users (for instance, the person responsible for computing, managers, computing staff, and end users), and/or by including IS productivity measurements. ## Summary

The present study has analyzed staffing, planning, and control of information systems in symphonies, based on a survey of symphonies in the U.S. and Canada. The following factors are different in symphonies' IS staffing, planning, and control, versus for-profit enterprises.

1. Reduced level of, and greater inconsistencies in IS funding. For instance, there is a low ratio of IS budget to overall budget.

2. Lack of professional IS staff, and greater dependence on non-IS staff to perform computing functions.

3. Increased dependence on IS consultants outside the organization. In particular, about half lead technical personnel are located outside the organization.

4. Salaries for IS staff that are significantly lower than comparable salaries in the private sector.

5. Reduced IS training, with dependence on employee-supervised training, a factor which may hold back knowledge advances to the state-of-the-art.

6. Lack of IS planning and IS capacity planning.

7. Inadequacies in many types of management control of IS. The inadequacies are greater in smaller-sized organizations, which also have a lower level of concern about lack of control.

Current MIS theories are not adequate to take into account differences stemming from for-profit versus nonprofit organizational types. The paper has introduced a new model of IS for resource-constrained nonprofits. The model is based on seven factors – including computer funding, computer equipment/software, IS staffing, training, planning, and control, and organizational satisfaction with IS. The model was largely confirmed empirically. It is suggested that the model be modified to broaden the concept of IS satisfaction.

From a practical standpoint, the following IS recommendations are offered to symphony management:

1. Place greater emphasis on IS in organizational goal setting. Although these organizations are known to have multiple goals [25], IS should be viewed both as a major goal in itself, and as a comprehensive service supporting most or all of the other goals.

2. Recognize the critical importance of technically-skilled IS support personnel, both formally employed and informal consultants. Develop better in-house computer skills, either by tapping the job marketplace of computer professionals or by encouraging enhanced training and educational programs for personnel in the arts sector.

3. Include IS as a standard budget item in the symphony's annual operating budget.

4. Seek to improve IS management, especially in planning and control. Institute standard requirements for IS planning, IS cost/benefit analysis, and documentation.

5. Institute policies limiting and restricting donation of IS equipment and services, in order to gain improved management control.

As with all contemporary enterprise, information systems in symphonies are vitally important. Enhanced computing will improve the prospects of these organizations to survive and prosper in today's fragile environment for arts organizations.
