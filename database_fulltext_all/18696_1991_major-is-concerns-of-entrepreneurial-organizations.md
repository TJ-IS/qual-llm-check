# Major IS concerns of entrepreneurial organizations \*

Paul Alpar

University of Illinois at Chicago, Chicago, IL 60680, USA

Phillip Ein-Dor

Tel Aviv University, Ramat-Aviv, Tel-Aviv 69 978, Israel

A survey was conducted of the information system concerns of small entrepreneurial businesses. The 526 firms which responded to the relevant question on computer concerns exhibited a broad range of factors concentrated in several major categories. A comparison of the results of this survey with previous studies of IS issues indicates considerable differences in the nature of the concerns, and in the rank ordering of the concerns. These differences are attributable to the nature of the firms involved and to methodological differences in data collection. In particular, the effect of open versus closed questions is discussed.

Keywords: Comparison of Studies, High Technology Businesses, IS Concerns, IS Issues.

## Introduction

The last six or seven years have seen a number of interesting attempts to identify the major information system (IS) issues of concern to IS and top management. Given that most such studies were published in the highly regarded MIS Quarterly, there can be no doubt concerning the significance and level of interest in this area among both practitioners and academics.

The discussion section of this paper analyzes all these five papers. A synthesis is suggested for four: they are sufficiently similar in terms of the methodology to allow this. In chronological order, these studies are Ball and Harris [3], Dickson et al. [7], Hartog and Herbert [11], and Brancheau and Wetherbe [4]; the fifth study is by Martin [12].

All studies focused on large organizations, typically those with organizationally distinct data processing (DP) departments. However, a number of researchers have suggested that organizational size is a significant variable (e.g., [2,8,14,17]). Thus, it was hypothesized that the IS concerns of small businesses are different. Our study relates to small, entrepreneurial organizations in order to ascertain how their problems and concerns are different, at least in order of priority, from those of large organizations.

## Methodology

Our research is based on data collected in a survey of young, technology oriented companies. The survey related to a broad range of issues; the findings reported here are based on a question relating to IS concerns and another on the purpose of computer use.

Addresses of participant firms were obtained from the commercially available CorpTech database. $^{1}$ This contains addresses and some additional information about high-technology firms in the U.S.A. Many government and state agencies purchase it and it has recently been used in several research projects related to entrepreneurship (e.g., [10,15]). The survey was conducted between October 1987 and April 1988 with the majority of responses received in November and December of 1987.

The survey questionnaire was only sent to companies which were founded in 1980 or later. Due to mergers, acquisitions, and possible errors in the database, a few companies indicated an earlier date of foundation; this causes no problems, as there were only 30 such older companies (they were included in the analysis). The questionnaire was sent to the most senior executive listed in the database. In most cases this was the owner who also managed the firm. In other cases, a professional manager (owning less than 25% of the company) or a non-managing business owner filled out the questionnaire. Three thousand three hundred questionnaires were mailed, 250 of which could not be delivered by the mail service; 636 firms completed and returned the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 20.9%. An analysis of response rates in entrepreneurship research [1] revealed that response rates vary widely, depending on the source of addresses and on additional contacts. Since our addresses were obtained from a commercial source and no additional contacts with potential respondents were made, the response rate is not unusual.

The principal question for the study, that on computer system concerns, was formulated as an open question to induce answers unbiased by researchers' preconceptions. It contained no professional terms (see Appendix A, Question A). The open question was phrased in such a way as to suggest the kinds of issues raised in previous surveys. The effects of open versus closed questions and the relative desirability of each are dealt with later. It should be noted that the survey contained many additional questions, but these are of no interest for this report.

Five hundred and twenty six of the 636 respondent firms answered the question on computer concerns. Eight respondent firms which omitted this question did not use a computer at all (as disclosed by another question). After deducting these, the item omission rate is 16.2%. This is higher than the average for the complete questionnaire (5.47%), but is better than the average omission rate for all complex questions (20.1%); complex questions are defined in the literature as those which require some thought or retrieval of records by the respondent. Comparison with other complex questions is more meaningful, since it has been shown that different question types lead to different response rates (e.g., [5,9]). Thus, we may assume that there was no special reluctance to answer the question on computer concerns. There was no significant difference in size (as measured by number of employees and sales volume) between companies for which an answer to the question was entered and those for which the answer was omitted; a t-test for difference in the number of employees yielded t = -0.72 with a significance level of 0.472 and the same test for sales volume yielded t = 0.56 and a significance level of 0.577. Thus, we may assume that there is no size related bias in non-responses.

After the questionnaires were returned, the answers were classified by the authors in several steps. First, each independently grouped the responses into homogeneous concerns based on semantic similarity. Second, these concerns were grouped, again independently, into broader categories such as system quality or data management. Then, the two independent classifications were reconciled at both levels. There was disagreement in only a few cases. The high agreement was facilitated by the surprisingly high coincidence in the wording of the responses. This procedure produced 37 concerns and 14 categories. Table 3 shows the concerns and categories, with the frequencies of responses for each.

## Computer firms versus others: Major concerns

Over half the responding companies were in the computer industry; this naturally raised the hypothesis that the major concerns of computer companies are not the same as those of the rest of the respondents. The frequencies of main concern groups are given in Table 4. The concern groups are ordered according to their frequency in the total population. The categories no concern and miscellaneous have been excluded from ranking to facilitate comparison with previous research.

First considering the total population, the variation of concerns among all categories is highly significant (p < 0.001). This is still true when no concern and miscellaneous are excluded. The variation of concerns among the sub-categories is also highly significant (p < 0.001) in all cases. A Chi-square test for similarity of response frequencies for computer and non-computer companies showed that the distribution of concerns varies significantly between the two population groups (p < 0.007 including the categories no concern and miscellaneous and p < 0.026 without them). Computer firms are more concerned about cost and development; given the size of firms in our sample, the non-computer firms probably do not engage very much in system development and so avoid both development problems and uncertain costs. This assumption is supported by other studies of small businesses. Raymond and Magnenat-Thalmann [13] found that only 31% of the small businesses they surveyed had their own personnel do application design and programming. In retrospect, it would have been interesting to compare computer firms engaged primarily in hardware with those specializing in software; however, no data were collected to permit this.

## Computer firms versus others: No concerns

In order to establish the differences between the two groups of firms, frequencies were computed for all concerns (including the miscellaneous ones) as a single category versus the no-concern category. This distribution was also significantly different for the two groups (p < 0.029). On the basis of these findings, subsequent analyses were performed separately for the two industry groups; when comparing this survey with previous ones, we follow their practice and do not differentiate industry groups.

## IS concerns as a function of use

The answers on “reasons for use” were cross-tabulated with “concerns.” The results indicate that they are hardly related.

## IS concerns as a function of IS experience

The questionnaire also contained questions related to:

\- number of years of computer use in the firm;
- existence of an information systems plan in the business plan;

\- application areas;

\- total data processing costs;

\- hardware costs; and

\- amount of software developed in-house.

It seemed interesting to analyze whether concerns were correlated with these variables. For example, one could ask: Do companies which spend more money for data processing relative to their sales have different concerns than those which spend less?

Surprisingly, none of the many tests indicated a significant variation. This would seem to indicate that, for the firms surveyed, the issues are independent of most attributes.

A number of points are worthy of mention. First, the sample sizes are generally quite small; only that of Ball and Harris approaches ours. Second, the study by Brancheau and Wetherbe contained two classes of respondents – IS managers and general managers; we also treat these two groups as two separate studies.

Turning next to the topic of the studies, four of the five related to critical or important issues. Here the word concern was used. Issue by itself does not always have the same connotation as concern. Hartog and Herbert asked separately about the importance of the issue (which invokes a more neutral interpretation) and the difficulty in implementing the needed solutions (which comes closer to a concern). They reported that the separation did not lead to any additional insights. Although there may be some dissimiliraities in the semantics of the studies, the finding of Hartog and Herbert leads us to believe that it is valid to compare the studies.

The study by Martin related to critical success factors; these may again have somewhat different connotations. But it is clear, methodologically, this study is closest to ours, employing open questions.

We first compare our results with Martin's, and then with the remaining four. Martin ranked critical success factors, as reported by IS managers in 15 organizations, by the frequencies of managers reporting each factor. A comparison of the resulting factors with those in our study (see Table 7)

exhibits considerable overlap, again indicating that there are probably no serious semantic problems arising from lexical differences in the questionnaires.

Of the seven critical success factors in Martin's study, two were not reported in ours. This is because Martin's respondents were DP managers while ours were owner/managers who did not have to worry about reconciling their activities with management's objectives. While the rankings are not the same, those of the issues mentioned in Martin's study have, by and large, high rankings in our study. There are three exceptions; our sample rated cost, reliability, and integration among the top 6 concerns. Other than that, the relative similarity of results is striking given the size difference and the time gap between the studies.

The other four studies constitute something of a “series” covering the period from 1982 to 1987 with each built on its predecessors. Table 8 exhibits their findings; an attempt has been made to correlate the results between these studies by aligning common issues in a single row and by grouping the individual issues into larger categories, where we made use of the factor analysis performed by Hartog and Herbert (they found four categories and four unclassified issues). A caveat is in order here, since the significance of Hartog and Herbert’s factors is not clear; for example, end user computing and centralization each appeared in two factors while recruiting and training appeared in the telecommunications factor. Consequently, we utilized this factor analysis as a basis for our categorization but did not adhere to it rigorously.

The lack of stability in the catalog of issues is highlighted by the number of “transients” – i.e. items which appear and then disappear. These fall into two categories; the first consists of items which appear only once in one of the surveys; e.g., management science and the MIS environment or quality assurance. A further element of instability is the differences in rank of issues between studies. Given this synthesis of previous studies, it is possible to compare the results to our survey (Table 10). The results are quite striking. Two of the most highly ranked issues in our study, reliability and change, do not appear in previous studies. Five of the six most lowly ranked issues in our study parallel the five top categories in previous studies (in almost the same order).

Two hypotheses suggest themselves as the causes of these differences. The first deals with the populations surveyed. In previous surveys the respondents were, generally speaking, information system executives or general managers in large corporations. In our case, the respondents were owner-managers of small, young businesses; this could explain at least some differences in the major concerns. There is no reason to expect owner-managers of young businesses to have much experience with computer systems, while general managers of large corporations, do, especially when they are IS executives. Needs of a small company are different from large; there is less need for alignment, detailed planning, and coordination, because there is direct communication between top management and employees. On the other hand, resources are scarce and cost consciousness is high.

The series of four studies began with that of Ball and Harris, which employed closed questions; it is reasonable to assume that not all aspects of the opinion area were disclosed. Except for the study by Dickson et al., all began with a preset list, though additions were allowed. Closed questions and the Delphi method may lead to results which reflect respondents' intuitive feelings and contain suggestive biases, inducing respondents to offer what they think are socially desirable answers. A good example is the term planning; this is the top issue in the four surveys yet does not appear in Martin's and ours. $^{2}$ Another good example is IS funding levels, which appeared in the top ten issues of 37.8% of the IS managers in the first round of the Brancheau and Wetherbe study but was reduced to 4.4% by the final round. Which ranking better reflects managers' real concerns? It seems to us that the Delphi approach would enhance the importance of the more faddish issues to which respondents had recently been exposed in seminars, conferences, etc.

A somewhat different problem in using closed questions is the effect of minor variations in the wording or definition. A prime example is the software development issue. In two of the studies, software quality is included, whereas in the other two it is not. When quality is included, this issue moves up ten positions in the ranking. In our categorization, system quality is a separate issue: the second most highly ranked.

Our major problem in using open questions was in categorizing the responses; in spite of all the precautions, we cannot guarantee that we did not introduce some of our own biases. We do believe, however, that the extent of the bias is much less than that caused by peer group influences under the Delphi method. This clearly calls for further study in order to permit correct evaluation of the results of opinion studies.

## Conclusions

There is a core of major IS issues which appear in all surveys on this topic. We assume that these are indeed universal problem areas. There are several issues that appear in some surveys and not in others. Furthermore, the rankings of the common issues vary considerably between surveys. These findings may be due to differences in the types of organizations and people surveyed, to different methodologies, and to a degree of faddishness in IS issues.

The number of studies addressing this topic and the interest that they arouse clearly attest to its importance. The lack of clarity and consistency in the findings calls for research to locate the reasons for these problems and to permit identification of the truly major issues; once these issues have been unequivocally identified, they represent a set of research problems to which the academic IS community may wish to address itself.
