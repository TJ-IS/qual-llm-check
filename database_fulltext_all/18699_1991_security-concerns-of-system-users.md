# Security concerns of system users

# A study of perceptions of the adequacy of security \*

Dale L. Goodhue

and Detmar W. Straub \*\*

Curtis L. Carlson School of Management, Information&Decision Sciences Department, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455. USA

In the past several years, a number of researchers have raised the issue of the level of security concern among system users, suggesting that security may be undervalued in both centralized and decentralized IS departments, and among IS staff as well as end-users. Since protective measures often require significant managerial vigilance, an appropriate level of awareness and concern may be a prerequisite for adequate security protection. Given its importance, there is a need for a better understanding of what leads to security concern. This paper focuses on users' perceptions about the security of their systems. Based on previous work on individuals' attitudes and beliefs about IS and IS environments, it is hypothesized that a user's concern about security is a function of three different constructs: industry risk, company actions, and individual awareness. The study tests the main assertions of the model, using a cross-sample comparison of perceptions from two different survey instruments. The first sample used 570 randomly selected DPMA members. The second sample surveyed 357 end-users. The theoretical model does provide some explanation for level of concern among IS professionals in the mainframe and minicomputer environment. Both company actions and individual awareness were statistically significant, although the explained variance was not large. Problems with using post hoc analysis may have contributed to the partial and weak support for the model.

Since nearly half of all computer abuses are being discovered by users $[31]$ , a lack of awareness of the danger may lead to weak vigilance by users, and greater potential for abuse. In this sense an appropriate level of concern may be a prerequisite for adequate protection. This heightens the importance of understanding what leads to higher levels of concern among users. How is individual concern related to the actual risk faced? Can individual concern be explained by industry-specific risk factors, or concrete actions taken to contain that risk? Is the level of concern greater among certain types of users such as those who are more computer literate or those who use departmental or corporate data rather than personal data?

Based on previous work on individual attitudes and beliefs, this paper presents a theoretical model that suggests that an individual's belief in the adequacy of security is a function of:

(1) the potential for abuse in a given industry, (2) company specific action that has been taken to maintain security, and

(3) individual factors such as computer literacy, managerial role, etc.

The main assertions of this model were tested using a cross-sample comparison of security perceptions from two different survey instruments. The first used a sample base of 570 randomly selected DPMA members. The second included 357 end-users in 10 organizations.

Although the two samples used different instruments, it was possible to test at least some aspects of the theoretical model with each of the samples. A comparison of the results suggests that the model has some explanatory power, at least for IS professionals. Both company actions and individual awareness were found to be significant, though the explained variance was not large. Problems with using post hoc analysis may have contributed to the partial and weak support for the model.

## 2. Concern About Security

Little prior empirical work has been done on system user attitudes toward computer security.

Bailey and Pearson [5] included questions on security of data on their questionnaire measuring user information satisfaction (UIS). However, when Ives, Olson and Baroudi [22] refined the Bailey and Pearson instrument, the security questions were eliminated because they did not correlate highly with measures of overall user satisfaction, even though they had been rated as moderately important by Bailey and Pearson's respondents.

Within the systems community, security and control have been cited as key issues in opinion surveys of IS managers. Since the early 1980's, security and control have appeared consistently among the top 20 issues for IS managers [6,25,14,9]. In Hartlog and Herbert's study [18], it ranked fifth among IS managers' key issues, and other authors believe it will be rising in years to come [28].

Although IS managers have at least marginal concerns about security, non-IS managers seem to be less concerned $[10]$ . In one study, general managers did not even rank security among the top 20 information management issues $[9]$ . In spite of the fact that security issues are frequently stressed by both Internal Audit and IS managers $[26]$ , the value of system access controls is not self-evident. Without a major loss due to lax security, it may be that security concern will generally be quite low. There is evidence, in fact, that it takes a major loss from computer abuse to initiate or reinforce the security administration function $[30]$ . Top executives, therefore, may continue to be reluctant to grant status and commit resources to the computer security function $[23]$ .

As computer technology spreads throughout organizations in the form of end-user computing (EUC), another area of potential risk emerges. Since EUC occurs when end-users control their own inputs, processing, outputs, and even software development, the security risk in this environment is high [3]. Compounding the problem, there is little or no security training for systems users [24].

Thus, there are good reasons to believe that computer abuse is a major problem that will not diminish on its own. While managers of IS and Internal Auditing bear a particular responsibility for containing this risk, the awareness of all users must be heightened. It is appropriate, therefore, that research efforts reveal the circumstances in which low security consciousness is likely to occur. With this knowledge, all managers will be able to exercise greater control over the computing environment. In situations where security consciousness is too low, it could be heightened.

3. Developing a Model of Perceptions of Security Concern

## 3.1. Perceptions as a Theoretical Construct

There are numerous methodological questions about how to clearly measure security concern. Although many studies have employed user perceptions as empirical measures [e.g., 36,32], such measures may lack theoretical clarity, because they lack a theoretical underpinning [33,27]. The most commonly cited reference discipline for these measures has been job satisfaction research [e.g., 5]. However, “IS satisfaction” has not been well enough defined to clarify how it is similar to or different from “job satisfaction”.

In addition, job satisfaction has been shown to relate only very weakly to performance $[34,21]$ . To address these problems, Goodhue $[16]$ draws upon theory developed in job satisfaction research that distinguishes between job “satisfaction” and individual “satisfactoriness” $[12]$ . He expands on the original theory to present a model of user attitudes about their systems environment that includes perceived “satisfactoriness” of the systems (the extent to which the IS environment assists the user in performing tasks), and perceived “satisfaction” (whether the individual’s personal needs are satisfied by using systems). He suggests that user-assessed satisfactoriness should be closely related to performance, and thus is a far better surrogate for IS success than satisfaction.

According to this theory, a user's assessment of the satisfactoriness of his/her systems environment depends upon three interacting constructs: the task characteristics (his/her demands on the IS environment), the characteristics of the IS environment itself, and individual characteristics. Thus a person whose job-related tasks were fairly straight-forward and did not require much from the systems environment would probably find the system environment satisfactory, regardless of its characteristics.

## 3.2. Satisfactoriness Applied to Security Concern

If we apply the theory of satisfactoriness to the perceptions of data security, the concepts of task, IS environment, and individual characteristics must be placed in the domain of providing organizational security. In order to do this, we must first define the task users face.

The task is protecting the data of the organization from intentional or unintentional misuse. The difficulty should vary as the potential for gain on the part of the abuser or loss of the company varies. For example, in the information-intensive financial industry there would be more potential for gain from unscrupulous persons manipulating data for their own advantage than in the manufacturing industry. Thus the difficulty of achieving satisfactory security should be greater in the financial industry. All other things being equal, we would expect people in industries with a high degree of security danger to be more concerned about security and to feel that their environment is less satisfactory.

The second major factor contributing to perceptions of the satisfactoriness of the security environment is action taken by corporations to prevent violations. In firms that allocate significant resources to providing security (for example many person-hours and considerable software devoted to protecting data from misuse), we would expect that individuals would be less concerned and would feel that the security was more satisfactory.

Finally there are individual factors. Awareness of potential problems is a major consideration. We might expect that people who are more aware of the potential for abuse would be sensitized to the dangers of inadequate security and would more likely feel that security was unsatisfactory. We would expect that greater awareness of potential abuse would lead to more concern about security and perceptions that the environment was less satisfactory. To be more specific, we might expect that people who were more computer literate would be more aware of the potential for abuse, DP personnel more aware than non-DP users, and data accessors and data analysts more aware than decision makers with low contact with the data storage technology.

Figure 1 shows the Security Concern model. This model suggests the following:

Hypothesis 1. As the risk to the organization increases (i.e., potential gain for the perpetrator, and/or potential damage for the organization), perceptions of the satisfactoriness of security measures will decrease, ceteris paribus.

Hypothesis 2. As the resources applied to maintain security increase, perceptions of the satisfactoriness of security measures will increase, ceteris paribus.

Hypothesis 3. Users who are more aware of the potential for computer abuse (i.e., they are more computer literate, and/or more closely associated with the use of the computer technology), will have lower levels of perceived satisfactoriness of security measures, ceteris paribus.

Hypothesis 4. There should be an interaction between user awareness and sensitivity to risk and company action. That is, perceptions of users who are more aware of the dangers should be more affected by differences in risk and company action than those who are unaware of the dangers.

## 4. Methodology for Testing the Model

These four propositions were tested using data from two different data collection efforts. The first effort used a base of 570 randomly selected DPMA members. Since this sample included measures of all the key concepts in the propositions, it could be used to test all four propositions. The second sample surveyed 357 end users in 10 organizations. This sample had more extensive coverage of individual characteristics but no coverage of the resources applied to security measures. Thus only proposition 3 could be tested with this data.

## 4.1. Sample No. 1

Data for the first sample were drawn from a victimization database obtained in a prior study of computer abuse and deterrent countermeasures [30]. The survey instrument was validated via extensive field interviews with 35 system professionals, interviews and questionnaire responses from an independent group of 88 people, and, finally, pilot study questionnaire returns from 170 respondents. The validated survey was mailed out to randomly selected DPMA members in 1986. After combining questionnaires from the DPMA and pilot group, and after removing duplicates and those with missing values for the key variables studied here, the remaining sample size was 570. The validation process and tests for non-response bias are discussed in Straub [29].

## 4.1.2. Testing of the Predicted Relationships

The research procedure was to test a main effects model first, and then empirically determine whether adding the hypothesized interaction effects improved the fit to the data. The main effects model (with no interaction effects) was tested by regressing the dependent variable (concern about security) against the predicted independent variables (industry risk, organization actions, and individual characteristics). The results of this regression analysis are shown in Table 2. This model explains only 5% of the total variance. (The adjusted $R^{2}$ statistic is only 0.026.) However several of the key variables are significant predictors of security concern.

Notably, of the 12 dummy variables for industry risk factor, only one is significant: the variable for transportation services. Since there is no a priori reason to believe that the only industry risk differences are between the transportation industry and all other industries, this single significant coefficient very likely reflects a random association rather than an underlying relationship.

As a test of this hypothesis, a second model was analyzed without the 12 dummy variables for industry risk. These results are shown in Table 3. Since the adjusted $R^{2}$ does increase when the industry risk variables are removed, there is no empirical support for including them in the model.

## 4.1.3. Immediate Conclusions from Sample No. 1

The low $R^{2}$ statistic in sample No. 1 suggests that only a small portion of the variance in our dependent variable was explained by the independent variables. However, both company action and managerial level had regression coefficients significant at the 0.05 level. Interpreting these results, we see that the more actions a firm takes to address security, the less concern will be expressed by IS users, supporting hypothesis 2. There was ambiguous support for hypothesis 3: on the one hand technical managers were less likely to feel that their security was effective than managers more removed from the technology, but on the other hand, there was no relationship between years in the MIS field and level of security concern, as we had expected. There is no support for hypothesis I which argues that concern increases with industry risk, nor for hypothesis 4 which postulates an interaction between user awareness and sensitivity to risk and company action.

## 4.2. Sample No. 2

Data for this sample was drawn from 357 questionnaires administered in 10 organizations in a study of user assessments of the satisfactoriness of their environments. The companies may have volunteered based on their interest in end-user computing, and thus this sample may represent a bias toward forward-thinking companies or companies where EUC is appropriate.

In each of the 10 companies, at least two groups of users were targeted, and for each group about 20 users were selected on as random a basis as practical considerations would allow. In general, the respondents were non-IS “users” of computer data in some form, but there was no effort to identify users who were conscious of or involved in data security issues. Since the bulk of the respondents in this sample were end-users of computer systems, rather than systems professionals, this sample can be characterized as a sample of end-users.

The response rate in each company varied from about 50% to 100%, with an overall response rate of 75%. Five percent of the total were unusable because of incomplete or unconscientious data (for example, answering all questions with “neither agree nor disagree”). When these were removed, there remained 357 usable questionnaires, or about 70% of the number sent out.

## 4.2.1. Measures

Measures from the questionnaire, summarized in Table 5, were designed to gauge the level of concern users felt about their security and to gather data about independent variables that presumably correlate highly with, and affect the level of concern. The actual questions are shown in Appendix II. There were three questions measuring the level of concern with security. These were randomly placed in a list of over 50 questions about all aspects of the IS environment. These three questions were more highly correlated with each other than with any other questions in the analysis; this is evidence of their reliability and discriminant validity. Cronbach's alpha for these variables was 0.84.

Sixteen questions asked about individual characteristics. Because there was no a priori identification of the key individual characteristics, and many of these questions addressed similar concerns, the 16 questions were factor analyzed to identify the underlying dimensions. Seven factors were extracted, with the factor loadings as shown in Appendix III. The seven factors are identified as:

(1) Computer literacy and hands-on use of computers as a source of data;

(3) Use of stand-alone PCs and personal data;

(4) Locating or analyzing data as opposed to non-involvement with data in one's job;

(5) Involvement in data analysis;

(6) Using departmental as opposed to corporate data; and

(7) Using external data.

The factor analysis of the individual characteristic questions showed weak support for both reliability and discriminant validity of those measures. In addition, we tested the nomological validity of the individual characteristics constructs, that is, the degree to which these measures can be used to reconfirm relationships in a larger theoretical context [4]. To do this we performed a regression of these measures on a well validated measure (concern with data accessibility) [17] that should vary with individual characteristics. This test supported the nomological validity of the measures. See Appendix IV for a discussion of this test. Thus, the individual characteristic measures have at least a moderately strong claim to validity.

## 4.2.2. Testing the Predicted Relationships

The concern a system user has about security is predicted to be a function of three sets of variables – industry susceptibility, company actions, and individual awareness. Since this sample included no measures for company actions, our ability to test the total theoretical model was limited. A portion of the model could be tested, however, by making the assumption that both the industry susceptibility and the company actions were constant within any given company.

By subtracting the company mean from the concern score of every respondent, we determined the “residual security concern”: the degree to which an individual has higher or lower concern than others in the same company. This is presumed to be a function of individual characteristics. Given a measure of residual security concern, the relationship between this derived variable and the measured individual characteristics can be tested.

The seven individual characteristic factors were regressed against the measure of residual security concern. As Table 6 indicates, only one of the factors had a significant coefficient, and the F-test for the regression as a whole was not significant at even the 0.1 level.

As a matter of additional interest, the respondents in Sample No. 2 gave security the lowest level of concern of 13 different aspects of the information systems environment, suggesting that this group of users was not very concerned about security.

## 5. Discussion

Our empirical results do not give overwhelming support for our theory. However, when empirical results do not agree with theory, measurement instruments and research design should be questioned before concluding that the theory is incorrect. Two concerns may explain at least to some degree the low explanatory power of our analysis. One concern is that post hoc analysis was used and thus there may have been weaker than desired correspondence between measures and constructs. Thus, for example, the measure of differences in security concern between industry groups may be weak, because respondents were asked to answer relative to their own industry group. A second potential concern is whether the respondents chosen were appropriate for the theory. The theory of satisfactoriness focuses on an individual and the tasks on which this individual is working. The theory presupposes that an individual understands her tasks and whether the IS environment is assisting her in accomplishing those tasks. If security is not part of her task, she may have thought little about it, and may have little appreciation for either the potential dangers or actions that could protect against those dangers.

It may be that our rational model of security awareness will only hold for a small number of security professionals who have some responsibility for and understanding of security, and who thus make it their business to understand both the risks and the protective actions that reduce risk. For users who are less concerned with security as part of their jobs, we can expect the security concept to be less salient, users to be less informed, and measures of security concern to contain more error.

The IS professionals surveyed in sample No. 1 did have direct knowledge of, interest in, or responsibility for aspects of computer technology and/or data security. They took the time to fill out a questionnaire clearly labeled as a “Computer Abuse and Computer Security Questionnaire.” Thus we can presume these people have some knowledge of or interest in security.

On the other hand, respondents in sample No. 2 were end-users who access, analyze, or interpret data as part of their job. The selection criteria had nothing to do with technical knowledge of security and computer systems. Thus, sample No. 2, like the general business population and the population at large [20], probably contained people with low security awareness, for whom a theory presupposing knowledge about security may not be applicable.

Three possible interpretations. The partial and weak support for the theory points up the difficulty of measuring and proving any theory, but especially with post hoc analysis. There are, finally, three possible interpretations and conclusions:

1. Our measures may be too weak to capture what they propose to measure. Thus the theory may be quite accurate as a description of security concern, but we cannot be certain.

2. Most individuals (including those in our samples) may be so uninformed about security, that it makes no sense to measure their concern. Concern is only related to risk and protective action for a very few knowledgeable people.

3. We may have succeeded in identifying knowledgeable, aware respondents, (at least in sample No. 1) and our measures, while not perfect, may capture the concepts they were intended to measure. We would then have to conclude that the theory has only some small explanatory power.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we have argued that insufficient computer and data security is a major problem in many organizations and that low levels of concern contribute to the danger. Drawing on previous work on user attitudes, we proposed a model of the determinants of perceptions about the adequacy of the security of systems and data. According to this model, security concern is dependent on the risk to the company of a security lapse and the protective measures taken to protect against that risk. Several hypotheses were suggested as tests of the model. Empirical tests of these hypotheses found only partial and weak support.

Since the study did find significant results for some of the proposed relationships, we conclude that the theory itself may still prove to be viable. This might argue for a more carefully developed and validated instrument to measure the key constructs of the theory more precisely: knowledgeable professional assessment of the satisfactoriness of the security environment, potential risk, and amount and type of protective action taken. This new instrument could be used to validate the theory.

On the other hand, our theory may be more aptly described as a normative rather than a descriptive model. That is, we believe managers should be more concerned when the risk of a security incident is greater, and they should be less concerned when they know their company is taking stronger action to protect them. If managers do not behave as the model would predict, it may reflect a need for better managerial education.

The practical motivation for this research was to determine how low security awareness in the general business population could be raised, given that security awareness is essential for the effectiveness of many kinds of security measures. Even with its problems, this study suggests that security awareness is low for many individuals and that security concern may not be rationally based. It may make as much sense to document actual incidents of security lapses and build a stronger managerial case to educate managers about security threats and protective measures than to wait for further research validating this security awareness model. In addition, by improving managerial awareness of security issues, we may change how managers think about security and improve their fit to our normative model.

## Appendix IV

Testing Nomological Validity of the Individual Characteristics Measures

We can test nomological validity of the individual characteristics constructs [4] by testing the relationship between these individual measures and a well-validated measure that should vary with individual characteristics. To do this, we repeated the regression analysis with a new dependent variable, residual data accessibility concern instead of residual data security concern. This alternate dependent variable was chosen because it had been demonstrated to be a valid measure for user perceptions of data accessibility, and it was shown to be sensitive to changes in either the task or the data environment [17].

When this validated measure was used as the dependent variable in a regression with the seven factors of individual characteristics, the seven factors explained 6% of the variance, with an adjusted R-squared of 0.04. The regression as a whole was significant at the 0.01 level. The interpretation of the individual regression coefficients was also consistent with expectations. Users more involved in accessing data perceive corporate data as more accessible, users more involved with external data perceive corporate data as less accessible, and those involved in stand alone PCs and personal data perceive corporate data as less accessible. Since these measures of individual characteristics can be used to reconfirm relationships in a larger theoretical context, it is evidence of their nomological validity [4]. Thus the individual characteristic measures have at least a moderately strong claim to validity.
