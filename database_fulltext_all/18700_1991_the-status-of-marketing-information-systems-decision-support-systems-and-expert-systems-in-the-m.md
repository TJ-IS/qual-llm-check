# The status of marketing information systems, decision support systems and expert systems in the marketing function of U.S. firms

Mary A. Higby  
University of Detroit, Detroit, MI 48221, USA

Badie N. Farah

Eastern Michigan University, Ypsilanti, MI 48197, USA

A survey of the current status of Marketing Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and Expert Systems was made in a group of U.S. firms. A questionnaire was first developed from an extensive review of the marketing and information systems literature. Major issues addressed were: (1) the level of use of marketing information systems, decision support systems, or expert systems in the marketing function, and (2) the level of use of the various systems in specific marketing activities. The study identifies the development, use, and possible future applications of these systems in marketing.

Keywords: Marketing Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Expert Systems, Marketing, Marketing Research.

## Introduction

The marketing manager has more information from both internal and external sources available today due to improved computer technology and improved data sources. However, this implies a need to process data for both problem solving and decision making for both tactical and strategic issues, especially those associated with the allocation of the firm's resources in such areas as promotion, new product planning, distribution channels, and pricing strategy.

The importance of computers in marketing was first highlighted by Philip Kotler (1966). Cox and Good coined the term marketing information system (1967). They provided the initial description of a marketing information system which was further refined by Brien and Stafford (1968). The focus in the 1990's will be marketing Decision Support Systems (DSS), expert marketing support systems and marketing Expert Systems (ES). Little (1979) stated that a marketing DSS is a coordinated collection of data, systems, tools, and techniques with supporting software and hardware. With this the organization gathers and interprets relevant information from business and its environment and turns it into a basis for marketing action.

During the 1980's, we saw a growing interest in the use of marketing DSS and marketing ES. As both the consumer goods and industrial goods markets become more competitive, there is a growing use of the data analysis as a part of the decision process. The increased use of data is in part due to the increase in available computing power (Mentzer, Schuster and Roberts 1987). At the same time, there is an increasing amount of available software.

## Decision Support Systems (DSS) in Marketing Mix Decisions

Gorry and Morton introduced DSS in 1971 to capture the idea of computer-based routines to support managers in solving specific, semistructured problems. At about that time, the Boone and Kurtz study (1971) revealed that 14 percent of the Fortune 500 firms were using mathematical models. By 1982, Fortune 500 firms were using decision support systems in various parts of their marketing activities (McLeod and Rogers, 1982).

The Brien Stafford model (1968) for marketing information systems consisted of separate subsystems for each ingredient of the marketing mix: product planning and development, pricing strategy, channel and distribution strategy and promotion strategy. The Crissy Mossman model (1977) showed how marketing mix subsystems assisted marketing managers in addressing major planning questions. Since its development, many have proposed the utilization of DSS for planning for and deciding on the marketing mix.

The use of DSS in product management was proposed by Little, who suggested that both models and measurements should be used to manage the product through its life cycle. Raw data rarely provides answers, it must be manipulated to provide meaningful answers to issues raised by brand managers, etc. More recently, Goslar and Brown (1986) have proposed the use of DSS for new product evaluations of consumer products. Knuckles (1986) has suggested their use in both new product development as well as for tracking brand performance. Conlon (1986) has also advanced the importance of DSS for managing the product by suggesting such applications as business and financial analyses. Choffray and Lilien (1986) recommended them for planning and controlling new industrial products.

Although models have been used in distribution planning for warehouse locations and inventory control, the literature does not specifically refer to DSS, per se. Current literature in this area offers limited channel and distribution applications.

For the promotions area of the marketing mix, the suggested applications for DSS are numerous. Green and others (1984) outlined their importance in developing retailing advertising campaigns. They described a DSS called BENEMAX designed to optimize product feature/benefits in light of target audience characteristics. Knuckles (1986) recommended them for promotion planning.

The importance of using DSS to manage the sale force was noted by Lodish (1980) He recommended their use in sales force allocation. Collins (1987) suggested the use of sales support systems as a method of increasing sales force productivity.

Pricing models have been used by both marketing managers and economists in suggesting prices for consumer and industrial products, however, their current use for analyzing the pricing variables of the marketing mix are minimal.

Several authors have expanded their recommendations for marketing strategy development. Little (1979) cited the importance of marketing science in developing and managing the marketing strategy of the firm. He also suggested that DSS were necessary to develop regional marketing programs. DSS was suggested for improved marketing productivity (Van Nievelt 1984), regional marketing strategies (Smith et.al 1985) and competitive advantage (Knuckles 1987).

## Expert Systems (ES) in Marketing Mix Decisions

The suggested use for ES in the marketing function has been growing recently. Schowoerer and Frappa (1986) recommended their use in new product development, media planning and copy evaluation. Kastiel (1987) proposed using ES in evaluating consumer and industrial promotion evaluations for direct ma iI or telemarketing situations. She also promoted their use in developing price quotes, as well as making pricing decisions. In the marketing research area ES has been recommended for interviewing, questionnaire design, coding, sampling, statistical analysis, qualitative research and project design (Schowoerer and Frappa 1986). Petrison (1987) suggested ES offers great potential to retailers in the use of their scanner data.

With the usage of computers expected to increase in business, the impact of computers in management (and specifically within marketing and marketing research) becomes more important. Studies have examined computer usage by salespeople (Taylor 1986) and computer usage by marketing professionals (Mentzer, Schuster and Roberts 1987). Other studies have examined the usage of marketing information systems by the Fortune 500 and Fortune 1000 (McLead and Rogers 1982, 1985).

## Methodology

A four page questionnaire was developed to gather information on computer usage for marketing purposes: types of systems usage, software for these information systems, satisfaction with these information systems, and demographics. The survey instrument was sent to a random sample of 2993 marketing executives taken from a list of 43,395 marketing executives compiled by Alvin B. Zeller. One mailing of the questionnaire resulted in 212 usable questionnaires, or a response rate of about seven percent. Due to mailing list issues, no attempt was made to contact nonrespondents. This response rate is similar to the response rate for similar research conducted by Berry (1983) and Mentzer, Schuster and Roberts (1987). Berry found little evidence of nonresponse bias. The level of nonresponse poses no problem as long as results only serve as an indication of the usage of information systems in the marketing function. Our study is a step toward understanding information systems utilization in marketing functions.

## Results

The respondents exhibited a range of demographic characteristics. Many were marketing managers (44.4%); however, other titles included director (19.8%); vice president (30.4%); and president, chief executive officer and chief operating officer (2.4%). Marketing or marketing research activities were given as areas of responsibility by almost all of the respondents.

Corporate size was reflected in annual revenues and number of employees, as presented in Tables 1a and 1b. Table 1a suggests that firms with a wide range of corporate revenues responded to the questionnaire. Table 1b indicates that the median number of employees in the firm was in the 101 to 500 range. Tables 1c and 1d suggest a wide cross section of both location and industry. The marketing managers were asked questions about the availability and accessibility of hardware and software (Table 2). The results for microcomputer usage and mini and micro linkage to a mainframe are similar to the findings of Mentzer, Schuster and Roberts (1987). The wide availability of micro (personal) computers and software suggests that marketing professionals are adapting computer technology very rapidly across firms of different size.

In response to the question, “Do you or your staff use software?” over 87 percent responded affirmatively. The most frequently used software for marketing activities was Lotus 1-2-3. The second most frequently used software were custom designed programs for specific marketing activities. Opportunities appear good for adoption of marketing programs which incorporate Lotus software.

Marketing information systems were used by 91 percent of the marketing managers. Approximately 10 percent of the respondents with such systems indicated that they used a computer for marketing activities but did not recognize that they had marketing information systems. The 9 percent who did not have such systems had sales of less than \$30 million dollars or did not respond to the question requesting information on annual sales.

The increase in the use of these systems is dramatic when previous research is examined. In 1971 about one third of the Fortune 500 had marketing information systems (Boone and Kurtz 1971), but by 1985 approximately three quarters of the Fortune 1000 had them (McLeod and Rogers 1985). In this study the use of these systems by customer classification: consumer, industrial or both showed little difference (Table 3).

The respondents ranked middle managers as the most important users of marketing information systems. Lower levels of managers were ranked second and upper level managers ranked third. These findings are different than those reported by McLeod and Rogers (1982), who found that marketing information systems were most important for upper level management, then middle managers, and finally for lower levels. These findings are perhaps not so different when one considers that now both lower and middle management are dependent upon marketing information systems to perform many of their daily activities.

## Conclusions

Our study sought to assess the penetration and usage of computers, marketing information systems, DSS, and ES by marketing professionals. The penetration of microcomputers and MIS within the marketing function is high. Size of firm had little impact on acceptance.

Many of the firms within the study used LOTUS 1-2-3. Several had also developed custom software to meet specific needs for analysis in specific marketing activities.

Based on our research the profile of the DSS user is the middle to large size firm (more than 100 employees and annual revenues in excess of \$100 million). Middle level managers appear to be receptive to DSS, since they are frequent users. The marketing activities that appear to offer potential for DSS are sales forecasting, budgeting, pricing decisions, competitive analysis, statistical analysis, customer profile analysis, inventory control, price quotation, and market research project design. The study suggests that many firms would favor DSS designed for micro (personal) computers.

Our work suggests that the profile of an ES technology user is the large firm with more than 1000 employees and annual revenues in excess of \$300 million. Within the firm, middle level marketing managers are likely to be receptive to information systems technology, since they are thought to receive the most benefits from marketing information systems.

The use of ES technology in software designed for marketing activities such as inventory control, order processing, sales forecasting and facility location is expected to be received favorably by marketing managers in large firms. Software designed with ES technology for micro (personal) computers for applications in new product evaluation, sales force analysis, media evaluation, pricing decisions and statistical analysis can be expected to receive positive response from marketing managers. In addition, current DSS users are likely to be future users of ES technology.
