# The information loop as a general analytic view

E. Burton Swanson

Anderson Graduate School of Management, University of California at Los Angeles, Los Angeles, CA 90024, U.S.A.

Current predominant views of information systems (IS) are motivated more by design and engineering aims, than they are by the aims of empirical study. To better support the latter, we advance a general analytic view based on the notion of “information loops” (I-loops) in organizations. An I-loop is understood to be anchored in an information context (I-context) from which it is pulled out conceptually for examination. Information systems (IS) are formal I-loops comprised of information acquisition (I-acquisition), information processing (I-processing), and information employment (I-employment) processes. An information store (I-store) provides data, models, and procedures in support of I-processing. We show the usefulness of the I-loop view for empirical studies, both for basic IS description and for IS problem diagnosis and treatment.

Keywords: Information Systems, Information Loop, Information System View, Information System, Description, Information System Problems, Model Conflict, Model Resolution.

## 1. Introduction

Information system (IS) concepts serve two fundamental aims. They serve first to support IS design and development. Here they provide the basic rubrics for the creation of IS as purposeful organizational artifacts (Simon, 1969). They serve principally as tools of design and engineering. They are incorporated into design methodologies, as variously described in Olle et al. (1982, 1986). They focus primarily on the internal structure of the IS to be created, rather than on the fit of the IS to the organization. Their foundations are normative, more than they are descriptive or explanatory.

IS concepts may also be applied to IS which already exist in the world; that is, to the empirical study of IS as they are “embedded” in their organizations. Such studies may be of either theoretical or practical bent. They may aim to build fundamental knowledge about IS in organizations (see, for example, Kling and Scacchi, 1982), or they may aim at an understanding and assessment of a single existing system (in terms of its impacts, for example), in order to make decisions about its future. In both instances, the concepts serve primarily descriptive and explanatory purposes (although normative criteria also apply, in that the knowledge gained is expected to be useful; e.g. in adapting a system). They also focus primarily on the fit of the IS to the organization.

Views or “paradigms” of IS, such as those discussed by Verrijn-Stuart (1986), frame the composition of their various concepts. Comparison of concepts from different views is problematic. Concepts are not right or wrong in and of themselves; rather they are good or bad according to how they work within their respective views. Because views are holistic, finding terminological equivalence across views necessitates examination of the views themselves.

Current predominant views of IS are motivated more by design and engineering aims, than they are by the aims of empirical study. This is understandable, in that IS is a relatively young field, dominated by the concerns associated with bringing new systems into the world. However, IS increasingly populate this world and need to be studied empirically. It is important, therefore, that our IS views be well suited to this purpose.

In this paper we summarize three prevalent IS views. We argue that all are deficient in various important respects, especially for purposes of empirical study, and we advance a more general analytic view based on the notion of “information loops” in organizations. We seek to strengthen the conceptual foundations for the empirical study of IS in organizations and also to reconcile certain differences among the current prevailing views.

## 2. Alternative Views of Information Systems

Among the various views of IS, three are widely held. These may be termed the data processing, the organizational mapping, and the organizational control views. We comment briefly upon these and compare them.

## 2.1. The Data Processing View

Computer-based data processing is a central feature of traditional IS in organizations. It motivates what is perhaps the original and dominant view. In this, data are input to a transformation process producing information, as shown in Figure 1. The computer program is understood to provide the instructions for the transformation. The concept is isomorphic to that of a computer system in which input devices (the conveyors of data) and output devices (the conveyors of information) are connected to a central processor and memory (in which the program carries out the transformation). IS are seen essentially as computer application software.

![](/api/attachments/C68ZD6AD/fulltext/images/3be77c29c709016334482a9abec6ee2c251f4ea6492b0ecf809d4b8b1b9110fb.jpg)  
Fig. 1. The Data Processing View.

Two critical processes are ignored by the data processing view. First, the acquisition of data for processing is not considered. Second, the disposition of the “information” is left untreated. The latter is especially significant inasmuch as information is characteristically defined as “data that has been processed into a form that is meaningful to the recipient and is of real or perceived value in current or prospective actions or decisions,” (Davis and Olson, 1985, emphasis added).

If “information” is thus data presented and interpreted in context, the significant flaw in the data processing view is its failure to incorporate or provide any linkage to this context. The IS is in effect disconnected from the organization it serves. How data are transformed into “information” through processing alone remains a mystery.

## 2.2. The Organizational Mapping View

If the data processing view is inspired by the transformational power of the computer program, the organizational mapping view is inspired by the representational power of the computer data base. Information context is explicitly incorporated in the form of an “object system” which is understood to be modeled by the IS, which is now conceived in extended data base terms (Verrijn-Stuart, 1986). Approaches and formal languages for organizational mapping dominate the concerns of adherents. (See, e.g., Gustafsson et al, 1982, and Sernadas et al, 1985.)

From this mapping view, an IS is understood to circumscribe a “universe of discourse” about its object system. Effective modeling of this object system requires semantic richness appropriate to the problem context, together with the formal discipline of completeness and consistency.

The weakness of the organizational mapping approach is that while it is strong on semantics, it is relatively weak on pragmatics. (This is so even though the mapping notion may be extended from structural to behavioral characteristics. See, e.g. Kung and Solvberg, 1986.) Its sense of IS purpose, that of an accurate mapping, is unappealing to many, especially those who would focus on the value of information (however accurate) in an organizational context.

## 2.3. The Organizational Control View

The organizational control view provides the pragmatic context. Its origins are old, and reflect the notion that an IS is a system for decision and signalling in a larger system of which it is a subsystem (Langefors, 1966). Verrijn-Stuart (1986) presents it as a straightforward extension of the data processing view, where inputs are understood to derive from observation of an object system (the organization), and outputs are understood to be reports directed to decision and action centers that act upon this system for control purposes. Control of the object system is the raison d'etre of the IS.

The organizational control view thus incorporates the context missing in the data processing view and also provides pragmatics which are missing in the organizational mapping view. However, it also presents some problems. First, IS is still seen in data processing terms. While context is now incorporated (as the object system), it is also substantially externalized. So too is observation, as well as decision and action. IS itself remains focused upon data processing. Secondly, the control metaphor hangs rather heavily on its subject. It invokes rational management action and centralized control of organization in ways which, it may be argued, may not be necessary (Verrijn-Stuart, 1986).

It is worth asking whether our prevailing IS views may be substantially improved.

## 3. Toward Another View

IS views worthy of the term are shared views among researchers and practitioners. A new view does not emerge suddenly in finished form. Rather, it emerges as a consensus of those who share it, usually implicitly, in their work. What follows, therefore, is only a sketch for another IS view.

## 3.1. The Information System Loop

We explore a view based on the concept of an information loop (I-loop). We portray the basic form in Figure 2. The name derives from a metaphor, that of the loop of thread or yarn pulled from a clothing fabric. This fabric from which the I-loop is pulled we term the information context (I-context). For our purposes, an I-context represents an organizational situation in terms of those relationships and communications which bind an organization together and connect it to its environment. The single I-loop is an integral part of this organizational fabric, and is merely pulled out conceptually for closer examination. (It is in this sense that an information system may be said to be “embedded” in an organization.)

![](/api/attachments/C68ZD6AD/fulltext/images/a0846722cc9e438fa7f26160c2b2ce70aca210f8f900c7dd4eae09205ac8d288.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The Information Loop.

The modern organization employs a complex weave of formal and informal I-loops (Stamper, 1973). We interest ourselves here with the more formal of these, which comprise an organization's information systems (IS), wherein information is purposefully and systematically acquired, processed, and employed under the organization's auspices. Here, the usual objects of acquisition, processing, and employment are formally recorded linguistic expressions of various familiar forms (data, rules, queries, reports, algorithms, etc.). We term all of these (formal) I-expressions.

As shown in Figure 2, an IS (when pulled out of its I-context) incorporates a chain of three component processes: information acquisition (I-acquisition), information processing (I-processing), and information employment (I-employment). I-processing keeps and is supported by an information store (I-store). An I-store holds data, models, and procedures. We will have more to say about all of these.

The basic I-loop concept is the simplest of forms, and lends itself to substantial elaboration. It is also abstract in formulation and nonspecific to organization or information system type. Broadly speaking, it extends the original data processing view. It further incorporates important aspects of the organizational mapping and organizational control views.

The notion of I-processing expands upon that of data processing. The view is also extended on both the input and output sides of I-processing. On the input side, it is recognized that data or other I-expressions are the product of an I-acquisition process, which is often a human activity. On the output side, I-expressions such as management reports are seen as provided to an I-employment process, which is also likely to be a human activity, involving inquiry and decision making. I-acquisition makes or draws upon observations of the I-context, describing some aspect of the organizational situation. I-employment directs action upon this same I-context, closing the I-loop.

The principal flow of I-expressions in an information loop is counter-clockwise as portrayed in Figure 2. I-acquisition serves I-processing, which, in turn, serves I-employment. However, a clockwise flow is also understood to exist. Thus, I-acquisition involves an intervention in the I-context which may itself shape the future course of organizational events (Mason and Swanson, 1981). I-processing may be proactive with respect to I-acquisition, rather than merely waiting for data or other I-expressions to be provided. Similarly, I-employment may obtain reports from I-processing by means of queries, as opposed to receiving only those reports thrust upon it. Those who employ I-expressions also shape their world views by the direct observation which comes from their interaction with the organizational situation (Boulding, 1956).

We propose that the basic concepts associated with the I-loop are necessary and sufficient for a useful, though admittedly somewhat superficial, view of IS. By “superficial” we mean that the general surface structure, rather than the deep underlying structure, is seen. To be useful, such a view is understood to satisfy several desiderata: (i) it is accessible to the nonexpert, the intelligent IS user; (ii) it does not distort from the perspective of the expert, the professional IS designer; (iii) it is essentially complete and consistent, though at a high level; (iv) it is parsimonious; and (v) it is robust across IS of varying types, in terms of applicability and insights. We elaborate briefly upon our basic concepts next, and establish their correspondence to terminology employed elsewhere. In the spirit of providing a view accessible to a user, we proceed informally rather than formally.

## 3.1.1. I-context

The I-context corresponds to the “object system” of the organizational mapping and organizational control views. As in these views, an I-context represents some organizational situation. However, an I-context is not limited to that of a single organization, nor is the management control model (Anthony, 1965) necessarily invoked. The purposes of an IS may vary with respect to its I-context. In one situation, for example, organizational learning may be the dominant objective, in which case the actions taken may be the equivalent of experimental probes (Daft and Weick, 1984). In another situation, the purpose may be to provide an electronic market among otherwise independent organizations and individuals (Malone et al, 1987).

An important principal here is that no information is generated by an IS out of its I-context. Any I-loop is “anchored” at both ends (or else it is incompletely described). In general, these anchors do not coincide; that is, the point of intervention for observation usually differs from the point of action for directing change. For example, basic data in a manufacturing control system may be gathered on the shop floor by means of work stations, while summary status information may be directed to management offices where schedules are decided. Implicit, however, is the notion that actions effect changes in the organizational situation, and that these should eventually be reflected in future observations. Thus, a formal or informal communication path exists between the two anchors within the organizational situation. In the manufacturing control system, schedules will be communicated by management so as to direct the work done on the floor.

## 3.1.2. I-acquisition

I-acquisition extends the commonplace notion of data acquisition. It incorporates all tasks which produce I-expressions in an I-context, for I-processing purposes. Included, apart from traditional data acquisition, is the acquisition of those models and procedures also necessary to I-processing. Illustrative of model acquisition is the “knowledge acquisition” which is characteristic of expert system development. Illustrative of procedure acquisition is the formal specification of needed application software.

I-acquisition is largely taken for granted by the organizational control view, which nevertheless attaches heavy significance to decision making and action. This is unfortunate, inasmuch as the latter depends ultimately upon the measurement foundations of the original data acquired (Mason and Swanson, 1981). The importance of I-acquisition is perhaps better appreciated by the organizational mapping view, precisely because of the widely recognized problems and challenges associate with conceptual modeling. (See, e.g., Bubenko, 1986, Hull and King, 1987.) From our vantage point, any such modeling activity constitutes a form of I-acquisition. So too does the programming process which gives rise to the procedures of an IS. The significance of I-acquisition to an IS can therefore hardly be overstated.

## 3.1.3. I-processing

The concept of I-processing extends the common notion of data processing. It incorporates all tasks whose basic inputs and outputs are mere I-expressions. This of course defines the task domain for digital computing and communication. I-processing is thus what computers do. But humans may of course also carry out I-processing tasks. Moreover, even where the computer is the major tool, the task is likely to be embedded in some institutional arrangement, e.g. a data center, where human support is also involved. Data processing is therefore itself embedded in complex social arrangements (Kling, 1987).

I-processing is organized in support of I-employment much as I-acquisition is organized in support of I-processing. Among the most important of I-processing tasks is the maintenance of its I-store.

## 3.1.4. I-store

The concept of the I-store extends the data base concept. The I-store is composed of data, models, and procedures, where each is necessary to the others. While data and procedures are always represented explicitly, models may be represented (in whole or in part) implicitly. Indeed, the task of explicating the models independently from associated data and procedures is among the most challenging problems facing researchers and practitioners. (See, e.g., Brodie and Silva, 1982.)

The data base is a collection of data which typically portrays the state of an organizational situation. Ontologically, its I-expressions are a set of factual assertions about this situation. The semantics of these assertions are not self-contained; rather they presume a data model in which the underlying concepts may be explicated.

The models of an I-store may be of various types. We mention four, where each is formed as a set of assertional I-expressions. The well-known data model asserts certain integrity relationships (rules of association and attribution) to hold among the data of a system. Typically, an integrated data base is thereby defined and described. The analytic model asserts certain (mathematical) functional relationships to hold among given data. An example is the “linear program” formulation common to management science. The inferential model asserts certain (logical) conditional (if-then) relationships to hold among a set of assertions about the world. It is illustrated by the “expert knowledge” common to artificial intelligence. The procedural model specifies a procedure of the overall system, which, when executed, gives rise to its dynamic behavior. Formal program specifications are illustrative (Maibaum, 1986).

Model development requires the use of some modeling approach and language. Chen's (1976) Entity-Relationship (E-R) approach to data base models illustrates, as does Geoffrion's (1988) Structured Modeling for the development of analytic models. Significantly, current modeling approaches and languages are not mutually exclusive across the model types with which they are primarily associated. Rather, their semantic expressiveness frequently overlaps. Thus, Farn (1985) shows that E-R modeling is subsumed by Structured Modeling. Chari (1988) explores the extension of Structured Modeling to knowledge representation in artificial intelligence. This suggests that the development of integrated modeling approaches is an important pursuit (Hull and King, 1987; Geoffrion, 1988). It suggests too that any typology of models is necessarily provisional, pending further studies of semantic correspondence.

The procedures of an I-store incorporate the software employed in I-processing. They include traditional application software for data processing. They also include the “solver” software (e.g. linear program algorithms) applied to management science models (Geoffrion, 1988) and the “engines” applied to making inferences based on expert knowledge (Hayes-Roth, 1985). The concept may further be extended to include the human procedures followed in support of I-processing.

## 3.1.5. I-employment

I-employment incorporates inquiry and decision making as classically understood (Marschak, 1968). In general, I-employment acquires (inquires) and puts the I-expressions produced by I-processing to use in their I-context. It thus generalizes upon the concepts of decision and action centers.

I-employment is best conceived as a human activity, where individuals necessarily bring their “world views” to bear upon the interpretations, choices, and actions (Boulding, 1956; Churchman, 1971). This is not because computers cannot be constructed to emulate intelligent inquirers and deciders; rather it is because human concerns form the basis for acting (ourselves or by means of our computers) upon our organizational world.

I-employment therefore suggests that language be fully empowered, when considering the uses of “information” derived and deployed within an I-context. Such power comes from recognition of the variety of intentionality which may be associated with I-expressions issued in context. Ordinary sentences may be seen to constitute “speech acts”

facilitating social interaction and organizational exchange, for example (Lyytinen, 1985; Winograd and Flores, 1987). “Performative” as well as “informative” I-expressions may be understood as central to individual and organizational action taking (Lee, 1986, 1988). Symbolic information, in the form of conformance to organizational norms and values, may be recognized to be conveyed by I-expressions whose “face value” signals rational inference and choice (Feldman and March, 1981). Organizational information may be seen in general as “two-faced,” in that it is intended to both inform and influence (Swanson, 1978).

We thus argue for a rich concept of I-employment. Without it, we are unlikely to understand how an IS is truly embedded in an organization.

## 3.2. Correspondence With Other Views

The I-loop view of IS is easily related to prevailing existing views. Its concept of I-context corresponds to the object system concept used elsewhere. Its concept of I-acquisition expands upon that of “observation” as it is peripherally understood in the organizational control view. Its concept of I-processing generalizes upon the notion of data processing common to both the data processing and organizational control views. Its concept of I-store incorporates the extended data base of the organizational mapping view. Its concept of I-employment generalizes upon the decision and action centers of the organizational control view.

## 4. Information System Description

The concept of the I-loop has been used in teaching a graduate course in IS applications over a number of years. Here, student teams are required to study real world systems. Each team must: (i) identify and describe an organizational situation in which an IS has been designed and implemented; (ii) describe the IS as it currently exists; and (iii) critique the IS. The concept of the I-loop facilitates the analysis, which emulates a task familiar in one form or another to most IS professionals.

In describing an IS, a feature analysis is frequently helpful. It enables the student to distinguish among different types of IS. The concept of the I-loop supports the analytic task. We illustrate here with a short tutorial on the varieties of data acquisition, data processing, and inquiry and decision common to traditional IS.

## 4.1. Varieties of Data Acquisition

Forms of data acquisition in traditional IS vary substantially. First, observation and acquisition may be periodic, as with an annual physical count of inventory by a merchandiser, or it may be continuous, as with the monitoring of all deposits to and withdrawals from this same inventory, enabling a “perpetual” count of items in stock to be maintained. Periodic and continuous observations may also complement one another, as in the annual reconciliation of inventory records to account for spoilage, theft, etc..

Periodic observation may employ either fixed or variable intervals. The former is illustrated by a weekly project progress report, the latter by the unannounced safety inspection associated with a plant safety program.

Observation may be pro-active or reactive. Pro-active observation may take the form of a scan or a search. In a scan, a particular “space” associated with the organization and its environment is closely examined, and significant objects, events, or situations are “recognized,” as classically illustrated by an air defense system (Sackman, 1967). A search is likely to be more pointed. For example, the legal staff of a business firm may engage in focused fact gathering in support of certain specific litigation.

Reactive observation is likely to be event-driven. Here, a real-world event triggers the data acquisition. For example, in a municipal court system, the initiation of a case through the filing of a formal complaint may trigger the gathering of case data.

Data acquisition may further be manual, as with merchandise ordering in a retail store system, where a buyer completes a worksheet for batched order entry, or it may be automatic, as with sensor-based data gathering in an automatic steel production process. It may also be semi-automatic, as with an online order entry system to replace the manual merchandise ordering method. Where it is semi-automatic, it may further be tightly coupled to data processing, in which case effective human-computer interaction may be crucial.

Data may be originated according to open or closed schema. In the latter case, the items acquired are fully pre-defined; e.g., in merchandise order entry, the worksheet format is likely to be fixed, with department number, manufacturer number, style numbers, order quantities, etc., specified as to position. Where the structure of data is not well-defined and common, an open schema is implied; e.g., as with the facts gathered by the legal staff in support of litigation.

Data acquired may also be classified as internally or externally based, according to whether they are originated inside or outside the organization (Swanson, 1978). The purchase order initiated by the merchandiser is internally based; the invoice received from the vendor is externally based.

## 4.2. Varieties of Data Processing

Forms of data processing also vary. Certain aspects are closely related to those of data acquisition.

Briefly, data processing may be periodic or continuous in its operations. It may further mix these frequencies in its data base update and management reporting tasks.

Data base update may be batch, i.e. periodic, or realtime. Management reporting may similarly be periodic, or it may be continuous, as-needed. As-needed reports may be by exception or on-request. The exception report is triggered upon recognition of some organizational condition; the on-request report is issued in response to the decision maker's query. Exception and on-request reporting may also be undertaken periodically.

Reports may further be status-indicating, alternative-evaluating, or action-initiating. Each type has several variants.

Status-indicating reports characterize the present or past states of the organizational situation. Most management reports are of this type. A simple extension occurs in projecting the past and present into the future, as with a sales forecast extrapolated from a historical time series.

Alternative-evaluating reports provide answers to “what if” questions, enabling the consequences of alternative choices and uncertain events to be explored. They rely upon both data and analytic models of the organizational situation. Simulation is illustrative. Additional evaluation occurs when an alternative is identified as best; optimization models are employed for this purpose.

Action-initiating reports may simply recommend a human action, e.g. placing an inventory order. Or, they may communicate the action directly to an implementation mechanism, as in process control.

## 4.3. Varieties of Inquiry and Decision Making

Forms of inquiring and deciding vary according to decision contexts. Whether the context is structured or unstructured (Simon, 1960, Gorry and Scott Morton 1971) is perhaps the most basic notion. Decisions are structured if well defined controlled variables are specifically related to relevant uncontrolled variables to indicate possible outcomes and their values (Ackoff, 1978). Where such structure exists, inferences and decisions may be straightforward; e.g., in approving a purchase order. Where such structure is absent, much judgement may be necessary; e.g., in interpreting and acting upon a reported budget variance. Where such structure exists and is modeled, deductive aspects of the inquiring and deciding task process become computable.

Inquiring and deciding may be periodic, or it may be continuous. The former occurs in the annual planning and budgeting cycle of an organization; the latter occurs at an airline reservations desk. The airline reservation process is an example of a realtime control system; it requires continuous coordinated operation of its data acquisition, data processing, and inquiring and deciding functions.

Inquiring and deciding may also be pro-active or reactive. In the pro-active case, a manager might originate a query in response to a perceived problem. In the reactive case, an exception report might provide the signal which engages the manager's attention. Increasingly, the two cases are likely to be integrated, requiring tightly-coupled human-computer interaction.

Inquiring and deciding may be individual, that of single person, or it may be organizational, that of a work group or other organizational unit.

Finally, inquiring and deciding is typically conceived as inner-directed, though it may also be other-directed (Swanson, 1978). When inner-directed, the focus is on internal organizational choice. When other-directed, the focus is on influencing the environment, i.e. on affecting choices outside the organization. Information for both purposes may also be involved.

## 4.4. Summary

Our tutorial has shown how traditional IS may be described and compared in I-loop terms. Techniques of IS analysis and design may similarly be introduced from an I-loop perspective. An example is the IS-flow diagram described in the Appendix.

## 5. Information System Problems

The empirical study of IS may be usefully focused upon IS problems; these often arise because of lack of fit between the IS and the organization it serves. From the I-loop view, the issue is one of the effective weave of IS within the organizational fabric.

In general, problem diagnosis and treatment may be facilitated by examining the linkages of an IS, both among its components and to the context in which it is anchored. A focus on the models of an IS – the data, analytic, inferential, and procedural models – is also likely to be helpful. Models provide the semantic foundations of an IS. Where problems arise, they are likely to be reflected in one way or another in the models employed. Unfortunately, our understanding of the relationship of models to IS problems is at present rather primitive.

Early IS incorporated few analytic models, and their data and procedural models were typically implicit, rather than explicit. More recently, the explication of the models underlying IS data and procedures has received much attention. Nevertheless, the underlying models of the many existing IS remain mostly implicit; they are not articulated by means of formal I-expressions. Yet the IS's they support are often deeply embedded in their organizations. From the point of view of empirical study, it is natural to focus on how this occurs, and with what apparent consequences.

Research attention may reasonably be directed to the problems of using existing IS, that is, to I-employment. We speculate that many problems arise here because of model conflict; e.g., where users of a management report make inconsistent or wrong assumptions about the data employed, or because the assumptions of an analytic model are not shared among decision makers, or because the inferential models of experts pose problems in reaching a consensus, or because a transaction processing procedure incorporates features, say administrative rules, which are incompatible with organizational policy.

Model conflict is likely to arise when IS models collide (rather than coincide) with the assumptions of decision makers within the organization. It does not necessarily arise because IS models are incomplete or even inconsistent; nor does it always occur because they have not been explicated. We suspect it arises most often when organizational actions diverge rather than converge, whether at the level of routine administration of organizational procedures or at the level of strategic planning or policy making. Such troubles are likely to invoke organizational reasoning about these actions, revealing the existence of underlying model conflict.

Where there is model conflict, there are opportunities for model resolution. Such resolution may, but need not, involve model explication and formal choice among alternatives. Organizational resolution may also occur because parties simply come to share certain assumptions, explicit or implicit, about their actions, eliminating the source of conflict. Resolution need not be complete or even consistent, we conjecture. On the contrary, much model resolution is probably implicit among organizational participants. Some model conflict is probably also simply tolerated or ignored, as with the use of “work-arounds” (Kling and Scacchi, 1982). Research here is much needed. We know little of why and how model resolution takes place in existing IS.

Empirical studies of IS model conflict and resolution should also improve our design of formal model acquisition processes. Problems in IS model explication are currently well recognized, but not well understood. In part, this reflects naivete as to the nature of these models. Data models, for example, are sometimes seen as describing that which is given in the object system (I-context). From the viewpoint of the I-loop, there are no such “givens,” however. Data models are more taken, than they are given. They embody theory about the I-context, as do all other models. The problems of model acquisition are therefore in part the problems of theorizing about the I-context. Support tools must be recognized to be the tools of everyday theory building in organizations. Provision for model testing in the acquisition process, by examining the fit of a proposed model with that already “known” by the IS, is especially needed.
