# Draining the swamp \*

# Defining strategic use of the information systems resource

Ronald B. Wilkes

Department of Management Information Systems and Decision Sciences, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, Memphis State University, Memphis, TN 38152, USA

There has been a great deal of discussion in information systems (IS) journals and elsewhere on strategic use of the IS resource. These discussions describe very different views of what is meant by strategic IS use. This paper clarifies the concept of strategic use of the IS resource and identifies the issues to address in using the IS resource as a strategic business factor. A model is presented to help distinguish among the differing uses of the term “strategic use of the IS resource” and to guide executives and IS managers in choosing among the many alternatives in using IS strategically.

Keywords: Strategic Planning, Strategy Formulation, Competitive Advantage, Strategic Use of the Information Systems Resource, Strategic Information Systems.

## 1. Introduction

In the early 1950's there were only about fifty computers in use in the world; now there are several millions. Trends in the size, cost, speed, and reliability of computer technology have been such that today, only about thirty-five years after the sale of the first commercial computer system, virtually any organization can afford to own and use computers more powerful than any that existed in 1960. Computers are becoming (and in many organizations have already become) ubiquitous.

The rapid development of computer technology brought with it the attendant problem of transferring to the individuals within organizations the knowledge of what is possible through the use of computers. “Technology transfer” is necessary to combat two opposing problems:

(1) establishment of user expectations that exceed what can reasonably be delivered, and

(2) lack of awareness about areas where computer technology can be deployed to improve organizational efficiency and effectiveness.

There has been a great deal of discussion in IS journals (and elsewhere) of the use of computer-based IS as a strategic business factor. There is evidence $[26]$ that even though IS managers are reading and getting excited about strategic factors, the impact on the use of IS is minimal.

This apparent failure of computer-based IS to affect (rather than just effect) organizational strategy may stem from confusion about the meaning of strategic use of the IS resource. The IS literature has used terminology related to strategic use of the resource in very different ways. This paper clarifies the concept and identifies the issues to address in using it as a strategic business factor. A model is presented to guide executive and IS managers in choosing among the many alternatives.

## 2. The Need and the Failure

The perceived need for strategic use of the IS resource has been described widely in IS literature. More than a decade ago, King [11] observed that “strategic planning for the information needs of the organization is both feasible and necessary if the MIS [management information systems function] is to support the basic purposes and goals of the organization.” Cash et al. [7] identify organizations whose IS function should be managed strategically because “smooth functioning of the IS activity is critical to their operation on a daily basis and… applications under development are critical to their competitive success.” Alloway [1] identified “strategic IS planning and allocation of resources” as one of the main criteria of IS success. Many authors have observed that the IS resource may be used strategically as a “competitive weapon.” Brancheau and Wetherbe [3], in a recent survey, found strategic planning and competitive advantage to be the top two issues of concern to both IS managers and the managers to whom they report.

Despite the many pronouncements of the need for and importance of strategic use of the IS resource, it appears that there is little progress in practice. Although some IS are described as strategic by many writers, the same systems appear to be described ad nauseum. But more important, there is little evidence of a “track record” by organizations in developing strategic information systems (SIS). After years of gathering information about the strategic impact of the IS resource on organizations, Wiseman [28] identifies only a very small number of companies that have what he terms a “strategic information systems vision.”

Further, it appears that IS managers and executive level managers view strategic use of the IS resource differently. Although, as the Brancheau and Wetherbe survey cited above indicates, strategic issues are important to both executive managers and IS managers, Wilkes and Dickson [27] found that IS managers are much more complete and accurate in identifying potential strategic IS strengths and weaknesses than are executives. In analyzing information about IS organizations, IS managers identify more of the types of information that give evidence of the strategic use of information systems and interpret this information more accurately than do executive level managers.

Lederer and Mendelow [14] found that IS planning, and hence strategic use of the IS resource, is impeded by the difficulty in convincing senior management of the potential strategic impact of IS.

Thus, while there is clearly a need to pursue strategic use of the IS resource, it is equally clear that there is a difference in perspective about what this means. To clarify what strategic use of the IS resource entails, it is useful to return to the foundational literature of organizational strategy and strategic decision-making.

## 3. Organizational Strategy and the Strategic Planning Process

Discussion of strategy and strategic processes involves the following three factors: the scope of strategic decision-making, the domains, factors, or forces to be considered in strategy formulation, and the approach to or process for strategic planning. Each of these factors is discussed below.

## 3.1. Scope

The scope of a decision defines its impact on the organization. For decision making to be strategic the decisions must

\- be directed toward defining the organization's relationship to its environment,

\- take the organization as a whole as the unit of analysis,

\- depend on inputs from a variety of functional areas,

\- provide direction for and constraints on administrative and operational activities, and

\- be important to the success of the enterprise. [23]

Thus, strategic decision-making deals with the relationship of the various functional areas of an organization taken as a whole with the external environment. Additionally, strategic decision-making must have some impact on the success of the organization.

## 3.2. Domains

As applied to decisions, the domain is the area of focus or influence. The following domains are typically considered to comprise strategic decision-making:

\- basic mission,

\- customer mix,

\- product mix,

\- service area,

\- goals and objectives,

\- competitive advantage, and

\- outside relationship. [23]

Porter [21], whose work is the basis for much of the recent literature on the strategic use and impact of IS, offers another perspective. He has identified several competing “forces” which need to be considered in strategy formulation. These are characterized as bargaining power of customers, bargaining power of suppliers, current competitors, threat of new entrants into the market, and threat of substitute products or services.

Barriers to entering and exiting markets (and market segments) have significant impact on these forces. A company should employ its assets as flexibly as possible to facilitate: (1) its entry into a product/market area, (2) its mobility within the market, and (3) its exit (in the event that the market sours sufficiently to warrant leaving the market). Employing the assets of a company flexibly to facilitate entry into, mobility within, and exit from a product/market area appear to be fairly obvious strategies. A less obvious strategy is to position a company in a way that increases barriers to entry and mobility of potential competitors. A company can gain strategic competitive advantage by blocking the movement of competitors into its market or market niche and can then potentially reap higher profits. In many instances, blocking the entry or mobility of competitors might entail reducing the flexibility of employment of a company's own assets. Cornering the market on specialized skills or equipment may block potential competitors from entering a market or market niche but may burden the company employing this strategy with assets that may not be easily disposed of or adapted for an alternative strategy if market conditions change.

## 3.3. Strategy Formulation Process

The strategy formulation process consists of the following activities:

\- Develop a strategic profile of the organization identifying how the company defines its business, its competitive posture, and its concept of itself.

\- Develop a strategic forecast of the future political, social, economic, market, product, competitive, and technological environment.

\- Perform a resource audit of the strengths and weaknesses of the organization.

\- Generate a set of strategic alternatives in consideration of the opportunities in the external environment and the strengths and weaknesses of the organization.

\- Test the consistency on the alternatives to insure that they fit the internal capabilities of the organization, the external opportunities, and the profile the organization has defined for itself. This is aligning what it is possible for the company to do in its environment, what it desires to do, and what it is able to do based on its capabilities.

\- Make a strategic choice. [25]

This strategic management literature should be considered in determining the issues involved in strategic use of the IS resource.

## 4. Issues in Strategic Use of the IS Resource

To identify and address the issues in strategic use of the IS resource, it is necessary to determine the nature of this strategic use. The meaning of strategic use of the IS resource in a given context may be determined through answers to the following questions.

Does strategic use of the IS resource refer to support for the strategic decision-making process or to IS as an organizational resource?

Is the focus on classifying existing systems or on the process of identifying potential systems which will have the greatest impact of the success of the organization?

4.1. Supporting Decision-Making or a Factor Considered in Decision-Making

One clarification of the meaning of the term “strategic use of the IS resource” involves distinguishing whether the resource is:

\- used to support the strategic planning process, or

\- a factor considered in the strategic planning process.

IS may be used to support or facilitate the process of strategic managerial decision-making. For example, King [12] observed that IS technology (IST) may be used to support the decision processes of managers involved in strategy formulation and has identified the following requirements of a strategic decision support system (SDSS):

\- An SDSS must have the characteristics of a decision support system,

\- An SDSS must address a class of problems that are strategic in nature,

\- The objective of the system must be to achieve an information-based strategic competitive advantage through: supporting an organizational process, or directly supporting strategic decision-making, and

\- In supporting strategic decision-making, an SDSS must directly address the “unstructuredness” of these decisions.

Of course, not all IS support for the strategic planning process must come in the form of SDSS's. More traditional reporting systems may also serve this function. In a study, White [26] found that when IS managers say that IS is being used strategically in their organizations, they mean that reports generated by IS are providing information which is used in the strategic planning process.

In addition to using IS to support the strategic decision-making process, the IS resource may be considered as an organizational resource in the same sense as capital, labor, etc. in the formulation of strategic plans. It is now widely recognized that information is a valuable organizational resource for many enterprises. There are many examples describing the value of information to organizations. Market research data identifies the types, characteristics, locations, and preferences of customers for firms so they can maximize the effects of advertising, promotion and distribution of products and services. Seemingly less glamorous, simple lists of names and addresses (mailing lists) can have tremendous value. Knowing who supports particular social, political, religious, or humanitarian causes or makes particular mail order purchases is extremely valuable to direct mail solicitors. Some mailing lists are sold for thousands – even tens of thousands – of dollars.

A different issue, but one related to the value of information, is the quality and associated value of the IS which deliver this information to users. Organizations may have both the information and the need for this information to function effectively but have poor mechanisms for delivery of this information to decision-makers. Also, while information and systems to deliver information may currently exist, the ability of the IS organization or function to maintain these systems and to provide additional required systems in the future is critical.

The issue of determining the strategic resource (information, a computer-based system for its delivery, the organizational function responsible for the development and maintenance of IS, or some combination of these) parallels the determination of strategic use. This issue will not be further addressed here, but will be implicit in much of the discussion of how to use IS strategically.

While the use of information and associated systems to support the strategic planning process is important, most of the published literature regarding strategic use of the IS resource deals with the issue of IS as an organizational resource to be considered in strategic planning.

## 4.2. Classifying Existing Systems or Identifying Potential Systems

An important distinction which should be made in using IS strategically is whether the focus is on - classifying existing systems as strategic or not, or

– identifying potential systems that will have the greatest impact on the success of an organization.

Failure to recognize and address this distinction has rendered the term “strategic information system” almost meaningless.

McFarlan et al. [17] developed a framework for assessing the strategic significance of IST to an organization. The framework is used to determine the characteristics of and operational dependence on an organization's existing and planned IST applications and to relate these two categories of applications on a two dimensional matrix. Based on the analysis, the company is classified as a support, turnaround, factory, or strategic organization with respect to the impact of IST.

Companies classified in the strategic category “are both critically dependent on the smooth functioning of the [IS] activity for their daily operations and have applications under development that are vital to their competitive success.” Companies classified as turnaround firms “are not absolutely dependent on the uninterrupted cost-effective functioning of” current IS, but “applications under development… are absolutely vital for the company’s strategic objectives.” Companies in the factory classification “depend heavily on [IS] support for smooth operations” but, new applications “are not fundamental to [their] ability to compete.” Companies considered in the support category are not dependent on either existing systems or new applications of IST for success. The purpose of this classification is to determine the appropriate way to manage the IS resource based on whether it serves a support or strategic role in the organization.

While the work by McFarlan et al. is important in understanding how to manage different types of systems with differing types of impacts on organizations, their work has had an unfortunate (and presumably unintended) side-effect. There is currently wide acceptance for the assertion that some systems are inherently strategic and others are not. But the existing definition of strategic systems omits many systems which appear rather mundane but whose proper functionings are essential to the survival of the organizations that use them.

Furthermore, the classification of an organization in the McFarlan strategic framework can be misinterpreted. Even if a firm is classified as a “support” or “factory” organization, IST can be of strategic importance. The current and projected use of IST may not be the best or even appropriate use of the resource. Moreover, factory or support firms can be threatened with the strategic use of IST by competing firms.

A relevant issue is how to identify (through the planning process) those systems which would offer the greatest contribution to supporting the strategy of the organization (in consideration of the trade-off between costs and benefits). Rather than just classifying IS as “support” or “strategic” after they are identified and developed, it is important that the planning process identifies systems with the potential to enhance the competitive position of the organization significantly.

## 4.3. Consideration of the IS Resource in Planning

There are three approaches to considering strategic use of IS in the planning process:

\- IS are developed without explicit consideration of corporate strategy.

\- The IS resource is evaluated in the implementation of corporate strategy.

\- The IS resource is evaluated in the formulation of corporate strategy.

## 4.3.1. Traditional Role of Information Systems Technology

Historically, the use of IST has focused on issues such as lowering operating costs, improving operating efficiency, and automating routine procedures. There was little consideration of how such systems could increase managerial effectiveness. Even less attention was given to the ways IS would affect the strategic competitive balance among the businesses within an industry.

Applications developed under the traditional approach are “back office” in nature, supporting operating activities such as processing customer orders, maintaining inventory records, and automating accounting processes. Justification for investment in these systems is largely based on cost displacement or avoidance, or intangible benefits $[13]$ , particularly as they apply to automation of high volume and repetitive tasks.

Organizations gradually recognized the value of computer and communication technology for the timely delivery of accurate information. Managers have also increased their awareness of the ways in which computer-based decision-support systems can support their decision-making. But until recently there was little discussion or recognition of the potential role of IS in implementing or formulating the strategy of an organization.

## 4.3.2. Using IST to Implement Strategic Plans

Students of management and IS have long sought to invest the firm's IS resources in a way that best supports the mission and strategy of the organization [13]. One well-known approach [11] is to:

\- Identify the “strategy set” of the organization. (A strategy set consists of a firm’s mission statement, the organizational objectives, the organizational strategy, and strategic organizational attributes, such as management strengths).

\- Identify stakeholder groups of the organization.

\- Identify the operating objectives of the stakeholder groups and constraints that pertain to the development of IS.

\- Develop information strategies using these results.

This “strategy set transformation” technique is widely recognized. In their 1981 evaluation of a large number of planning tools and methods, strategy set transformation was the only method Bowman et al. [2] identified for conducting strategic planning of the use of the IS resource.

As this series of activities suggests, organizations should first determine corporate mission and strategies and then fit their IS plan to the organizational strategy. This approach, however, considers only the way that IST can help implement existing organizational strategy. It does not take into consideration the ways in which IS can influence and help define the organizational strategy.

More recent research has focused on the role of IS in the formulation of organizational strategy.

## 4.3.3. Consideration of the IS Resource in Strategy Formulation

Two dimensions of consideration of the IS resource in the strategic planning process are described below:

(1) the type of impact that IS can have on an organization, and

(2) how to determine what systems will deliver the greatest positive impacts.

Clearly, it is important to understand how the IS resource can affect the success of an enterprise and how to realize the greatest possible positive impact from this resource.

Parsons [20] identified a three-level impact of IST on businesses: industry, firm, and strategy. This is described below.

IST can have the following effects on an industry:

\- Change the products and services offered. The publishing industry is now providing electronic versions of its publications. This changes the distribution channels of its products and reduces the time lag for receipt of news and other information.

-- Change the markets serviced by an industry. Automated teller machines and electronic funds transfer have expanded the geographic presence of banks and other financial institutions. The reduction in the cost of offering many financial services has also made these services available to a much broader range of customers. The growing availability of teleconferencing and the reduction of cost in that area will create more customers for that technology but may also reduce the amount of business travel with consequent implications to airlines, hotels, and restaurants.

\- Change the economics of production. In some cases it will create economies of scale where none existed before and in other cases will negate the effects of current economies of scale. It may also reduce or eliminate the tradeoff between standardization and customization for products in some industries.

IST can affect the competitive forces identified by Porter [20] which act on a firm.

\- A competitive advantage over the buyers of a firm's products or services can be created in two ways.

(1) Costs of switching to alternative suppliers can be increased. This might be done by installing order entry terminals in a buyer's business, facilitating quick and relatively error free response to the orders but also causing the buyer to integrate its operations with the supplying firm and incur certain training costs in how to use the system. If the buyer subsequently elects to go with another supplier, then it must incur the costs of modifying its procedures and re-training its personnel. Just-in-time inventory systems can also tie buyers to the supplying firm and increase switching costs.

(2) Better information can be gathered and processed to improve the negotiation edge of the supplying firm.

\- A competitive advantage over the suppliers to a firm can be gained through the use of IST. Labor needs can be reduced (and bargaining power gained over the labor force) through the use of robotics, computer aided design, and computer aided manufacturing. Information systems to track current economic information can aid banks and other financial institutions in having complete and current information when dealing with savers who supply the money that the banks lend.

\- Barriers to the entry of new businesses into a market is one of the major strategic benefits to be derived from the use of IST. “Locking in” customers through the use of intercompany information systems is occurring throughout industry. On-line order entry systems, automated teller machines, and just-in-time inventory systems are just the tip of the iceberg. More and more companies are being tied together electronically and the companies which manage these systems best will experience significant competitive advantage.

\- Rivals within an industry can use all of the tools described above. Also, companies can gather information about the competition within their industry to gain a competitive advantage. Information about the status of other companies in the industry and the forces affecting them can now be gathered and processed more easily.

IST can have several effects on the generic strategy of a specific firm in an industry. Parsons [20] describes many types of IS that can be developed to support low cost producer or product differentiation strategies for product design and development, operations, marketing, sales, and administration functions in an organization.

The second dimension of strategic consideration of the IS resource deals with how the resource is considered in the strategic planning process. It is in the areas of performing a resource audit of the strengths and weaknesses of the organization and in generating alternatives in consideration of these strengths and weaknesses and the opportunities in the external environment that the potential of the IS resource has been largely overlooked.

McFarlan [16] has posed the following questions (based on Porter's work) to help determine if an opportunity exits for an organization to use IS strategically.

\- Can IST be used to build barriers to entry?

\- Can IST change the basis of competition?

\- Can IST be used to generate new products?

\- Can IST be used to build in switching costs?

\- Can IST change the balance of power in supplier relations?

Viewing the role of the IS resource from Porter's competitive forces perspective, the IS resource appears to have the ability to offer a company advantages over potential competitors through blocking these competitors' entry into and movement within a product/market area and still allowing the company the flexibility to move within and exit the product/market area if conditions warrant.

Much of the technology and expertise necessary to develop computer-based information and control systems is not industry specific, but companies which possess high levels of expertise in developing these types of systems and which have good general purpose computer systems can adapt them to the needs of a specific product/market area. It is generally much easier to build computer-based systems when there is a solid base of relevant experience and equipment.

Data communications capabilities are valuable for any geographically dispersed organization which requires the sharing of information on a timely basis. Communications networks facilitate the flow of information among the various locations of a company, regardless of the type of company business. These communications networks and the expertise gained in developing them can also be applied to specific product/market areas. Data communications capabilities are also critical to the competitive advantages provided by interorganizational IS [6] [10] [24].

Ives and Learmonth [9] present a prescriptive model - The Customer's Resource Life Cycle

Model – for “discovering new [strategic] opportunities for the successful application of information system technologies.” In the context of this model, an application of IST is considered to be strategic “if it changes the firm’s product or the basis for competition within the industry.” This model is an extension of work by Burnstine [4] and IBM [5].

Each of the thirteen stages in the model are to be considered separately in determining if there is some way that IST can provide a strategic “edge” during that stage of a resource’s life cycle. Examples of strategic use of IS for several stages in their model are given below.

\- Specify - Some suppliers send out products (such as greeting cards and books) with attached computer cards that are returned when the product is sold. These cards then inform the suppliers what items were sold and need to be restocked.

\- Order - Several firms are now providing “electronic catalog” systems which allow customers to place orders through computer terminals and have the products delivered to their homes or offices.

\- Authorize - Nationwide networks of automated teller machines determine in seconds if a customer may make cash withdrawals or purchases.

\- Acquire - Customers of commercial data base providers can receive news and other information directly through computer-based communications systems.

\- Test and Accept - Computerized pharmacy systems check "patient profiles" before filling prescriptions to determine if the patient is taking other drugs that may interact in some way.

\- Integrate (into inventory) – Television stations use computerized air-time inventory management systems to pre-book commercial advertising orders.

\- Monitor - Inventory systems monitor usage patterns to plan proper stock levels and reorder times.

Upgrade - Supermarkets use sophisticated systems to track sales and plan future shelf space allocations.

\- Maintain - Retailers (e.g. Sears) keep track of purchases of appliances to notify customers of the availability of service contracts.

Rackoff et al. [22] prescribe a planning process to identify opportunities for the development of SIS. They consolidate the major competitive moves that may be made by a firm into five “strategic thrusts” (differentiation, cost, innovation, growth, and alliance) and look for opportunities to make these moves in relation to each of the three “strategic targets” (suppliers, customers, and competitors). Their planning process attempts to identify SIS opportunities through a series of “SIS idea generation meetings” comprised of the following steps:

\- present a tutorial on competitive strategy and SIS,

\- apply SIS concepts to actual cases,

– review the company's competitive position,

\- brainstorm for SIS opportunities,

– discuss SIS opportunities,

\- evaluate SIS opportunities, and

\- detail SIS “blockbusters” (i.e., opportunities having the potential for strategic dominance).

While these models and frameworks for determining and identifying systems which can have positive implications to the success of organizations are important and valuable, this is clearly an area which requires much more development. It is essential that we provide better guidance to practitioners to help match the strengths and weaknesses of their IS resource to the problems and opportunities in their markets.

## 5. Implications

Figure 1 presents alternative meanings of strategic use of the IS resource. This can guide organizations in determining ways the IS resource can and should be employed to further their performance and success.

It should be pointed out that the various alternatives are not necessarily incompatible. For example, an organization may appropriately choose to support its strategic planning process through the use of an SDSS and to also use IS to implement known strategic plans. What is evident from the model is that there are several alternatives for using the IS resource in a manner that will impact the success of an organization.

Recent studies have looked at the factors which facilitate the development of SIS. Nath [20] identified the following factors as highly ranked by both MIS and general managers in facilitating “alignment of MIS with business goals:”

educating upper management about the potential for SIS,

\- gaining upper management commitment to MIS,

\- having/developing a strong set of organizational goals and objectives about MIS,

\- involving MIS management in the strategic planning processes of the organization, and

\- educating MIS management about the goals and objectives of the business.

Lederer and Mendelow [15] propose the following techniques that IS organization can use to overcome the reluctance of top management to recognize the potential strategic impact of IS:

\- educate top management about the strategic role is IS,

\- market the accomplishments of the IS organization to top management,

\- have the users “sell” the strategic importance of IS,

\- promote a business image for the IS organization,

\- respond to outside forces (customers, suppliers, competitors, etc.),

\- captilize on changes to management, and

\- perform IS planning.

Neo [19], while identifying some of the same factors cited above as important in facilitating the development of SIS, determined that the existence of current systems (which in many cases had been developed to improve internal operational efficiency) and opportunities or threats from the external environment were key to the development of SIS. The external opportunities or threats acted as “triggers” for the modification or enhancement of existing efficiency oriented systems into strategic systems. Thus, it is important when looking for opportunities to facilitate the development of SIS, the potential of existing systems not be overlooked.

The cost of IST is rapidly declining while reliability and capability is improving. Knowledgable and creative managers are recognizing that there is a much wider application of IST than was previously thought. To succeed in the business climate of the future, companies must become aware of the strategic impact that IST may have on their organization, their industries, and all the competitive forces with which they must contend. And as Hufnagel [8] has observed, this competitive environment is very dynamic and SIS planning will require continuing enthusiasm and creativity.

Much of the material describing the strategic implications of the use of the IS resource has created a misunderstanding of the meanings and implications of strategic planning. The model provided in this paper is intended to clarify the issues to be addressed in strategic use of the IS resource and to serve as a guide to help managers identify opportunities for this strategic use. Managers who ignore these critical issues and the impact that IST can have on their business environments do so at their own peril.
