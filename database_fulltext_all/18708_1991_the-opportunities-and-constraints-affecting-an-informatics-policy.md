# The opportunities and constraints affecting an informatics policy \*

# The India experience

Kailash Joshi and Vicki L. Sauter

University of Missouri-St. Louis, School of Business Administration, St. Louis, MO 63121-4499, USA

We present an analysis of the issues that have influenced the development of informatics policy of India. Factors such as impact on employment, trade, and foreign exchange have acted as constraints in the adoption of information technology (IT). At the same time, IT presented a significant opportunity for accelerating progress towards meeting India's goals of self reliance and faster economic development. IT was also viewed as necessary for India's strategy of development, which is based upon centralized planning and management of the economy. We also discuss the role of potential demand for computer systems, the existing industrial base, R&D infrastructure, availability of technical manpower, and regulations for import, foreign investment, and licensing in determining the policies for indigenous manufacture of computer systems. We conclude with a discussion of India's informatics policies and experience in implementing them.

Keywords: Developing countries, India, Information systems, Implementation, Development management, Impact of information technology.

## Introduction

Several developing nations have faced the issue of developing a suitable policy framework to address the opportunities, challenges and, in some cases, dilemmas posed by developments in information technology (IT) (e.g., [1,12]). After a humble beginning as a tool for speeding up calculations and reducing clerical effort, IT has assumed strategic dimensions. Today it is widely recognized as a strategic technology that can impact all aspects of development and economic activities. The potential of IT for increasing productivity in a wide range of industrial and service sectors is fairly well recognized [23]. In addition, IT has the potential to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of government and development processes as well [26,28,29]. Indeed, in the long run the level of innovation, technology, international competitiveness, and defense capability of a nation are likely to be influenced by the extent and the degree of sophistication in its use of computer technology.

However, it may not be easy to incorporate the technology into existing systems, especially in developing nations [25]. Such incorporation may be costly and may be viewed as reducing employment opportunities and threatening existing social and power structures. Therefore, to encourage the optimum use of IT and to minimize its negative impacts, several developing nations have formulated informatics policies [e.g., 30]. These policies address issues of import, manufacture, and usage of computer hardware and software technology and their products. Informatics policies also cover the full range of potential and actual applications arising from the convergence of computer and telecommunication technologies.

In order to better understand the problems and opportunities faced by developing nations, it is useful to examine the issues associated with the development of informatics policy in developing nations. This paper examines some issues and constraints in the formulation of informatics policy in India, which illustrate the development problems of nearly one sixth of the human race. India is a country with a very low gross national product, yet it is also among the leading developing nations in terms of science, technology, and industry. In order to come to grips with these seemingly contradictory characteristics, India has attempted to develop and implement an informatics policy. The Indian experience can be useful for policy makers and consultants of other developing nations.

## The Indian environment affecting the informatics policy adoption

Indian planners have faced the need to strike a balance between those factors against computerization versus those favoring its use by developing an informatics policy. Throughout the seventies, the Indian government adopted a closed door policy and ignored the growing acceptance of computers in other countries. Only a few large corporations were permitted to import computers for their own use. Towards the end of that decade, however, the balance between factors against and for computerization was shifting in favor of computerization; there was a growing recognition that India could ignore IT only at the peril of its advancement and international competitiveness. The efficiency and productivity of government, industry, and service sector could not be enhanced without IT. The labor costs in organized sectors had risen considerably (causing the recognition of the need for productivity enhancements); at the same time, the cost of computer hardware and technology was declining, making IT more attractive. As a result, in the early eighties, the government made some minor relaxations in the import policy towards computer technology. Yet, there was no dramatic change, because the political parties and the labor unions were still entrenched in their old stand against computerization.

However, the introduction of the strong interest of Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi, with his strong belief that India should become involved in this technology, forced the government to re-examine the opportunities and constraints of using IT, and to consider India's needs to develop a suitable informatics policy. The government was actively considering the development of a domestic computer industry to meet India's ambitions to join the information age. Conditions in four general areas affected the development of the plan: (1) the impact on employment; (2) the impact on trade and foreign exchange; (3) perceptions about India's role in the community of nations, and (4) India's goals and her strategy for economic development. Each of these are now discussed.

## Impact on employment

Among the factors against computerization, the foremost is its potential impact on the work force. India has a very large population base with problems of unemployment and under-employment. Indian planners, political parties, and trade unions opposed the introduction of IT believing it would worsen the unemployment problem. Even now, labor unions and opposition parties oppose further introduction of IT (see, for example, [5]). Historically IT has been viewed as a capital intensive strategy that would reduce employment and drain capital resources, therefore not a attractive proposition for a poor country like India.

The opposition to IT may also be rooted in the impact of IT on the relative power of different constituencies. Labor unions feel that increased automation would reduce their bargaining power by reducing the dependence upon manual work. Such structural changes have taken place in the US; for example during a recent strike at various Bell Telephone companies, the telephone systems worked without serious interruptions at least for an initial period. The government bureaucracy may also feel threatened due to the potential loss of control over information resources.

## Trade and foreign exchange

The second factor against computerization is the acute scarcity of foreign exchange for importing computer technology, hardware, and software. India needs to import petroleum, capital equipment, technology, arms, chemicals, food products, and many other products. However, India cannot earn sufficient foreign exchange to pay for its imports in view of limitations on the export front [2]. The declining foreign aid and India's attempts to adjust to it only exacerbates this problem. As a result, India has experienced an acute shortage of foreign exchange resources that are required for many competing sectors of the economy.

To address the problems of the balance of trade, the government maintains a strict control over foreign exchange usage in an attempt to conserve and use it to meet the most critical and high priority needs. In view of the low priority accorded to computerization and the high costs of imported technology, the government actively discouraged the import of computer technology and hardware. The policy included restrictions on software and hardware importation and heavy import duties on them. Therefore, only a very small number of large corporations were permitted to import computers. Hence, India had very little experience with computer technology and people were not aware of what it could do for the country.

Perceptions about its role in the community of nations

After nearly two centuries of foreign rule, India achieved independence in 1947 with one of the lowest per capita incomes in the world, at about \$54 a year [21]. The literacy rate, only 14% at that time, was among the lowest. The infrastructure was quite underdeveloped and the annual rate of growth in agriculture and GNP was below 1% during the last one hundred years.

India, however, views the two hundred years of foreign rule as a period of aberration in its long history. Though India had become politically weak before foreign rule, it had a strong economy with exports in demand all over the world. Some Indian analysts [20] have noted that India was as advanced industrially, commercially, and financially as any other country before the industrial revolution and foreign occupation in the eighteenth century. The strong Indian economy acted as a magnet for the emerging powers of Europe, who were actively seeking a sea route to India.

In times of peace and political stability (particularly between the 4th and 15th centuries), Indian scientists exhibited high creativity. The tradition of a strong mathematical orientation in India dates back thousands of years. Indian mathematicians introduced zero and developed the decimal place system for representing numbers, which has been described as important as the development of computers in human progress. Indian mathematicians had also calculated the value of Pi (3.1416) to 17 decimal places, developed methods for extracting square roots, cube roots, areas of triangular, trapezium, circle, and volume of spheres and pyramid, summation of series, and solutions for equations of first and second degree [22,27]. Some of these mathematical endeavors appeared in Europe nearly a thousand years later [6,32].

India's accomplishments later declined sharply due to political instability. Under British occupation, it lost the ability to keep pace with the rapid technological developments. Though, during the first half of the twentieth century, Indian mathematicians such as Ramanujam, Bose, and Mahanobolis made a significant contribution in the area of mathematics. Indian planners recognized that, given peace and stability, through suitable scientific and industrial policies and initiatives India would regain its lost position in the scientific and economic spheres consistent with its size and population. IT offered new opportunities for accelerating India's plans for development and for meeting Indian planners' emerging expectations. Therefore, this factor motivated a favorable response to the adoption of IT.

## India's goals and development strategy

The major goals of India's economic plans have been faster growth, equity, and self reliance since the beginning of the planning process in the fifties. India has attended to the choice of appropriate and advanced technologies for development of its economy as well as to the development of industrial capacity by allocating increasing sums to science and technology since independence [19]. Furthermore, India has especially attempted to retain independence in all spheres of scientific and industrial endeavors in order to remove or reduce foreign influence, pressure, or control. This has also meant increasing attempts to move towards self-reliance in all fields of economic activity, such as defense production, nuclear energy, heavy industries, and agriculture. The quest for independence has also meant stress on rapid absorption and indigenization of new technologies, which also includes IT.

India has adopted a planned approach to economic development through a central planning commission which attempts to develop five year plans for the country. In order to formulate and monitor development plans effectively, information is needed from more than 400,000 villages. However, with manual systems it is very difficult to collect and aggregate data in a useful time frame. Often the data is years old. Computers can play a major role in the planning process by greatly speeding up the collection and aggregation of data and improving the monitoring and implementation of plans.

Computers are also very important for effective management of large government owned centralized operations and services, such as railways, banks, food grain procurement and distribution, airlines, telephone systems, and electricity generation and distribution. Therefore, India's centralized approach to management of many sectors of the economy and its objective of self-reliance demand adoption of IT.

## Economic and industrial infrastructure

Perhaps the most important factor affecting the development of the specific parts of an informatics policy with regard to manufacture and import of computer technology is the country's economic and industrial infrastructure. Policy decisions are largely governed by such factors as the nature and extent of indigenous demand, availability of the necessary infrastructure, and manpower resources.

Several factors are likely to influence the viability and survival of an indigenous computer industry. O'Connor has discussed several factors that may be relevant in formulating a policy. Some of the relevant factors are: (1) the nature and extent of the potential market; (2) the technological base; (3) a research and development infrastructure; (4) a highly skilled and educated labor force; (5) policies regulating import, foreign direct investment, and licensing activities. These are discussed in the light of India's strengths and weaknesses.

The size of the domestic market is a critical factor in assessing the prospects of an indigenous computer industry. However, the nature of informatics policy may itself have significant implications for the use of computers in public and private sectors. Therefore, the rate of expansion and the nature of demand in the local market can be manipulated through a suitable informatics policy.

India's capacity to absorb computer systems is likely to be very high. India's industrial output has been ranked twelfth internationally, and it has large service sectors. For example, the Indian railway system is one of the largest in the world, covering 62,000 kilometers and catering to 10 million passengers daily. Also, there are 57,000 bank branches, and the number is expected to grow to meet the requirements of about 400,000 villages. The government and public utilities apparatus is also massive, with more than 20 million employees. India's electronics goods market is also rapidly growing. Around 120 million middle class people represent a large growing consumer market [13,14]. Already, the electronic goods production has been increasing at a rate of about $80\%$ per year since 1984 [8,9]. Therefore, a suitable informatics policy could create a substantial demand with a high growth rate that is likely to be sustained for a long period.

Second, India has a technology base. The existence of an accumulated basis of technical know-how is necessary for progress beyond initial assembly type operations. India has developed a large pool of technical personnel who can absorb new manufacturing technologies, though much of the manufacturing technology and sophisticated capital equipment would need to be imported. However, with the extensive demand, the cost of importing the technology can be spread over a wider base.

The third issue is the existence of a widespread research and development (R&D) infrastructure. The existence of a comprehensive R&D infrastructure is essential to the development of a computer technology infrastructure. In India, the government has paid attention to the development of this infrastructure since independence [16]. Research institutions have been established in the area of agriculture, defense, atomic energy, space, medicine, science, and industry. Indian R&D has achieved success in areas of agriculture, atomic energy, space, and defense among other areas. However, technological innovation remains weak. R&D programs in Indian industries are relatively small. At its current stage of development, import of developed technology has been viewed as less risky and more economical by the industrial sector. However, lately Indian R&D has successfully designed commercial vehicles, electronic exchange, process for the manufacture of pure silicon, etc.

In terms of scientific publications, India is ahead of all other developing nations and ranks at the same level as Italy. In a review of world scientific literature (based on the Science Citation Index), India ranked eighth in terms of publication; indeed it is the only Developing Nation in top ten. Long notes that the international status of Indian scientific and technological system is high by these four measures: number of scientists and engineers, expenditure on science and technology, production of scientific and technical knowledge, and establishment of R&D programs oriented to national development. Therefore, India has the potential to establish R&D centers for computer hardware and software.

The fourth issue is the extent of a highly skilled and educated labor force. The computer industry is knowledge-intensive. For the computer industry, skilled and highly trained scientific, engineering and technical manpower is the single most important asset. Without a suitable manpower resource base, the computer industry is unlikely to develop, even if backed by adequate financial resources.

The issue of manpower resources represents a major strength for India. Though inadequate to meet the needs of the total population, the Indian education system represents one of the most extensive and advanced scientific and technical education systems among developing nations. India has about 150 universities with more than 3 million college level students. Consequently, India has the world's third largest pool of technical and scientific manpower.

India allocates a high proportion of education expenditure for higher education (nearly 30%) compared to most developing nations. The Indian percentage spent on higher education is comparable to that of high-income countries, such as the United Kingdom, the United States, and West Germany. Based on the number of students enrolled in higher education as a percent of total population aged 20–24, India is a considerable distance ahead of other countries of comparable income level. In 1979, India had 9% of the relevant population enrolled in higher education, compared to 1% in China, 2% in Pakistan, and 3% in Malaysia. Furthermore, India has a large number of students studying abroad. Long estimates about 20,000 Indian students are studying in the United States alone and that about half of them will return to India.

Finally the issue of policies regulating import, foreign investment, and licensing is also important for the development of informatics policy: India has some strengths and weaknesses with respect to this. In order to promote self reliance, India follows a policy of controls and high tariffs on imports. This favors indigenous manufacture. However, controls on import of technology have also meant delays and difficulties in obtaining technologies for indigenization. India's policies on foreign investment have discouraged both it and the development of an indigenous capacity. India imposes a limit of 40% on the foreign owned equity for most foreign investments; this is intended to conserve the outflow of foreign exchange in the form of dividends, but it limits the attractiveness of foreign investment and discourages the flow of technology. Attempts to regulate the market through licensing have also caused delays and lack of competition. The overall result has been a high price structure, lack of competition, and slow development of industry in general. However, if the government follows a more liberal policy, faster growth is likely.

These factors should help determine the specific details of informatics policy with regard to the import and indigenous development of the computer industry. Consideration of the strengths and weaknesses of these areas would help determine the best strategy for the development of a computer industry which is consistent with the overall informatics plan.

## The Indian informatics policy

After the 1984 elections, Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi, put into effect a new policy designed to promote the electronics and computer industries in India. The government recognized IT as a strategic input for improving the productivity of the governmental, industrial, and service sectors. It was recognized that the Indian plans for scientific, technological, and defense progress could not be achieved without corresponding progress in the use of IT. A decision was made that scarce foreign exchange resources should be allocated for importing computing hardware, software and related technologies in order to develop their application and indigenous manufacture in a phased manner.

The Indian government announced a new computer policy in 1984, followed by a revised software export policy in 1986 [17] to put into effect its new perceptions about the role of IT in India's development. The objectives of 1984 computer policy [11] were to:

(1) promote appropriate application of computers that are of a development catalyzing nature, with due regard to the long term benefit of computerization for the country as a whole;

(2) enable manufacture of computers in the country using the latest technology and at comparable international prices while progressively increasing the indigenization consistent with economic viability; and

(3) simplify existing procedures to enable users to obtain needed computers either from indigenous sources or from overseas, as regulated by fiscal measures.

The policy laid down specific reductions in import tariffs and liberalization of import and licensing regulations to encourage domestic manufacturing and progressive indigenization. While import of micro and mini-computers was deregulated, import of mainframe computers was to be cleared on a case by case basis.

Indigenous manufacture of computers was to be protected from foreign competition through import tariffs on items that were being manufactured locally. However, the duties were to be gradually reduced to encourage reduction in the price of indigenous products to come into line with international prices. The policy also simplified the import of computer software with relatively lower levels of tariffs.

The software export policy of 1986 further liberalized the import of computer hardware and software for supporting software exports [18]. Unlimited import of software and hardware was permitted for software exporters, however, stringent conditions were specified to ensure that the foreign exchange outlay is recovered through software exports within a few years.

The government recognized that software export offered India a unique opportunity to exploit its competitive advantage and strengths relative to other developing nations. Software exports provided an opportunity for earning foreign exchange and offsetting the cost of importing computer technology. It also provided employment opportunities for the large pool of English speaking technical manpower that is 10 to 20 times cheaper than the manpower available in developed nations. Improvement in employment opportunities could also help reduce the flight of talented graduates to developed nations. Finally, contacts with Western corporations should accelerate the flow of new technology from abroad and promote accelerated development of indigenous skills and technology. The government hoped that with these advantages India could develop into a major software development center.

In order to facilitate the development of suitably trained manpower, the government is setting up four new institutes of IT at the national level along with increased emphasis on computer programs at nearly 250 institutes of higher education. Further, computer education is to be introduced at an additional 100 higher education institutes each year. The government has also announced incentives for the establishment of private sector institutes for computer education and training to encourage rapid expansion in trained computer manpower. Provisions have been made for easier import of computer hardware and software by the education/training sector, hosting of foreign experts, and import of consulting services in education technology, along with training equipment and aids.

The government has also set up a center for the development of advanced computing technologies (C-DACT). C-DACT has been asked to develop a high speed computer for scientific applications within the next three years, along with the necessary hardware components and software modules using a parallel processing architecture [4]. The government has also announced plans for setting up dedicated data communications networks for banks, railways, central and state governments, and corporate sector. In addition, the government has announced plans for setting up a dedicated network with overseas satellite links to promote software exports through these links.

The results of the policy incentives have been positive. The total electronics output jumped from \$926 million in 1984 to \$5420 million in 1989. In the area of computers, production increased from \$50 million in 1984 to an estimated \$584 million in 1989. If falling prices, such as a 50% drop in 1985, are considered, the increase in volume would be even higher.

Nearly 750 technical collaboration efforts have been approved involving the import of technology in electronics and computers since 1985. In addition to manufacturing, a large number of western corporations have entered in the field of software export through either fully owned enterprises or through tie-ups with local entrepreneurs [7,24]. Some of these include Microsoft, Ashton-Tate, Texas Instruments, Digital, Hewlett Packard, Intergraph, UNISYS, Citicorp, Grindleys, Nixdorf, AT&T, and Bell South. Some corporations, such as Bull, have also decided to establish hardware and software R&D centers in India. Indian engineers are also working in France on the redesign of an operating system for Bull. An Indian firm has decided to put up a plant in the USA for the manufacture of computer hardware designed in India, having obtained initial orders from the US market.

The result is that the export of software has gone from \$22 million in 1984 to \$38 million in 1986, \$54 million in 1987, and about \$100 million in 1988; the optimistic target for 1990 has been set at \$230 million. Further, nearly all sectors of the economy have announced plans for the adoption of IT [15]. Most private sector enterprises have initiated the development of computer systems, and the government sector has initiated computerization in all its sectors, ranging from management of criminal records, land ownership records, food distribution, and state and central government offices to service sectors, such as airlines, banks, railways, etc. Because state sponsored institutions cannot keep pace with the unprecedented demand for computer education and training, there has been a rapid proliferation of private training institutes for diploma and certificate courses [3].

There has been no significant resistance to computerization, except in the case of government owned banks. There, trade unions succeeded in imposing severe restrictions on the scope and extent of computerization, at least in the initial stages. However, since then the public attitude towards computerization has changed favorably, following the successful implementation of a railway reservation system and the resultant improvement in service. With a favorable public attitude toward computerization and increasing public pressure for better service, it is expected that bank management should be able to persuade unions to drop restrictions on computerization in the next round of negotiations.

With this expected success, the resistance to computerization in India has been largely overcome. Factors such as economics, availability of hardware, software, and resources to acquire them, perspective of individual decision makers, and organizational issues would continue to affect the development of computer systems in each organization. Therefore, the informatics policy initiative of Indian government has succeeded in removing public resistance and in fostering favorable attitudes for future growth of computerization in all sectors of economy. However, it may take some time before all sectors of the economy are fully computerized, given the large size of India and its resource constraints.

## Discussion of issues in the formulation of informatics policy

The foremost issue in the formulation of informatics policy has been the likely impact on employment. Unfortunately, there is little to guide the planners in the assessment of the impact of computerization on employment. In the absence of any objective basis, the debate has been heavily influenced by the political parties who have discouraged computerization, with a eye on the large trade union voting block.

Such apprehension may be unwarranted, however. For example, Gold [10] notes that the potential effects of computerization on competitiveness in a manufacturing environment “ensures its increasing diffusion not only in advanced industrial countries, but also in developing nations which recognize that resulting gains in competitiveness may increase employment despite the labor displacing effects of this technology.”

Therefore, the impact of the introduction of IT upon overall employment is unclear. On the one hand, the demand for routine clerical skills may decline but the demand for higher level skills is likely to increase. Hence, there is reason to believe that if appropriate educational policies can be embraced, the impact of IT upon employment may be smaller than anticipated. Further, as noted by Gold, improvement in productivity is likely to generate higher levels of employment.

The relevant question is whether or not a country can obtain a competitive advantage by pressing its manpower to perform non-productive tasks at a higher cost, especially when compared to the adoption of available automation technology. It would appear that the optimum solution would be to permit the automation technology to compete with the existing labor intensive technology on the basis of cost in a free market. In this case, the relative cost/benefit ratios would guide the development and adoption of the new technology. For example, if developing nations adopted a strategy that is employment intensive, there would be no automatic telephone exchanges; only operator assisted telephone exchanges would be installed. However, no developing nation has opted for the manual exchange. Even in developing nations, labor is not free and more productive uses of labor are possible. The benefits of higher productivity can be realized even when social and political factors prohibit layoff or reduction in staff levels. For example, in India, government-owned banks are also assigned many development-related tasks. In this environment, if some manpower is freed from clerical work, it can be usefully redeployed for loan processing, follow up on current loans, providing various new financial services, extending banking services to rural areas, and participating in various extension and development oriented schemes assigned to lead banks for each district. It should be possible to attract employees to take new jobs where they could make a contribution, via proper incentive structure and training schemes, leaving computers to perform the drudgery of routine documentation and clerical work.

One important element of India's informatics policy is that it has not attempted to prohibit or prescribe areas for computer application. In a controlled economy, an argument could be made that informatics policy should attempt to identify high and low priority areas and attempt to direct the development of computer applications according to them; the direction of computerization has been left to market forces. This permits automation technology to compete with manually operated systems, and encourages adoption only if the new technology proves to be economic; i.e., promotes productivity.

## Conclusion

Various factors have shaped the informatics policy of India over the past two decades. Being a democratic state, the government has been very sensitive to possible negative public reaction to computerization. Strong opposition was expressed by trade unions, political parties, and some academics. Public anxiety was fueled by fears of widespread loss of white collar jobs. Economic planners were also concerned about the likely outflow of foreign exchange for importing IT.

At the same time scientific, technical, and business sectors were pushing for computerization to improve productivity and to overcome the increasing technological gap between India and other countries. The Indian government's objective of economic development and self-reliance demanded rapid adoption and indigenization of IT. Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi took the initiative to revise the informatics policy to facilitate the introduction of computers on a large scale into India.

While the private sector could easily adopt computer technology, there was some apprehension about its use in government departments and enterprises. However, the successful implementation of the railway reservation system and resulting improvement in service proved to be helpful in creating a positive attitude towards computer technology in the general public. In some government departments, initial resistance was overcome after computers systems provided salary slips for the first time and updated a two year backlog in pension fund accounting for the first time in many years.

The fear of loss of foreign exchange on a long term basis has been counterbalanced by focusing on the export of computer software. These have already started to make a contribution to foreign exchange earnings and the total may soon exceed the annual outflow of foreign exchange due to the import of IT. Indeed, software exports may turn out to be a significant source of foreign exchange earnings for India in the long run. Rapid increase in computerization and progress in software exports has also created additional employment opportunities for India's pool of technical manpower. If India proves successful on the software export front, its employment opportunities are likely to increase many times in the near future.

Therefore, initiatives in the area of informatics policy have been successful in overcoming the resistance to computerization in India. Further liberalization of imports, investments, and licensing, along with an increased emphasis on the development of infrastructural facilities (such as computer education, training, communications, and control of piracy in software) could be helpful for the long term development and growth of the computer industry in India.

The experience of India can be useful for other developing nations; even if public opinion is unfavorable at the beginning, it is possible to overcome negative attitudes through the implementation of an informatics policy that emphasizes early implementation of computer systems in service sectors, where early success can make the biggest impact on the quality of service to the general population.
