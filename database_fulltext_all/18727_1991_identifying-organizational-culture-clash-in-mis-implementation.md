# Identifying organizational culture clash in MIS implementation

# When is it worth the effort?

Tsilia Romm

Department of Management, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, N S W 2500, Australia

Nava Pliskın

Department of Industrial Engineering, Ben-Gurion University, Beer-Sheva 84105, Israel

Yaakov Weber

School of Business Administration, Hebrew University, Jerusalem 91905, Israel

Allen S Lee

College of Business Administration, Northeastern University, Boston, MA 02115, USA

A prerequisite to succeeding in MIS implementation is analysis of both technical and organizational validity prior to implementation. In this paper we show that of the various organizational validity aspects, organizational culture presents a particularly difficult challenge. On the one hand, without a match between the culture of an organization and the cultural assumptions embedded within the MIS, a costly implementation failure is likely. On the other hand, a significant effort to detect a cultural clash prior to implementation might not be worthwhile for each and every MIS. This paper identifies two characteristics of the MIS, culture content and predictability of outcome, as relevant to the question of when is an early detection of a culture clash worth the effort. The following general guidelines are offered for MISs characterized by high culture content and low predictability of outcome, an effort to detect contrasting orientations of culture prior to implementation is justified, for MISs characterized by low culture content and high predictability of outcome, it is relatively safe to avoid early detection. The guidelines are accompanied by a series of mini-cases. The paper concludes with suggestions for future research

Keywords MIS implementation, Organizational culture, Cultural clash, Culture content, Predictability of outcome

## 1. Introduction

Schultz and Slevin [37] formally distinguished between technical and organizational validity as relevant to implementation of Operations Research models in the managerial environment. Ever since, these two facets of validity have been considered either in postimplementation studies, or in attempts to assess implementation prospects before and during implementation. Similarly, implementation researchers in the field of Management Information Systems (MIS) have emphasized that analyses of MIS projects must consider not only technical validity but also organizational validity [24]

In MIS, technical validity pertains to the technical features of the MIS that may be involved in its implementation, e.g., response time, system design, screen layout, or complexity of the supported task [15] Organizational validity, on the other hand, concerns the interaction between the organization and the MIS [24] The following topics can be categorized as micro aspects of organizational validity user familiarity with the task [14], user motivation [19,26], user involvement [11], and user cognitive style [28,33,41] The following topics can be categorized as macro aspects of organizational validity organizational hierarchy and channels of communication [26,30,31], organizational distribution of power [3,7,20,21,22,23,32,40], deployment of information systems as strategic weapons within the organizational environment [25,43], and organizational culture [4,23] Of the various macro aspects, organizational culture presents a particularly difficult challenge

The term “organizational culture”, which has many meanings and connotations, has been defined in a number of different ways with no consensus emerging among theorists [2,39] In this article we take the management and control (rather than anthropological) view of culture. The management and control orientation assumes that, in trying to manage organizations, the culture can be studied by observing behavior patterns in and out of the organization

Organizational members may share two principal types of important basic assumptions beliefs and values [35] Beliefs include facts about the world, how it actually works, and cause/effect relationships [35,42] For example, managers may believe that a certain level of long-term debt may lead to bankruptcy Values represent preferences for ultimate end states or ideals that are desirable or worth striving for [27,34,35,36] For example, top management may value a zero level of long-term debt but may permit a higher level which is believed to be safe enough from bankruptcy

All of the assumptions held by members of the organization interrelate to form the culture that shapes decision making processes and influences specific choices and behaviors $[6,35,38]$ . The interrelated sets of assumptions that form culture act as a filter through which organization members perceive the realities facing their organization, and serves two essential functions. One is to translate a world that can be overwhelmingly complex and ambiguous into comprehensible and familiar terms. The other is to provide continuity and stability when change threatens to undermine the lessons of experience and to serve the control function of deviance, detection, and reduction $[1,18,29,39]$ .

The complexity of what is meant by an organization's culture has been discussed extensively in the literature. Especially in large organizations, various organizational entities and subsets of each are likely to have different, unique, and conflicting values and beliefs. However, since perceptions of top managers are expected to permeate to the subcultures existing at lower organizational levels [5,36], one accepted way of dealing with subcultures is to focus on shared values and beliefs and to put more weight on top management culture [8,42]. It should be acknowledged that even under this simplification, cultural analysis remains rather complex

Discouraged by the complexity of cultural analysis, can MIS implementers afford to initiate an MIS implementation and worry about organizational culture later on? If they do, they risk an implementation failure as a result of a concealed clash between the culture of the organization and the cultural assumptions embedded within the MIS design

## 2 The Effort to Identify Organizational Culture Clash

Because of the under-the-surface aspects of culture, a concealed conflict may go undetected for a long time. The sooner the detection of a culture conflict, the lesser are the damages, perhaps only slightly exceeding the cost of the system. The longer it takes to detect a culture conflict, the heavier would the losses be, perhaps even threatening the survival of the organization

It is virtually impossible to improve the fit between the organization and the cultural assumptions embedded in the MIS and minimize clash-related losses by altering the culture of the organization since, in the short run, culture is a binding constraint in MIS implementation Cultures are not built overnight, nor can they be changed overnight Culture change is slow and is impacted in the long run by many factors, including MIS implementations If changing the organizational culture to match the MIS is not a viable option, the culture presumed by the MIS designers must match the actual culture of the organization Else, if a culture clash goes unnoticed, the likely result of cultural clash is implementation failure

An early study of the fit between the actual culture of the organization and the culture presumed in the MIS design can help avoid conflict-related losses. If a culture clash is identified, one way to avoid it is to withdraw from the implementation altogether. Alternatively, early identification of a potential conflict may provide opportunities to minimize conflict-related losses by taking corrective action that alters the MIS design, and thereby avoid cultural clash

What does it take to investigate organizational culture in the search for a potential clash? Laboratory research and industrial studies have isolated several important dimensions of culture [42] In an extensive literature review we found that there is no agreement on what the most important dimensions are [5,10,12,13,36,42] However, there are seven measures of cultural differences which are agreed upon [6,13,36,38] and have been used with high levels of reliability and validity in many studies [8,42] These measures, as detailed in Table 1, are innovation and action orientation, risk taking, integration – lateral interdependence, top management contact, autonomy in decision making, performance orientation, and reward orientation

Within each of the seven measures, organizational researchers distinguish between visible and invisible levels [13,36] At the visible level of artifacts “one can look at physical space, the technological output of the group, its written and spoken language, artistic productions, and the overt behavior of its members” ([36], p 14) Artifacts are those visible and familiar aspects of culture that a person hears, sees, or feels upon entering an organization

Although artifacts are often, in themselves, symbols of an organization's culture, they are only the visible expressions (or surface manifestations) of internalized assumptions. The invisible expressions of internalized assumptions that members of an organization share are hidden deep below the surface and cannot be measured easily. In other words, for a complete mapping of organizational culture, it is not enough to comprehend the visible artifacts alone. An extensive effort is commonly required for studying the invisible [35,36]

An investigation of culture may employ a variety of instruments from the fields of anthropology, sociology, and psychology including textual analysis, questionnaires, interviews, and observations [6,36] Deployment of such diagnostic measures, routinely practiced by organizational behaviorists, may require substantial effort and consume scarce resources such as time and money

MIS implementers thus face a dilemma. They certainly have a vested interest in early clash detection and loss avoidance by either withdrawing from implementations underlined by a culture clash or, if possible, by taking corrective action. However, being appreciative of the effort required for detection, they cannot afford an indiscriminant clash detection prior to each and every MIS implementation. For coping with this dilemma, MIS implementers need a typology to guide them in a quick determination of whether or not it is worthwhile to detect a culture clash prior to implementation

## 3. The Risk Grid

The examples that serve for explaining and illustrating the Risk Grid are drawn from the experience base of The Computerized Appraisal Services Group (CASG), a subsidiary of a supplier of organizational psychological services (The names of all CASG's client firms discussed hereinafter have been altered to preserve anonymity) CASG had developed and has successfully implemented several MISs in the field of human resource management CASG practices off-site processing, i.e., input data originating at the client organization is processed on CASG's computers and reports are generated for the client

As mentioned in the introduction, difficulties in MIS implementation can be explained by different aspects of technical and organizational validity. Most of these explanations can be ruled out in the case of CASG Two sample explanations help demonstrate why “Poor system design” is not valid given CASG’s impressive success record with most client organizations over many years “User cognitive style” is not valid since, due to the off-site processing, a distance is maintained between users and systems. However, as explained by the Risk Grid, CASG did encounter some problems regarding the cultural aspect of organizational validity

Each of the four cells of the Risk Grid displays one of CASG's MISs (ATTEND-SYSTEM, TEST-SYSTEM, ABSENT-SYSTEM, and EVALUATE-SYSTEM), and the risk associated with avoiding a pre-implementation study of cultural fit (see Table 2). The level of risk is a function of the interaction of two variables, predictability of outcome and culture content. Although these variables are continuous in nature, our simplified typology refers only to the high and low values bounding each variable. These variables are defined next, setting the background for mini-cases of MIS implementations that demonstrate how the two variables interact to yield the respective risk levels

Predictability of outcome reflects the extent to which the outputs of the MIS are pre-determined. High predictability of outcome means that the outputs of the MIS are pre-determined from the moment of conceptualization. Conversely, low predictability of outcome means that the outputs are subject to managerial judgment and are vulnerable to change in implementing organizations. Lower levels of predictability of outcome introduce ambiguities to the implementation process and therefore tend to be associated with an increased likelihood for late detection of a culture clash

Culture content of an MIS reflects its degree of cultural relevance and its visibility in terms of familiar artifacts. An MIS is considered to be of low culture content if its implementation concerns only a few (i.e., one or two) of the cultural dimensions in Exhibit 1, and if this cultural relevance is visible in terms of familiar artifact(s). Conversely, an MIS is considered to be of high culture content if its implementation concerns many of the dimensions in Table 1 but this cultural relevance is not visible in terms of familiar artifacts. Higher levels of culture content cloud the cultural picture and thereby increase the likelihood that a culture clash would surface too late. Furthermore, high culture content may also increase the magnitude of clash-related losses since the consequences of a clash are likely to hit the organization hard

## 4. The Attend System - Low Risk

CASG's most popular MIS is The Attendance Recording System (ATTEND-SYSTEM) designed to capture attendance data through magnetic identification cards and computerized time clocks and thereby eliminate the old punch time cards and mechanical time clocks, respectively. Normally, after a short period of parallel processing in one or two time-clock locations, the new system becomes operational. The implementation involves not only the substitution of punch cards by magnetic identification cards, but also a decrease in the number of computerized time clocks compared to the number of the old time clocks, in order to contain system costs. Despite a history of successful implementations of ATTEND-SYSTEM in the past, the implementation at one of CASG's client organizations, which we call "Food Production Firm" was not as smooth as in most of the other cases

Soon after the period of parallel processing ended, CASG detected a certain degree of discontent and was able to respond effectively through minor system modifications. Specifically, since workers were missing the accountability offered by the punch card, CASG had introduced individual attendance reports whose format resembled the old punch cards. In addition, since workers objected to the new architecture of the computerized time-clock network, nodes were added to maintain the old architecture

Thereafter, the implementation proceeded smoothly and data capture at the source became the accepted way of recording of attendance data Top management at Food Production had been pleased with the accurate and useful attendance database thus created. The union had been supportive having been given assurances that the system could in no way hurt workers. Indeed, following the two design modifications, the system had been well received by workers, who were hoping that this new approach to attendance recording would be the first in a series of innovations aimed at improving productivity and increasing compensation

Our post-implementation analysis traced the need for corrective design to a clash between the actual organizational culture of the Food Production Firm and the culture presumed by CASG Implementation of ATTEND-SYSTEM concerns the sixth and seventh dimensions of Table 1 At the Food Production Firm both performance orientation and reward orientations were very strict and formal Performance expectations were made very clear and employees were held accountable for their performance (performance orientation) Moreover, the competitive and equitable salaries were pegged directly to performance (reward orientation) Therefore, employees routinely monitored their punch cards to make sure that over a pay period they delivered the expected number of work hours Furthermore, the locations of the old time clocks had been optimized over the years to match this formal orientation and to minimize time wasted CASG, in presuming lower performance and reward orientations, was unaware that employees were so much dependent on attendance recording and cherished both the hard copy nature of the old punch cards and the convenience in the location of the old time clocks

ATTEND-SYSTEM is of low culture content because its implementation concerns only two of the cultural dimensions in Table 1 and this cultural relevance is visible in terms of familiar artifacts, the attendance recording medium, and the location of the time capturing mechanism. This was manifested at the Food Production Firm, where cultural concerns along the dimensions of performance and reward orientations surfaced early on during the implementation of ATTEND-SYSTEM in terms of familiar artifacts, namely the magnetic card and the location of microcomputer-based time clocks used to capture attendance data at the source Being at the artifact level, this happened early enough for CASG to take corrective action swiftly and insure that workers receive frequent individual attendance reports and find the microcomputer-based time clock in the usual location

The outcomes of ATTEND-SYSTEM are highly predictable because all parties involved can be informed prior to implementation regarding the details of input, processing, and output. At the Food Production Firm it was clear from the start that new time clocks had to replace the old ones, that new magnetic cards had to replace the old ones, that data capture at the source eliminated data entry from punched cards, and that such attendance recording was faster and more accurate. The high predictability of outcome was instrumental to early clash detection

Thus, ATTEND-SYSTEM exemplifies a combination of low culture content and high predictability of outcome. Under this combination, the risk of late detection in the instance of a culture conflict is low. It has been CASG's experience that even in cases when implementation of ATTEND-SYSTEM met with some resistance on cultural grounds, clash-related losses had been contained. Because of the low culture content, a culture clash could only hit the organization superficially and any corrective action taken could not be expensive. Also, the interaction of low culture and high predictability of outcome were instrumental to early detection, and enabled CASG to respond fast to clash-related resistance by corrective action

In summary, for MISs in cell No 1 low culture content and high predictability of outcome compound to yield a very low risk. Low culture content means that a culture conflict is above the surface and that culture-related losses are contained. The low culture content also interacts with the high predictability of outcome to lower the likelihood of late detection and thereby further contain the associated losses

## 5. The Test-System - Medium Risk

CASG's Psychological Testing System (TEST-SYSTEM) is composed of computerized screening tests which are accompanied by statistical processing tools. The new personnel manager of an Electronics Manufacturing Firm had approached CASG about the possible implementation of TEST-SYSTEM. She had hoped to improve the screening of candidate employees, as had been successfully done at the company that employed her previously. The firm's top management supported this move and signed the implementation contract within weeks, hoping that TEST-SYSTEM would help evaluate efficiently and effectively the personality of a candidate, and that the hours usually invested by senior personnel managers in interviewing candidates would be saved

The first signs of troubled implementation emerged after several months when many complaints about recruiting mistakes began to surface. There were stories about candidates whom TEST-SYSTEM recommended but who were not really suitable for the firm. Later on, an even worse type of mistake became evident. Candidates that TEST-SYSTEM rejected eventually joined the competition and were doing great. The chief executive officer finally instructed personnel managers to reduce reliance on TEST-SYSTEM and return to personal interviews. At the end of two years the CASG contract was terminated

Our post-implementation analysis traced the implementation failure to a clash between the actual organizational culture at Electronics Manufacturing and the culture presumed by CASG Implementation of TEST-SYSTEM concerns the first, second, fourth, and fifth dimensions of Table 1 Prior to CASG's intervention, within the framework of the prevailing organizational culture, interviewing personnel managers were innovative while taking the initiative (innovation and action orientation), bold yet intuitive (risk taking), friendly and informal (top management contact), and independent in the hiring process (autonomy in decision making) The system introduced by CASG left no room for managerial innovation and intuition, and assumed a high level of formality and less autonomy in the hiring process and in candidate assessment

TEST-SYSTEM is of high culture content because its implementation concerns four of the dimensions in Table 1, but this cultural relevance is not visible in terms of familiar artifacts. The report produced by TEST-SYSTEM, containing the test results, was not familiar to personnel managers and could not substitute the old intuitive and informal expressions of cultural values and beliefs. Because of the high culture content of TEST-SYSTEM, a culture conflict could have remained hidden below the surface until late in the implementation, leading potentially to massive losses (e.g., losing good candidate employees to the competition)

Fortunately, TEST-SYSTEM was characterized by high predictability of outcome because its outputs, candidate appraisal reports, were well defined in advance. The high predictability of outcome negated some of the effects of high culture content, and therefore stagnation of the implementation occurred in time to avoid heavier losses that would otherwise be associated with high culture content

In summary, under the combination of high culture content and high predictability of outcome, there is a medium-level risk (higher than in Cell No 1) The high predictability of outcome acts as a counter force in shedding light over circumstances clouded by high culture content and thereby reducing the time to clash detection and limiting clash-related losses

## 6 The Absent-System - Medium Risk

CASG's highly reputable system for Absence Analysis (ABSENT-SYSTEM), provides managers with various reports that analyze attendance and absenteeism Its implementation is less straight forward than that of ATTEND-SYSTEM or TEST-SYSTEM because adaptation of the reports to the specifications of the implementing organization is required A Textile Marketing Firm contracted CASG to implement ABSENT-SYSTEM following a successful implementation of ATTEND-SYSTEM Their personnel manager had hoped that his department would utilize the ABSENT-SYSTEM reports in a way that would help reduce absenteeism and improve negotiations with the union regarding employee firing

After working with management to adjust the system's reports to the needs of Textile Marketing, the implementation at Textile Marketing began CASG expected that the absenteeism reduction and the negotiations improvement objectives would be met within six months. However, even a year after starting the implementation, these expectations were not met. Periodic negotiations with the union deteriorated and an unprovoked aggressiveness of union members became noticeable, culminating to a union's threat to call an expensive strike

Consultants, who were called in to look into the situation, hypothesized that negotiating union members felt confused with the new system. In their mind, since they were not exposed to the attendance reports that ABSENT-SYSTEM produced for negotiating personnel managers, the computer replaced management in deciding who got fired and triggered alienation between them and management. The consultants recommended that CASG would make available to negotiating union members hard copies of ABSENT-SYSTEM reports, in a format similar in structure to the old and familiar punch cards. This was well received and ABSENT-SYSTEM has been operational at Textile Marketing ever since

Our post-implementation analysis traced the implementation difficulties to a clash between the actual organizational culture of the Textile Marketing Firm and the culture presumed by CASG The implementation of ABSENT-SYSTEM concerns the second and fourth dimensions of Table 1 Prior to implementation of ABSENT-SYSTEM, intuitive (risk taking), friendly and rather informal negotiations (top management contact) between union leaders and personnel managers took place In designing ABSENT-SYSTEM, less intuition and a higher level of formality were presumed in the process handling employee absenteeism and firing

ABSENT-SYSTEM is of low culture content because its implementation concerns only two of the cultural dimensions in Exhibit 1 and this cultural relevance is visible in terms of a familiar artifact, namely an attendance report which is similar in structure to the familiar punch card Thus, at the Textile Marketing Firm ABSENT-SYSTEM's clash-related losses should have been low and time to clash detection should have been short

Unfortunately, ABSENT-SYSTEM was characterized by low predictability of outcome because of the adaptation to organizational specifications and the discretion that managers have in using the reports that the system provides They are free to view that data from various angles, and to determine how outputs are used for controlling absenteeism, for motivating maximum attendance, and in negotiations with the union At Textile Marketing this low predictability of outcome clouded the picture and left any cultural concerns hidden until consultants became involved Both the personnel managers and CASG's personnel failed to detect the problem early despite the fact that a simple corrective action at the visible level of a familiar artifact (the attendance report in a time-card format) had the capacity to eliminate the culture clash. Therefore, despite the potential for a culture clash to surface at the visible level of familiar artifacts, the low predictability of outcome clouded the picture and increased the likelihood of late clash detection

Thus, ABSENT-SYSTEM is characterized by a combination of low culture content and low predictability of outcome. Under this combination, the risk of late detection in case of a culture conflict is not as low as in cell No 1. Although clash-related losses are bounded because of the low culture content, when implementation of ABSENT-SYSTEM meets with resistance on cultural grounds, potential losses as a result of a culture clash cannot remain low. This is due to the inability to take timely corrective action because of the masking effect of the low predictability of outcome over an extended period of time. The more time it takes to detect a culture clash, because of low predictability of outcome, the more delayed would be withdrawal from the implementation or corrective action

In summary, low culture content and low predictability of outcome compound to yield an intermediate level of risk. Low predictability of outcome interacts with low culture content to lower the likelihood of early detection and thereby increase the associated losses

## 7. The Evaluate System - High Risk

CASG's Employee Evaluation System (EVALUATE-SYSTEM) centers around an appraisal questionnaire, a database, statistical models aimed at removing biases from evaluations, and various reports pertaining to special employee populations (those with managerial potential, those excelling in marketing, those of deteriorating performance, etc.) CASG's analysts usually begin implementation of EVALUATE-SYSTEM by conducting a series of in-depth interviews in order to map the organization structure, design a personnel evaluation questionnaire, and create an evaluation database

Top management of a Chemical Processing Firm had sought to implement EVALUATE-SYSTEM, following a strike that broke after thirty years of excellent labor relations, and in response to union pressures for improving evaluation, feedback, and promotion processes

Initially, CASG enjoyed the full cooperation of management, plant managers in particular Complete cycles of appraisals followed and personnel managers received the agreed-upon reports The first indications of a troubled implementation emerged after three yearly appraisal cycles, when CASG attempted to introduce more advanced modules of the system (e.g., a module for handling promotion and compensation and a module facilitating computerized evaluation interviews) Despite explicit instructions from the president, managers did not promote according to the system's reports and refused to conduct computerized evaluation interviews with subordinates Support for EVALUATE-SYSTEM slowly diminished the director of personnel expressed doubts as to its importance, the president began to distance himself from meetings with CASG's representatives, and resistance was mounting among many managers. Finally, the project was terminated after seven years

Our post-implementation analysis traced the implementation difficulties to a clash between the actual organizational culture of Chemical Processing and the culture presumed by CASG Implementation of EVALUATE-SYSTEM concerns the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth dimensions of Table 1 CASG presumed maximal profitability (innovation, action orientation, and risk taking), centralized management, formal communication between managers and subordinates (integration – lateral independence, top management contact), and equality in managerial privileges (autonomy in decision making) These contrasted with the realities at the Chemical Processing Firm Favorable environmental conditions allowed it to survive by maintaining the status quo rather than by striving to maximize profitability. Furthermore, a hybrid of centralized and decentralized management, inequality of managerial privileges, and informal relationships between management and workers were in effect

EVALUATE-SYSTEM is of high culture content because its implementation concerns five of the dimensions in Table 1 but this cultural relevance is not visible in terms of familiar artifacts. The new reports produced by EVALUATE-SYSTEM were not familiar enough to become visible expressions of cultural values and beliefs immediately upon implementation. Throughout the implementation at Chemical Processing, a cultural clash that was routed deep below the surface was never visible in terms of familiar artifacts

EVALUATE-SYSTEM is of low predictability of outcome. Its outputs are not defined from the moment of conceptualization because adjustments to the implementing organization are always needed and because personnel managers exercise ample discretion in using the outputs. At Chemical Processing, low predictability of outcome further obscured a deeply routed culture conflict, and therefore neither project termination nor corrective action could have been contemplated early. It is noteworthy that a turning point at Chemical Processing was the attempt to introduce two additional modules. These additions, a module facilitating computerized evaluation interviews and a module for handling promotion and compensation, concern the sixth and seventh dimensions of Table 1. Their implementation would have meant a comprehensive culture clash concerning all seven dimensions of Table 1

EVALUATE-SYSTEM exemplifies a combination of high culture content and low predictability of outcome. This combination is associated with the highest risk. In this case, not only Chemical Processing paid dearly for the failure CASG also lost money because of heavy investment in searching plausible non-cultural explanations for the obvious implementation difficulties. All CASG's personnel affiliated with the project either resigned or were fired. CASG did not realize the strategic objectives envisioned, namely selling EVALUATE-SYSTEM to firms in non-sophisticated industries, and was forced to seek markets abroad at great expense. The resulting financial drain led to a significant reduction in CASG's importance within its parent company

In summary, the risk in cell No 4 is highest because of the increased losses usually associated with higher levels of culture content, and because of the increased likelihood of culture clash going undetected due to the compounding of high culture content and low predictability of outcome

## 8 Discussion and Conclusions

Our model focused on how the interactions between high and low levels of culture content and predictability of outcome represent different levels of risk associated with a concealed culture conflict From a practical standpoint, assessing the level of risk is crucial to deciding about early detection of a culture clash A substantial detection effort is worthwhile in case of high culture content and low predictability of outcome because their mutual interaction compounds to the highest level of risk The low predictability of outcome on top of high culture content could magnify the obscurity of a deep-below-the-surface culture clash related to internalized beliefs and values On the other hand, very little effort can be justified when culture content is low and predictability of outcome is high The remaining combinations of the culture content and predictability of outcome call for an intermediate and sparing detection effort

This typology, which is preliminary in nature, is based on two explanatory characteristics of an MIS. For added robustness, both characteristics merit further investigation and development. Furthermore, both dimensions are viewed as equals in determining the level of risk. It is plausible that unequal weights could be more appropriate (e.g., a stepwise evaluation starting, for instance, with the dimension of predictability of outcome) It is also conceivable that under circumstances dissimilar to those of our mini-case studies, issues such as project size, technology, implementation experience with the same MIS within and across industries, top management support, and user involvement might become more dominant

We recommend further testing and extension of the proposed typology in the following directions

1 Looking at MISs in fields other than personnel management, such as marketing and production

2 Applying a variety qualitative and quantitative research methods in addition to case studies [16,17]

3 Investigating the applicability of instruments for cultural study borrowed from the social sciences of psychology, sociology, and anthropology (e.g., ethnographies, questionnaires, and interviews), just as for this paper the concepts of late and early detection were borrowed from the medical field

4 Providing guidelines regarding periodic re-assessments of the justification for an effort to detect a cultural clash during the implementation, in addition the one contemplated prior to implementation

5 Extensions in terms of treatment rather than diagnosis (i.e., comparing and contrasting circumstances under which corrective action such as altering the design of the MIS is preferred to avoiding the implementation altogether)

6 Considering the cost side (e.g., the cost of the MIS, the cost of a culture clash, or the cost of avoiding implementation)

Hopefully, our typology will stimulate further discussion and research leading to more sophisticated modeling regarding early detection of culture clash in MIS implementation. Then, the next step should be to harness information technology for actual application of the model by MIS implementers. The design, development, and implementation of a computerized decision support system or expert system could aid MIS implementers in coping with the dilemma of cultural detection in MIS implementation. Our work only scratches the surface with respect to specifying requirements for such a system. Following further model enhancement, research should be directed at expanding the understanding of the requirements and at making progress toward a useful system

In closing, we offer some advice to practitioners who cannot await further research. Of utmost importance are awareness of culture and the potential for a cultural clash both before and after implementation as well as willingness to engage in clash diagnostics. Since in the short run organizational cultures are fixed constraints, upon suspicion of a cultural clash, prior to implementation, either the option of implementing the MIS must be ruled out or the option of corrective action with respect to the MIS design must be explored. If implementation is avoided but the MIS is viewed essential and desirable to the organization, even after clash detection, cultural monitoring is recommended to determine if and when the organizational culture has evolved to a point where a clash no longer threatens. If implementation is pursued cultural monitoring is also a sensible tactic for several reasons. First, if despite early detection efforts a clash was not detected, cultural monitoring can help uncover a hidden clash and thus guard again diagnostic errors. Second, if the design of the MIS was adjusted in response to clash detection, cultural monitoring can help ascertain the sufficiency of such corrective action. Finally, in either case, cultural monitoring can help avoid the implications of a cultural evolution within the organization in directions that conflict with the MIS design
