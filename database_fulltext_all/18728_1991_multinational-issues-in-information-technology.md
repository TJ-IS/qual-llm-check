# Multinational issues in information technology A perspective from less developed countries

Satya P Saraswat and John T Gorgone
Computer Information systems, Bentley College, Waltham, MA 02254, USA

This paper presents a survey of the emerging multinational issues in information technology from the perspective of the Less Developed Countries The methodology of structured content analysis of published articles is used to examine 132 papers presented at international conferences and published in journals Emerging from the debate, twenty three frequently raised issues are identified and ranked Countries of authors of these papers have been ranked according to the importance of information technology in their international trade. The most frequently addressed multinational issues, important from the perspective of less developed countries, are Information Technology Aid, Employment Creation with Information Technology, Indigenous Information Technology, Regional Information Technology Cooperation, High Cost of Information technology, Training of Information Technologists, Domination by Multinational Corporations, and Domination by Industrialized Countries Twenty three issues identified from the study are categorized as Perceived Advantages, Perceived Disadvantages, and Implementation Issues The challenges and opportunities created by the eight most important issues for U.S multinational corporations in computer industry are also discussed

Keywords Multinational corporation, Informatics, Global issues in information technology, Information technology diffusion, International perspective, Information technology management, Strategic information systems, International business, Global information systems

Surveys of the issues in management information systems conducted by MIS researchers in recent years have been limited in their scope to micro level organizational considerations in domestic settings [8] Traditionally, the subject of information technology as a development of international importance has been discussed primarily as a subsidiary issue related to developmental economics. However, a number of notable international conferences have recently been held specifically on the subject of information technology and its international implications [4,19,33] Although a large number of IT issues have been addressed in these forums, a survey of the relevant literature indicates a general lack of interest and efforts to identify and categorize these issues, and quantify their importance for different groups of experts. In order to bring these issues to the attention of the information systems community, the objectives of this paper are (a) to identify these issues, (b) determine their relative importance, (c) examine their implications to LDCs, and (d) identify the countries which are addressing these issues most frequently because of their importance in the international trade of these countries

## Methodology

A structured content analysis of published material is the research methodology employed in this investigation. The primary objective of content analysis is to draw conclusions from methodical observation and analysis of the content of a medium of expression [22,34]. The medium of expression, or information source, can be a written document, an oral communications, or an audio-visual source. Since the conclusions drawn from the casual observation of an information source can be heavily influenced by the biases of the observers, the application of a more formal and structured approach provides a higher degree of objectivity to the analysis. Application of Structured Content Analysis in journalism, anthropology and history has demonstrated that this methodology maintains the objectivity of analysis with the flexibility of interpretation of the information content [46,47,48,49]

In business related disciplines, variations of Structured Content Analysis have been utilized in the studies of cross cultural differences in organizations and international marketing issues. This methodology has been found suitable for areas in which other empirical methods are very difficult to apply because of the complexity of the environment, high financial costs, and conceptual difficulties of comparison and measurement [40,45]. A content analysis schedule was prepared to obtain information in two categories of analysis (1) source identification, and (2) content analysis. The source identification included the country of the author, organization to which the author belongs, and general information about the source of publication of the journal. Content analysis was performed to identify the major issue discussed in the

## Ranking of countries

Several working groups on computer technology at the United Nations have undertaken the classification of countries on the basis of their Computer Industry Development Potential into four broad categories Advanced, Operational, Basic, and Initial [23,36] The high computer technology developmental potential of a country does not necessarily mean that a given country attaches high degree of importance to the multinational issues in information technology. Nor does it imply that the experts from this country are more actively engaged in the discussion of global issues in international conferences and forums. It can be argued that the countries in which the interna-

tional sector of economy is dominated by information technology are more actively engaged in formulating these issues. Therefore, a different measure was used to determine the importance of global issues in IT for the twenty five countries. This measure is named Information Technology Trade Index (ITTI) and it is determined as follows. The import and export figures were obtained from the International Trade Statistics Year Book published by the United Nations. These totals are for 1987, the most recent year for which complete trade statistics are available. The total import and export figures were summed to determine the Total Trade Volume for each country in Table 1. The import and export volumes for computer technology related products were also obtained for these countries from the same source. These volumes were summed to obtain the Computer Technology Trade figures for each country in Table 1. The ITTI represents the percentage of Computer Technology Trade over the Total Trade Volume for each country in this table. The ITTI index is taken as the measure of the importance of IT in the international sector of a country's economy. Table 1 contains the rank ordering of the ITTI values for the countries of the authors of the papers analyzed in this study. A higher value of ITTI indicates a higher level of importance of information technology trade for a country. In spite of the fact that the United States, United Kingdom, and France are the largest exporters and importers of computer technology products, the share of computer technology in the total trade of these countries is not very large as indicated by the ITTI index, due to the immense size of their economies. On the other hand, Ireland is a country that has the largest share of computer technology in its total international trade, indicating a greater importance of multinational issues to Ireland than to the United States of America. It may be noted that there are other countries in the world that have a relatively large share of computer technology in their total international trade, but they are not included in this list because none of the information sources selected for this analysis were published by authors from these countries

Table 1 also identifies the number of papers analyzed from each country. A regression analysis of this data demonstrates that the experts from the countries with a high value of ITTI have presented or published a larger number of papers on the subject of information technology and economic development The results of this analysis, performed with ITTI as the independent variable and the number of papers analyzed from each country as the dependent variable, indicate that this conclusion is significant at 11% level Two possible explanations for this observation are (1) experts from the advanced industrial countries with higher ITTI, probably have easier access to the forums in which these issues are discussed, and (2) the countries with higher IT Trade Index are inherently more concerned about the multinational issues in information technology, regardless of whether these issues are important for advanced countries or the less developed countries The results of regression analysis are summarized in Table 3

The content analysis indicated that specialists from many international organizations are also active in promoting the concept of globalization of information technology. Therefore, the papers presented by authors affiliated with the United Nations (UN), Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), and the Intergovernmental Bureau of Informatics (IGBI) are also included in the study. These organizations are listed in Table 1 but not ranked according to ITTI index because of their lack of direct involvement in international trade

Perceived Advantages One of the most important perceived advantages of information technology is that tremendous employment opportunities can be created in LDCs with computer related technologies. An inordinate amount of emphasis on this subject by experts in international forums is rather surprising because most LDCs have traditionally viewed computers as labor substituting technology. With a surplus of manpower in most of these countries, the labor substituting role of information technology has been viewed with skepticism by governments and labor unions. Creation of vast employment opportunities by information technology in advanced industrialized countries is now altering the perceptions of experts in LDCs on this issue

The need for accelerating the industrial development in LDCs and the important role information technology can play in this process is widely recognized in the debate by experts in the countries that have relatively high ITTI [2,11,14,28,33,36] The rapid transformation of industrialized economies into service economies and the indispensable role of information technology in this process has not yet been widely appreciated in international forums because none of the papers established a direct link between the growth of service economy and the creation of employment opportunities in the industrialized countries

One of the most important needs of developing societies is the improvement of administrative efficiency in local, state and federal levels of government which are dominated by immense and unmanageable bureaucratic structures in many countries. Application of computer technology in government organizations has been recognized as an important benefit of information technology and the experience of countries such as Brazil, India and Mexico provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate this point [50] This issue ranks ninth in Table 2 and is perceived to be an advantage of information technology

There is a growing recognition of increased opportunities for international trade created by information technology. Export of software is repeatedly emphasized as an area where less developed countries can obtain favorable terms of trade and such exports are already making an important contribution to the balance of trade of many countries with relatively low Information Technology Trade Index. Processing of microchips, assembly of circuit boards, and large scale data entry have already become predominant IT oriented activities in LDCs [11,17,21,30,31]

Perceived disadvantages Experts, predominantly from the countries with relatively low ITTI, share a widespread concern that the cost of developing an information technology base may be prohibitive for many LDCs. It is also feared that the acquisition of industrial capability in information technology will divert scarce economic resources from other vitally important industrial development projects. In the economic priorities of these experts, development of information processing industry is lower than agricultural self-sufficiency and the development of other heavy industry

The issues of information technology being used by the industrialized countries as an instrument of economic domination, and the use of computer technology by multinational corporations for the domination of industrial activity in LDCs have been expressed in international forums, primarily by experts in countries with relatively high ITTI [1,9,15,16,20,24,29,32] On the other hand, the concerns about the privacy of individuals and information security, which have become so widespread among social scientists in the United States and Western Europe, are considered rather unimportant in the international debate on information technology

Information technology can create a widening information gap between industrialized countries and less developed countries. The likely effect of this imbalance may be the division of the world into two antagonistic groups of countries called “information haves” and “information have nots”

Rapid development of information industry in industrialized countries and the inability of LDCs to harness their own resources will reduce the LDCs to the role of the suppliers of raw data for the information and R and D industries of the countries with advanced information industry. There is also a possibility that LDCs will be permanently relegated to a subordinate role in which the assembling of microchips for R and D industries of advanced countries is the dominant economic activity. The effects of this industrial restructuring on a large scale are likely to be felt beyond the economic horizons in a society [5,11,14]. These themes emerge from the analysis of discussions as important, although not widespread, concerns and no evidence of the systematic politicization of these issues has been found in the views of the experts on the subject

Employment restructuring implies a rapid and disruptive growth in demand for highly trained labor force for information processing industries, and a decline in demand for labor in other sectors of industry. Concerns about the detrimental effects of this transformation on the educational infrastructure of LDCs are recognized by experts, in international forums [3,7,10]. Educational infrastructure in most LDCs may not be flexible enough to meet the specialized training and education requirements of IT industry. With relatively high wages in electronics and information processing industries, income imbalances between various sectors of economic activity within LDCs, and between the advanced and developing countries are likely to be exacerbated. This issue has been identified as income restructuring and the possibility of such restructuring as a negative effect of information technology has been raised by many experts from countries with high ITTI [6,12]

Implementation Issues The need for massive foreign aid from advanced industrialized countries to LDCs for the development of their IT potential is the most frequently addressed implementation issue in information technology A recognition of the need to provide such aid through private or public sources, development of suitable channels for the dissemination of information technology, determination of appropriate types of information technology to be transferred, and the long term benefits of such transfer to the donor and the receiver countries have been emphasized by experts in their discussions [13,27,36,39]

Experts from countries with relatively low ITTI recognize the need for developing indigenous sources of information technology in LDCs [9,15,17,20] Rapid growth of indigenous information technology in the newly industrializing countries of the Pacific rim supports the viability of this approach. The need for equitable sharing of IT resources across national boundaries is perceived as an important issue. Equitable sharing of IT resources means the availability of computer technology to LDCs at affordable prices, reasonable terms of trade, non-restrictive conditions of technology transfer, and appropriate laws to govern the distribution of information across national boundaries

Training of information technologists is an issue raised primarily by experts in countries with high ITTI, and the OECD The experience of countries providing such training to LDCs on a contractual basis has been described in many articles and the importance of developing national training resources in information processing industries has been emphasized [3,27,33]

Unavailability of timely and relevant information is considered as one of the most important causes of slow industrial development in many LDCs. Application of modern technology for regional sharing of information is an efficient solution to this problem. These regional data banks will contain comprehensive data on agriculture, industry, demographics, national accounting and other vitally important subjects in LDCs, and will be readily accessible to all segments of population

Creation of IT infrastructure is another consistent theme emerging from countries primarily with low ITTI Telecommunications and technical education are the two industries which will provide the infrastructure for IT development Specialists from the United Nations and IGBI have also argued in favor of an enhanced role for international organizations, such as the UN, IGBI, and OECD in facilitating the development of information technology in LDCs. Development of appropriate information technology for LDCs, as opposed to the implementation of existing off-the-shelf technology from advanced countries, is recommended by some experts and the need for national planning for information technology is recognized by others $[2,5,15,17,21,23,35]$

## Implications of the Study

U S based multinational corporations in the computer industry are most likely to be affected by global shifts in the perceptions about information technology since corporations such as IBM and DEC have operations in less developed countries throughout the world Social, economic and political transformations occurring in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union, and the impending integration of the economies of the countries in the European Economic Community in 1992, amplify the importance of these issues for multinational corporations. These issues are important not only for developmental economists and international business experts but also information technologists and information systems designers. Each issue identified in this study is likely to create many challenges and opportunities to the multinational corporations. In the 1980's, the debate on information technology in industrialized countries has focussed on four important issues (1) assimilation of information technology in the corporate value chain for competitive advantage, (2) application of information technology for improving productivity (3) implementation of communications intensive information systems for organizational restructuring, and (4) protection of individual privacy and data security in a proliferating information technology base [41,42,44] These second generation issues have evolved in advanced countries only after three decades of extensive experience with IT at the operational level of decision making in organizations Most LDCs are still in the first generation of data processing technology growth and the emphasis is on automation, control, and improvement of operations in organizations Therefore, it is not surprising to find none of these themes emerging as an important concern in international forums during the 1980's As the implications of these issues become clear in advanced countries and as the knowledge of these advanced applications disseminates in the global environment, these issues are likely to be addressed by experts from less developed countries in international forums

## Conclusion

Increasing globalization of the economic and industrial activity, traditionally confined to national political boundaries, is making it imperative for national corporations to think internationally. Unprecedented trade and investment opportunities exist for multinational corporations based in the United States, Western Europe and Japan in the markets of less developed countries, and MNCs are positioning themselves to take advantage of these expanding opportunities. Consequently, the foreign direct investment in the U.S has increased 400%, from \$83 billion in 1980 to \$329 billion in 1988. At the same time, the U.S. direct investment abroad has increased by 52%, from \$215 billion to \$327 billion [43]. The coordination of global economic activity on such a vast scale among MNCs and the governments of the host countries in LDCs requires an adroit and vigilant application of information technology at selected strategic points in the organization. In addition, it requires a clear understanding of the issues from the point of view of the users of this technology in less developed countries

This study has identified the important global concerns and issues in one of the fastest growing fields of modern technology. In the age of interdependent international economic systems, regional issues and concerns influence the flow of trade, capital and technology throughout the world. It is even more important in a continuously changing IT environment since new issues are constantly evolving and old ones are changing in relative importance. The evolving international issues can be compared to the domestic concerns of the U.S. computer industry on a periodic basis and multinational corporations can utilize this information for developing innovative applications of information technology to gain competitive advantage in international markets.
