# The effect of computerized decision aids on decision time and decision quality

Richard Coll

New Jersey Institute of Technology, Newark, NJ, USA

Joan H. Coll

Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ, USA

Donna Rein

Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ, USA

This article presents an experiment which examines the efficacy of computer decision support systems. The results are that in the circumstances of this experiment (a decision problem requiring non-overload multidimensional analysis and integration), a computer decision support system neither reduces the time required to come to a decision nor improves the quality of the decision. Analysis suggests that “computer reluctance” on the part of members of the DSS group was an important factor in the results obtained. Implications for implementing a DSS into a work environment are that significant pre-installation resources must be allocated by management to create a climate of “felt need” on the part of the recipients.

Keywords: DSS, Decision Support Systems, Decision-Making, Computerized Decision Aids, Expert Choice.

Drucker (1967) emphasized that the “Effective decision … results from a systematic process, with clearly defined elements, that is handled in a distinct sequence of steps.” This systematic sequence has been defined by a number of researchers (Kepner & Tregoe, 1976; Newell & Simon, 1972; Shepard, 1964) and is typically reported as being composed of the following steps:

1. Become aware of the problem.

2. Analyze the problem and the subject process.

3. Determine alternative solutions.

4. Evaluate the alternative solutions.

5. Select the most promising solution.

6. Implement this "best" solution.

7. Follow up with evaluation and fine tuning.

It is axiomatic that in the decision making process sufficient investigatory time and sufficient information regarding all alternatives are rarely available. Further, the diminishing return on investments of additional time and effort to produce “perfect” decisions renders “perfect” solutions non-cost effective. The most salutory workplace decisions are, in the main, non-optimizing. To describe these circumstances Simon (1967) coined the term “satisficing”.

Producing superior decisions under the constraints described above is obviously important to managers. It would seem that they should try to amass as much pertinent information as they can, organize the information and, based on this work, objectively and dispassionately formulate the decision. However, it is well documented that non-rational judgements, i.e., intuitive judgements, play an important role in the actual management decision making process (for example: Simon, 1967; Soelberg, 1967). Managers base much of their decision activity on how they “feel” about a problem.

The use of decision support software has been expanding rapidly (Carlson, 1977; Sproull, 1984). A DSS provides the capability to organize and simultaneously analyze as many pertinent factors as desired. Typically invoked during the third and fourth steps of the decision making process, the DSS is an aid to arriving at a decision and does not itself make the decision (Keen, 1980; Ntuen, 1986). Such responsibility rests with the manager.

The manager uses the DSS to create a model consisting of criteria and alternatives that fit his/her particular needs. As part of model creating, the manager must provide qualitative judgements concerning the importance of each criterion with respect to the overall goal and then provide qualitative judgements concerning the importance of the alternatives with respect to each criterion. The DSS integrates the judged priorities of the criteria and alternatives and produces an overall ranking of the alternatives.

How well does an unstructured approach to decision making stand up against a structured approach which utilizes a DSS, in situations where problem analysis requires material effort but does not exceed the overload threshold of the decision maker? This paper examines the solving of such a business problem by each of the following processes: Decision Support System (DSS), Structured Problem Solving (SPS), and Unstructured Problem Solving (UPS).

## Method

Material

The Case. The decision case (adapted from Kolb, Rubin and McIntyre, 1983) centers on choosing the best of three alternatives for dealing with an employee: Fire, Transfer within the unit, Leave as is. In this experiment, obtaining a solution specifically required analysis along four orthogonal dimensions: (1) Time to perform the assembly job, (2) Quality of the product, (3) Job enrichment for the employee and 4) Years of employment of the worker. The case was presented to three groups of undergraduate business students. To arrive at a solution Group 1 (DDS) used a DSS, while Group 2 (SPS) used a structured non-computer procedure (simplified pencil analog to the DSS method) and Group 3 (UPS) was given no aids.

In synopsis, the focal employee is the third member of a five worker team responsible for assembling components into a high precision cardiac monitor. He is 58 years old, has been with the company 13 years and is extremely meticulous about his work. But he is slow and mother-boards accumulate at his work station. Although quality control rejects for his work are almost nil, other managers are not willing to accept him into their units because of his lack of speed. Concomitantly, industry competition has become severe and there is pressure from above to meet production targets. As manager of this assembly team, what do you do with this employee?

Of course, the actual presentation to the subjects was more elaborate, so that they would be able to interrelate the problem criteria. The “best” solution, as determined by the experimenters (using Expert Choice) and unanimously by a panel of 20 experienced managers working individually (no DSS), was to transfer the individual to the least demanding (timewise) position within his unit.

Case Rationale. The inability of humans to effectively integrate different types of data, especially when the materials involve unrelated scales of measurement has been experimentally documented (Slovic, 1972; Slovic, Fischhoff and Lichtenstein, 1977; Swinth, Gaumnitz and Rodriguez, 1975) and is universally acknowledged (Benbasat and Taylor, 1982; Davis and Olson, 1985). The author' desire was to compare DSS-aided decision making with conventional rational (as opposed to superficial, "seat-of-the-pants", totally intuitional) decision making. Given these circumstances, the authors had to develop a case which was of sufficient scope to allow meaningful use of a DSS, but not so complex that members of the unstructured problem solving group would experience overload, abort analysis and make totally intuitional decisions.

The authors chose the assembly line case with its four orthogonal dimensions as a good fit with respect to their goals and the abilities of undergraduate business major subjects. (The tone of the literature lead the authors to expect that more than four dimensions would produce cognitive strain with concomitant deterioration of analysis and integration.) Specific instructions were given to all subjects requiring them to perform analysis along these four dimensions:

Base your decision on the following criteria:

Time - Time required to complete the assembly process.

Quality - Quality of the finished product.

Job enrichment – Challenge of the tasks presented to the employee.

Service - Years of service of the employee.

Because of the directness of these instructions and the emphasis placed upon them, it was expected that all subjects (and members of the non-structured group specifically) would focus on the four presented dimensions and arrive at “rational” integrative solutions.

The DSS. The specific DSS chosen for use in this experiment was Expert Choice (Saaty and Forman, 1986). The Expert Choice model consists of a hierarchical structure, referred to as an upside-down tree. It permits a maximum of six levels of criteria, the number of criteria levels used depending on the complexity of the decision.

Expert Choice comes with a detailed and readable user manual. The computer package is easy to use an extremely user-friendly. Three examples of its user-friendliness follow. Help screens are available from all menus. The user can back out of commands. There are a variety of ways to invoke commands. Additionally, the Expert Choice literature contains an impressive list of government facilities and major corporations which use the product. The point of this paragraph is to establish that Expert Choice is a respectable product with a solid user base and that results of this experiment which do not find in favor of the DSS group should not be attributed to defects in Expert Choice but to variables of the experiment.

To some small extent the reader can judge the usability of Expert Choice for him/herself from the operating descriptions in the following paragraphs. It is to be remembered that the actual system has innumerable options not mentioned here, such as seeking out inconsistent judgements, calculating priorities and conditional priorities at any time, producing a glossary, mark and jump (useful when there are many many nodes), and importation of information screens produced separately on a word processor.

The Expert Choice process is comprised of four steps:

1. Build the Model. The user inputs the criteria and the decision alternatives that pertain to the decision to be made.

With respect to the experiment case, the DSS user would type the command /FR (either upper or lower case, Expert Choice is not case sensitive) for File Retrieve and be prompted to enter a model name. In response the user might type: Assembly Line Worker. The user is then prompted for a goal description and might type: Decide to Fire, Transfer to Leave as is. The user now types EDIT (or E) followed by I (INSERT mode) and is prompted to enter the decision criteria. The user might type the four following criterion names: TIME, PRODUCT QUALITY, EMPLOYEE JOB ENRICHMENT and YEARS OF SERVICE. As the user types, Experts Choice creates an on-screen tree of the user's responses. At this point there would be two levels on screen. The top level would be the single goal node. Lined up horizontally beneath this would be the four nodes containing the four names entered. (Up to seven sub-nodes could now be created for each of these nodes, etc., to a total depth of six levels.)

Moving the cursor beneath the first criterion node (TIME) the user types the three alternatives (FIRE, TRANSFER and LEAVE AS IS). By typing R (the REPLICATE command) the user may then copy this list to the other nodes). The lowest level is automatically presented on screen as a list, because Expert Choice knows this level contains the to-be-evaluated alternatives.

2. Prioritize the criteria. The user rates the criteria (pair-wise) presumably based upon experience and/or quantitative research and analysis. The system then integrates the priorities entered for the criteria, prints a ranking of the criteria and an inconsistency ratio.

To start the prioritizing process the user would move the cursor to the parent (in our case, the goal node) of the nodes he/she wishes to compare and types the command C (COMPARE). Expert Choice produces a table containing the heading "TIME is EQUAL TO PRODUCT QUALITY". The body of the table consists of a list of choices: EXTREMELY, VERY STRONGLY, MODERATELY and EQUAL. An arrow can be placed opposite or between items in this list. The heading changes appropriately. For example if the user placed the arrow above EQUAL but below MODERATELY, the heading would change to: "TIME is EQUAL to MODERATELY MORE IMPORTANT THAN PRODUCT QUALITY". Pressing I inverts the positions of the two criteria in the sentence.

In turn Expert Choice then presents TIME to PRODUCT QUALITY, TIME to EMPLOYEE JOB ENRICHEMENT, TIME to YEARS OF SERVICE, PRODUCT QUALITY to EM-

PLOYEE JOB ENRICHMENT, etc. until all possible pairs have been compared. (Note that alternatively, comparisons can be done numerically, for example, “2.3 times as important”, or all at once using percentages, which of course must total to 100%.)

3. Prioritize the alternatives list for each of the criteria. The user ranks the alternatives on each criteria, following the same round robin procedure described in 2 above. (Any of the alternative procedures noted above could be used.)

In the experiment case, the user would rank FIRE, TRANSFER and LEAVE AS IS four times, once each for TIME, PRODUCT QUALITY, EMPLOYEE JOB ENRICHMENT and YEARS OF SERVICE.

4. Synthesize and evaluate. The system integrates all the entered information, ranks the alternatives and prints the resulting ordered list, including a bar graph and numeric weights.

To implement this step, the user types S (SYN-THESIZE). Following a series of questions concerning presentation details the results are printed.

Procedure. Group 1 (15 subjects) was instructed in the use of Expert Choice in sessions occurring prior to the task administration session. In this training the subjects solved two decision problems (unrelated to the case in the experiment) and became proficient in Expert Choice. At the start of their experimental session these subjects received a booklet containing 1. instructions, 2. the case to be read, 3. the three decision alternatives and 4. the four decision criteria (Time, Product quality, Employee job enrichment and Employee years of service). Their task was to use the decision support software to obtain the best solution. This group is referred to as the DSS group.

The members of Group 2 (27 subjects) each received a booklet at the start of their session which contained 1. instructions, 2. the case, 3. the three decision alternatives and 4. the four decision criteria. This group was required to solve the case and arrive at a decision without the use of computer support. However, this group was provided a pencil and paper structure (similar to that of the DSS) for making their decision. That is, the subjects were given detailed instruction on how to perform criteria ranking and how to perform alternatives ranking for each of the four criteria. They were given worksheets which they were required to use to come to a decision. This group is referred to as the Structured Problem Solving group, SPS.

The members of Group 3 (20 subjects) at the start of their session each received a booklet containing 1. instructions, 2 the case, 3 the three decision alternatives and 4. the four decision criteria. This group however, was not given any requirement to rank or weight. These subjects were directly instructed to use the four criteria in deciding which of the three provided alternatives was best. This group is referred to as the Unstructured Problem Solving group, UPS.

The central instructions to the subjects of all three groups required them to perform analysis along the four criterion dimensions, as detailed in the Case Rationale section of this paper. For all subjects, timing started when they were told to turn their booklets face up and begin. Timing ended for a subject when he/she raised a hand to signal that he/she had recorded a solution. The SPSS package was used for all statistical tests reported in this paper.

Subjects. The three groups of subjects comprised three equivalent undergraduate management classes from a middle sized suburban school of business (total N = 62). Demographic data regarding cumulative Grade point average, Year in college, Gender, and Major were collected. An ANOVA performed on Gradepoint average versus Group (DSS, SPS, UPS) yielded no significant difference (p > 0.1). A Kruskal-Wallis ranked data analysis of variance performed on Year in college versus Group yielded no difference (p > 0.1). Similarly, a Chi-Square performed on nominal variables Gender and Major yielded no significant differences (p > 0.1).

Dependent Variables. The following dependent variables were measured:

1. The time for each subject to arrive at his/her solution.

2. The number of subjects per condition achieving the best solution.

3. Subject satisfaction with his/her solution.

4. Subject judgment of the creativity of the experimental method he/she used to arrive at a conclusion.

Subject satisfaction with the method used was ascertained via a Likert-scale question involving five choices which ranged from Very Satisfied to Very Dissatisfied. Subject judgment of the creativity of the method used was similarly ascertained via a question with choices ranging from Very Creative to Very Dull.

## Hypotheses

It was expected that: 1. There would be a difference between groups in the time required to arrive at the solution, with the DSS group using the most time, because they had to follow a detailed computer procedure, and the UPS group taking the least time, because they were not required to do any written ranking or weighting.

2. The DSS group would achieve more “best” results because of the automated nature of this method.

3. The DSS group would be more satisfied with their system because of its thoroughness, its completeness (no possibility of elements falling between the cracks) and because they had control over the process.

4. The DSS group would judge their activity the least creative because they were constrained in their approach to the avenues Expert Choice provided and because the computer "called the shots".

## Conclusions and Discussion

While it was expected that the DSS group would take the most time (as was the case) it was curious that the UPS group did not take significantly less time than the SPS group, since the latter was required to make written ranking of the criteria and written ranking of the alternatives with respect to each criterion. However, the important finding was that the DSS group did not produce more “best” decisions than the other two groups. Also, it was surprising that there was no statistical difference between the satisfaction or creativity judgements of this group and the other two.

The results indicate that, under the present circumstances of a decision problem requiring non-overload multidimensional analysis and integration, a DSS does not serve to sharpen the decision making process. What is the explanation for this presumably counterintuitive finding?

As presented earlier in this paper, it is well known that humans have difficulty integrating information (see for example Benbasat & Taylor, 1982). It is also well known that humans have difficulty judging the probabilities of events (Slovic, 1972, among others) and that there is a limit to the amount of information a human can process in a fixed time period (see Davis & Olson, 1985). Yet in the current experiment, subjects provided with a tool which performed these activities and automatically provided a ranking of alternatives did not do better than the non-DSS groups in analysis and integration of four dimensions of information. Rationally, one must accept that a DSS provides significant benefits to the decision making process. Why, in this experiment, did the DSS not have a salutary effect?

Human information processing is experiential and relies on simplification. Under pressure, decision makers discard information and avoid bringing in expertise and exploring alternatives. They simplify a problem to the point where it becomes manageable (Keen, 1981). The typical decision making process is emotive, intuitive and only partially cognitive and many models suggest that better information has negligible impact (Cohen & March, 1974).

The computer is a powerful agent external to the decision maker and may require significant energy to use. As a result, in time of decision its use is often viewed by decision makers as 1. a threat rather than as an avenue for simplification and 2. as an implicit criticism of their human abilities.

These reasons and a whole related psychology explain the reluctance of many people to dealing with the computer. It is recognized that a considerable number of humans are uncomfortable in the presence of a computer and (at the extreme) feel that computers are hostile and dehumanizing. A companion ailment has been documented, computer phobia: sweat, nausea and associated symptoms brought on by proximity to a computer.

Many of the DSS subjects in this experiment did appear to be at least computer reluctant. During training they lacked motivation and it required effort to keep them focused on the training tasks. They possessed an inertia which was hard to overcome (see Keen, 1981). In retrospect the experimenters believe that they overestimated the attraction of their DSS subjects toward computer decision activity.

The message is that many employees, managers as well as workers, do exhibit computer reluctance when faced with a DSS implementation. Unfortunately, this often comes as a surprise to the implementors who, satisfied that what they offer is of great value, naively believe that those affected will view the system with equal “rationality”. In the main, such naivete seriously compromises the success of an implementation project.

In the future, when generations have experienced the computer as a companion from earliest youth, computer reluctance will, likely, become extinct. For the present however, it is a phenomenon which must, as this experiment indicates, be treated seriously since computer decision support systems are becoming increasingly important to corporate well-being.

This experiment highlights the point that the success of a DSS (or other information processing) implementation requires substantial time and effort in the predesign stage to create the appropriate climate for change. A large amount of indoctrination must be performed so that the work force becomes “self-motivated” and calls for the system based on a felt need. A growing number of management teams are recognizing the importance of allocating substantial resources to this purpose.

In light of the above discussion, the authors suggest that future experimental work involve greater indoctrination of the DSS group and comparison of DSS groups with different amounts of indoctrination and different amounts of positive affect. Studying the effect of problems of greater (and lesser) immediacy would also be illuminating.

The authors believe that this experiment's most important aspect is its relation to the issue of computer reluctance. This experiment stands as a reference for additional work. Apparently a DSS is only as useful as potential users believe it to be. If a climate of felt need is lacking, DSS availability will probably not enhance decision making activity. Currently, the behavior demonstrated by the experiment DSS group is a significant factor working against industry and institutional implementation.
