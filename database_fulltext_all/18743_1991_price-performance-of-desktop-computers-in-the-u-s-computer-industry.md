# Price/performance of desktop computers in the U.S. computer industry

Dinesh S. Dave and Kathy E. Fitzpatrick
Department of Decision Sciences, College of Business, Appalachian State University, Boone, NC 28608, USA

Throughout the history of computing, a topic of major interest has been the relationship between the cost of a computer system and its power. Several researchers, including Grosch, Knight, and others have attempted to quantify the relationship between price and power for computer systems in general. However, these studies did not focus on the price/performance ratio for desktop computers. The purpose of the present study was to develop a reliable model that can identify the factors which most significantly affect the price of desktop computers. The model is based upon various hardware characteristics, such as clock speed, RAM, inclusion of hard disk as standard equipment, type of microchips, and other time related variables (e.g. year of introduction). Once the model has been developed, it can serve as an aid in managerial decision-making process.

The power of desktop computing has been a shifting quantity in the PC industry. While the machines are converging in price and in potential uses, there is still a difference in computing power. Recently, Sircar and Dave (1986) analyzed the relationship between the cost of a computer and its power. They thoroughly revised the previous work of Grosch (1975), Knight (1966), and Cale et al. (1979). The authors developed a highly reliable model relating cost to memory size, MIPS, machine cycle time and year of introduction. They divided computer systems into four different classes: IBM machines, IBM plug-compatible machines, IBM competitor machines, and super minicomputers. Their model can be used by both users and manufacturers to determine appropriate computer system prices. The previous studies focused on the price/performance relationships of computers in general; no attempt has previously been made to quantify this relationship for desktop computers.

The purpose of the present study was to develop a reliable model that can identify the factors which most significantly affect the price of desktop computers. The model is based upon various hardware characteristics such as clock speed, RAM, inclusion of hard disk as a standard feature, availability of optional hard-disk drive capacity, etc.. Once the model has been developed, it can be used for managerial decisions by determining whether a given desktop computer system is over- or under-priced. Further, an attempt is also made to support the previous studies by Cale et al. (1979), Sircar and Dave (1986), and others in which Grosch's Law was found to be invalid.

## Procurement of Data

Research in the field of information systems is severely hampered by the paucity of meaningful data. Fortunately, though, for this research effort, enough data were found to establish a data base covering a reasonable subset of desktop computing systems. The data, which demonstrate the effects of time and other characteristics on system price, were published by PCWeek during the period 1987–1989. The data collected are presented in Appendix D. Some observations were eliminated because of missing values of computer characteristics. Further, the machines priced at \$23,000 or more were also eliminated because there could be implicit variables affecting price which were not available in the source of data.

Two price levels were provided for some system configurations in the data set - one for minimum system configuration and one for maximum configuration. Therefore, a dummy variable indicating the appropriate configuration was included in the model. Moreover, some prices included the availability of a hard disk; therefore, the model included another dummy variable indicating presence of a hard disk.

## Development of the Model

In order to determine the effects of computer characteristics on the price of desktop computers in the United States, a model was developed that directly related system price to system component measures (i.e., clock speed, RAM, hard disk standard, type of microchip, etc.). In addition, in order to measure the effect of time on price, year of introduction was included in the model. Of different linear and nonlinear models tried (Draper and Smith, 1966; Graybill, 1976), the model which demonstrated the highest predictive capabilities was:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{Ln} (\text {Price}) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{Ln} (\text {CLOCK}) \\ & + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{Ln} (\text {RAM}) \\ & + \beta_ {3} (\text {Hard Disk Standard}) \\ & + \beta_ {4} (D Y _ {1}) + \beta_ {5} (D Y _ {2}) \\ & + \beta_ {6} (D C _ {1}) + \beta_ {7} (D C _ {2}) \\ & + \beta_ {8} (\text {MAX}), \end{array}
$$

where $\beta_{0}$ , $\beta_{1}$ , $\beta_{2}$ , … are the parameters to be estimated using a least squares program (SAS, 1985).

The system of binary variables was defined as follows:

$$
D Y _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if   year   of   introduction   was   1988. } \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

$$
D Y _ {2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if   year   of   introduction   was   1989. } \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

Note that the base case, if $DY_{1}=DY_{2}=0$ , covers computers introduced in 1987.

Further, the binary variables $DC_{1}$ and $DC_{2}$ discriminate between different microchips, e.g.,

$$
D C _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if   machine   contains   80286   microchip. } \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

$$
D C _ {2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if   machine   contains   80386   or } \\ & \text { 80386SX   microchip. } \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

If $DC_{1}=DC_{2}=0$ , then the machine contains an 8088 microchip.

Additionally, a binary variable (MAX) was used to differentiate between minimum and maximum system configurations. MAX = 1 represents the maximum configuration. Another binary variable (Hard Disk Standard) was included in the model to measure the effect of inclusion of a hard disk on system price. Notice that the variables “serial ports standard”, “expansion slots standard”, “optional hard-disk capacity”, and video board and monitor standard” were not considered in the model. The reason is that in all formulations which were tried for the model, the coefficients derived for such measures were not significantly different from zero.

## Results

Table 1 shows the results of the model run on all observations. The validity of the model is demonstrated by the high value of the F-statistic, $R^{2}$ , the low value of the estimator ( $s^{2}$ ) of the variance of the random error, and the values of t-statistics for all parameters.

The hypothesis $H_{0}$ : $\beta_{1} = \beta_{2} = \ldots = 0$ was tested against the alternative hypothesis $H_{a}$ : At least one of the $\beta s$ is non-zero. The value of the F-statistic was found to be 149.59, which is higher than $F_{(8,742)} = 2.51$ , even at the 99% significance level. Also, the observed significance level of the test was found to be 0.0000, which strongly supports the alternative hypothesis. The value of $R^{2}$ was found to be 0.6173, which is high considering the fact that price can also be affected by some qualitative variables (for example, service available from the vendor) which are difficult to quantify. Further, the value of the estimator ( $s^{2}$ ) of the variance of the random error pertaining to the model ( $\sigma^{2}$ ) is very small (0.1620), which indicates that the model has high predictive capability.

Additionally, a test for the presence of autocorrelation was performed. The calculated value of the Durbin-Watson statistic was found to be 1.207, which is smaller than $d_L = 1.38$ at $\alpha = 0.01$ , $k = 8$ and $n = 751$ observations, indicating uncorrelated error terms. Furthermore, residual analysis was performed by plotting residuals against the predicted values and residuals against individual variables. These plots showed a random pattern and an absence of outliers, as well as a constant error variance, indicating absence of heteroscedasticity. This demonstrates that the assumptions of the model are satisfied and the error terms can be considered to be identically and independently distributed.

The effect achieved by varying the independent variables individually for a given configuration, while holding the other independent variables constant, is shown graphically in Appendix C. This graphical analysis allows questions such as “How would price be affected if any system attribute was changed?” to be considered. Specifically, it provides information for particular types of system configurations as to which characteristics have the greatest effects on price.

The graphical analysis indicates that the type of microchip used in the machine has the highest effect on price; specifically, 80386 or 80386SX microchips have the greatest impact on price, followed by 80286 microchips. Other variables found to impact price were (in descending order of influence) “hard disk standard”, “RAM”, and “Clock Speed”. As expected, “Year of Introduction” had a negative effect on price of desktop computers. This indicates that the widely held belief regarding the effect of advances in technology on reducing system prices is true.

## Prediction of Price Using Model

Table 2 shows the predicted and actual prices of some desktop computers with different configurations based upon type of microchip used, clock speed, RAM, etc.. These machines represent a reasonable subset of desktop computing systems. The predicted prices are compared with the actual prices and found to be quite close, indicating that the model has high predictive capacity. The model may, therefore, be utilized by managers to determine whether a given system is overpriced or underpriced. Similarly, computer manufacturers may use the model as a guide for establishing pricing strategies.

## Conclusions

The model developed to estimate price of desktop computers, given clock speed, memory size (RAM), inclusion of hard-disk, effect of time, type of microchip, and system configuration was found to be reliable, because the calculated $R^{2}$ and F-statistics are fairly high. The price is not simply the square root of speed, but is also affected by these other characteristics. Therefore, the validity of Grosch's law for desktop computers appears to be questionable. In order to disprove Grosch's law, one would have to examine a more heterogeneous class of computers, and a definition of "power" other than clock speed would be required.

One of the parameters having the greatest effect on system price is memory size (RAM), which supports the findings of previous studies (e.g. Sircar and Dave, 1986; Cale et al., 1979). This is consistent with the fact that memory size is still one of the most expensive components of a computer system. In addition to memory size, other parameters which were found to greatly influence price included type of microchip and clock speed.

The pricing strategies for computers with different types of microchips were rather interesting. For systems using 80386 or 80386SX microchips, average prices were higher than for systems using 80286 microchips. The 80286 machines were priced higher than systems with 8088 microchips. Furthermore, the model as expected confirms the widely held belief that the effect of advancing computer technology has been to reduce system price over time. For example, average system prices for machines introduced in 1989 were lower than prices for similarly configured systems introduced in 1988.

The chief contribution of the model is that it provides a method of price/performance comparisons among desktop computers. A model such as this can enable a potential purchaser to determine whether a particular system is over- or under-priced relative to other available systems. It can also be useful to computer manufacturers and vendors in making managerial decisions regarding price/purchase strategies.

## Appendix A

The Effects on Price Due to Year of Introduction

The model which possesses the highest predictive capability is given by:

Ln(Price)

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{Ln} (\mathrm{CLOCK}) + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{Ln} (\mathrm{RAM}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {3} (\text { Hard   Disk   Standard }) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} (\text { Effect   for   1988 }) + \beta_ {5} (\text { Effect   for   1989 }) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {6} (\text { Effect   for   80286   Microchip }) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {7} (\text { Effect   for   80386   or   80386SX } \\ & \quad \text { Microchip }) + \beta_ {8} (\mathrm{MAX}). \end{array} \tag {A1}
$$

To gauge the effect on price due to the year of introduction for identical configurations with 80286 Microchips, the model can be stated as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{Ln} (\text { Price }) & = C _ {Y} + \beta_ {4} (\text { Effect   for   1988 }) \\ & + \beta_ {5} (\text { Effect   for   1989 }) \\ & + \beta_ {6} (\text { Effect   for   80286   Microchip }), \end{array}\tag{A2}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C _ {Y} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Ln(CLOCK)} + \beta_ {2} \text {Ln(RAM)} \\ & + \beta_ {3} (\text {Hard Disk Standard}) \\ & + \beta_ {7} (\text {Effect for 80386 or 80386SX}) \\ & \text {Microchip)} + \beta_ {8} (\text {MAX}). \end{array}\tag{A3}
$$

For example, to observe the change in price due to the years 1987 to 1988 for 80286-based desktop computers (see Table 1), the values of the binary variables “Effect for 1988” and “Effect for 1989” will be zero. Then the model can be written as:

$\mathrm{Ln}(\mathrm{Price}) = C_{Y} + 0.47103336,$

(A4)

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C _ {Y} & = 6. 7 0 4 8 8 4 5 + 0. 1 5 5 0 4 5 2 \mathrm{Ln} (\text {   CLOCK   }) \\ & + 0. 1 8 2 2 2 2 2 \mathrm{Ln} (\text {   RAM   }) \\ & + 0. 3 6 1 9 0 2 5 (\text {   Hard   Disk   Standard   }) \\ & + 0. 9 9 2 1 2 1 1 (\text {   Effect   for   } 8 0 3 8 6 \text {   or   } \\ & \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \text {   Microchip   }) \\ & + 0. 2 8 4 3 6 4 9 (\text {   MAX   }). \end{array}\tag{A5}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\text { Price } = \exp \left\{C _ {Y} + 0. 4 7 0 3 3 3 6 \right\}.\tag{A6}
$$

Similarly, the model which depicts 1988 price can be obtained from equation (A2) as: $\mathrm{Ln}(\mathrm{Price}) = C_{Y} - 0.1507443 + 0.4710333$ . That is, $\mathrm{Ln}(\mathrm{Price}) = C_{Y} + 0.320289$ . Therefore,

$\mathrm{Price} = \exp \left\{C_Y + 0.320289\right\}$ .

(A7)

Then the change in price in 1988 as compared to 1987 can be computed as:

$$
\begin{aligned} & \frac{\text{Price in 1988}}{\text{Price in 1987}}\times 100\\ & = \exp \left\{\left[C_{Y} + 0.320289\right] - \left[C_{Y} + 0.4710333\right]\right\} \\ & \quad \times 100\\ & = \exp \left\{-0.1507443\right\}\times 100\\ & = 86.01\%. \end{aligned}
$$

This indicates that the decline in price in 1988 as compared to 1987 in the 80286-based desktop computers is 13.99%.

The model which possesses the highest predictive capability is given by:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{Ln} (\text { Price }) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{Ln} (\text { CLOCK }) + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{Ln} (\text { RAM }) \\ & + \beta_ {3} (\text { Hard   Disk   Standard }) \\ & + \beta_ {4} (\text { Effect   for   1988 }) \\ & + \beta_ {5} (\text { Effect   for   1989 }) \\ & + \beta_ {6} (\text { Effect   for   80286   Microchip }) \\ & + \beta_ {7} (\text { Effect   for   80386   or   80386SX } \\ & \text { Microchip }) + \beta_ {8} (\text { MAX }). \end{array} \tag {B1}
$$

To measure the effects on price due to the use of different types of microchips for identical configuration, the model can be written as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{Ln} (\text { Price }) & = C _ {M} + \beta_ {6} (\text { Effect   for   80286 }) \\ & + \beta_ {7} (\text { Effect   for   80386   or   80386SX } \\ & \text { Microchip }). \end{array} \tag {B}\tag{B2}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C _ {M} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Ln(CLOCK)} + \beta_ {2} \text {Ln(RAM)} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {3} (\text {Hard Disk Standard}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} (\text {Effect for 1988}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {5} (\text {Effect for 1989}) + \beta_ {8} (\text {MAX}). \end{array}\tag{B3}
$$

$$
\text { Price } = \exp \left\{C _ {M} + 0. 4 7 1 0 3 3 3 \right\}.\tag{B4}
$$

$$
\text { Price } = \exp \left\{C _ {M} + 0. 9 9 2 1 2 1 1 \right\}.\tag{B5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\left[ \{\text { Price   for   80386 - or   80386SX - based } \right.} \\ & {\qquad \text { desktop   computers }} \\ & {\qquad / \{\text { Price   for   80286 - based   desktop   computers } \} ]} \\ & {\times 1 0 0} \\ & {= \exp \left\{\left[ C _ {M} + 0. 9 9 2 1 2 1 1 \right] - \left[ C _ {M} + 0. 4 7 1 0 3 3 3 \right] \right\}} \\ & {\qquad \times 1 0 0} \\ & {= \exp \{0. 5 2 1 0 8 7 8 \} \times 1 0 0} \\ & {= 1 6 8. 3 9 \%.} \end{array}
$$

Therefore, 80386 or 80386SX based desktop computers are about 68% more expensive than the machines based on 80286 microchip.
