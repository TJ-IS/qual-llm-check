# Information technology and global competition A framework for analysis

Boon Siong Neo \*

School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore

Despite current concerns about the international dimensions of business, the strategic potential of information technology in enabling a firm to compete internationally has been ignored. This paper addresses the topic by proposing a framework for considering the strategic role of information technology in global competition. Three key issues face multinational companies: configuration, coordination and responsiveness. Information technology helps multinational companies resolve these issues by providing them with effective means to support their foreign subsidiaries, integrate their worldwide operations more efficiently, respond to local market needs more flexibly, and serve their customers innovatively. Several implementation problems are also discussed, including lack of a strategic vision, lack of infrastructure and expertise, host government intervention, and difficulty in transferring applications.

Keywords: Information Technology, Globalization, Global Strategy, Global Competition, Strategic Information Systems, Management of Information Technology.

Calls by business and government leaders to consider the international impacts of strategic decisions are becoming a cliche in conferences and business publications. In the information systems field, literature on the strategic role of IT (Information Technology) has implicitly assumed competition in a domestic industry; the potential of IT for a firm competing internationally is mainly ignored. This paper addresses this neglect by (1) discussing the nature of competition in the global market, (2) identifying strategic issues for MNCs (Multinational Companies), (3) suggesting a framework for evaluating whether and how IT may be deployed (and their implications for managers and researchers), and (4) discussing critical implementation issues. Actual examples are used throughout for clarification and illustration.

## MNCs and International Strategy

MNCs are companies which have significant operations and market interests beyond the geographical boundaries of their home countries (Hout et al., 1982; Anthony et al., 1984; Vernon and Wells, 1986). Their various international operations are characterized by common ownership, common strategy, and reliance on a common pool of resources like finance, technology, systems, patents, and management.

Corporations are motivated by various factors to expand their business internationally (Porter, 1980; Doz, 1980, 1986a). First, MNCs can increase their revenue by exploiting strategic advantages like a technological lead, a strong trade name, and strong management or marketing skills. Second, there may be significant economies of scale; production costs can be driven down the learning curve at a faster pace; capital can often be raised at lower costs; and top managerial personnel can be attracted and retained more easily in an expanding business. Third, needed skills may be available overseas; for example, the availability of skilled English-proficient software programmers has attracted many computer firms and software houses to India. Fourth, lower factor costs have attracted many MNCs to set up subsidiaries in the NICs (Newly Industrialized Countries, like South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore). Fifth, some countries (e.g., Brazil and India) with large markets require firms to operate within their borders before they are permitted to sell their products there. Sixth, MNCs may reduce risks through diversifying both supply sources and product markets. Finally, the international presence of a firm may be a significant advantage in dealing with governments, bidding for international contracts, and in collecting and processing crucial information.

## Strategic Issues in International Strategy

An MNC's environment is more complex than a comparable domestic company for a multiple of reasons: it operates (1) within and among different sovereignties, (2) under widely different economic, legal, regulatory and cultural conditions, (3) with people of different value systems, (4) over great geographical distances, and (5) in national markets varying in structure, population and area (Robinson, 1984; Leontiades, 1985). The instability of the international financial system, with exchange rates movements that are often unpredictable adds to the environmental uncertainty in managing an MNC.

Strategic issues are the key choices that MNCs must address to be successful in this more complex and uncertain context (Bartlett, 1986). The value of IT will depend on its ability to assist MNCs in addressing and resolving the three strategic issues of organizational configuration, coordination of subsidiaries, and responsiveness to host governments.

## 1. Configuration

Configuration concerns the choice of where to locate a firm's activities worldwide (Porter, 1986).

Configuration decisions are important, because they often represent major strategic moves and heavy investments and may restrict future strategic options if investments are not mobile.

Configuration advantages are contingent upon comparative advantage in a country of operation as well as competitive advantage of value activities performed in that country (Kogut, 1985a).

Comparative advantage between countries (e.g., low factor costs and high market potential) may be important to a firm's worldwide position and be exploited through a strategic investment in that country. For example, Apple Computer expanded its operations overseas because of incentives offered by Ireland and Singapore, although it was not at the time actively considering geographical expansion:

“During the summer of 1978, no fewer than a dozen government agencies visited a single Cupertino, California, computer manufacturer. The agencies hailed from three continents.... The objectives of all these agencies were the same: to get the management of Apple Computer to consider a new plant location in their jurisdictions. The job of these contenders would not be easy. Apple’s management was not actively considering expanding to a new site. Yet three years later Apple had established two new plants: one in Ireland, the other in Singapore.” (Encarnation and Wells, 1986).

Comparative advantages offered by countries are susceptible to changes in government policies and political regimes, requiring constant monitoring of these developments so that appropriate actions may be taken to preserve the advantages.

Competitive advantage is firm-specific (Porter, 1985) and influences the choice of value activities, resources, and technologies to invest in a country. Although changes in value activities at a location may be within the control of a firm, the phasing in and out of different activities consumes a substantial amount of resources and may disrupt current synergies in the network.

## 2. Integration / Coordination

Integration deals with linkages among activities performed in various countries. Increased uncertainty and complexity resulting from international competition makes coordination a critical success factor (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978). Complexity measures the number of variables a firm has to consider in making major decisions, while uncertainty refers to the relative lack of knowledge concerning the values, direction, and outcomes of those variables.

Integration provides MNCs with the potential to maximize their total system-wide margin between prices and costs, to exploit imperfections in labor and financial markets, and to improve bargaining power with host governments.

Integration also increases risks for MNCs. Interdependencies inherent in a network make coordination more complex and difficult and disruptions in individual subsidiaries may have significant negative impacts on the entire firm's performance. Exchange rate fluctuations may also play havoc with integration strategies. Although the unique feature of an international strategy may lie in creating the operational flexibility to profit from uncertainty in exchange rates, government policies, and competitor moves, the key issue in achieving global advantage is whether a firm has the managerial skills and organizational resources to coordinate its international activities in response to market and political fluctuations (Kogut, 1985b). Resolving what needs to be coordinated and how this is to be implemented is at the heart of an international strategy.

The essence of coordination is the communication and processing of information (Malone et al., 1987). Therefore, a firm's ability to coordinate a worldwide business successfully is contingent upon the creation of appropriate organizational structures and relationships within which flows of information can take place efficiently and effectively (Galbraith, 1977).

## 3. Responsiveness

MNC success are often dependent upon their responsiveness to host governments (Doz, 1986b; Doz and Prahalad, 1980; De Woot, 1982; Fannin and Rodrigues, 1986; Leontiades, 1986; Mahini and Wells, 1986). Since MNCs operate in many social, political, and legal systems, sensitivity and contributions to host governments' objectives and desires are critical to their continued operating presence. Depending on their relative bargaining positions, the degree and form of responsiveness may vary from financial support for social programs to being an agent to carry out social policy objectives like other national companies.

Responsiveness to host governments is especially important for firms in a multidomestic industry, since national markets are usually protected by trade barriers. The position of firms in each national market is often the result of negotiations with the government rather than decided by market and economic forces alone. MNCs which depend on host government policies for business advantage must ensure that the legal, bureaucratic, and political environment in the host country is stable enough to risk the investments.

A key contingency is whether an MNC can negotiate with, and obtain from the host government, a favorable agreement relative to other firms in the host country. Sources of MNC leverage in negotiations include possession of technology considered important by host governments, economies of scale that make it difficult for local companies to compete, and access to world markets. In contrast, host government leverage includes control of access to local markets and incentives in the form of grants, tax breaks, and participation in government contracts.

## Role of Information Technology in International Strategy

IT has been defined to include storage, processing, and communication of information (Bakos, 1985). Galbraith's information processing view of organizational design provides a theoretical foundation for considering the strategic role of IT in international strategy. He argued that as organizations face increased complexity and uncertainty they require more information for decision-making. However, managers' cognitive limits for processing the overload in information is limited. Therefore, organizations have to choose between reducing the need for additional information by reducing performance standards (through slack resources or self-contained tasks) and increasing their capacity to process information (through information systems or lateral relationships). Thus Galbraith contends that increasing organizational information processing capacity is the only way to cope with increased complexity and uncertainty without reducing performance standards.

Applying this theory to international strategy leads to one major conclusion: as companies expand overseas and face greater complexity and uncertainty, to maintain performance they must increase their information processing capacity. Investment in IT is a major means of increasing organizational information processing capacity.

Porter and Millar (1985) suggest that the strategic value of IT will be greater for firms with more information intensive products and value chains. Although internationalization may not affect the basic nature of products, information intensity in the value chain may be increased, because geographical dispersion of value activities tends to increase the number of suppliers and customers that an MNC must deal with. Therefore, MNCs may find IT to be of greater strategic importance than comparable domestic firms.

Increased complexity, uncertainty, and information intensity resulting from participation in the international arena provide a climate in which IT can play a significant role of supporting and shaping MNC strategy.

## A Proposed Framework

The first dimension of the framework, MNC structure, differs markedly from industry to industry, varying along a spectrum from “multidomestic” to “global” in competitive scope. In a multidomestic industry, MNCs pursue separate strategies in each of their foreign markets and view their competitive challenges independently from market to market. The key advantage of a multidomestic structure is a firm’s ability to be fast and flexible in responding to individual markets without a need to rationalize and justify actions on an overall worldwide basis. Each subsidiary is autonomous and competitive outcomes over time are determined by conditions in each country.

In a global industry, MNCs compete across their entire worldwide system of products and markets. Subsidiaries are highly interdependent in terms of strategy and operations, and MNCs must integrate their activities on a worldwide basis to capture the linkages among countries. The key advantage of a global structure is increased efficiency, resulting in lower cost production and higher margins.

The second dimension, MNC strategies, are approaches that firms adopt to outperform competitors (Dess and Davis, 1984). Porter (1980) argues that firms pursue one of three generic strategies: cost leadership, product differentiation, and focus. However, the focus strategy encompasses both the type of advantage desired (cost or differentiation) and strategic scope (broad or narrow). Therefore, only the first two generic strategies will be considered, since our primary interest is in the type of advantage rather than strategic scope.

The first strategy, overall cost leadership, may require aggressive construction of efficient-scale operations, tight cost and overhead control, avoidance of marginal customer accounts, and cost minimization in areas like R&D, service, sales force, and advertising. The second strategy, differentiation, aims at creating products that are perceived industrywide as being unique. This may be achieved through design or brand image, technology, features, customer service, and dealer network.

Combining MNC structures and strategies into a matrix enables us to evaluate the role of IT under different postures chosen by MNCs. Such a framework is shown in Table 1.

Each description in the matrix (Subsidiary Support, Response Flexibility, Operational Integration, Service Innovation) indicates a key issue for success under each structure and strategy. The lower case words in each cell describe the roles which IT may play in allowing MNCs to address their critical issues.

A basic assumption in deriving the critical success factors in each cell is that success is achieved only when there is a fit or congruence (Venkatraman and Camillus, 1984) between MNC structure and strategy. It means that consistency between strategy and structure is a necessary condition for achieving global competitiveness. For example, a multidomestic MNC pursues a cost leadership strategy by providing whatever support is necessary for each subsidiary to achieve cost objectives without jeopardizing operational autonomy while meeting local needs. On the other hand, a global MNC pursues cost leadership goals by aggressively rationalizing and integrating their worldwide activities to achieve lowest operational costs. Similarly, multidomestic and global MNCs pursue differentiation strategies by emphasizing flexible local responsiveness and innovative customer service, respectively.

## Cell 1: Subsidiary Support

When an MNC pursues a cost leadership strategy with a multidomestic structure, one of its major considerations is how subsidiaries may be effectively supported without eroding their operational autonomy. Although multidomestic firms treat each market as strategically different, subsidiaries rely heavily on intangible assets of the parent company to formulate appropriate responses in each market. Such intangibles are usually based on knowhow and information not easily or cheaply available to local firms. Examples include company reputation, trade name, management and technical support, new product and manufacturing process technology, and company experience in other markets.

The availability of such support is critical to a cost leadership strategy, because subsidiaries can lower costs by avoiding unnecessary experimentation in situations new to the subsidiary but “old” to the company, locating equipment problems more rapidly and reducing operational down-times while avoiding the need to set up technical support units in all locations. These units (e.g. specialized service personnel) can be grouped together to obtain synergies in training, supervision, and recruiting.

IT facilitates parent company support to cross-border subsidiaries by enabling rapid transmission of information across great geographical distances.

Although multidomestic MNCs give their subsidiaries autonomy to make operational decisions tailored to local needs, linkage to headquarters often means that some uniform policies must be followed by all subunits. When cost policies are instituted to implement a cost leadership strategy, rapid and accurate reporting on compliance to these policies enables close monitoring by corporate headquarters and increases divisional motivation for cost control (Arvai, 1986). For example, firms have used IT to save millions of dollars by managing inventories distributed over several continents.

## Cell 2: Response Flexibility

The key to a multidomestic firm's success in a differentiation strategy is its responsiveness to specific local needs. Yet its affiliation to an MNC makes it different from other national firms, even with no manufacturing integration, intersubsidiary trade, or centralized management. For instance, even when subsidiaries are independent profit centers with full autonomy over national price and product decisions, an MNC can still offset gains and losses among subsidiaries. Therefore, its subsidiaries may be willing to take higher risks (for potentially higher returns) than a typical national firm.

The potential to gain differentiation advantage over firms within national markets stems from the accessibility to the subsidiary of available knowhow and information learned elsewhere in the company. IT gives subsidiaries an advantage because such information may be made available to local customers as a means to differentiate the product/service of subsidiaries, especially when such knowledge, information, or experience, are not readily available.

A case in point is Medicare-Glaser, which implemented an information system that warns a customer of potentially dangerous effects caused by the interaction of certain prescription drugs (Hammer and Mangurian, 1987). The easy accessibility of this information through communication technology to all subsidiaries serves to differentiate its product/service from other (local) firms without access to the same knowledge. As another example, Xerox service personnel all over North America and Europe can access the company's system by phone for client and diagnostic data, thereby enabling Xerox to give faster and more accurate service (Keen, 1986).

These examples illustrate the critical difference between available and accessible information. IT makes available information accessible, thereby increasing its value. Dun and Bradstreet have been able to sell the Official Airline Guide online for twice what it charges for the printed one. IT makes a difference by giving subsidiaries access to critical, but non-public information.

For multidomestic firms, the choice of possible markets is a critical decision. IT gives the firm flexibility in the choice of markets, because headquarters can still maintain control and give the necessary support to subsidiaries in far-away locations. IT collapses both time and distance. The availability of fast and accurate information and communication channels help remove some of the constraints in establishing a presence in potentially high-return markets around the world.

## Cell 3: Operational Integration

Integration and coordination are central to the success of a global firm. Integration involves the division of labor and specialization among subsidiaries across borders in an integrated multinational production/distribution network. The result is that a collection of relatively small subsidiaries can operate as though they were one big system and gain economies of scale normally lost as a result of dispersion.

Various schemes for integration have been used by MNCs, including specialization by products, processes, and activities in the value chain. The value of IT lies in its ability to transfer information between remote sites as readily and efficiently as can be done within a single physical site (Clemons and McFarlan, 1986). IT makes available information that is needed by an MNC for managing interdependencies of geographically dispersed subsidiaries as it rationalizes its operations worldwide in terms of lowest possible costs.

Although EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) has often been cited as a key means for achieving integration (Robinson and Stanton, 1987), it is by no means the only way; e.g., many MNCs (like IBM, AT&T, and Hewlett Packard) have set up purchasing offices in the Far East to buy cheap, high-quality electrical components for their operations around the world. Telecommunication technology has enabled them to obtain requirements from plants worldwide, and compare prices and component availability before committing to purchase. Cost savings thus obtained outweigh additional administrative and transportation costs. As another example, IBM has long been using communications technology to coordinate and configure computer systems for their customers from plants around the world.

MNCs have typically set up manufacturing plants overseas to take advantage of lower labor costs while retaining R & D operations at headquarters, where the technical expertise is concentrated. However, manufacturing engineers' involvement in the design process is critical to shortening the development cycle, increasing the number of alternative designs considered, reducing production costs and enhancing manufacturing quality. IT applications allow design engineers, manufacturing engineers and manufacturing analysts to be involved in the design process without them being physically together (e.g., Xerox's ECN system as described in Malone et al., 1987).

Further, IT reduces integration cost by reducing communication costs. Owens-Corning estimates that by using electronic mail rather than conventional message channels (mail and long-distance calls), it saves for each message, \$0.25 transmission costs and \$4 employee time (Keen 1986). In fact, Texas Instruments's cost per message for worldwide electronic mail averages less than four cents. Telecommunications also reduce cost by capturing and validating data at its source, eliminating the need for re-entry of data upon receipt and reducing costly errors. One firm estimated that eliminating data re-entry saves it about 35% of the transaction costs!

## Cell 4: Service Innovation

IT is allowing global companies to differentiate through innovations in customer service. IT enables a firm to provide enhanced customer service by increasing the speed of the firm's operations as perceived by its customers, expanding coverage beyond traditional activities, and providing integrated, one-stop services.

For example, an international bank installed a worldwide communications system that allows loan applications originating in East Asia to be transmitted electronically to New York for analysis and approval. This resulted in a significant reduction in the time required to respond to loan requests. The bank used the responsiveness perceived by customers to establish an advantage over competitors who still use conventional, paper-based communications.

A major trend in international banking, resulting from advances in telecommunication technology, is a move away from specialty-type credit cards to one all-purpose card which may be used to access the whole range of banking services (Grant, 1986).

Innovative IT applications that have been used successfully in the home market may be transferred to overseas subsidiaries for implementation (McFarlan, 1986). In an industry where the customer needs flexible and fast access to the supplier, a terminal in the customer's office is a means for redefining service by eliminating time and geography as barriers to customer relationships.

IT also speeds up a firm's penetration of overseas markets. A market entry can be established quickly with a skeleton staff, while most of the support can be received from corporate headquarters through a telecommunication link. Further, restrictions to market entry are often not physical but legal and regulatory. A communications-based application may be used to establish an electronic presence, thereby overcoming regulatory restrictions. However, changing laws may complicate the situation.

Communications technology enables a firm to enter markets by expanding the time window during which it is allowed to do business on an international basis; e.g., a brokerage firm can operate in multiple trading markets effectively on a twenty-four-hour basis, thereby permitting its customers to place and execute orders at any time of day.

## Implications for Managers

The framework may be used to consider how IT applications can be used to gain competitive advantage in the international arena. Depending on whether a firm has a multidomestic or global structure, and whether it is seeking a cost or differentiation advantage, the framework provides a starting point for analysis and identification of ideas which may be implemented. For example, a multidomestic firm pursuing a cost leadership advantage should look for applications that emphasize support using telecommunications for easy access and fast reporting.

Conversely, if a firm possesses distinctive IT expertise, the framework may indicate which structures or strategies may be profitably pursued.

A multidomestic firm with sophisticated and integrated information systems that enables it to exchange information amongst its subsidiaries efficiently should consider the potential afforded by adopting a more global structure where coordination is a critical success factor.

The framework also suggests that a fit between MNC structure, strategy, and IT deployment may result in maximum effectiveness. Thus, firms can use this as an analytical tool to evaluate the current degree of fit and take appropriate steps to achieve greater congruence. Change may take one or more of the following forms: change in strategy; change in structure; developing the necessary IT architecture; acquiring the needed equipment; developing the essential software; recruiting the essential technical and managerial personnel; reorganizing the IT function to support the firm's strategy and obtaining ideas for strategic IT applications.

## Implications for Research

We have suggested that an appropriate fit among MNC strategy, structure and IT utilization may maximize the effectiveness of firms operating in the international arena. This is a basic proposition derived from applying contingency theory (Drazin and Van de Ven, 1985); this can be transformed into a set of four testable hypotheses:

H1: An MNC pursuing a cost leadership strategy will achieve maximum competitive advantage by employing IT applications that support communications, management reporting, and large scale integrated operations.

H2: An MNC pursuing a differentiation advantage will achieve greatest competitive advantage by employing IT applications that give flexibility in entry decisions, responsiveness in meeting local needs, improvements in customer service and penetration of new markets.

H3: A multidomestic MNC will achieve greatest competitive advantage by employing IT applications that give information support to subsidiaries without eroding flexibility in making operational decisions to meet local needs.

H4: A global MNC will achieve greatest competitive advantage by employing IT applications that integrate worldwide applications.

## Issues in Implementation

Using IT for international business is not without its problems. Advances in technology have often outpaced our ability to manage its implications, and IT is no exception (Vitale, 1986). Four key implementation issues are now discussed.

## 1. Strategic Vision

Managing IT as a strategic resource requires a vision of the potential of IT for accomplishing business objectives (King, 1984; Benjamin et al., 1984; Rackoff et al., 1985; O'Loughlin, 1986). Simply because IT has the potential to become a strategic resource does not mean that it will be realized in any firm without managerial initiative, support, and the appropriate formulation of a strategic IT plan. Keen suggests a three-step process for building a strategic vision for IT:

i. Define the outside vision - Look at the industry and marketplace in order to get a sense of strategic trends and options.

ii. Build the internal vision - Use the information to stimulate discussion and to move the issue up to the executive committee. The goal is to get a formal policy commitment.

iii. Turn the vision into a plan - Use that commitment to create the necessary plan, make the business case, and move on to action.

## 2. Infrastructure and expertise

Without the necessary infrastructure and expertise, any strategic vision for IT will remain a dream (Keen, 1981). Grant (1986), in an assessment of major trends in international banking, concluded that:

“Countries that will lead in the provision of enhanced electronic financial services will be those countries that have a right mix of an innovative and aggressive national banking system, national retailers, high acceptance of ATMs and plastics by consumers and a flexible, market driven, telecommunications infrastructure.”

Further, there is a lead time in acquiring the needed hardware, software, personnel, and in developing the application, which may involve substantial expenditure with uncertain payback periods. Therefore, unless the IT infrastructure and expertise are in place, MNCs will not be able to exploit its strategic potential.

## 3. Host government regulations

Transborder dataflow issues have been the subject of much discussion and speculation (Robinson, 1978; Leeson, 1984; The Conference Board Report No. 852, 1984; United Nations Centre on Transnational Corporations Technical Paper, 1982; Diebold, 1987). It is recognized that any impediment to international data transmissions will have serious effects on world trade in general and the internationalization of business in particular. Diebold has labeled governmental regulations of transborder data flow as protectionistic and recommended that they be brought under the ambit of the GATT agreements.

From a host government's perspective, the advances in IT have both potential benefits as well as costs. Transborder dataflows give users access to people all over the world, as well as to a rapidly expanding pool of up-to-date knowledge. This may be significant for developing countries seeking to attract top talent to speed up their development, since geographical distance need not lead to intellectual isolation. Doctors in less developed countries have also been able to tap into diagnostic facilities in more advanced countries via telecommunications. Accessibility to information can potentially improve resource use and productivity, and enable firms in less developed countries to be more competitive in world markets.

However, developing countries also view developments in IT and transborder dataflows as threats. Most developing countries have a weak technical base to build a computer and telecommunications industry. At the same time, data export for processing abroad comes to be regarded as a sign of underdevelopment and becomes an issue of national pride. The information advantage of MNCs may also become a new barrier to market entry for local firms. There is also the fear that MNC subsidiaries will be subjected to greater control from headquarters resulting in less responsiveness to local needs and a decrease in the bargaining power of host governments.

In the final analysis, the issue of national sovereignty is involved. National sovereignty is a country's ability to influence the direction of its political, economic, social, and cultural changes and is eroded to the extent that transborder dataflows involve the “migration of key decision-making functions” abroad. Further, international satellite transmissions tend to blur national borders and governments increasingly feel a loss of control. A related issue is national security, especially when sensitive information is transmitted for processing in foreign locations. There is also the fear that ease in international data transmission (e.g., electronic mail) may reduce usage of postal and telegraphic services run by governments, resulting in loss of revenue.

In a survey of more than a thousand companies in twenty countries, the Conference Board asked executives for the obstacles that their companies have encountered in international data transmission and the impact that this has on their businesses. Privacy and data-protection laws, especially as applied to customer data, personnel data, and registration of databases were the most frequently cited restrictions. This has often led to firms setting up local data processing operations. Other obstacles include poor quality of transmission equipment in many parts of the world, high costs of data transmissions, and the requirement by several countries that companies use locally produced computer and telecommunications equipment. Another concern reported is the desire by some governments to protect national security interests by monitoring all international transmissions (e.g. Taiwan).

As a result, MNCs reported the following problems: need to set up local data processing operations; duplicate databases and other systems; need to use alternative means for communications; additional administrative procedures; transmission delays; incompatible equipment and the need to monitor development in government policy. The net effect for many firms is an increase in the cost of doing business internationally. Many service firms which depended heavily on efficient dataflow report that this would be a critical variable in deciding to set up future overseas subsidiaries.

## 4. Transfer of strategic IT applications

Many MNCs which have used strategic IT applications successfully in their home countries have attempted to transfer these systems to their overseas subsidiaries in the hope of gaining similar competitive advantages abroad. However, McFarlan (1986) observed that “the pattern that emerges is that successes gained through the use of competitive systems are the successes of the US affiliates only, not the entire company. Within their own corporate structures, great difficulty has occurred in attempting to transfer these concepts beyond our borders". Several reasons were offered: multilanguage skills needed, geographical distance, different organizational and institutional structures, and a "not invented here" attitude, with its cultural sensitivities.

## Summary and Conclusions

This paper represents an initial attempt to define the key issues that IT managers and researchers must address to extend the scope of strategic IT applications into the global marketplace. The proposed framework shows that IT allows MNCs better to support overseas subsidiaries, integrate worldwide operations, provide innovative services, and respond to local market needs. IT can enable MNCs to gain competitive advantage in the global market.

We have also discussed implementation issues which may hinder the utilization of global strategic IT applications. If this paper results in greater focus on the potential global role of IT, more discussion on the key issues to be addressed, more precise elaboration of IT impacts, or more discussion on how implementation problems may be overcome, it will have achieved its purpose.
