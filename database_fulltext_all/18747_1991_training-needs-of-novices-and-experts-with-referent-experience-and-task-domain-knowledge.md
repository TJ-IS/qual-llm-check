# Training needs of novices and experts with referent experience and task domain knowledge

Jane M. Mackay

and Charles W. Lamb, Jr. \*

Texas Christian University, Fort Worth, TX 76129, USA

This research examines the similarities and differences in the ways that novices and experts with referent experience and task domain knowledge used spreadsheet software to make decisions. Results in a laboratory setting suggest that software training needs differ for novices and experts, depending upon their levels of expertise in the task domain knowledge and the referent experience areas.

Keywords: Training, Expert/Novice Users, Human/Computer Interaction, Mental Models, Man/Machine Interface.

One critical factor that can enhance the likelihood that microcomputers will be used, and actually perform the tasks that they were purchased to perform, is effective training $[8]$ . Unfortunately, reports of unused personal computers and software indicate that effective training programs are exceptions rather than the norm.

Apparently computer manufacturers, vendors, buyers, and users have underestimated the amount of time and training needed to produce proficient microcomputers users. One recent study revealed that companies were spending less than 2 percent of their IS resources on microcomputer and software training. A 1984 study by the Bureau of Labor Statistics [6] showed that only 37 percent of all information workers had undergone skills training. Twelve percent had received training in school, eleven percent had participated in formal on-the-job programs, and fourteen percent had received informal on-the-job training.

The importance of effective microcomputer and software training has been acknowledged by both practitioners $[13,14,16]$ and researchers $[2,3,5,9,11,17,18,24,27]$ . Unfortunately, most of the empirical literature is descriptive, reporting the results of user perceptions and/or satisfaction studies. Little empirical research has focused on the training needs of microcomputer users who use computers and software programs to solve unstructured or semi-structured problems.

One exception is an experiment reported by Mykytyn [19]. Financial specialists were trained to use a financial analysis software package and instructed to use the software to solve either a short-term cash forecasting problem or a long-term financing problem. Then they were asked to provide their perceptions of the value of software training. Respondent perceptions differed, depending upon the type of problem that they solved. He concluded that training methodologies might need to be tailored to particular software usage applications rather than on the usage of the software in general.

Our study addressed two related questions. Specifically, what are the differences and similarities in the ways that novices and experts use software to make decisions? And what are the implications of these differences and similarities for training?

This study is important for two reasons. First, increasing productivity at all levels, and in all functional areas, is an important goal of many organizations. Improving productivity and the need for faster and more accurate decisions are often cited as reasons that the business community originally embraced the personal computer. Effective training should produce computer user productivity gains $[28]$ . These gains may be experienced at all levels, and in all functional areas, of organizations. “IS-related education/training begets the acceptance and usage of IS technologies throughout the organization” $[20]$ .

A second reason that our study is important is that many office administrators, secretaries, and a broad range of persons with other titles and responsibilities who do not have to background and training of accountants and financial analysts are now expected to utilize business software in their jobs. More information is needed regarding how various user groups use programs such as Lotus 1-2-3, and how improvements in training can be facilitated.

## Conceptual Underpinning

Potential computer users possess a set of individual characteristics that are unique. Two characteristics that specifically affect their aptitude and influence their training needs are (1) task domain knowledge, and (2) referent experiences.

Task domain knowledge is the experience that an individual brings from his or her job. Some individuals have substantial task domain knowledge that has been acquired over several years. Others have little or no experience. A trainee in a new position, for example, may possess very little task domain experience.

Referent experiences are the previous interactions that the individual has had with computer systems and software. A person's referent experience may range from zero to several years.

This background, combined with training in the task domain and/or referent experience area, should change the trainee's expected level of expertise in one or both domains. But simply learning how to manipulate a specific software package or perform specific job tasks is an incomplete outcome.

An important purpose of training is to help users develop accurate mental models of the software, the task domain and the relationship between them. Norman [22] defines the term “mental model” as people’s own view of the world, of themselves, of their own capabilities, and of the task that they are asked to perform, or topics they are asked to learn. In a problem solving situation, people develop mental models regarding the problem and the relationships among its components and/or the alternative solutions. Users’ mental models evolve naturally and, through interaction with the system, users formulate new mental models. These are constrained by the knowledge that users bring to the task and the experience they have with the system. Users' mental models are apt to be deficient in a number of ways, perhaps including contradictory, erroneous, and unnecessary concepts.

Mental models aid users in making inferences about the system, reasoning about it, and guiding actions. To be effective, a training program should provide aids that will help users form initial mental models. After training, end users are expected to be able to solve problems using both their task domain knowledge and referent experience expertise.

## Purpose

Our research examined the problem solving strategies of (1) task domain knowledge experts who were referent experience experts; (2) task domain knowledge novices who were referent experience novices; (3) task domain knowledge novices who were referent experience experts; and (4) task domain knowledge experts who were referent experience novices.

The purpose of this exploratory study was to identify the differences and the similarities in the ways that the four groups use software to make decisions, and to suggest implications of these differences and similarities for software and computer user training. An important question is: what training strategies are best suited for enhancing the performance of the three groups of users other than task domain and referent experience experts?

## Study Design

Twelve subjects, six health care marketing analysts and six accountants were selected to participate in the study. Subjects were divided into four groups based upon whether they were task domain knowledge novices or experts (i.e., health care marketing nonexperts versus experts), and whether they were referent experience novices or experts (i.e., basic versus advanced knowledge of Lotus 1-2-3). A referent experience novice was defined as a person who had basic familiarity with IBM personal computers or so-called “clones” and very limited knowledge of the Lotus 1-2-3 software or other spreadsheet packages. Referent experience experts had substantial experience with the Lotus spreadsheet software.

The experimental task was an unstructured, multi-attribute problem composed of twelve different categories of demographic data for each of seventy-one U.S. Postal Service zip codes (geographic areas). Subjects were instructed to locate, by zip code, six sites for emergency medical clinics using Lotus 1-2-3 version 1a. The data were arranged in the form of a database of thirteen columns by seventy-one rows. Subjects were given two hours to complete the exercise. Each subject was told to 'think aloud' while solving the problem. All subjects practiced 'thinking aloud' while solving two problems unrelated to the actual experimental task. This particular procedure was based on the procedures described by Ericsson and Simon [10]. After practicing 'thinking aloud' with the two warm-up tasks, subjects were given a sheet of paper containing the problem and a sheet of paper with the column headings of the Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheet. Recordings and transcriptions of the subjects' verbalizations during the problem solving process were produced. Additionally, each subject's computer keystrokes were recorded by a VHS camera and transcribed along with the verbal transcriptions.

## Protocol Analysis

Frequently researchers studying cognitive phenomena use the think-aloud method. "Protocol analysis" is the process of translating this chaotic collection of verbalizations into more accessible representations [4]. Criticisms on accuracy and completeness of subjects' verbal reports [21] have been addressed by Ericsson and Simon. They contend that concurrent verbal protocols are usually accurate and representative measures of cognitive processes, a condition met in this study as subjects verbalized while solving the problem and not after the fact.

## Results

Table 3 shows a summary of problem solving strategies used by members of each expert/novice group. All groups except the task domain knowledge expert/referent experience expert used basic commands in Lotus 1-2-3 with the addition of the data sort command.

Task Domain Knowledge Expert / Referent Experience Expert Group. As Table 1 shows, this group of subjects used a different combination of problem solving strategies in their mental models from the other three groups. They solved the problem using conditional (if) statements to extract records meeting specified criteria, then ranked or weighted the records, and copied them to a different section of the spreadsheet. This problem solving approach allowed the subjects to evaluate multiple attributes of the data simultaneously. Subjects in this group had been expected to use the data query extract command as their primary command for solving the problem since this is an expedient way of selecting records matching specific criteria. This group did not rely heavily on one of the data elimination commands such as worksheet delete or range erase. Subjects demonstrated an ability to synthesize values of the weighted attributes and solve the problem without using the elimination commands.

Task Domain Knowledge Novice / Referent Experience Novice Group. Subjects in this group solved the problem using similar problem solving strategies to the two groups not having expertise in both dimensions. As shown in Table 1, this group had the highest percentage of comparisons of values of attributes in a column, in a cell of a record, or of one record against another record. Subjects tended to make the comparisons and (1) select records as potential winners and copy them to a different location on the spreadsheet, or (2) eliminate them using the worksheet delete or range erase commands. Subjects spent much of their time making comparisons without any use of the spreadsheet commands.

Subjects did not use the help screens. They avoided utilizing the move command, which operates similar to the copy command. They may have decided not to use the move command for fear of destroying the original database and then not being able to retrieve it back in its original format. Another possibility is that they did not fully understand the difference between copy and move.

Task Domain Knowledge Novice/Referent Experience Expert Group. Subjects in this group had the second highest use of comparisons of records, cells values, or attributes for selecting solutions. This group, being more skilled in the use of the spreadsheet, used the data sort command in conjunction with making comparisons. They sorted attributes and then moved or copied the top records to a different location of the spreadsheet. Next, they compared the various groups of sorted records against each other to make decisions. Records were then selected or eliminated depending upon their criteria for selection.

Task Domain Knowledge Expert / Referent Experience Novice Group. Subjects in this group were third in the use of comparisons of attributes, records, or cell values for solving the problem. This group's mental model consisted of comparing attributes of columns or records and then selecting whether the record should be chosen or eliminated. However, this group was willing to change the visual image of the spreadsheet by using the move command rather than relying on the copy command as did the task domain knowledge novice/referent experience novice group. Both referent experience novice groups relied heavily on visual comparisons of values of attributes as part of their problem solving strategy.

## Discussion

Table 3 summarizes the specific problem solving strategies used by the four groups of users. Through the use of protocol analysis we have been able to determine how individuals with different levels of expertise utilized spreadsheet software to solve a managerial problem. The main conclusion that can be drawn from this exhibit is that both task domain knowledge and referent experience expertise influenced subjects' problem solving strategies. Importantly, only one group (referent experience expert/task domain knowledge expert) used advanced concepts: the conditional (if) statements of the software package. Members of the three other groups relied on basic commands. This finding has important implications regarding how prospective users of software should be trained.

The design of software is important, but appropriate training can sometimes overcome software design limitations. The subjects in this research provided an excellent illustration of what happens when users are not sufficiently trained in new applications of a software package. Even referent experience experts may resort to using basic, rather than advanced commands. This finding supports Mykytyn's [19] conclusion that training methodologies might need to be modified for different task types. Trainers need to utilize problem solving strategies and mental models of referent experience and task domain knowledge experts to build a training program. If these users demonstrate consistency, as was shown in this research, then a goal should be to train novices to gain expertise in such a manner that they will shorten their learning time to solve similar problems. The problem solving strategies and mental model of the experts provide a basis for designing such programs.

Training is critical for any user technology. Concepts must be taught before users try to learn by making mistakes. This research found users attempting to use commands without appropriate understanding of the commands; e.g., subjects did not see the difference between the range erase command and the worksheet delete command and did not understand that a range may be a block of cells, a cell, a row of cells, or a column of cells. The concept of a database/spreadsheet was missing, and perhaps it was because such concepts are not always combined in software packages.

Novice users need to be taught the concepts underlying an electronic spreadsheet before being trained in its use. Each time a new command is introduced, users should be introduced to the concept and its rationale before beginning to use the command.

Prior to summarizing our major findings and implications, a few limitations should be noted. First, the small number of subjects decrease the power of any statistical comparisons. Two reasons for the small size of the group as a whole are: (1) the high cost of transcribing and coding the protocols, which require approximately thirty hours each, and (2) professional precedent [1,7,15]. The direct cost of protocol analysis has caused researchers using the method to restrict sample sizes dramatically. The second limitation is that a variety of the variables were uncontrolled, such as individuals differences regarding the use of health care clinics.

## Summary

The purpose of the experiment reported here was to compare the problem solving behavior of four groups of Lotus 1-2-3 users. The most important finding was that the referent experience and task domain knowledge experts solved the spreadsheet problem different from the other groups, and the other groups followed similar procedures regardless of their referent experience or task domain knowledge expertise. These results suggest that training needs vary, depending upon a user's knowledge of the task domain and his or her familiarity with the software. We need to examine the mental models of the task domain knowledge and referent experience experts, not only in this problem solving environment, but in other environments, to determine how users with different levels of knowledge should be trained. From studying these strategies, we should design training programs which will teach the novice expertise in the most expeditious manner. In order to develop this type of training program, more research is needed on a wide range of questions regarding novice and expert usage of electronic spreadsheets and other types of computer software.
