# Towards a theory of strategic use of information resources

# An inductive approach

Rajiv Sabherwal

Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Florida International University, Miami, FL 33199, USA

William R. King

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Considerable attention has recently been given to strategic applications of information resources. Numerous frameworks to identify and categorize such applications have been proposed, but a systematic theory has been glaringly missing. This article builds upon existing frameworks in an attempt to move towards a theory of strategic use of information resources. It systematically utilizes the wealth of information available in real world examples of strategic applications of information resources and takes a first step towards such a theory. Several useful propositions, “discovered” from success stories using an “inductive” approach for theory building, are provided for strategic use of information resources. These propositions should serve as a base for testable models and hypotheses and should contribute significantly towards future empirical research.

Keywords: Information Resources; Strategic Applications; Competitive Advantage, Inductive Theory Building; Industry Information Intensity.

## Introduction

There have been numerous popular stories about companies using information resources for strategic purposes, or “to gain a competitive advantage”. These applications exhibit a good deal of variety. Thus the information resource utilization may concentrate on the company’s internal operations or on its relationship with outside forces. It may be aimed at internal, competitive, or business portfolio levels of the company’s strategy, and may be caused by technological change or by organizational issues. However, despite – and to some extent due to – all these variations in the success stories, the message about the strategic impact of information resources comes out loud and clear.

Several frameworks have been proposed to identify and categorize strategic applications of information resources (2,15,16,17,18,26,29,34). These frameworks have been based upon theories from other fields, especially the industrial organization economics literature (28). However, there has been a notable absence of a systematic theory to explain and predict information resource utilization (1,20). Here we attempt to move beyond frameworks by taking a first step towards building a theory of strategic use of information resources.

An “inductive” approach for theory building is followed in this article (8,11,32,37). In this approach, the theory is built by looking for, and analyzing, significant patterns in data. It draws upon the wealth of information available in the real world examples of companies which have strategically utilized information resources. Though numerous authors (3,4,5,12,31,35) have used such examples in the past, they have done so primarily to illustrate and support the proposed frameworks. We take a different approach, as the propositions themselves are “discovered” from the success stories.

Rooted in successful business practice, the resulting theory of strategic use of information resources should enhance our understanding of the differences between the less successful applications of information resources and the more successful ones. It should also help us understand how industries where information is a critical resource differ from those where information is less important in strategic applications. Such a theory should be highly relevant to managers and should help them in looking for and predicting strategic use of their information resources.

## Strategic Applications of Information Resources

Strategic applications of information resources have been discussed in several articles. Based on these (especially 13, 19) we arrived at, and employed in this study, the following definition for strategic application of information resources:

A strategic application of information resources is one that has a profound effect on a company's success and destiny, by (a) influencing or 'shaping' the company's strategy, or (b) playing a direct role in the implementation or support of the company's strategy.

## The Inductive Approach To Theory Building

The inductive approach to theory building involves four steps (8,11,32,37):

Step 1: Select a phenomenon and list all its characteristics.

Step 2: Measure all the characteristics of the phenomenon in a variety of situations (as many as possible).

Step 3: Analyze the resulting data carefully to determine if there are any systematic patterns among the data “worthy” of further attention.

Step 4: Once significant patterns have been found in the data, they, as theoretical statements, constitute the “laws” of nature.

A number of concepts (the basic building blocks for a theory) are identified in step 1, and the various applications are mapped onto these concepts in step 2. Analysis of these application-mappings is conducted in step 3, resulting in the generation of propositions discussed in step 4. These propositions are relationships that specify the underlying process, or mechanism from which the theory derives its strength and meaning.

## Step 1: Identification Of Concepts

In this step, use is made of the various frameworks that have been proposed to study the strategic use of information resources. The concepts should be basic but general enough to fit many different contexts. They should be comprehensive and include all those relating to significantly different perspectives of the phenomenon. After a detailed survey of the literature, eleven concepts were selected for the theory-building exercise.

## 1. Process initiation

This indicates how the process leading to the strategic application of information resources was initiated; it is discussed in the strategic management literature (especially 25). According to these authors,

"Decisions may be categorized by the stimuli that evoked them along a continuum. At one extreme are opportunity decisions, those initiated on a purely voluntary basis, to improve an already existing secure market share. At the other extreme are crisis decisions, where organizations respond to intense pressures. Here a severe situation demands immediate action ... opportunity and crisis decisions may be considered to form two ends of a continuum. Problem decisions ... fall in between, evoked by milder pressures than crises."

## 2. Process driving force

This concept is based on a study by Huff and Munro (13) who found “the Information Technology Assessment and Adoption (ITAA) related behavior” of some firms to be driven by business issues deemed to be critical at that time. An example of such an issue is the rapid increase in the interest rate which made inventory holding cost vital for distribution companies such as McKesson and American Hospital Supply Company (now part of Baxter Travenol). In other firms, the ITAA process was primarily driven by new technology, as exemplified by the effect of satellite communication technology on information resource applications in Gannett and Dow Jones. In some others, they found that neither issues nor technology were an obvious driving force. They did not, however, encounter any case where both issues and technology were driving the ITAA process.

## 3. Information resource used

Two kind of information resources – information and information technology – have been identified:

“Information technology … includes both the hardware components that make up the information system architecture and the systems software that enables it to function as an integrated whole. Information can be thought of as data that have been evaluated in such a way that it alters our expectations or our views of the alternatives that are available.” (20)

It would thus be said that it is not the information technology, but the information itself, which is the key factor in achieving competitive advantage if the application is based on the use of important new information or of available information in new ways. In some situations, both can be used to gain competitive advantage.

## 4. Information systems skill utilized

Information system applications require at least one of three generic skills: information storage, information processing, or information communication (1,34).

(a) Information storage has two important aspects: the volume of information and the time for which it is stored (providing history, trend analysis, learning by experience, etc.).

(b) Information processing, which may be associated with (or even be part of) a specific product or service, parts of the firm, or the entire firm.

(c) Information communication, which is related to acquisition of information from outside the firm, release of information to the external world, or information flows within the firm.

## 5. Value chain / Product focus

Porter and Millar have discussed the way in which information technology is permeating the value chain. This concept “divides a company’s activities into the technologically and economically distinct activities it performs to do business”. The value chain includes primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service) and secondary activities (firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement).

They also discuss the way one or more products of the company could be affected by information technology through information provided with the product. The focus then is on the product rather than the system leading to production, sales etc., of the product.

## 6. Level of corporate strategy affected

One or more of three levels of corporate strategy can be affected by an information resource application. These three strategy levels (1) are: (a) Internal strategy, concerned with the development of efficient and effective organization structures and processes, (b) Competitive strategy, focusing on competitor moves within the industry where the organization does business, and (c) Business portfolio strategy, concerning the choice of business and how to position the firm within these industries. These components of corporate strategy are closely related, and information resources can affect more than one of them simultaneously.

## 7. The effect on key competitive forces

This concept is based on Porter's work on industry analysis (27,28). He has identified five key competitive forces: (a) the threat of new entrants, (b) the rivalry among existing competitors, (c) the pressure from companies providing substitute products, (d) the bargaining power of buyers, and (e) the bargaining power of suppliers.

He maintains that firms wishing to gain a competitive edge should consider building defenses against these competitive forces and formulate specific actions to directly influence them. Information technology can alter the balance with these competitive forces (22,29).

## 8. The form of competitive advantage

Five different forms of competitive advantage may result from a strategic application of information resources (30,36): (a) differentiation advantage, which may be achieved by distinguishing the company's products/services from competitors, or by reducing the differentiation advantage of rivals; (b) cost advantage, which may be achieved by reducing the firm's costs, its suppliers' costs, or its customers' costs, or by raising the costs of its competitors; (c) innovation advantage, which may be achieved by introducing a product or process change that results in a fundamental transformation in the way business is conducted; (d) growth advantage, which may be achieved by volume or geographical expansions, backward or forward integration, product-line or entry diversification; and (e) alliance advantage, which may be achieved by forging marketing agreement, forming joint ventures, or making acquisitions related to the thrusts of differentiation, cost, innovation, or growth.

## 9. Degree of innovativeness

A distinction has been made in the innovation literature (7,9) between incremental and radical innovations. An incremental innovation is a minor improvement or adjustment, while a radical innovation is a fundamental change that represents a risky departure from existing practice. Degree of innovativeness increases from incremental to radical innovations.

In the context of strategic applications of information resources, which are a special case of innovations, degree of innovativeness represents the degree to which the company strategically utilizing information resources is relatively earlier in adopting new ideas in comparison to its competitors.

## 10. The size of competitive advantage

"A firm has competitive advantage if it is able to obtain returns on its investments (in a given industry) that are better than normal" (6). This definition of competitive advantage is difficult to operationalize. Also, an application is strategic if it either provides the company with a competitive advantage, or reduces the competitive advantage of a competitor. In the latter case, the size of competitive advantage can be considered as the extent to which the application reduces the competitor's advantage.

## 11. Information intensity

In addition to these concepts, which are all characteristics of the specific resource application, an industry-level characteristic, information intensity is also used in this study. Linder and Ives (21) have thus defined information intensity:

“Information intensity is defined as the amount of intellectual work done by people as they conduct their affairs … products are information intensive if their selection, purchase, use, and maintenance require careful research and thoughtful consideration by the customer. A firm’s value chain is information intensive to the extent it requires intellectual effort by employees.”

Several other authors have also touched upon this concept in the past. Thus McFarlan, McKenney and Pyburn (23) point out that

"For some organizations, IS (information system) activities represent an area of great strategic importance, while for others they will always play a cost-effective and useful, but distinctly supportive, role .... Some companies, like many banks and insurance companies are ... critically dependent on the smooth functioning of IS activity ..."

Similarly Porter and Millar (29) state that

“Although a trend toward information intensity in companies and products is evident, the role and importance of the technology differs in each industry.”

## Step 2: Mapping Of Applications

## The collection of information

An extensive survey of business articles yielded several unstructured case discussions about each application; together these provide detailed information about the application, its context, and its results for the company.

Cases were believed to be of particular value since they provide a vivid and detailed account of the strategic application of information resources. In reporting a similar use of cases published in business magazines, Miller and Friesen (24) have identified the following advantages of such published data:

“Cases also often supply objective information on the industry of the firm rather than the subjective impressions of an executive of the company. It is more difficult to hide the real situation from a case writer, normally a professional who studies a firm in detail, than from a remote researcher who asks a busy executive to rate a number of quantified scales. Finally cases often separate fact from the opinion of company managers.”

Naturally, there are also some drawbacks associated with case data. The case writer might have misrepresented an information resource application as strategic when it actually provided the company with only minor benefits. Also, different cases provide different type of information. Some cases may not address our research variables. Furthermore, two levels of abstraction are involved in the use of case data – first the case writer must interpret the situation, and then the researcher must evaluate the written cases. To offset such disadvantages resulting from the use of case data a number of precautions were taken.

Strategic applications of information resources have been quite rare. This was evidenced by the fact that though the data-collection was started for 91 information resource applications, only 34 (described briefly in Appendix I) were considered valuable for mapping. The elimination of the other 57 applications was for one of two reasons. In some cases, the application was not considered to be strategic after looking into it in greater depth. In other cases, not enough information was available from secondary sources to obtain an uncontroversial mapping onto the concepts (especially the process initiation variable).

To ensure that each application was considered from several perspectives, and thus to reduce possible misinterpretation of the cases, we sought out multiple sources of information on each application. A total of 144 articles/cases were examined, yielding a set of 34 applications, thus giving an average of about 4.2 per application. A list of the articles and cases used is provided in Appendix II.

## The mapping of applications

The extensive information resulting from the above process was used to map each application onto the eleven concepts. Each of the selected applications was independently mapped by four judges who had all done some research on strategic information resource applications. Before examining the information about the application, these judges and another researcher discussed the concepts and arrive at consistent ways of mapping the applications. At the conclusion of this meeting, each judge was provided the information about the 34 applications.

The judges were then requested to identify any applications which they did not consider strategic. The fact that no judge (based on the considerable information available about each application) identified any of the applications as non-strategic, indicates that they may be indeed considered strategic.

Each of the eleven concepts, except size of competitive advantage, degree of innovativeness, and information intensity, incorporates several alternatives. The judges mapped the applications onto these concepts by checking one or more alternative for each. For example, a judge could check both information storage and information communication, if it was felt that the application used both these skills. When an alternative within a concept was considered true by a judge, it was scored as a 1 by that judge. Otherwise, a score of 0 was assigned. To arrive at the composite score, the ratings of the four judges were averaged. If the average was below 0.5 a score of 0 was assigned to the composite, a 1 was assigned otherwise.

For size of competitive advantage, a different procedure was used. The judges were asked to use a 3-point scale. Scores of 1, 2 and 3 were assigned for 'low', 'medium', and 'high' respectively. The ratings of the four judges were then averaged. The applications were then ranked in descending order of the resulting average score. From the resulting order, the first 17 applications were considered as yielding 'high' size of competitive advantage (score of 1) and the lower 17 applications considered as yielding 'low' size of competitive advantage (score of 0). A similar procedure was used for degree of innovativeness.

## Step 3: Analysis Of Data

According to Zaltman et al. (37),

"Step 3 would entail analyzing the data to determine if any systematic patterns can be identified. This might involve generating frequencies and cross-tabulation. If sample sizes are small, nonparametric statistics could be used. With larger samples, a host of multivariate methods are available. Any statistical analysis used must go hand in hand with the intuitive judgment as to the validity of the findings."

Chi-square tests were conducted for a number of combinations, e.g., size of competitive advantage (high/low) and information intensity (high/low) with all the other concept mappings; information resource usage with the information-related process used, degree of innovation, and process driving force, etc. Several interesting results were found significant (Yates correction for small cell frequencies was applied where necessary).

## Step 4: Statement Of Propositions

According to Zaltman et al.,

“Step 4 would focus on generalizing this pattern and presenting it as a theoretical statement” (37).

The observations made in step 3 have been used to arrive at some propositions for future research, which are discussed below.

## Propositions concerning process initiation

1a Opportunity-triggered applications contribute at higher strategy levels than problem-triggered applications, which are primarily internally oriented.

1b Opportunity-triggered applications are highly innovative and result in high size of competitive advantage.

Problem-triggered applications of information resources are commonly utilized at internal strategy level (obs. 12b) and result in low cost advantage (obs. 12c, also 17a). They are thus internally oriented. On the other hand, opportunity-triggered applications of information resources affect new entrants into the industry (obs. 12e) and provide growth advantage (obs. 12g). Opportunity-triggered applications thus contribute at higher levels of corporate strategy (competitive, business portfolio) than problem-triggered applications which seem to be primarily oriented towards increased efficiency and/or reduced costs.

Moreover, opportunistic applications are more innovative (12h, 12f), and yield high competitive advantage (1). Clearly, firms can benefit more from strategic applications of information resources when they are on the lookout for opportunities to develop and deploy such applications. Information resource applications are less beneficial when developed to deal with a problem or a crisis. Of the 34 applications that were considered strategic in this study, as many as 23 had an opportunity trigger. This is a further indication of the need to be more proactive and innovative in developing information resource applications.

## Propositions concerning process driving force

2 Applications that use information processing skill are commonly issue driven while applications that use information communication skill are commonly technology driven.

Strategic applications of information resources for which the assessment and adoption process was issue driven commonly used information processing skill (obs. 13a), while the applications for which this process was technology driven commonly used information communication skill (obs. 13b).

The information processing applications seem to be caused and driven by problems and deficiencies or opportunities for improvement in the existing information system. On the other hand, information communication applications are caused less by the problems/opportunities associated with the existing system and more by excitement about new developments in information communication technology.

## Propositions concerning information resource used

3a Information storage skill supports strategic use of information while information communication skill supports strategic use of information technology.

3b Applications that use information technology are highly innovative and yield high competitive advantage.

The study showed a significant relationship between the use of information (rather than information technology) and the use of information storage skill (obs. 14b, 14c). This result shows that for firms to use information strategically the information storage function is vital. This appears intuitively appealing: for a firm to devise new ways of using information, its available information-base should be high.

In contrast, information communication skill was found significantly related to the use of information technology (obs. 14d). This shows that while information storage helps firms to use information strategically, information communication skill improves strategic use of information technology. This result fits well with proposition 2, according to which also information communication applications are driven by new technological developments.

Moreover, it was found that applications which use information technology (rather than or along with information) are highly innovative (obs. 14f) and yield high competitive advantage (obs. 2). As a result of the rapid developments in information technology, and especially in information communication, technology provides increasing opportunities for innovative and highly beneficial applications.

## Propositions concerning information system skill utilized

4 While information storage skill contributes at business portfolio strategy and competitive strategy levels, information communication skill also contributes at internal strategy level, especially by reducing costs.

Information storage skill does not support applications aimed at improving the firm's position with respect to its competitors (obs. 15b). It does not provide low cost advantage (obs. 15c, also 14a). And it does not contribute at the internal strategy level (obs. 15a). On the other hand, information communication skill helps the firm attain an advantage over its competitors (15d), and also supports low cost applications (15e).

Information storage skill seems to be used primarily at business portfolio or competitive strategy levels. At the competitive strategy level it is used mainly for differentiation (out of 18 information storage applications, 14 provided differentiation advantage, though a significant correlation was not found) and does not contribute much towards low cost advantage. Information communication skill, on the other hand, does provide contribute cost advantage, and contributes at all the three strategy levels.

## Propositions concerning value chain / product focus

5 Value chain applications are oriented more within the organization than product focus applications.

The study shows that value chain applications do not commonly provide innovation (obs. 16a) or growth advantage (obs. 16b), and are not commonly targeted at new entrants (16c). On the other hand, product focus applications are not commonly utilized at internal strategy level (obs. 16d), and do not commonly provide low cost advantage (obs. 16e), leading us to the above proposition.

It is intuitively appealing that the value chain applications contribute more than product applications internally to the organization. Value chain applications improve processes or value activities within the firm or in its link with suppliers and customers; they do not provide advantage with respect to new entrants and do not directly help growth. In contrast, product applications do not help the processes internal to the company by reducing costs or increasing efficiency. Instead, such applications contribute at the competitive strategy level – product improvements can cause differentiation, increase switching costs and improve customer loyalty etc. – and also at the business portfolio strategy level – as new products facilitate growth.

Propositions concerning industry information intensity

6a Applications in high information intensity industries yield high size of competitive advantage.

6b Applications that yield innovation advantage are more common in high information intensity industries than in low information intensity industries and yield high competitive advantage.

The study showed that information resource applications in industries with high information-intensity are associated with greater use of information resources to attain innovation advantage (obs. 10). This finding appears reasonable since the fact that these firms have greater familiarity with the use of information resources enables them to attain innovation advantage by using them. As higher degree of innovativeness and innovation form of advantage are associated with higher size of competitive advantage (obs. 5b, 6), the applications in high information industry firms would be expected to yield greater competitive advantage (obs. 11).

7 Product focus applications are more common in high information intensity industries while value chain applications are more common in low information intensity industries.

As may be expected, information resource applications that improve existing products or result in new products are more common in high information intensity industries (obs. 7a). In high information intensity industries, the products, by their very nature, are more amenable to improvement through information resource applications. It is therefore easier to obtain new and/or improved products using information resources.

On the other hand, value chain applications are negatively associated with information intensity (obs. 7b). The value chain of a firm may use information and be improved using information system skills, regardless of the industry information intensity; of the 34 strategic applications considered in this study only 4 did not improve the firm's value chain activities. However, in some high information intensity industries, value chain activities may already be in such a state that the opportunity to improve these activities using information resources is limited in comparison with the low information intensity industries. This is probably the reason for the somewhat surprisingly negative association between information intensity and value chain improvement, further supported by the fact that the four applications that did not improve the firm's value chain were in highly information intensive companies and yielded high competitive advantage.

8a Applications directed at new entrants are more common in high than in low information intensity industries, and they yield high competitive advantage.

8b Business portfolio applications are more common in high than in low information intensity industries.

The study showed that the information resource applications most frequently concentrated upon the customer as the key competitive force (obs. 4a), in agreement with the recent findings of King et al using different firms. Information resource applications focusing on customers may be expected to be more common as they result in more directly observed benefits. However, the study shows that the applications oriented towards new entrants are more common in high information intensity industries (obs. 9). Moreover such applications lead to higher competitive advantage (obs. 4b).

The study also shows that the information resource applications are predominantly at the competitive strategy and internal strategy levels, with business portfolio strategy level applications being relatively rare (obs. 3). However, as the information intensity increases, there appears to be a shift in level towards business portfolio strategy (obs. 8).

## Discussion

There is little doubt that the goal of building a theory of strategic use of information resources is an important one, and that the task involved is monumental. This study is only a start. It has several implications for future research. It serves to show that an “inductive” approach to theory building could indeed be followed in building such a theory. It demonstrates how patterns emerge from an extensive data collection exercise and how a study of these patterns without previously setting hypotheses can provide useful insights. The study should be of value to future attempts at theory building, especially those employing an inductive approach.

Several useful propositions emerge from the data. They can be empirically tested (using a deductive approach) in future research. They should improve our understanding of strategic use of information resources.

And finally, since high information intensity firms have a substantial understanding of the use of information resources, a move from low information intensity firms to high information intensity firms may be considered similar to progress of a firm from a state of relative inexperience with information resources towards a more experienced state. It would be interesting to see whether firms exhibit similar learning behavior by comparing firms in less information intensity industries with those in high information intensity industries. Longitudinal studies of the change in the utilization of information resources over time would be useful in investigating this process.

Our work also provides some useful practical insights. First, Table 4 compares applications that yielded low competitive advantage with those that provided high competitive advantage. Table 5 summarizes the strategic applications of information resources by industry type. These exhibits offer useful comparisons for managers who want to use information resources strategically.

Future information resource applications should also benefit from the results of this study. For example, managers might find it interesting that information communication contributes more towards low cost and at an internal strategy level, while information storage is more useful at business portfolio and competitive strategy levels. This and other such findings should aid managers as they search for opportunities to use information resources strategically.

Propositions 6 to 8 deserve a special mention. Together they imply that firms in high information intensity industries develop information resource applications that (a) are more frequently product oriented, (b) more often provide innovation advantage, (c) are more commonly directed at new entrants, (d) more often contribute at the business strategy level, and (e) yield higher competitive advantage. Moreover, if an increase in industry information intensity industry may be considered similar to progress of a firm in its utilization of information resources, then these findings have even further implications: they suggest how a firm would “learn” in its strategic utilization of information resources as it gains experience with such applications.

Some limitations of the paper need to be mentioned. Firstly, the paper is based only on 34 applications. This adversely affects generalizability of the results.

Secondly, the study used an inductive approach, necessitated by its early nature. No prior hypotheses were stated. This limitation may be overcome in future research by testing the propositions “discovered” here.

Thirdly, the study suffered from a lack of any objective measures for size of competitive advantage, degree of innovativeness, and industry information intensity. In our study multiple raters, using a vast amount of information about each application, did show considerable agreement in rating variables. However, our findings could be confirmed by developing and using objective measures.

And finally, it should be recognized that the focus of this paper is on the nature of successful strategic applications. It does not investigate failed strategic information resource applications. Also the processes – decision-making, development, and implementation – that led to these applications were (with the exceptions of process initiation and process driving force) difficult to capture and considered beyond the scope of this paper. A well-developed theory of strategic use of information resources should incorporate failed strategic information resource applications and also the decision-making processes that precede the application and the development and implementation of the application. This study is only a first step towards a theory of the strategic use of information resources, but future research efforts should cover these aspects in greater detail.

## Appendix 1: The Information Resource Applications

1. American Airlines developed the Sabre reservation system which lists flight schedules of major airlines in the world, and American gets paid for any reservation made via Sabre on other carriers. The system also gave American preferential treatment, which has been now reduced due to legal/government pressures.

2. American Hospital Supply Corporation (which is now a part of Baxter Travenol), a leading manufacturer and distributor of health-care products, set up computer links with hospitals. The system simplified ordering processes for customers, reduced costs for both AHSC and the customer, and led to increase in customer loyalty and AHSC market share.

3. American Express Co. uses information resources to provide preferential services for its customers. The services include arranging itineraries while at the same time cutting travel costs. Computers search for the lowest airplane fares, track travel expenses for card-holders, and issue monthly statements. This preferential service provides American Express with a significant differentiation advantage.

4. Starting with data-processing and card issuance services for Merrill Lynch, Banc One has made agreements with other brokerage houses and its Financial Card Services Division processes credit-card transactions for several companies. It has grown at a tremendous pace, from \$ 150 million in assets in 1959 to \$ 15 billion in assets in 1986, and a major factor in its growth has been the way it has used its information resources.

5. Car / Puter, an automobile dealer, maintains information on “pricing packages” including for each make and model of car a dealer carries, the manufacturer's list price and dealer cost, option costs, dealer freight and profit. It locates buyers and sells them a computer printout listing the pricing package. Computer printouts help convince buyers they're getting the best deal in town. 6. Citicorp utilized ATMs for customer servicing and marketing by creating much "friendlier" machines than the ones which had earlier proved unsuccessful. It reduced considerably the cost of processing low-profit consumer transactions at branch offices. The ATMs have helped Citicorp more closer to its goal of becoming a nationwide consumer bank despite federal laws prohibiting interstate banking, as Citicorp has access to over 6,500 ATMs in U.S. alone.

7. Citicorp is also taking a lead in global banking telecommunications through its Global Transmission Network which provides up-to-the-minute investment information to 190 cities around the world.

8. Delta Airlines uses data obtained from the Air Traffic Publishing Company to monitor the daily fare changes by their competitors. Its system monitors reservation data, comparing current sales against historical patterns, and advises agents to shrink or expand the number of discount seats available on particular flights. Using the reservation system, Delta can respond to competitors' actions within 2 hours.

9. Digital Equipment Corporation uses two expert systems that help configure equipment orders. This helps reduce the time needed to fill orders and increases accuracy, thus differentiating DEC as a quality provider.

10. Dow Jones & Co., publisher of The Wall Street Journal, has used its page transmission technology to reduce the costs of producing a national – and international – newspaper to the point where it is economically feasible to do so. The company has, as a result, been able to expand into the global market while maintaining control over its operations.

11. Dun & Bradstreet recycles and repackages the accumulated information from its various divisions into new products to increase revenue. To obtain information on Company A, Company B not only pays Dun & Bradstreet, but must also submit its own financial information, and as a result, new information is always coming in.

12. Equitable Life Assurance the nation's third largest insurer, has developed an on-line system that ties the firm's field offices with its regional offices, warehouses, and the corporate headquarters. The online information from the inventory control and purchasing system gives the company leverage during vendor negotiations. The system has resulted in large annual savings for the firm, and has also enhanced the reliability and quality of its deals with its suppliers.

13. Federal Express Corporation has a data-base management system, Cosmos, which tracks all letters and parcels sent by the company. As a result, the company can tell its customers where their packages are in 30 minutes or less. The company maintains a very high delivery rate and high market share.

14. The newspaper chain Gannett Co. has successfully used satellite transmission technology for its national daily, USA Today. The system transmits the four-color paper from its area editorial offices in Washington, D.C., to 30 U.S. printing plants and to Singapore and Zurich.

15. General Electric has built, and continues to augment, a data-base from the information obtained through a toll-free hotline for customer complaints and questions. The accumulated experience and knowledge is used to provide improved customer support, to improve new products, and develop new ones. Some 94 percent of the customers who use the system express satisfaction with the results.

16. McGraw Hill Inc. has set-up several systems to enable its magazines, books, newsletters, economic forecasting units, and financial data operations, to pool information, and in some cases, be cross-marketed. Instead of gathering data for a single publication, reporters store their file in giant computer databases, which some call the McGraw Hill turbine. The turbine churns out several new products, from specialty print publications to online services for customers with personal computers.

17. McKesson Corporation, the nation's largest drug distributor, provides its drugstore customers with terminals. It allows customers to enter orders directly, in return for which they are guaranteed delivery within a certain specified period. This has led to broadened product lines and increased customer loyalty.

18. Mead Corporation's subsidiary Mead Data Central Inc. has used electronic delivery of information to provide users with quick access to vast amount of information. It spent \$ 30 million on a communications network and special software and in 1973 launched its Lexis service which helps lawyers do extensive case research on short notice. By 1986 most law firms used Lexis, which accounted for 70% of Mead Data's \$154 million in annual revenue and generated pretax profits of about \$20 million.

19. Merrill Lynch & Co. combined information on a customer's checking, savings, credit card, and securities accounts into one computerized monthly statement, with the idle funds being automatically "swept" into interest-bearing money market funds. The product, Cash Management Account, has shattered the traditional boundaries between the banking and securities industries. Introduced in 1978, the product had about 1 million customers by 1983, bringing Merrill Lynch \$20 billion in assets.

20. Metpath, a large clinical laboratory, keeps records of patient data on file and offers financial processing services through billing and accounts payable applications, thus achieving differentiation in an otherwise commodity service. This differentiation increases the loyalty of physicians who normally have a tendency to switch from lab to lab in search of lower costs.

21. Navistar International, earlier International Harvester, offers a system Focus to truck fleet operators. The system, which runs on a portable computer, helps customers to estimate the number of maintenance personnel, the investment in spare parts inventory, and the size of the maintenance facility needed to maintain a particular truck or bus fleet. The company has used the system to develop an ongoing relationship with large fleet owners who can now benefit from a more tightly run maintenance operation. Since the system is based on data Navistar has collected over a long period of time, it is difficult for competitors to compete.

22. Owens-Corning Fiberglass Corporation uses data on energy efficiency to help builders evaluate insulation requirements for new building designs. It provides the package “free” to builders as a service for their customers, the home buyers. In return, the company demands the builders to carry its insulation materials exclusively resulting in considerable increase in company sales.

23. Pacific Intermountain Express, a California based trucking firm, uses an information system that allows shippers to call the computer to track the status of a shipment. This is important for misplaced or delayed shipments, emergencies etc. Strategically, it created a way for the company to differentiate its service from its competitors, and to attract shippers, thus increasing its profit opportunities.

24. Procter & Gamble Co. uses phones and terminals to log and disperse customer complaints. It acts as an early-warning system and enables Procter & Gamble to continually refine its products, correcting deficiencies and making improvements.

25. Red Lion Inns chain uses a computerized inventory-control kind of system to monitor the status of rooms in its 52 hotels, to set discount rates, and to enable guests to make reservations from its hotel lobbies. The system enables the chain to have a 70% room-occupancy rate, 2–3% above the national average, and a repeat business of 85%.

26. Red Lobster's nationwide communications network provides management personnel with next day access to sales data at both corporate headquarters and divisional offices across the country. This enables the management to monitor and evaluate results and, if necessary, modify prices or retarget promotions. This pricing strategy ensures some consistency in pricing nationwide while still permitting menus to be carefully tailored to regional audiences.

27. Red River Construction, serving the heavy utilities industry in Texas, uses computerized bids for construction contracts. This permits the company to make numerous last minute changes in a reliable fashion, and, as a result, the bids are more accurate. Such use of information resources helps the company to wait till its last possible moment to finalize its cost and increased the number of times it was the lowest bidder.

28. Using its computerized data and retrieval system for banks and stocks and commodities, Reuters has become a gatherer and disseminator of financial data for world capital markets. As a result, Reuters' profits rose tenfold to \$ 58 million in the five year period 1977–82. Sales of data dwarfed sales to news organization by 9 to 1.

29. Toys “R” Us, the nation’s leading toy chain, relies on a sophisticated information system and point-of-sales terminals in its outlets to keep track of what’s selling so that it can take quick markdowns to rid itself of slow movers. While the toy-retailing industry grew 37% from 1980–1985, Toys “R” Us sales increased 185%. Clever use of information resources is commonly considered to be a major factor responsible for the company’s success.

30. United Airlines developed Apollo reservation system five months after American developed Sabre. Sabre lists flight schedules of major airlines in the world, and United gets paid for any reservation made via Apollo on other carriers. The system also gave United preferential treatment, which has been now reduced due to legal/government pressures.

31. A series of systems that automate policy and claims processing have enabled USAA to do sales and service over the telephone, eliminating agents that boost premium. The company ranks near the top in customer satisfaction with automobile and homeowner claims handling.

32. Wetterau, a large food wholesaler diversified into retailer arena based on its expertise with electronic check out scanners and computerized inventory systems. Wetterau's scannerequipped stores are on-line with its host computer, which updates prices, tracks reordering information, generates shelf labels, and performs other functions.

33. Xerox Corporation has developed a customer support system that provides the customer service representatives with information about the problems he/she will encounter and parts most likely to be needed. It also provides useful information to field support representatives for scheduling the customer service representative to the next site. he system has improved customer satisfaction through faster, high quality response time, and improved productivity of the large technical representative force by increasing the number of calls each can make.

34. Xerox Corporation has a system that supplies master manufacturing schedules online to major suppliers, so they can ship parts precisely at the time Xerox needs them, thus enabling the company to keep its inventory low.
