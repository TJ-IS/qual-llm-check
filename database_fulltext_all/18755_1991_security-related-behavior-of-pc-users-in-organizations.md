# Security-related behavior of PC users in organizations

Jonathan Frank, Boas Shamir and Warren Briggs

Suffolk University, School of Management, Boston, MA 02108, USA

This paper discusses the problems involved in controlling the security-related behavior of personal computer users in organizations. It presents a framework for analyzing these issues and discusses how it was applied to a field study of PC users' behavior and attitudes towards backup, documentation, data storage, and file access practices. Seventy PC users from 12 organizations in the Boston area were interviewed. The variables with the most significant relationship to security-related behavior were PC user knowledge and informal department norms. The existence of formal policies regarding PC security did not appear to be associated with security-related behavior. Interesting interactions were also revealed among formal policies, informal norms, and PC user knowledge; policies and norms are more influential when the users' level of knowledge is low; and knowledge was found to be more influential when there are no policies and when norms are weak.

Keywords: End-User Computing, Microcomputer, Personal Computer. Security-Related Behavior, Motivation, PC Security.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing use of personal computers (PCs) in organizations raises important yet conflicting control issues. On the one hand, employees with a PC have a powerful tool that enhances their productivity and effectiveness. Yet, on the other hand, use of this tool, especially when it is not linked to a central organizational communication network, is difficult to monitor and control. As it becomes more pervasive and critical to an organization's mission, the dangers inherent in poor control and lax security become worthy of senior managers attention.

## 2. Nature of Problem

The growth of “micro-electronization” in the workplace is claimed to offer possibilities for increased worker autonomy and participation $[10]$ . The provision of personal computers to employees, rather than terminals linked to a mainframe computer, represents an organizational choice that may be followed $[22]$ . It may reflect a level of trust in the employees and an expectation that the increased trust and autonomy will be reciprocated by increased levels of responsibility and involvement on the employees’ part. This choice, however, involves certain problems and risks. The increased level of autonomy, combined with the potential power and flexibility of the personal computer, present the organization with new problems of coordination and control.

In the Management Information Systems (MIS) field, these problems and risks are generally discussed within the framework of End User Computing (EUC). There is a growing concern that EUC activities are poorly managed and getting “out of control” [1,2,16]. It is recognized that while organizations have maintained procedural, physical, and technological controls over their large databases and “production” systems, for the most part these control structures are nonexistent in EUC. As a result, decentralized PC users create, update, and eliminate applications and data files without observing any well-defined and standard procedures for quality assurance, creation of audit trails, etc.

Some of the specific risks concern the safety and security of data from accidental loss or misappropriation, disruption of organizational activities due to non-documented applications, unauthorized use of time and equipment for personal purposes, and pilferage of hardware and software. The MIS literature contains numerous recommendations regarding backup procedures, physical storage procedures and documentation to counter these risks [6]. Such procedures are currently being integrated into training programs and written into organizational policies.

The problem remains, however, of how to ensure compliance with these policies and procedures in a “personalized” work situation. Technically, it is possible to control or monitor employees’ use of their personal computers by connecting them to a communication network, implanting monitoring software in the PCs, using video cameras, or even through biometric identification techniques which sense individual’s fingerprints, typing rhythms, hand geometry, etc. [15]. Most of these methods, however, reinforce the traditional patterns of organizational control. Their extensive use would erode individual rights of privacy and the potential enhancement of the individual’s sense of liberty and responsibility associated with the introduction of PC’s.

Furthermore, “remote supervision” of output is generally not applicable in this situation [18]. The quantity and quality of individual or group output would normally not indicate whether the individual has violated security, safety, backup, or documentation requirements. Such violations are likely to be discovered only by accident or through crises, such as loss of important data, applications which have no documentation, or when an individual leaves the organization. Programmers, planners, data analysts, and managers have a high “time span of responsibility” [12] in these matters, and output control practices cannot solve the problem without interfering with their autonomy.

## 3. Model Development

The organizational behavior literature does not offer clear models for the analysis of this problem. In the literature, the subject of control tends to be discussed from the standpoint of motivation theories $[13,14]$ . Such a perspective is not very useful here, because these PC security activities do not have clear outcomes that can be measured on a regular basis. Therefore it is difficult to attach organizational rewards or sanctions to their performance. PC security-related behavior activities are preventive in their nature, rather than producing any regularly rewardable positive results. For the same reason, it is difficult to see how the performance of such activities can have continuing value for the individual: we are dealing here with such phenomena as fastening seatbelts in motor vehicles, and these are difficult to address with conventional motivational models. Users at risk (of automobiles or PC's) tend to develop a self confidence which rationalizes away cautionary actions. Also infrequent users are unlikely to have enough personal experience with disasters to motivate risk-avoidance.

Broad organizational formulations of control are also difficult to apply in this situation. The simple cybernetic model requires constant monitoring of performance, which (as discussed above) has limitations in this case. For the same reasons, two of the three control strategies identified by Ouchi [19] – market and bureaucratic control – are not applicable. The third, “clan” control (control through values, traditions, and shared beliefs) may be theoretically more relevant in the case of high employee autonomy. However the mere recency of the phenomenon under discussion makes the possibility of the existence of shared traditions and values in this case not very likely.

For these reasons the study could not rely on a consolidated theoretical model. Consequently, we decided to study some of the factors related to the security behavior of PC users in organizations. The study was nevertheless guided by some hypotheses found in the Organizational Behavior literature; we followed the general guideline that performance is a function of three classes of variables: motivation, ability and role clarity [20].

## 4. Hypotheses

Figure 1 presents a summary of the hypothesized relationships that guided this investigation, though motivation, ability and role clarity have not been assessed separately in this study.

We expected the prime factors in this figure to affect the degree to which users would exhibit security-related behavior in their use of PC's in organizations. Each is now briefly discussed.

## 4.1. Perceived Personal Responsibility

We hypothesized this to be a critical variable in motivating behavior under circumstances of high autonomy. According to one model [9], perceived personal responsibility for the results of the work is a critical psychological state in determining the employee's level of intrinsic motivation. We measure this variable by assessing internal vs. external attribution of "blame" for the results of misuse of the PC.

## 4.2. Impact of Loss of PC Files

It seems reasonable that a PC user's security-related behavior would increase when PC files are considered to be important; i.e., their loss or mishandling would cause considerable damage to the user or others in the organization.

## 4.3. Informal Norms

This variable represents the “clan” control effect. Under conditions of high autonomy and ambiguity which often accompany the introduction of PC’s, it is expected that a group of PC users will establish their own informal norms regarding the proper use of PC’s. Such informal, unwritten social norms should reflect not only the standard behavior, but also the normative (expected) behavior of the group. To the extent that these support security-related behavior, individual behavior should be affected in two ways: first by increasing role clarity for the individual, and second by increasing the individual’s motivation to comply with the expectations or demands of the organization.

The effects of perceived norms on motivations are well established in the social-psychological literature [8]. Further, since formal policies and informal norms can be seen as alternative forms of behavior controls, an interaction effect was hypothesized, with norms becoming more influential when there are few formal policies, and policies being more influential in the absence of informal norms.

## 4.4. Formal Departmental PC Policies

The existence of clear organizational policies regarding such matters as regular backup, offsite storage of diskettes, documentation, and copying of software is expected to contribute to good security-related behavior on the part of PC users, by drawing attention to the importance of such activities and by clarifying the expectations of the organization. Other things being equal, role clarity should increase performance in general. The relevance of role clarity to end-user behavior has recently been noted by other researchers $[11]$ .

## 4.5. PC Users' Experience and Level of Knowledge

The previous variables represent motivation and role clarity, but users' skills and experience should also be important. For example, PC security-related behavior often requires (in the absence of specialized software) a working knowledge of the appropriate MS-DOS commands. It was therefore hypothesized that a PC user's experience and their level of PC knowledge would be related to security-related behavior. It was further hypothesized that PC knowledge would be particularly important in the absence of formal policies or informal norms.

## 4.6. Summary of Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1: Security related behavior of PC users will be positively correlated with:

(a) Perceived personal responsibility.

(b) Impact of loss of PC files.

(c) Strength of informal norms.

(d) Existence of formal policies.

(e) User's PC experience.

(f) User's level of PC knowledge.

Hypothesis 2: There will be an interaction among user's PC knowledge, informal norms and formal policies in determining security-related behavior.

(a) PC Knowledge will be particularly important and therefore positively correlated with security related behavior in the absence of formal policies or of strong informal norms.

(b) Informal norms will be positively correlated with security related behavior, in the absence of formal policies or of a high level of PC knowledge.

## 5. Research Method

In the study we gathered data by personal interview rather than by a mail survey. Although considerably more expensive in terms of time and resources, interviews have several important advantages over mail questionnaires. In particular we needed to observe some sensitive behavioral variables which a trained interviewer could elicit more effectively and accurately. Also, virtually 100% of the selected targets were eventually included in the final data, thus eliminating the concern about self-selection response bias (for example: are respondents more PC literate and motivated than non-respondents?).

The sample of PC end-users was selected with the help of part-time MBA students enrolled in an introductory MIS class. The students were each asked to identify four PC users in their organization with experience in managerial rather than clerical-type PC applications. Hence the study was confined primarily to users of PC spreadsheets and databases; and excluded those doing primarily data-entry and word-processing. It was felt that this sampling method was appropriate in this research, but one should be careful in generalizing the results to significantly different populations of PC users.

Seventy PC users were subsequently identified and interviewed. Respondents were mainly drawn from New England hospitals (20%), banks and insurance companies (33%), and manufacturing organizations (40%). All participating organizations had over 1000 employees, including many users of managerial PC applications. The interviewers were briefed and drilled in the interview instrument, with several hours of classroom discussion and workshop exercises devoted to their preparation. Each interview task lasted about 35 minutes.

## 6. Measures

## 6.1. Perceived Personal Responsibility

Interviewees were presented with descriptions of five hypothetical incidents (presented in Table 1) in which some harm to the organization was caused as a result of mishandling a PC or not taking precautionary measures.

The respondent was asked to divide the share of responsibility in each case among the PC user, other members of his/her department, and the MIS department. The percentages of responsibility attributed to the PC user were averaged across the five incidents as an index of perceived PC responsibility (RES) for each respondent.

## 6.2. Impact of Loss of PC Files

Respondents were asked to identify which of their PC files would cause the most disruption if

## 6.4. Formal Departmental PC Policies

For each of these eight possible controls (Table 2), the respondent was asked whether his/her department had a formal written policy in place. The total number of items (0 to 8) for which the department had formal policies was the index for this variable (POL). Its low mean (Table 3) is an indication, at least in the organizations studied, of the apparent lack of formal policies for these particular controls.

## 6.5. PC User's Experience and Level of Knowledge

PC experience and knowledge were expected to influence PC security-related behavior through their effect on a user's PC ability to implement controls. PC experience was measured in terms of months of use (EXP).

To measure PC knowledge (KNO), we asked six questions about MS-DOS commands, file management techniques, and PC terminology. In computing an index, we distinguished between respondents who answered incorrectly and those who answered don't know. For example, respondents that answered questions on FORMAT and COPY commands incorrectly were considered a far greater security risk to their organization than those who recognized their lack of knowledge and refused to guess.

## 6.6. Reliability of Predictive Indices

Reliability of each of the above six indices, which hypothetically predict security-related behavior, was validated by assessing the degree of error in the measurement using the Cronbach alpha test applied to inter-item scores. This method has been used in previous studies of end-user computing [17]. The results presented in Table 3 indicate that the reliability of the indices is well within the limits of acceptability ( $\alpha > 0.6$ ).

## 6.7. Security-Related PC Behavior

Assessment of the dependent variable presented the greatest challenge. Ideally we would have liked to observe objectively the subjects over time, to assess their implementation of the controls shown in Table 2.

Objective external observations of performance are generally difficult to obtain, but in this case it was even more difficult. PC use could not be externally monitored without disrupting the user's level of autonomy and violating the premises of our investigation. Even superiors' evaluations, which are commonly used in organizational research as proxies for performance evaluation, could not be used here due to the personal nature of the behavior in question plus the long time span of responsibility. Therefore, we had no alternative but to rely on the subjects' own assessment of their behavior.

However, in order to minimize the effects of memory and social desirability biases, we attempted to address the relevant behaviors with questions that were as objective as possible. For instance, in the case of backing up disk storage, respondents were first asked to identify their most critical files. They were then asked: 'When was the last time each of these files was backed up, and how many times has each of the files been backed up during the last week?' Following this, respondents were asked which programs or menus were used for performing the backup task and where backups were stored. Responses were averaged over six such items measuring each users' backup, documentation, floppy-disk storage and hard disk access controls to form an index (BEH) of security-related behavior. The index reliability was validated using the Cronbach alpha test. The results ( $\alpha = 0.66$ ) indicate that the reliability of the index is within the limits of acceptability.

## 8. Discussion and Implications

Our data support the hypotheses regarding the effect of PC knowledge and informal group norms on security-related PC behavior. Three variables emerged from the analysis of interactions as particularly relevant to the studied phenomenon: formal policies, social norms, and personal knowledge. These variables represent three forms of organizational control – bureaucratic control, clan control and professional socialization – whose importance goes far beyond the particular issue studied here [5].

The single factor which emerged as the most significant predictor of the desired behavior was the informal group norms. Obviously corporate management cannot directly legislate the creation of informal group norms. However, skillful leadership and well-planned incentives can encourage an environment which may nurture the growth of useful group norms. For example, it might be important to hold educational and informational activities among groups of users to discuss the importance of security-related behavior and to participate in the formulation of relevant policies. Participation in such discussions could increase the likelihood of developing desirable informal group norms. The recognition and rewarding of individuals who demonstrate security conscious PC behavior might further the groups' identification and acceptance of appropriate norms. Our evidence suggests that such leadership actions might be more constructive than the development and issuance of formal policies.

Additionally, several motivational factors were not significantly related to the studied behavior. Failure of the two motivational factors to influence preventive behavior is surprising. One possible interpretation of these finding is that both perceived impact of data loss or damage and perceived responsibility may be strongly influenced by prior occurrence of such events, and therefore may have been relevant only to a part of the sample. Thus, it may have been difficult for respondents to appreciate these consequences prior to having experienced a security problem.

The desirable behavior regarding PC security which the study seeks to predict is uniquely difficult to measure and reward. Nonetheless such behavior will become more critical as PC applications become more pervasive and important throughout our professional tasks. While the creation of formal policies regarding PC security controls is understandably much advocated, this study does not reveal a significant correlation between the existence of such policies and the desired behavior.

Future research could explore the impact of other factors on security-related behavior. For example, it is likely that the users' attitude toward the organization will affect security-related behavior, since the risks involved in not taking preventive measures tend to be organizational rather than personal. A person who is highly committed to the work organization may be more likely to adopt preventive measures than a person who is alienated from the organization. Personality variables, such as rigidity and locus of control [21], may also impact this behavior.

The results of our study perhaps should be tested and extended with larger and more varied samples of participants, and with different research methods. If these results are confirmed, the practical implications are obvious. Specifically, the formalization of policies and procedures for security-related behavior of PC users is unlikely to be effective unless supported by educational and other actions designed to increase users' PC knowledge and their understanding of the necessity for such procedures.

Recent trends in the field of end-user computing and end-user development will no doubt increase awareness of security issues. Such trends may lead to more explicit and extensive policies and training regarding these issues. Policies and training in turn are likely to affect informal norms, PC user knowledge, and perceived personal responsibility. We intend to follow up these dynamics with a longitudinal study in these same organizations. Such a study will hopefully enable us to extend our current conclusions into more rigorous statements about causal relationships.
