# Success factors for strategic information systems \*

Helmut Krcmar

Hohenheim University, Stuttgart, Germany

Henry C. Lucas, Jr.

Leonard N. Stern School of Business, New York University, New York, USA

The use of information technology to create a strategic advantage is a major priority of many organizations and their information services departments. Because strategic applications often involve external organizations and because they have a potentially high impact, it is important that they be successful. This paper describes key factors in the development of strategic applications and illustrates them with a case study. To successfully manage the development of strategic applications, an organization needs to understand the different requirements of these systems.

Keywords: Strategic Information Systems, Success Factors, Implementation, Cash Management System

## Introduction

A 1982 paper proposed a classification of information systems into three different categories: those which support the business, applications which support strategic planning, and systems which are a part of a firm's strategy (Lucas and Turner, 1982). This third type of system has received a great deal of attention in the past five years. Books and papers by Parsons (1983), Ives and Learmonth (1984), McFarlan (1984), Wiseman (1985), Krcmar (1986), Wiseman (1988), Benjamin and Scott Morton (1988) and Hohe and Schumann (1988) have all discussed various aspects of the strategic use of information technology. Though the topic has been discussed extensively, few in-depth studies on the development of these systems exist.

Henry C. Lucas, Jr. is currently Research Professor of Information Systems at the Leonard N. Stern School of Business, New York University. He received his B.S. from Yale University and his M.S. and Ph.D. from the Sloan School of Management, Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Professor Lucas has also served on the faculty of the Graduate School of Business at Stanford University. His research interests include implementation, systems analysis, expert systems and the management of information processing. He has published articles in computer and management journals including Management Science, Decision Science, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, JMIS, and The Computer Journal.

Professor Lucas is the author of the books Managing Information Services, Macmillan, 1989, Introduction to Computers and Information Systems, Macmillan, 1986, The Analysis, Design, and Implementation of Information Systems (3rd ed.), McGraw-Hill, 1985, Information Systems Concepts for Management (3rd ed.), McGraw Hill, 1986, and Coping with Computers: A Manager's Guide to Controlling Information Processing, The Free Press, 1982. He also has written four monographs, Toward Creative Systems Design, 1974, Why Information Systems Fail, 1975 and Implementation: The Key to Successful Information Systems, 1981, all published by Columbia University Press, and The Implementation of Computer-Based Models, National Association of Accountants, 1976.

A Strategic Information System (SIS) is generally considered to be an application which allows the developer to gain an advantage over the competition, or at least to keep up with competitors. The classic examples of these systems, the Merrill Lynch Cash Management Account, American Hospital Supply's ASAP system and the airline reservations systems, generally produced increased market share and revenues for their developers. Some of these systems have also created significant barriers to entry for competing firms.

In a 1984 paper, McFarlan suggested five possible impacts from using information technology strategically:

1. The creation of barriers to entry

2. The creation of high switching costs

3. A change in the basis of competition

4. A change in the balance of supplier relationships

5. The creation of new products.

One would not expect a particular strategic application to have an influence on all of these categories, but it should impact some of them.

Parsons (1983) offers an expanded list of potential strategic application impacts; he groups the impacts into three levels: the industry, the firm, and the company's strategy.

Ives and Learmonth (1984) propose a customer resource life cycle model to determine what opportunities exist for strategic applications and what applications should be developed. Ives and Vitale (1987) apply a life cycle approach to the after sale phase to identify strategic application opportunities.

Benjamin and Scott Morton (1988) focus on the integration chain to demonstrate the impact of strategic applications.

Benjamin et al. (1984) present a strategic opportunities framework to help identify ways in which the technology can be used strategically. Their framework is a matrix, with one axis continuing the opportunities, from competitive marketplace or internal operations, and the other axis the scope of process change, which can be a significant structural change or the improvement of traditional products and services.

A large number of strategic applications have been cited in the literature (see Wiseman 1988, Hohe and Schumann 1988). From a functional point of view strategic systems can be grouped (Hohe and Schumann, 1988) into the following categories:

ordering systems with customers or suppliers,

value-added services (in the acquisition or after sales phases or to intensify relations),

new products or services, information technology as a distribution channel, electronic markets.

From a developmental point of view these systems appear to fall into two broad categories (Krcmar, 1986).

The first is evolutionary, in which modifications to an existing application enable it to be used strategically. Examples of evolutionary implementation include American Hospital Supply's order entry system and various airline reservations systems. A different type of problem exists when a strategic application is totally new and innovative. Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account is frequently cited as an example of the use of technology to create a new product or service.

Based on a case in this paper, the authors propose a third type of strategic application: a system to interface and integrate existing applications. These may be an evolutionary extension to an existing system and, at the same time, a new product in its own right.

## Developing Strategic Information Systems

The purpose of this paper is to discuss some of the success factors in developing a strategic information system and to illustrate them with a case study of an innovative treasury workstation. The following factors are likely to be key in developing strategic applications.

1. Recognizing and seizing an opportunity. Based on the evidence to date and the case presented below, the origin of a strategic system may follow two different paths (see Figure 1). First top management should engage in top-down scanning. Managers scan the environment looking for competitive threats and opportunities; such a scan may suggest a new strategy for the firm, a strategy which relies on technology and its use.

Another route to the strategic use of technology is bottom-up inventing. An individual who is not part of senior management develops a system to meet a perceived need. The focus here may not be strategic or competitive until someone else points out the potential of the invention. Many firms have found strategic products through a senior manager searching the organization to find a bottom-up invention.

Fig. 1. The origin of Strategic Applications.

A reconsideration of four well-known SIS-applications, e.g. Baxter's ASAP, McKesson's Economost, America Airline's SABRE and the French Videotex reveals the large extent to which bottom-up inventing and top-down scanning have influenced the development of these SIS (Ciborra, 1990).

2. Dealing with a lack of cost justification. It is difficult to cost justify strategic applications because their success is often dependent on the behavior of individuals external to the organization developing the system. To the extent that the SIS is embedded in a firm's strategy, there may be little incentive to cost justify the system. Such a lack of cost justification means that an individual with the idea for a system will have to find a sponsor in senior management to provide resources and protection.

Why are strategic applications hard to cost justify? First, many strategic applications depend on external agents for their success. For example, Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account succeeded only when customers decided to purchase the services it provided. American and United Airlines gained a significant advantage when they were able to convince travel agents to install reservations terminals connected to the carriers' computers.

Strategic applications become a product which is “sold” to users; forecasting the market for this product is difficult and makes cost justifications a significant challenge. Managers reviewing proposals for strategic and competitive applications are unlikely to see the same certainty in payoff and benefits that come with a more traditional system.

Second, a strategic system typically does not focus on reducing the cost of doing business. Rather it generates new business or makes it possible to do business a different way. Thus there are no cost comparisons available. Furthermore, these applications are an integral part of the firm's strategy and cannot be independently justified on a cost or benefit basis. SIS will have to be justified as a necessary component of strategy; the system is then justified to the same extent as the strategy in which it is embedded.

3. The need for a systems's sponsor. Since a strategic application is more likely to be undertaken on its contribution to the value of the firm's overall strategy rather than on its individual ROI, the originator of a system who is not a senior manager will have to find a sponsor in the organization. The sponsor must be at a high enough level to provide resources for developing the strategic application and to protect the project from cutbacks or cancellation.

4. Marketing an application. Since strategic applications often involve external agents, it may be necessary to market the application. It took over a year for the Cash Management Account to become popular. The system described in our case study had to be marketed to clients of the bank; other vendors offer competitive systems. The strategic application may also have to be marketed inside the organization developing it to gain support and enthusiasm for it.

5. Building on an infrastructure. Many strategic applications cited in the literature represent the evolution of an existing transactions processing system; other strategic systems rely on existing systems to make them work. The airlines connected travel agents directly to an existing transactions processing reservations system. In our case, the innovative, competitive system was only possible because the bank developing it had an extremely good transactions processing system in place.

6. Taking a customer-oriented point of view. The most frequently cited strategic systems involve customers as the external agents: For these applications to succeed, developers have to take into account the views of the customer. In our case study, one crucial difference between the bank's system and those offered by competitors was the bank's consideration of customer needs.

The six factors described above for improving the success rate for SIS are supported by Hohe and Schumann's findings of reasons for project failure. After studying more than 150 American and European SIS they identified the following four factors leading to failure: (1) Reaction of the competition, (2) low customer acceptance, (3) regulatory and legal influences and (4) internal reluctance to the SIS itself. The success factors are consistent with these findings. Our success factor "seizing and recognizing" addresses the competitive and acceptance reasons for failure. The failure reason "internal reluctance" is countered by observing success factors “justification”, “sponsorship” and “marketing”, Whereas missing acceptance is addressed by our success factors “recognizing and seizing”, “marketing” and “customer orientation”. Infrastructure as a success requirement stands without a connection. Legal requirements can be seen as a seizing activity.

## A Case Study

## The Application

The development of a strategic system by a major U.S. money-center bank will help to illustrate the characteristics of a strategic, competitive application. The authors collected information about this system in a series of interviews with the developers of the system and with bank officials, including cash management and client relationship officers. The research also included demonstration of the system and visits to customers involved in its development.

The system is designed for the treasury function in a large corporation. Typically the treasurer is responsible for cash management in the firm; he or she may control multiple accounts in five to fifteen or more different banks. In the morning the treasury staff must determine the firm's cash position with all of its banks and accounts. By 1 pm the treasurer should make and execute decisions about whether to transfer funds among accounts, invest excess funds, and/or borrow cash.

Before computers, personnel in the cash management area had to contact banks by phone and collect information on balances. In the 1970s, a number of banks developed on-line or time-sharing applications; the bank computer posted a customer's balances in each account to the separate files of the bank's on-line system. A customer using a terminal could access these files without having to phone an individual at the bank. The customer could obtain information more quickly and reliably and the bank earned a fee from the use of its computer systems.

With either a phone call or computer access, the cash manager had to work with a series of hand-written numbers on various forms representing balances in different accounts. There tended to be errors and mistakes, aggravated by the time pressures involved. For a financial deci-

## The Cash Microstation

This bank which is a major money-center bank, e.g. one that serves as a funds concentrator, has developed a microcomputer system to assist the treasurer in making cash management decisions. The microcomputer contains the login requirements and reporting formats for 80 to 100 different banks. Early in the morning, it automatically dials banks with whom a company does business and retrieves the firm's balances, placing them in a spreadsheet for analysis. The treasurer can then use the investment/debt module to decide on investments or borrowings. He or she can also connect to the money-center bank and make various wire transfers among accounts. ## Development

Prior to the development of the micro system, the bank's time-sharing system offered cash balance information and wire transfers for customers. The service tended to be expensive, and of course, did not have information on the customer's relationship with other banks.

Several clients suggested to cash management personnel that a microcomputer could help them in cash management; they encouraged the bank to look into the development of such a system. A task force from cash management and the time-sharing group began to investigate the prospects for a microcomputer system.

The task force quickly became concerned over the possibility of other banks developing a similar system which could have an adverse impact on their own competitive position. At first, then, the bank's strategy can be characterized as defensive, to prevent migration: the loss of customers to someone else's system.

A development group began to work with the time-sharing group and the Cash Management department of the bank to develop the microstation. The programming and detailed design were the responsibility of the time-sharing group. The design team included the originator and several customers who were cooperating with the specification of requirements. It should be noted that the bank had a successful set of applications for processing transactions; these were a necessary basis of the new interface.

The original plan was for the microcomputer to interface with existing time-sharing services and products. The first application was to connect to other banks and retrieve daily balance information for an electronic spreadsheet. One of the design team firms served as a pilot test site. Soon, the system was installed and made available as a product.

The major problem with this first system was high fees to customers due to the connection with the time-sharing operation. The time-sharing developers began to exhibit different priorities from these of the development group: they wanted to continue the interface through their time-sharing computers.

About two years after development work began, the originator was seeking an investment/debt package to round out the system. He located a suitable package and was impressed with the developing software house. The software house was also offering a microstation for cash management, primarily to be adopted by banks and sold to their customers.

Given the frustration of the originator with the design goals of the time-sharing group, the originator and bank management made a critical decision to adopt and modify the software house's systems and to gradually drop the time-sharing connection. In Wiseman's terms, the cash management development group formed a strategic alliance with the software house.

In this process, the Executive Vice President in charge of Cash Management (and other functions) agreed to a major commitment of funding and staff positions to market the microstation. At this point, the microstation completed the transition from a defensive system to an offensive one.

## Analysis

The microstation is well received by customers and appears to be equal to or better than competitors' systems. It is a significant, new interface to the bank's existing, high quality transactions processing systems. A Cash Management marketing representative described the system as “a window on the client, a chance to see what the client is doing and to try and provide additional bank services…” The system ties the customer more closely to the bank since it is easiest to use the bank for making money transfers and to handle debts and investments. A bank account representative said, “competition in the financial industry is not just based on cost, but also on service”. The microstation is a major advantage for the bank in providing a service.

It is very difficult to cost justify strategic applications in advance; sometimes one can evaluate them after implementation. For the bank's Fortune 100 customers, the following is an estimate of business increase during the year after implementation as drawn from bank customer records:

Overall increase 11.0%
Firms with no cash micros 9.8%
Firms with bank's micro 13.6%
With other vendor's micro 19.9%
With other bank's micro 3.9%

It appears that the use of any microstation except one offered by another bank is associated with an increase in business with the bank in this study. The bank has little control over other vendor's systems (other vendors are non-bank firms offering neutral workstations). The major competitive problem, then, comes from microstations offered by and tied to other banks.

This case study also illustrates how difficult it can be to sustain a competitive advantage. The bank's system has been a success, but it faces competition from other banks and independent vendors. Bank officials feel that they would be at a significant disadvantage without the cash microstation.

These numbers support the investment in the microstation, but one cannot conclude from them that the system is necessarily justified on a cost basis. (The microstation group more than breaks even without overhead allocations). Instead, one must turn to the reactions of bank relationship officers who feel that the microstation helps them provide services that will keep clients more closely tied to the bank.

It is interesting to observe that the microstation began as a bottom-up invention. As it became successful, it obtained support and funding from senior management of the bank. The developers saw the system as strategic, but it was not until the microstation had proven itself and competitors were offering similar products that senior management began to appreciate its potential.

Fortunately, the development of the microstation coincided with a strategic change for the bank as a whole, a consistent shift toward becoming an electronic bank as evidenced by a series of advertisements in the financial press. To some extend the shift is characteristic of the entire banking industry. The microstation was available and visible to senior management when they realized that the bank should become electronic. The shift in bank strategy also helped the microstation development to change from bottom-up inventing to top-down development.

## Observations

What are the keys to success in the implementation of a strategic system? The first key is to recognize an opportunity or a threat. The suggestion for the microstation came from a customer; further study of the potential for the system revealed a threat to the bank from its competition.

Many strategic applications will involve customers and other actors external to the organization. Taking the customer's view is another key to success. A beta test site manager stated that "other workstation vendors wanted to look at cash management from the bank's point of view; the microstation is built around our view, the view of the customer".

Strategic information systems pose more risk factors than traditional systems. Several competitors made poor business and/or technical decisions that seriously hampered their workstation efforts. For example, one vendor chose an “off-brand” micro; the product did not achieve customer acceptance. Another major bank bought an outside system, but did not invest heavily in modifications or making the system a strategic product of the bank. A third bank bought a system from an outside vendor who went bankrupt; the bank had not developed the ability to maintain and modify the source code for the application while the vendor was still in business.

It also appears that all banks underestimated the ongoing service component of cash workstations. The banks tended to look at these systems as a manufactured product which could be sold to the customer. This kind of thinking led to a profit center approach to the cash workstation units in the banks. Extensive requirements for consulting, training and service caused a profit center approach to workstations to fail, and the banks had to find other ways to justify their investments in this technology.

During the implementation process, the banks' perceptions of competition changed. At first, banks were competing with workstations. However, they began to recognize that the workstation was a part of a much larger application, the banks' traditional transactions processing systems running on large mainframe computers. A bank without good transactions processing systems was at a tremendous disadvantage in trying to offer a cash management workstation. The microstation is a vital part of the bank's total offering of services encompassing existing transactions systems and the microcomputer's "front end".

In this case, the strategic application is both a new product and an extension of existing services. The micro is used to interface and integrate information from other banks' systems and to provide a window to the bank's own transaction processing systems. A comparison to the acceptance of electronic cash management systems offered by German banks indicates that the integration of several banks' information is considered of paramount importance by corporate treasurers.

It is also clear that the development of strategic systems will have to be well managed; broad technical and managerial competence are a prerequisite for success. During the development of the microstation, bank management made a number of key decisions which have helped the system succeed.

Traditional information systems are not usually marketed in any way nor do they face competition during installation. For strategic systems involving customers, one must market to the customer and hope that the system brings customers closer to the firm. The same approach applies to suppliers and other external actors affected by a strategic system.

Sponsorship and support are also very important. Strategic systems are likely to remain ideas if they have to be cost justified. The microstation cost “several” million dollars to develop; the bank will not see a direct return. The system is sold at a nominal price; the returns come from the additional business that the microstation is believed to generate. The system should draw customers closer to the bank and should increase fee-generating transactions from users of the microstation.

Another way to look at justification is that the system was probably necessary to avoid losing customers to other banks offering similar products. Even now it is difficult to attribute a specific amount of revenue to the microstation; it would have been virtually impossible to do so when the system was being planned.

It may be that SIS are justifiable to the extent that the strategy they are a part of is justified. However it will be difficult to show a direct monetary link from strategic technology to profitability in most cases. The microstation is a long-term, relationship building complement to other new financial products. The microstation becomes embedded in the customer's operational procedures and encourages frequent contact between the bank and the client.

Since the competition moves quickly and the development of a system like the cash microstation has to meet a schedule imposed by competitors, the feasibility and justification stages associated with traditional systems development may be replaced by a quick analysis and intuitive decision to proceed.

Conventional information services departments risk becoming isolated from strategic applications, at least those that innovate. The Information Services Department is held in low esteem in many organizations; it has a reputation of being unwilling or unable to innovate. These departments are too focused on traditional, cost-justified systems. If they want to develop strategic systems, they usually have no slack resources and little information of the strategic plans about the firm. To participate in the development of SIS, information services management will have to demonstrate the ability to understand and support strategy.

## Conclusions

The case study illustrates many of the characteristics of strategic applications described earlier. The originator of the system was not a senior manager; the system is a case of bottom-up inventing. When an executive vice president became convinced of the importance of the system, he became its sponsor and made resources available. The system was not cost justified in advance; cash management executives wanted to defend the bank against similar systems offered by competitors. The system clearly involved external agents, that is, existing and potential customers of the bank.

## Discussion

Firms that succeed in the future will systematically incorporate technology as a part of their strategy. Managers and information systems professionals need to be aware of the differences between strategic and traditional applications if these systems are to be developed successfully.

This paper and its case study are intended to emphasize these differences for decisionmakers.

It appears that successful strategic applications may come from top-down scanning or bottom-up inventing. A manager or sponsor at a high level greatly facilitates the development of these systems. One would expect a strategic application to impact external agents; managers are likely to find it difficult to cost justify a strategic system. The system may evolve and draw on the existing technical infrastructure of the firm, or it may be a new and novel application. It is likely that one will have to market the new system which means that it is good to adopt a customer view from the beginning of the systems design process.

For organizations to succeed in the future, managers must learn how to develop strategic applications to obtain the advantages that modern technology has to offer.
