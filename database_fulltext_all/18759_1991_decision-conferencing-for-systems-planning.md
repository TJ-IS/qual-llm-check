# Decision conferencing for systems planning

Sandor P. Schuman \*

Decision Techtronics Group, Rockefeller Institute of Government, Albany, NY 12203, USA

John Rohrbaugh \*

Rockefeller College of Public Affairs and Policy, University at Albany, State University of New York, Albany, NY 12222, USA

Although managerial support and involvement is critical to the success of information systems planning efforts, managers typically have limited time to devote to such efforts, do not understand their role, and do not make the necessary commitment. This paper describes the successful use of decision conferencing as a corporate tool to increase managerial involvement and support and to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of systems planning. In a two-day, computer-supported meeting, executives identify critical issues, anticipated decisions, and related information needs. Using a computer-based decision model, these needs are organized in an issue-based framework, evaluated according to their organizational value, and prioritized with respect to available system development resources. A case study involving the New York State Insurance Department illustrates the process.

Keywords: Systems Planning, Group Decision Support Systems, Decision Conferencing, Decision Making, Decision Modeling, Resource Allocation, Facilitation.

## Group Decision Support Systems and Decision Conferencing

During the past decade, much attention has been given to the development of Decision Support Systems (DSS) that are tailored to the unique needs of individual managers and executives. Now attention increasingly is being directed to the design of DSS technologies for use by groups of managers and executive teams $[8,9,16,17,19,20]$ . Although any application of information technology to support the work of groups may be considered a Group Decision Support System (GDSS), one of its common forms is the use of interactive computer-based sets of procedures to assist decision makers in using information collectively to explore possible implications and contingencies of alternative courses of action. Such GDSS aim to improve the process by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for better defining and structuring organizational problems, and systematically directing the pattern, timing, or content of discussion.

Of course, more dramatic interventions do not necessarily enhance the effectiveness of the group's decision-making process. DeSanctis and Gallupe suggested three distinct levels of group intervention with GDSS [10]. Level 1 GDSS are aimed specifically at removing common communication barriers by using such features as automated input of anonymous ideas and concerns, electronic voting, and large viewing screen; these components and others can be found in most “electronic board rooms" or "computer-supported conference rooms." Level 2 GDSS provide structure technologies for cognitive mapping (e.g., COPE) or system dynamics (e.g., STELLA) and preference technologies for decision modeling (e.g., HIVIEW) or judgment analysis (e.g., POLICY PC) with which a group of managers or an executive team can work collaboratively [18]. Level 3 GDSS are characterized by some form of computer-mediated communication that enforces a variety of possible rules for structuring the pattern, timing, or content of the electronically controlled interaction process of the group, for example, so-called "computerized conference" or "decision network" system designs.

Decision conferencing is perhaps the most widely applied type of Level 2 GDSS [1,5,13,24,27,28,29,30,33,34,35,36,37,38,39,40,46,47]. Decision conferencing employs a computer system, operated by a professional staff, to support the work of face-to-face groups of managers and executive teams on a wide variety of organizational problems. Unlike Level 3 GDSS, however, verbal and nonverbal communication in decision conferences is not restricted by electronic networking but, rather, takes a completely connected, “each to all” pattern enhanced by the presence of a group facilitator. Decision conferences are intensive working meetings used to solve a variety of problems such as resolving conflict among experts, negotiating multi-party agreements, and developing governmental policies. The widest application, however, has been in the area of organizational planning:

\- defining organizational goals and priorities;

\- making budget allocations;

\- establishing five-year strategic plans;

\- redesigning service delivery systems;
- clarifying staffing assignments and priorities;
- predicting long-term effects of financing options;

\- determining and allocating office space needs;

\- selecting sites for office locations;

\- evaluating alternative reorganization plans;

\- redesigning organizational structure; and

\- planning computer-based information systems.

## How Decision Conferences Work

Decision conferences are designed for groups that need to reach consensus about a complex, unstructured problem for which there is no “formula” or objective solution, a need increasingly common in the information society. As Cleveland has described the situation: [7]

Collegial not command structures become the more natural basis for organization.... The push for participation by all kinds of people, and the inherent leakiness of the information resource, combine to produce the modern executive's most puzzling dilemma: How do you get everybody in on the act and still get some action?

The decision conference provides an arena for bringing together people of diverse perspectives and for helping them develop a shared understanding of the problem and reach a consensus decision. The decision conference is computer-assisted, using structure and preference technologies that improve analysis, combined with group facilitation techniques that improve interpersonal communication and integrate support technologies in the group process. This approach systematically incorporates the role of human judgment in the decision-making process and enables groups to use information more consistently and coherently. As a strategy formation process decision conferences combine formal analytic thinking with the behavioral aspects of management [15]. As a method of managing interdependence, decision conferences use information technology to support teams [42]. A decision conference may be considered as having three phases.

Phase I: Understand the decision situation and develop a strategy. In preparatory meetings, the decision conference facilitator discusses the decision situation with the principal stakeholders, helping them to define, focus, and reframe the problem as necessary. On the basis of these preliminary discussions, the facilitator selects a structure or preference technology that seems appropriate to the decision situation. The facilitator also talks with the organization leadership about possible participants in the decision conference. Typically, the inclusion of key decision makers with diverse perspectives on the issues is advocated, especially of those who have a substantial stake in the decision outcome and will have essential responsibility for implementing the agreed solution. The facilitator also takes responsibility for ensuring that an appropriate conference facility is selected and all facility arrangements are adequate.

Phase II: Conduct the decision conference. The decision conference consolidates problem-solving activities in a focused, intensive session convened away from the regular workplace with its interruptions. In the decision conference, the facilitator, a decision analyst who is also skilled in group process techniques, engages the participants to make explicit their knowledge of the situation and build a framework for systematically structuring relevant aspects of the problem. While the facilitator works with the group in the front of the room, a second member of the staff, the “correspondent,” tracks the discussion on microcomputer, recording all important explanations and justifications distilled from the on-going discussion. As the facilitator elicits more precise assessments, a third staff member, the “analyst,” captures their estimates using the selected structure or preference technology.

With computer support, the group is able to explore and evaluate alternative solutions and their implications. This detailed level of analysis helps to create a common understanding of the problem, reduce inconsistencies in individual judgments, and isolate specific areas of disagreement for more efficient discussion. A successful decision conference allows the group to comprehend the decision situation in all of its complexity, test potential solutions under a variety of assumptions, resolve conflicts, and make a final decision. Before leaving the decision conference, the group develops an initial action plan for implementing their decisions; a fully annotated printout of the decision model is available by the end of the second day of the meeting.

Phase III: Follow Through for Effective Implementation. Within a few days conference participants receive a report that completely documents the discussion of issues, as well as the results. This “decision accounting system” is an important feature of GDSS, since it provides an “audit trail” of the reasoning and judgment of the group. Conference staff often will meet again with key decision makers over a period of weeks to refine certain aspects of the computer-based decision model or the steps of the final action plan.

Whereas computer technology comes into play in Phase II, the facilitator supports all three phases. The diagnostic and guidance role of the facilitator is critical for selecting and integrating techniques and technologies that support the emerging needs of the group.

## The Decision Techtronics Group

One of the most active centers of decision conferencing is the Decision Techtronics Group (DTG) at the Rockefeller Institute of Government, State University of New York. Since its inception in 1982, DTG has hosted over 80 decision conferences for private and public sector organizations located throughout the United States. A majority of these conferences have been conducted for departments of the State of New York. For this reason, DTG has been recognized for its innovative work in decision support for senior executive teams in government, as well as application of a diverse range of structure and preference technologies in the decision conferencing setting [14,22,26,45].

In brief, to address IS planning problems, the GDSS:

\- uses a two-day decision conference to determine the information needs and priorities of senior management;

\- engages senior management by focusing on key issue areas which they determine;

\- considers multiple levels of commitment for each issue area - not an all or nothing approach;

\- incorporates estimates of benefit and cost for each potential application allowing development of an investment portfolio informed by benefit/cost ratios; and

\- uses computer support to document the details of potential information resource investments and to calculate benefit/cost ratios and perform sensitivity analyses.

## Systems Planning at a State Insurance Department

## An Overview of the Situation

The New York State Insurance Department regulates the activities of over 900 insurance companies doing business in the state with combined assets of over \$700 billion. Regulation of the insurance industry is a state function, with substantial interstate collaboration accomplished through the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC). Regulatory activities include examination and licensing of insurance companies and surveillance and approval of insurance products, rates, and business practices to: (1) assure the financial solvency of individual insurance companies and their ability to fulfill contractual obligations and pay claims, assuring the overall stability of the industry, and (2) protect the consuming public against abuses including discriminatory insurance prices, excessive rates, unfair business practices, fraudulent or misleading products, and nonavailability of coverage.

Although the department's information resources were partially computerized, they were designed and used as independent databases with substantial replication of information, as well as significant gaps. Proposals for the centralization of separate databases had not been successfully funded. A 1983 management study had concluded that the department should:

Create a central information system to provide a more comprehensive database and automated surveillance mechanisms. The Department's existing information systems are discrete, decentralized and often incompatible. As a result, attempts to develop a central and unified information system have been severely impeded. Without a comprehensive and historically accurate data base, the department has experienced difficulty in aggregate industry oversight and trend analysis [32].

However, it was not until the liability crisis occurred in 1986 that support for new computer capacity and electronic data processing staff was forthcoming. In early 1986, the Governor created an Advisory Commission on Liability Insurance to develop a response to spiraling insurance costs and unavailability of liability insurance for broad classes of insureds, most notably public entities. The Commission concluded that the department's regulatory role needed to be strengthened and expanded. They recommended that the department be allocated additional resources, including

... human resources as well as sufficient computer programming staff and equipment capacity to receive and make full use of all required information concerning rates, reserves, claims, losses, and other data that will inform a regulatory process that monitors and regulates the insurance market on a thorough and timely basis [44].

Subsequently the department received approval to hire 15 additional electronic data processing staff and eventually was able to purchase a new computer. Although the overall direction and support for a centralized and enhanced information system was clear, the specific plan for the system was not.

Phase I: Develop a Strategy to “Integrate the Database”

Executives in the department talked about centralizing or integrating the database but did not necessarily agree about what had to be done. The task was viewed by some as a matter of deciding which information should be included in an integrated database versus which should be left in separate databases. When asked for their recommendations, one manager provided a chart that specified a data field structure, while another provided several pages of narrative describing information use in their unit. In short, the executives needed a common perception of the problem and a useful focus.

Reframe the problem. Underlying the “centralization” or “integration” question was the need for a comprehensive assessment of decision makers’ information needs and priorities. The problem thus was reframed from one of “how to integrate the database” to “what are the strategic issues and decisions facing policy and decision makers and what information will they need to deal with them.” This frame provides an issue-based focus for IS development that has been successful with senior executives [12]. It also responds to the need to integrate IS planning with the agency’s overall planning process which was identified as a key issue by 97% of the top managers in a survey of public sector MIS managers [6].

In this case, reframing the problem enabled executives to talk in terms that were meaningful to them, rather than in IS terminology they did not understand. Their task would be to identify their own information needs to reduce uncertainty and equivocality [21], while the computer services professionals would be concerned with the more technical questions of how to deliver that information through a centralized, integrated database.

Determine who should attend the conference. A total of 37 top-level executives and managers were scheduled to attend one of two, two-day decision conferences. Initially, a different group was scheduled to attend each conference with a mixture of people from various parts of the organization in each (a few individuals would attend both sessions to assure continuity). When the focal problem was clarified, however, it became clear that people needed to meet with others in their substantive area of expertise, not with a mix of people from throughout the organization. Participant lists were rearranged so that one conference focused on property and casualty insurance, while the other focused on life and health.

Select a decision model. A model provides a framework in which to detail the various aspects of a decision and specify their relationships. Depending on the decision situation, one or more structure or preference technologies might be selected. In this case, the resource allocation model was selected to help the groups identify and prioritize their information needs. Determining priorities is critical to information systems planning because resource constraints prevent decision makers from having all of their information needs met immediately – some information needs in this case would have to remain unmet for more than two years. The resource allocation model focuses on the incremental value of information, that is, the additional contribution to the reduction of uncertainty afforded by the acquisition of an additional piece of information.

Develop a process agenda. The act of determining how to decide assumes that there are well understood alternative approaches to making a particular choice. Which of a variety of procedures or methods of decision making is appropriate in a given instance? A process agenda specifies the steps the group will take. A broad variety of elicitation and group process strategies are available. In this case, the group was asked to:

\- identify and rank order issues that will be confronting the agency over the next two to five years;

identify the decisions that will have to be made regarding the high priority issues;

\- identify the information that would be helpful in making those decisions;

\- assess the benefits of having each piece of information available in the automated system;

\- estimate the costs of making each piece of information available, including the costs of information acquisition, database development, and application design; and

\- from examination of the benefits and costs, develop a two-year implementation plan that specifies which information should be incorporated in an integrated database.

Phase II: Conduct the Decision Conference to Identify Priority Information Needs

Focus on issues and decisions to identify information needs. In the initial stage of the decision conference, the facilitator reviewed the conference strategy focusing attention on the task of identifying issues, decisions, and information needs. This would result in the creation of a comprehensive inventory of information needs. Because of resource constraints, decision makers would have to specify their priorities so that they could systematically guide the incremental development of the information system.

Each group member was asked to make a list of the issues that would be confronting their organization in the next two to five years. After several minutes of individual work, each person had the opportunity, in round robin fashion, to introduce themselves and offer one of the issues on their list. While the facilitator recorded each item on whiteboards in front of the room, the analyst kept track of the list by entering it into a microcomputer. The facilitator also prohibited lengthy discussion or evaluation of the issues so as to maintain an atmosphere that encouraged creativity and participation and prevented anyone from dominating the group.

Over 70 issue areas were identified. Each participant was asked to select the five most important issues from the list and then to rank order them, with the highest priority issue receiving five points, and the lowest one point. These scores, summed across participants for each issue, were input into the microcomputer so that the group could immediately see their higher priority issues on a large projection screen. The top issues were:

availability problems;

\- property and casualty rates;

\- financial condition;

\- regulatory response;

\- holding companies;

\- consistency with laws, regulations, circular letters;

\- financial examinations;

\- adequacy of reserves; and

\- market conduct examinations.

These issues were grouped in two areas: those regarding the financial condition of an insurance company, and those regarding specific insurance products. Accordingly, the participants were divided into two work groups, each with its own facilitator and computer support. Each working group focused on one issue area at a time. They first listed the decisions that they would have to make regarding that issue and, second, specified the particular pieces of information that would be helpful in making those decisions. The purpose of this procedure was not only to provide a useful structure for eliciting information needs but to keep the work groups focused on information useful for decision making rather than information that might appear to be useful but lacked pertinence to decision making. This later enabled them to evaluate the benefit of having a particular piece of information, because the information was clearly connected to issues and decisions of importance to the agency.

Structure the variables and levels. The resource allocation model takes the form of a series of variables and the levels they contain, as illustrated in Figure 1. Each variable represents a category of potential resource investments that competes with others for the same scarce resource – for example, money, time, or space. Such categories are called variables, because the amount of the resource that could be invested in any one is not fixed and could be traded off against investments in others. Each of these variables is defined further by two or more specific “levels” of potential investment associated with ever increasing commitments of the resource.

In this case, each issue area formed a variable and the associated pieces of information formed levels. Within each variable, the levels were rank ordered by the group. By asking them to state which piece of information would be most valuable for organizational decision making as part of the computerized information system, the levels of information were prioritized with respect to their implicit benefit.

Evaluate the benefits. Each level was evaluated to describe the benefit the organization would receive by having that information available through a computer-based information system. In any one issue area, if resources were available to provide all of the information listed, 100% of the potential benefit would be attained. How much benefit would be attained if only the first piece of information were available? How much more benefit would be attained by adding the next piece of information? The group decided, for example, that having the information about availability problems (variable 3) down to level 4 (i.e., a benefit of 90) was twice as beneficial as information only down to level 2 (i.e., a benefit of 45).

Prioritizing the value of information listed for each variable. After the two work groups determined their information needs and benefits for each of their assigned variables, the conference participants were reconvened as one large group. After reviewing both groups' work, the participants were asked to prioritize their information needs by variable. In making this assessment they were asked to focus on the value of the information rather than the importance of the issue. This focus is important because, although a particular issue may be very important, the information that is available to support it may be weak or only tangential to the issue.

Consensus on the priority of the variables was established by:

\- asking participants to state their highest priority variable and tallying them on a whiteboard;

\- asking for supporting arguments that clarify why a variable is more important, and why others are less important;

\- re-voting to see if a clear consensus has emerged;

\- where consensus was still lacking, hearing additional arguments on the reduced field of contenders; and

\- in the few cases where consensus did not emerge, giving the organization leader the opportunity to make the judgment in the best interests of the organization, or recording two alternatives for later sensitivity analysis.

After prioritizing the variables, they were weighted in relation to each other to explicitly differentiate their value. The variable with the lowest priority arbitrarily was assigned the lowest relative weight of 10. Participants then indicated how much more value they associated with the information in the next more important variable. For example, if the next variable had twice as much value, it would receive a relative weight of 20. By comparing the information value of each variable to the next, relative weights were assigned to all; cross-checks were made to check for consistency.

Assess the costs of making each piece of information available. Relying largely on the computer services staff, in consultation with program staff, costs were estimated for each piece of information. Normally resource estimates would be fairly accurate for dollar, time, or space costs. In this case, because many assumptions had to be made, the participants used a relative scale where 100 points arbitrarily represented the amount of effort necessary to make the easiest pieces of information available; the scale had no upper limit. Considerations in estimating costs included: acquiring the information if it was not currently required by NAIC or the state; locating and sorting through the information if currently located in office files; data entry, if not available from electronic media; and application design and development.

Develop an implementation plan for meeting information needs. While the variables, levels, benefits, relative weights, and costs were developed by the group with the aid of the facilitator, the analyst was building these elements into the computer model. The resource allocation model assumes that an organization would want to get the most benefit from its use of limited resources, that is, investments ought to be made first in those levels that have the highest benefit relative to their cost. An investment progression can be developed by calculating the benefit/cost ratio for each level of information and sorting them in order of declining ratios. These are calculated as follows:

$$
\frac {R _ {i} B _ {i , j + 1} - R _ {i} B _ {i , j}}{C _ {i , j + 1}},
$$

where $R_{i}$ is the relative weight of variable i, $B_{i,j}$ is the benefit of level j in variable i, and $C_{i,j}$ is the cost of level j in variable i.

Figure 2 shows the computer output for a portion of the investment progression for property and casualty insurance. The investment progression lists each piece of information, defined by variable and level, along with the cumulative benefit (normalized to a scale of 1000) and the cumulative cost. The computer output illustrated in Figure 3, the efficient frontier, graphically portrays the accumulation of benefits and costs as one continues down the investment progression. For example, if the first seven investments shown in Figure 2 were made (i.e., up to the variable entitled “Solvency” and its level entitled “Quality of Reinsurers, LOCs, IRIS, etc.”) the organization would attain 474 benefit units (out of a total of 1000) at a cost of 2400 cost units (out of a total of 54,300). This dramatically illustrates the marginal utility of computerized information systems by providing nearly half of the available benefit for only about 5% of the cost.

In systems planning, the resource allocation model suggests the exact priorities in which components should be developed. Subsequent sensitivity analysis and re-examination of these priorities results in a refined investment progression. Part of the value of using the model to create the investment progression (rather than trying to do it entirely in one's head) is that it decomposes the problem into many smaller and more manageable parts. Each part can be revisited without disrupting the whole. In this case, a collection of 55 information needs identified during one decision conference was clearly ordered. As a well-ordered list of priorities, the investment progression can be used to direct systems development efforts as available resources increase (or diminish).

In this case, the exact order of investment was not so important as a clear indication of which components could be implemented in the first year. The IS staff were asked to indicate how far down the suggested list of investments they could expect to move. The entire group then was challenged to suggest any information need below the one-year cutoff that should instead be completed within the first year, and what information need above the one-year cutoff should be deferred in exchange. The participants examined the resulting investment progression and made adjustments to ensure that the resources available over the next year (and then the next two years) would provide the greatest amount of benefit to the organization.

Phase III: Follow Through for Effective Implementation

At the conclusion of the two conferences, each group had reached consensus on its priority information needs. At a follow-up meeting, expectations were clarified as to how much could realistically be accomplished in the next two years, and who would be responsible for implementing the tasks. Discussion with the computer services staff about one year after the decision conferences indicated that the resource allocation model proved useful. Interest was expressed in reconvening the groups to review progress and adjust priorities where necessary. Additional decision conferences have been conducted for the State Insurance Department focusing on specific strategic issues: to resolve a crisis in medical malpractice insurance and to prevent a crisis in insurance security funds.

## Discussion

Eight critical IS planning problems and needs have been identified in a recent survey of IS planners [23]. How were each of these problems approached in the decision conference for IS planning?

1. It is difficult to secure top management commitment. Management commitment lagged when the IS planning effort was framed in terms of a centralized, integrated database. Reframing the problem in terms of strategic issues and decisions inherently gained management interest and ensured that their participation effectively linked systems design to decisions of highest organizational priority. Focusing intensively on issues and decisions built support for the broader IS plan, because the payoffs became clear in ways that management understood. Securing commitment also is related to the length and expense of the planning process.

2. The methodology lacks sufficient computer support. Computer support is a major feature of the decision conference. Large screen projection of the computer display allows participants to view and modify ideas, evaluations, and relationships as they are entered. The decision modeling process allows complex issues and decisions and their related information needs to be elucidated in structured detail. Sensitivity analysis is readily performed to examine the implications of different assumptions about costs and benefits. Computer support also is closely connected with documentation.

3. The planning exercise takes a long time. It is very difficult to gain management commitment for a prolonged planning process, especially if the pay-off is not well understood at the outset. Asking for a limited two days of time allows management to commit to the planning process because it requires a clearly capped investment. The output produced by the decision conference technology in two days represents the bulk of the contribution required from management and provides a foundation that allows IS staff to complete the design.

4. It is difficult to obtain top management approval. Decision conferences enable a large number of stakeholders to participate meaningfully in the decision-making process, facilitating both a thorough exchange of viewpoints and a common understanding of the multiple characteristics of the problem. Management approval of the plan is the product of an astounding, two-day long, group cognitive effort that spans all of the available options and the varied perspectives that executives bring to bear on the problem. Simultaneous consideration of relevant features, made possible by the decision conference technology, contributes to the overall supportability and legitimacy of the decision. The computer support, documentation and decision model each contribute to management approval.

5. The documentation is inadequate. Computer output provides a detailed record of how critical priorities were made. While providing accountability for these decisions, this documentation also aids in communicating the results to others and offers a flexible framework for subsequent updates.

6. There are no priorities for developing databases. The resource allocation model clearly identifies priorities in a portfolio format. Projects are not considered as all-or-nothing options but are decomposed into do-able increments. Projects might be adopted in whole or in part. The priority of each project increment is apparent in the list of investments (the investment progression).

7. It is very expensive. Most of the expense associated with IS planning involves time spent by managers and IS personnel. Compressing and reducing the time it takes to identify information needs and priorities significantly reduces the expense.

8. No prioritization scheme is provided. The resource allocation model seems well-suited to the nature of the IS planning problem. The framework of issues and their associated information needs, evaluated by their costs and benefits, and sorted in order of their benefit/cost ratio provides a coherent framework and prioritization scheme. The model relies heavily on subjective estimates of both benefits and costs. Some observers might be bothered by the absence of fully reliable data or well-established facts to underpin the process. Of course, where such evidence is available, it plays an important role in aiding the judgment process. However, it is our experience that the decision making of senior executive teams rarely is bounded by the limits of precise information. At higher organizational levels, strategic decision making is jointly intuitive and analytical. For this reason, conference participants readily expressed their informed judgment about benefits and costs with considerable confidence in the reliability of their subjective estimates.

## Designing More Powerful GDSS

It might be contested that the results reported above were produced not so much through the support of the computer model but through the intervention of the group facilitator. Indeed, GDSS developers are recognizing the importance of facilitation to the successful use of GDSS [9]. Empirical results, however, suggest the value-added of computer support. One experiment has shown that, when compared to groups receiving neither facilitator nor computer support, facilitation (in the absence of computer support) provided larger gains than computer support (in the absence of facilitation). However, somewhat greater benefits were achieved when computer support and facilitation were combined [2]. A recent evaluation of multiple decision conferences found that most participants view the presence of a neutral group facilitator to be of considerable benefit. However, in the more successful decision conferences, participants also indicated that they derived considerable benefit from computer-dependent activities: building a computer-based decision model and reviewing computer output on the implications of alternative choices [25]. Clearly, computer support provides value-added beyond that of facilitation alone.

Nonetheless, the value-added of each component of GDSS – facility, hardware, software, procedures, and facilitation – should be examined to direct research and development efforts at the weaker components so as to create more powerful, integrated systems that support the full range of group decision-making processes. Such an examination should be mindful of the distinction between a technique and the technology on which it is implemented. A particular technique might be implemented using different information technologies. The brainstorming technique, for example, has been implemented with the whiteboard, flip chart, overhead projector, single microcomputer (as in most decision conferencing), and computer network. For many techniques, shifting to computer technology provides, at best, a marginal increase in value over that of “paper and pencil” technology. Greater value will be found with techniques that take advantage of the unique strengths of computer technology and that are otherwise impractical or unimaginable. For example, decision modeling techniques typically are impractical for use in meetings if implemented manually. Their application in group decision making did not occur until a minicomputer was brought into the meeting room [41]. The full potential of GDSS will be realized with the development of these intrinsically computer-based techniques.

The need for more sophisticated and diverse techniques that address the full range of group decision-making activities is supported by results of a recent survey including organizations that use some form of GDSS [3]. Beauclair and Straub concluded that users underutilize the voting and brainstorming features now commonplace in GDSS. Why? They suggested that this is due to either limitations in existing GDSS technology or a lack of understanding of the capabilities of these features.

Another explanation they offer is that perhaps these features are not as crucial as had been believed. Instead, they suggest that “... developers should look to applications that could serve the needs of decision makers faced with complex, multiple attribute, and unstructured problems” (p. 220). The added value of GDSS is greater in more trying situations where the cognitive and social aspects of decision making are more demanding, where unaided human cognitive processing is overloaded and convergence of diverse individual perspectives is especially difficult. Future GDSS development should integrate the work of decision conferencing GDSS developers, who have emphasized facilitator-assisted communication, decision modeling, and tasks involving convergent thinking, and that of network-based GDSS developers, who have emphasized computer-mediated communication, independent elicitation and evaluation, and tasks involving divergent thinking.

Although governmental agencies were noted as leading the group of organizations that planned to inaugurate some type of GDSS [3], there is relatively little research and development effort directed to meeting their needs (see for an example [43]). Additional attention should be given to the types of problems and decision-making processes that are encountered in the public sector. Indeed, the governmental problems tackled with decision conferencing GDSS have been remarkably varied. In Georgia a decision conference for 30 community leaders was convened to develop a \$100 million, 10-year strategic plan for their city. The New York State Commission on Domestic Violence created a plan that restructured their priorities and resulted in their permanent establishment as a state office. The Division of Economic and Social Services in Rhode Island responded to declining federal funds by redefining the roles of social workers in family and adult services. A group of Michigan leaders charged with planning for the use and protection of the state's natural resources developed an \$800 million, 5-year legislative agenda.

In the context of such diversified application of decision conferencing GDSS, the case described here must be considered as only one instance in the area of systems planning. More specialized

GDSS will likely be designed especially for strategic IS planning by senior executives. Much greater study is required to determine the most appropriate preference technology, decision model, and form of GDSS. A host of questions regarding appropriate GDSS design for particular types of problems, groups, and organizational environments will likely be the focus of research efforts for several years.
