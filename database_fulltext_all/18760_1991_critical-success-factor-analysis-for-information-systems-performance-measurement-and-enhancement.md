Case Study

# Critical success factor analysis for information systems performance measurement and enhancement

A case study in the university environment

Dennis P. Slevin

Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Paul A. Stieman

Computing & Information Services, University of Pittsburgh
Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Larry W. Boone

College of Business Administration, St. John's University, Jamaica, NY 11439, USA

The application of the Critical Success Factor (CSF) process as a performance measurement and enhancement device is described in this study. The stepwise implementation of the process over a one year period in a university information systems organization is described. The procedure, using the nominal group technique for eliciting and refining CSFs by area is described. Measurement issues are discussed, along with the establishment of performance standards. For CSFs that could not be measured by hard data, a detailed survey was developed. The survey model is described and the survey itself is presented. Case examples of actual behavior change (performance improvement) due to the use of CSFs are given. Finally, guidelines for the use of the CSF procedure in other Information Systems (IS) organizational contexts are discussed and prescriptive recommendations are presented.

Keywords: Critical Success Factor, University Information Systems, Information Systems, Nominal Group Techniques, Performance Measurement, Performance Improvement, Survey, Questionnaire.

Drucker coined the term “Management by Objectives” in 1957 to describe the process whereby managers would establish a few key objectives and follow them with detailed action plans. This process can be particularly useful in the complex world of Management Information Systems (MIS) management and has evolved into the more specifically prescribed “Critical Success Factor Analysis.” Zani [13] suggested that key success variables might identify the most important elements of the firm’s success and help specify priorities for information systems development. Rockart [8, p. 81] proposed the concept of “Critical Success Factors” as:

the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will ensure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where “things must go right” for the business to flourish. If the results in these areas are not adequate, the organization’s efforts for the period will be less than desired.

The Critical Success Factor (CSF) approach has been implemented in a variety of business IS settings and normalized models have been developed for the process [2.6].

Generic sets of CSFs for any organization's IS function, such as service, communication, human resources, and positioning/repositioning the IS function within the organization, have been proposed as reasonably robust across a variety of industries [9]. The process of developing CSFs has been shown to have a generally positive impact on organizations in terms of enhanced communication patterns and better understanding of goals and objectives and has been particularly useful as an input to MIS planning [5,11]. However, most of the literature focuses on the planning utility of CSFs and/or the integrative result of tying the IS function more directly into specific performance measures for the overall organization.

The CSF process has another significant utility that has been explored less completely; it can be useful as a performance measurement and enhancement device for the IS function. Rather than focusing on the planning and design benefits of the CSF analysis, this paper proposes an approach for using the CSF method to develop specific performance measures and to track performance in a complex information systems environment.

The experience of using the CSF analysis for such a purpose in a university information systems setting is described in detail. The process used to develop the factors is explained, and the factors themselves are listed and described. Examples of resulting performance changes are presented. Finally, an overall assessment of the strengths and weaknesses of this process for IS performance enhancement is reviewed.

## The University's IS Function

The computing environment in a modern research university is complex and challenging. The level of system differentiation is great [4]. Services must be provided ranging from conceptually simple word processing capabilities on PCs for thousands of undergraduates, to complex networking, mainframe and even supercomputing facilities for research faculty. Indeed, the IS function in a university is one of the most complex and differentiated to be found. Consequently, the integration needs are great: (1) facilities and labs must be accessible; (2) software must work and be controlled; (3) output must be made quickly available over a large geographic area; (4) networks must function effectively; (5) a wide range of consulting must be available, and so forth. The IS organization is constantly subjected to increasingly complex demands from administrators, research and teaching faculty, and graduate and undergraduate students. In this setting, a substantial effort concerning the development of CSFs for performance measurement and enhancement was launched. The university IS function that was studied can be profiled as follows:

Organization's Name: Computing and Information Systems (CIS)

Organization's Mission: See Appendix 1.

Operating Budget: \$9 million/yr. (approximately)
Capital Budget: \$3 million/yr. (approximately)
Users: Greater than 10,000 undergraduate students, graduate students, staff and faculty
Employees: 176 full time. More than 100 part time

Locations: The University's computing environment represents an investment of \$14 million in hardware and software. It has facilities in three locations - two on campus and one off campus - and a number of auxiliary sites both on and off campus. Most of the hardware is housed at the off campus facility and information is transferred between equipment located there and on campus facilities via a private microwave system. Ten public computing labs across campus provide student users with input and output devices and other resources such as user-oriented documentation and consultation. The Director of this function reports directly to the Executive Vice President of the University.

## The Critical Success Factor Process

The process used to develop CSFs for the university IS organization involved multiple steps and required substantial input from the key participants as well as two outside consultants. Three day-long group workshops were conducted, as well as a substantial number of “off line” meetings to clarify and review issues. The stepwise nature of the process is shown in the flow chart in Figure 2.

## Meet Director--review project mission

As in all consultancy relationships it was important to make sure that the external consultants understood the objectives of the Director of CIS in this project. He viewed the project as a means of institutionalizing the overall organization's customer service orientation: "How can we be more service-oriented and assure that we are responsive to the needs of our diverse set of clients?" From that perspective, the project had to assume more of a performance measurement, control, and enhancement orientation than an MIS planning orientation.

## Interview key managers

The consultants spent significant time interviewing 15–20 key individuals throughout the IS organization. The McKinsey 7 S Framework was used as a basis for discussion [7]. Managers were asked to discuss the strengths, weaknesses, and problem areas of the organization concerning the issues of (1) strategy, (2) structure, (3) systems, (4) shared values, (5) skills, (6) style, and (7) staff.

This step was useful in giving the external consultants a much better idea of the specific objectives and problem areas of each group, and how each group viewed itself in relation to the entire IS organization.

## Workshop one

The initial workshop brought together the Director and his five area managers to develop consensus on the CSF process. Participants were provided with a booklet of readings concerning approaches to improvement of IS quality and productivity. Some very useful material in the work of Smith and Morris [12] was provided, giving examples of the CSF process that had been used in university settings. The Nominal Group Technique was employed. After the CSF concept was discussed, participants were asked to generate a list of critical factors for the IS organization on $3 \times 5$ index cards. This was followed by a group discussion of what the items meant and how they might be sorted into various performance factors. The following list of 10 global factors, which represent the range of services provided by CIS, was generated at this stage of the process:

1. Network Reliability

2. Data Communications Network

3. I/O Services

4. Documentation

5. Problem Reports

6. Consulting

7. Hardware Repair

8. Proactive Planning

9. Facilities Management

10. Internal/Support

A rough prioritization followed, where each individual with the help of the assembled group attempted to indicate the relative contribution his/her own operational area made toward each departmental performance factor. Individuals then left Workshop One with the assignment of further specifying and clarifying how each factor related to his/her particular area of operation. That is: how did each operational area contribute to the accomplishment of each performance factor for the IS organization and how could the achievement of each factor be measured within the operational area?

A key aspect of this performance measurement and enhancement project was to have the managers of each operational area interact with their own personnel during each stage of the process. The need for two-way communication between management and area personnel was discussed at Workshop One. Each manager was encouraged by the Director and the consultants to take back the results of the workshop to their work places, share them with operational personnel, solicit their ideas, and revise the CSFs in each area, as the managers saw fit. Not only would additional insights be brought to the project from all hierarchical levels in this manner, but also the implementation of the new performance measurement system would be facilitated by maximizing communication with operational people during the development process. Managers' compliance with this suggestion and any related problems were checked during follow-up off-line meetings between the consultants and individual managers approximately one to two weeks after Workshop One.

## Workshop two

Much of the second full-day workshop, held five weeks after the first, was spent attempting to define the IS performance factors precisely. In a consensual fashion, each factor was briefly defined, then individual managers specified measures for each factor applicable to their own area's operations. In most cases an equation was specified. A typical factor might be as shown in Figure 6.

This involves a definition such as:

IS Performance Factor: System "C" Reliability

Z = Hours Down in Month

In addition, an attempt was started at this workshop to set specific standards, i.e., what performance level is deemed acceptable and, when not achieved, what changes should be instituted to improve performance?

As expected, setting specific performance standards proved to be a difficult step for participants. Generally accepted ideas, such as improving the quality of microfiche services, had to be translated into specific measures of performance. This translation generated questions which had very uncertain answers. What should be considered a microfiche defect? How many defects, if any, should be permissible on each document?

What percentage of documents should meet or exceed this quality level each month? What quality level do users actually require and expect?

The subjectivity of translating CSFs into specific performance measures and standards further necessitated the participation of all CIS personnel. Management did not want to force arbitrary or unattainable performance standards on their workers. Instead, workers were encouraged to participate in developing meaningful, yet challenging, measures of the IS Department's performance.

As the feedback loops in Figure 2 indicate, some measures and their associated standards of performance required several iterations to determine the most meaningful and practical measures of the achievement of a particular CSF.

## Workshop three

Five weeks later, at a one-half day workshop, standards were specified in more detail and sample monthly reports were presented. Managers individually reviewed the reports for relevance and appropriateness to the operational areas. It was discovered early in the process that much of customer service in this environment dealt with issues that are not measurable by objective data.

![](/api/attachments/MNWYJRVU/fulltext/images/fdb18eafa5700e04d6cc021c10a5e956ca009f7d91566b00845c5624a4461c59.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Conceptual Model of Survey Information.

Consequently, it was decided at this workshop that significant data must be collected through a survey of user opinion. It was agreed that all of the area managers would review their CSFs and attempt to generate survey items that were relevant to performance measurement in their respective areas. This would provide the beginning of a pool of survey items that might be used for performance measurement in areas where “hard data” were not available. These data would also help to validate the standards already set in “hard data” areas.

## Workshops four and five

These workshops, held within four months of the project origination, focused on specific survey developmental issues. The group managers had generated a variety of items “from the bottom up” based on their CSFs. However, a conceptual model was needed to ensure adequate breadth and depth of survey coverage. Consequently, the model in Figure 3 was developed. Five elements of user satisfaction were identified: comprehensiveness (system completeness), ease of use, support, quality/reliability, and timeliness. These broad areas of concern were then mapped over the three major service dimensions – the central computer systems, the network, and the computing labs. The 15 cell matrix shown in Figure 3 was used as a conceptual model for the academic user community to ensure that sufficient items were generated for adequate coverage in each area. Consequently, the survey was generated from both a bottom up and top down approach. The items thus developed by experienced group managers show an interesting similarity to those developed empirically by Bailey and Pearson [1], whose research with middle managers in several different organizations produced 39 items which affected user satisfaction. Typical items reflected on issues, such as accuracy, reliability, timeliness, relevancy, confidence in system, completeness, technical competence of staff, communication and relationship with staff, convenience of access, time required for new developments, and response/turnaround time. The similarity of items between the Bailey and Pearson work and our efforts can be noted by reviewing the survey in Appendix 2.

## Outcomes

As mentioned previously, the application of the CSF process resulted in the identification of 10 global factors for the entire Computing and Information Systems organization. However, some of the operational areas were concerned with as few as three of the global CSFs. Table 1 contains a list of area specific factors that were generated from the CSF process. These area specific factors came together to support the entire CSF organization performance. This segmentation was necessary due to the breadth of activity in which the Computing and Information Systems organization is involved. Obviously the Business Office, which administers much of the organization's internal operations, has substantially different problems than the Academic Computing area which attempts to service researchers, faculty, and the students. ## Case example 3: System "C" reliability

As Figure 6 indicates, System “C” reliability and availability was directly impacted by the measurement process. The manager in charge indicated, “We have been working on this and it shows for the last three months. The performance earlier in the year was not tracking in the right direction. We established this as one of the goals of our system. Periodic maintenance was employed to improve this factor over the last three months and we now appear to have it under control.”

It seems clear that some of the CSFs led directly to management intervention to make sure that the system was performing in an acceptable fashion. The mere collection of the data and review is salutary in itself. In addition, the Director receives (on a monthly basis) a complete package of all of the CSFs; this can be used to review changes that are occurring in the system and identify problem areas. Also, in meetings of the top managers, there is a better rapport and understanding of the problems across operational areas because of the explicitness of the CSF reporting system.

## Interactions among the five groups

Better understanding developed among the group managers and their own workers by focusing on CIS's overall goals and performance measures, then breaking down those global goals to apply to their own daily operations. This process helped each area appreciate how the other areas were trying to achieve the same overall CSF (e.g. Network Reliability) while still attending to their specific piece of CIS business.

Therefore, the CSF process facilitated the integration of activities among five operational areas. Common concerns were pointed out while cross-purposes were exposed, discussed and worked out to achieve the best possible integration and positive organization-wide results.

## Guidelines for Use in Other Contexts

A variety of interesting issues have evolved in the experience of applying the CSF process in a university IS setting. While this environment is arguably different from the corporate world, a number of similarities exist and some appropriate principles are suggested by this implementation experience. The described CSF process has great value, but is not trivial to implement and takes substantial amounts of organizational energy [10]. However, a number of principles emerged that may be relevant for applications of the CSF method in a variety of contexts.

1. Top management support is essential. Substantial amounts of key managers' time is required to define and use this technique. It must have the support and enthusiasm of top management and this must be shared with the management team.

2. Substantial group process is required. The climate of candor and trust must be fostered in order for factors to be defined and measured accurately. Much more than one-on-one consulting meetings is necessary to develop this. Consensual group process is a must. In this context, the Nominal Group Technique is often a useful approach at the initiation of the CSF process.

3. Data collection is a significant problem. We had the useful resource of a graduate student intern to provide many hours of internal consultation as well as collection of data and the development of appropriate graphic displays. This level of time and in-depth participation would have been difficult for external consultants.

## 6. Survey development will most likely be necessary.

For customer service performance measurement, as in this case, all factors are seldom measurable by hard and objective data. With a varied client group such as in a university setting, survey research is virtually “required” to tap all the CSFs.

## Conclusions

Measuring the performance of a technologically sophisticated set of services provided to a variety of users is a complex task. Using the CSF process to focus management's attention to the few important aspects of performance and pushing down the use of those factors to lower organization levels has been effective in a university IS environment.

The CSF approach is a well-established tool for the IS planning function. This article has demonstrated an adaptation of the CSF technique to the task of measuring and improving the operational performance of a large IS organization. The excellent results of both the process and outcomes of this approach suggest that CSFs are an effective starting point for the complex task of measuring the performance of similar IS organizations.
