# Key Article

# IT: running fast and standing still? \*

Paul B. Cragg

Department of Management Systems, University of Waikato, Hamilton, New Zealand

Paul N. Finlay

Loughborough University Business School, Loughborough University of Technology, Leics. LE11 3TU, UK

A currently held view is that Information Technology (IT) has high potential for providing competitive advantage. This paper critically examines this view, particularly how IT can confer sustainable, strategic advantage. The areas in which IT can confer advantage are reviewed, both within the Competitive Arena and in the associated Industry. The analysis then shifts to cover the wider, technological environment within which the Industry operates. The consequences of the technological environment to the types of task undertaken by an organisation are then analysed to determine where sustainable strategic advantage might lie. Our conclusion is that areas of sustainable strategic advantage through IT are not numerous and lie predominantly within the corporate culture.

Keywords: Competitive advantage, Information technology, Environmental analysis, PIMS, Information systems, Structured activities, Sustainability

## Introduction

Many organisations have realised that it is imperative that they develop an information technology (IT) strategy. The notions of using information resources for ‘strategic advantage’ and as ‘competitive weapons’ have now become commonplace (Gerstein and Reisman (1982), Ives and Learmonth (1984) and King (1983)). The argument is that the commercial arena is becoming ever more competitive. This not only requires margins to be watched very closely, but demands that companies become ever more selective in the identification of customers and segments.

Relationships between an organisation and those with whom it deals are being altered, sometimes radically, with the advent of IT. With such a change, a competitive advantage will come to organisations that wholeheartedly embrace IT and take advantage of it.

King et al. (1989) make clear the distinction between IT and information as resources that might lead to competitive advantage. The following examples were used to help clarify the distinction. American Hospital Supplies obtained advantage through the use of IT, by placing terminals in their customers' offices. Chrysler was able to continue to exist through the use of information made available through the use of IT. American Airlines obtained advantage through both the use of IT and through the use of the consequent information it provided.

This paper continues this analysis of the role of IT for competitive advantage. In particular, it is concerned with identifying those aspects of IT that are associated with sustainable, strategic competitive advantage. Are all businesses in the position of the Red Queen in ‘Alice Through the Looking Glass’ (Carroll (1965) – running fast (with IT) and standing still (vis-a-vis competitors)?

The paper begins with a brief restatement of where gains might be obtained through an appropriate use of information and of IT, and reviews the frameworks that have been proposed. The form of the technological environment within which industries operate is seen as a key factor in the sustainability of any competitive advantage.

## Previous Work on IT and Competitive Advantage

The literature contains many examples of the use of IT for competitive advantage. In his book on expert systems, Chorafas (1987) lists 20 examples where organisations have used IT for competitive advantage. Porter and Millar (1985) give a further 30 examples.

The successful uses of IT have been analysed and frameworks developed to help organisations identify strategic applications. Clemons and Kimbrough (1987) have considered IT within the framework of 5 situations. These are where information systems are a routine necessity, a strategic necessity, provide a comparative advantage, provide a temporary competitive advantage and where they provide a sustainable competitive advantage. The major contrast in their paper is between information systems that are a strategic necessity and those that offer sustainable competitive advantage. Information systems that are a strategic necessity are those that a business must have in order to operate effectively, but that are not available to all with equal assurance or at the same cost: information systems that offer sustainable competitive advantage provide an advantage that persists for a long period of time, despite competitors' desire to eliminate what for them is a source of disadvantage.

Perhaps the best known framework is that provided by Porter (1979) and developed by Porter and Millar (1985). Here, the forces that affect the nature of competition in an industry are examined, and the effects of IT on them are discussed. Before using this framework it is necessary to define two terms:

Competitive Arena: Those businesses that are producing products and services that are close substitutes of one another, and that would take market share away from a business if it failed to compete effectively.

Industry: The group of firms within the competitive arena, and the chain of business activities that impact on this group, both upstream and downstream.

IT can be used for competitive advantage both within the competitive arena and in the wider context of the industry. Taking the competitive arena first, the following shows four different ways by which firms can use IT to gain competitive advantage (after Bakos and Treacy, 1986). These categories are:

Improvement in operational efficiency (for example, the introduction of expert systems by DEC aimed to improve system configurations - advantage obtained through the use of IT).

Improvement in managerial effectiveness (for example, the Chrysler suppliers case with advantage obtained through the use of information).

Product and service innovations (for example, banks have used ATMs to provide customers with improved access to accounts – advantage obtained through the use of both IT and information).

Increasing bargaining power over the firm's particular customers or suppliers (for example, the use of terminals by AHS - advantage obtained through the use of IT).

There is no doubt that IT might give benefits in all these areas, allowing firms to compete better whatever generic strategy they are following. If they choose to follow a lowest-cost strategy, then the use of IT to increase operational efficiency would apply; if a strategy of differentiation were preferred, then an emphasis on using IT for product and service innovation could usefully be adopted. A niche strategy is likely to gain through the use of sophisticated information systems to identify special and specific requirements. All strategies are likely to gain from improvements in managerial effectiveness.

Additionally, strategic advantage/disadvantage may affect all the firms within the competitive arena, through the alteration of the relationships between the competitive arena and the rest of the industry. Such changes that would enhance the position of firms would involve:

\- Raising the barriers of entry into the competitive arena;

\- Reducing costs and adding value to alter the relative attractiveness of substitutes;

\- Increasing the bargaining power over the generality of customers and suppliers;

\- Bypassing the ‘next in line’ in the industry chain, to alter the leverage on the suppliers and buyers.

In all cases, it is difficult to see information as a result of the use of IT being a factor in improving the competitive standing of the whole of the competitive arena: this will be done through the use of IT per se.

Superficially, the case for using IT seems overwhelming. However, there have been disappointments. Sager (1988) found that no Australian retail bank had achieved any sustainable advantage: instead, consumers had obtained valuable additional benefits. The same situation seems to have happened after the “Big Bang” when the London financial markets were deregulated; a great deal of IT in the Stock Exchange did not make the brokers more profitable, although the customer obtained a better service.

Vitale's (1986) analysis provides a sober perspective in recognising that IT initiatives can have unintended, negative consequences. One of his examples concerned a manufacturer who gained market share through computerising the process of turning customer specifications for a building into a list of products and instructions. However, a competitor developed a system which worked on the IBM personal computer, and customers were provided with free software and an electronic rather than a postal connection. Such innovations enabled the competitor to more than recapture its lost market share, and thus turned an IT success for the initial firm into an ultimate competitive failure.

Clemons and Kimbrough (1987) discuss appropriate actions for an organization. Where there is a strategic necessity and the associated costs are high, they suggest that firms form a consortium or increase the industry concentration. They warn against an innovator placing itself at a competitive disadvantage and suggest options to mitigate the effects. They also consider what to do when confronted by a competitor's actions: they suggest matching the competitor if possible, forming a consortium or, as a last resort, liquidating or being acquired.

## The Need for a Wider Analysis

These disappointments show that the concept of using IT for competitive advantage is still relatively new and not fully understood. To understand these disappointments, it is necessary to take a wider look: one that considers the environment outside the industry. This environment is generally known in Strategic Management circles by the acronym PEST (Political, Economic, Social and Technological) and is depicted in Figure 1.

A PEST analysis is an analysis of how events and trends in the factors in a wider environment affect the industry under study. The technological dimension has a particular significance, because developments in IT hardware are overwhelmingly produced outside the industries that are using them. Similarly, much software comes from outside the industry. While this occurs in other fields, it is seldom as marked as in IT.

The characteristics of this environment and its offerings are:

1. Global marketplace: apart from artificial barriers erected for political and economic reasons (taxes, tariff barriers, national bank exchange controls etc.) there are no barriers to IT being available world-wide.

2. Many suppliers: The IT industry is in the growth phase of its life cycle. Although some amalgamations have taken place (Unisys, for example) there are many suppliers, particularly of software and microcomputers.

3. Information easily available: The keenness with which suppliers will tout their wares, the plethora of conferences and seminars, the multitude of brochures and articles in the press, all mean that information (and indeed misinformation!) on both products and product use is easily and cheaply available.

4. Many powerful and relatively cheap products: The advent of the microcomputer and the associated software, whose development costs are spread over many purchases, has led to IT being readily available, often within the budgets of middle and junior managers.

5. Products are ‘robust’ and user-friendly: The quality of hardware and software now offers reasonably inexpensive help in business for (almost) the computer illiterate.

## The Consequences of the Technological Environment

The major problem with attempting to use IT for competitive advantage is that it is easily transferable. Consider the early uses of computers for data processing. In a relatively short time, most large organisations introduced computerised systems for order processing, inventory control, and the common accounting routines. Any advantage that one organisation had over another quickly evaporated as others followed suit. Additionally, the first organisations using computers often suffered high development costs, which were not suffered by the followers as more expertise had been gained. Thus any (tactical) gains of being first in the field seemed to be counterbalanced somewhat by the disadvantage of being a pioneer.

The situation has altered somewhat, because appropriate computer power is now available to small and big organisations alike, and higher level languages, wider computer literacy, and inexpensive packages have brought sophisticated software to most organisations. The transferability of IT has increased and this is likely to continue.

Transferability is not however, restricted to general purpose hardware and software. For example, Operational Research techniques in areas such as transportation costs have not provided advantage for long. Similarly, DEC's use of expert systems has been followed by all the major hardware suppliers.

This problem of transferability also affects small firms. A small aluminium joinery firm introduced a system which integrated the design of windows with the cost of materials. This not only allowed them to give accurate quotes, but also to give them immediately over the telephone. It gave the firm an advantage once customers realised how much quicker they were. However, now many competitors have similar systems, partly because the writers of the original software made improvements to the system with the help of a computer manufacturer, who recognised the opportunity of a niche market. This is interesting, as it would have appeared useful for the joinery company to have kept the advantage to itself, but they chose to join the technological environment of the industry.

The above examples were examples of attempts to secure one-off advantages, that would be sustainable if the step change could only be copied through the damaging expenditure of a great deal of resource.

Vitale also discusses specific examples of this form of incremental advantage – where IT provided an initial advantage, that could not be sustained. He points out that Government intervention resolved the claims against United and American's airline reservation system. They also discuss an appliance company's initial information systems success changing into competitive failure as a major competitor implemented a similar but more modern and accessible system.

The first bank to introduce ATMs in New Zealand did reap an initial competitive advantage. However, their reasons were purely to reduce the load on the banks' tellers. If they had viewed ATMs as a way of attracting more customers, they would have given more thought to ATM placement. Instead, ATMs were placed where there already was a large number of customers. Competitors have followed suit, but learned from the leading company. As Johnston and Carrico (1988) found with a food products firm, "If a new practice is promising, we throw enough resources into our effort to overwhelm their lead. Management took pride in being a fast second".

Another way in which an organisation might achieve sustainable competitive advantage would be from continual (incremental) improvements to which competitors cannot react in time – and where they are always trying to catch up.

## Structured and Ill-structured Tasks

Simon (1977) and Dermer (1977) have looked at activities undertaken in Business and dichotomised them into structured and un- or ill-structured activities. In reality however, there is a continuum.

Structured activities are repetitive and routine; definite procedures have been worked out for handling them. Thus, they can be well defined, through professional standards (accountants and engineers are in the forefront of this), because of the success of the providers of methods and turnkey solutions and because in many cases activities can only really be performed in a few well-defined ways (order processing and stock control are examples). In contrast, unstructured managerial activities are novel and judgemental.

## IT and Structured Activities

When the structure of an activity is apparent and there are many providers of the IT tools with the needed characteristics, it is relatively easy for organisations to acquire the IT capability. Thus sustainable competitive advantage associated with structured activities can only occur through the acquisition of IT per se. The only known cases are those where an organisation was first and kept out the opposition, maybe only for a short time; e.g., for AHS, American Airlines, and Community Pharmacy, per (Linsky, 1989).

However, it may be argued that competitive advantage can arise through the use of these tools; i.e., through the information that is produced. But is this likely to bring sustainable advantage? Given the ‘obvious’ advantage of using the technology and the ‘obvious’ way of using it, this would appear doubtful. An exception may occur in the case of diversified organisations.

## The Consequences of the Increased Use of IT

Although IT is becoming relatively inexpensive, it is still a significant capital outlay for structured activities (especially in information intense businesses). The likely overall effect of such widespread capital investment might be gauged from looking at what has happened with other capital investment. The available evidence paints a rather sombre view.

The consequence to a competitive arena where the technology is transferable and economies of scale are not very powerful has been charted by the Boston Consultancy Group (1981) and by Lochridge (1981). They give an example from the paper industry. Broadly, paper mills come in one size, at a level of capacity that requires many mills to satisfy the paper requirements of a country such as the UK; each mill is not costly. Additionally, the technology associated with paper mills is easily transferable. In boom times, entrants are enticed into the arena, keeping profits low; in bad times, cost cutting to fill capacity results in very low profits and even losses.

The Profit Impact of Marketing Strategy (PIMS) program now run by the Strategic Planning Institute has used its database to examine the link between profitability and capital intensity (Schoeffler 1980).

Their database showed that as the capital intensity in a competitive arena increased the profitability of the firms in the arena dropped. The correlation is a very strong one. The reason given for such an inverse correlation was that in a capital intensive competitive arena the forces at work are very different to those when the capital intensity is lower. The major reason is that as the break-even point rises, creating the requirement that capacity be filled, price reductions must be made, and these lead to lower revenues and lower profits.

If this finding of the PIMS program is generally applicable, then it could be assumed that if IT is used in a capital intensive way, e.g. as in the banks, the firms in that competitive arena will show less returns. A strange form of strategic competitive advantage!

In his paper for the British Institute of Management, Kearney (1984) compared the financial performance of the ‘leaders’ and ‘laggards’ of a sector in the use of IT. He found that the companies lagging in the use of IT were six times more likely to have a poor financial performance than the leaders.

Although there may be problems of determining causality in this relationship, the finding would seem to run counter to those cited. However, there is no contradiction: for a company to be left behind in the use of IT will indeed put it at a competitive disadvantage, and thus give a competitive advantage to its competitors: those in the van of IT usage. However, the Boston Consultancy Group and PIMS findings do not imply that, within a competitive arena, any firm that became more capital intensive (by substituting IT for labour) would be less profitable than another individual firm that was less capital intensive. Rather, these studies considered the overall profitability of the competitive arena, not of individual firms.

For an organisation not to ‘follow the herd’ and invest in IT would almost always lead to competitive disadvantage. In New Zealand, the wholesale pharmacies that did not invest in IT during 1977–79 went out of business (Barr, 1989): today, all wholesale pharmacies have invested in IT and there are too many in the arena for all to make decent profits.

This implies that, to retain advantage, every company must keep up with IT developments, but the final result is a diminution of return on investment for all. Is there any reason to believe that this will not occur for capital investment in IT? If it does, then it is the providers of IT who will gain, at the expense of the industry they purport to serve! [This was, indeed, true of ATM introduction].

This is a rather sobering prospect. However, it does not prescribe a lack of investment in IT. What it says is that it is very unlikely that sustainable competitive advantage will accrue from investment in IT for structured activities, and that in the medium-to-long-term it will lead to the whole industry becoming less profitable.

## IT and Ill-structured Situations

The characteristic of ill-structured situations is their uniqueness: they have never before been encountered in the same form; discussion of ways of proceeding include judgement, for which no prescription or recipe exists.

Decision Support Systems (DSS) are computer-based aids that aim to help decision making in ill-structured situations. Finlay (1989) has categorised the various types of DSS. At one end of the spectrum of DSS are Executive Information Systems that passively provide information in a form acceptable to senior managers (and about which Harvey-Jones was so enthusiastic when he was chief executive of ICI (Harvey-Jones, 1988)).

In the middle of the spectrum are the Group DSS in which the tools of Management Science (consensual methodology, preference determination systems, etc.) are used in a group context: a good example of this approach is that of Decision Conferencing (Phillips, 1986). Finally, at the end of the spectrum are those systems that attempt to codify the thoughts of the executives seeking to act in an ill-structured situation: an example is the work on cognitive mapping undertaken by Eden and his colleagues (Eden, 1986).

With ill-structured activities, much of the value is added by the organisation, because it is organisation specific. If organisations allocate more effort to unstructured IT than they do to structured activities, they should get a greater return.

The creative challenge facing many firms is therefore to determine how to support ill-structured areas; here any solution is more likely to centre on the effective use of information rather than on information technology.

## IT and the Diversified Firm

Porter (1985) believes that the only value that a diversified firm has over the single product/service organisation is the synergy it can obtain from diverse activities; information can enable latent synergies to be realised. Management of linkages can provide competitive advantage because rivals cannot easily see them. Rockart and Short (1989) take a complementary view, arguing that IT's most important role is in allowing firms to manage organisational interdependence. However, they do not go as far as Porter and Millar, who see IT as enabling new and innovative lines of synergy to be developed. Here again the use of information is important, and the organisation itself is adding value, not the IT.

## Summary and Conclusions

We have considered the importance of the wider environment and of IT providers within it. For structured activities, it is unlikely that sustainable strategic advantage can be obtained. A few well-publicised cases do not invalidate this proposition.

It is presently strongly suggested that IT can convey sustainable strategic advantage. However, there is a need to consider the technological environment within which the industry is operating and the tasks that the organisation is undertaking. With well structured activities the use of IT can produce competitive advantage for a firm in a competitive arena, but since IT is generally easily transferable, any gains from the use of IT will soon become known, with competitors keeping broadly in step with each other. There is little in the way of informational gain, except through its use in unexpected ways; i.e., through its use in ill-structured contexts: any gains will be tactical and short-lived. At a strategic level, the increasing use of IT will not convey strategic advantage to the shareholders of the firms, rather it will give advantage to its employees, customers, the rest of the industry, and the economy as a whole.

With ill-structured activities, the situation is different, because the organisation adds value through the use of the information.

A number of frameworks help organisations search for strategic applications. Organisations have used these frameworks to generate many potential applications, some of which could be worthy of evaluation. In such an exercise, the potential for sustainability should be considered. The following rules are suggested (numbers 3 and 4 are derived from the work of Clemons and Kimbrough (1987)):

1. Only if it is perceived that being first will lead to a strategically sustainable advantage should an attempt be made to be first.

2. The change from a manual to electronic way of doing things is a disruption during which sustainable competitive advantage is most likely to be achieved.

3. If the IT development is a strategic necessity and seems likely to be expensive, endeavour to form a consortium with the competition. If this is not achievable, seek to increase concentration in the competitive arena, either through acquisition or by being acquired.

4. If the actions of competitors appear to be creating a new strategic necessity for all firms in the competitive arena, then match the development if possible. If this is not possible seek to form a consortium. If this is again not possible and there is no way to match the new offering, then liquidate or get acquired.

5. Advantage through the use of information in ill-structured situations has a much greater chance of being sustainable than the use of IT in well structured situations.

6. For unstructured activities, attempt to be first, but this is a long term effort and is intimately linked to company culture.

7. For structured activities, be second.
