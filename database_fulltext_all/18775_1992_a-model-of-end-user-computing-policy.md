# A model of end-user computing policy \*

# Context, process, content and compliance

Dennis F. Galletta

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

Ellen M. Hufnagel

College of Business Administration, University of South Florida, Tampa, FL 33620-5500, USA

One factor that has been cited as key to successful end-user computing (EUC) management is a formalized policy statement that defines the limits of users' discretion. In this paper, a model of EUC policy is developed as a mechanism for examining relationships between organizational context, the process by which EUC policy is developed and administered, and the content of resulting policy statements. Organizational compliance is introduced as an important outcome measure in the model. A mail survey of MIS executives in 107 Fortune 1000 companies provides preliminary support for this contingency theory of EUC policy compliance. The results suggest that firms which are procedurally bureaucratic are most likely to develop comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements. Organizational compliance levels were reported to be highest in companies with both comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements and formal mechanisms for monitoring compliance.

Keywords: End-user computing, EUC policy, Compliance, Contingency theory.

## Introduction

In recent years, research relating to the management and control of end-user computing (EUC) has increased steadily. Researchers have reported the results of numerous studies tracking its growth $[5,28,53]$ , while others have suggested a variety of control strategies $[2,43]$ . Dimensions of EUC and specific factors that lead to successful implementation have also been identified, along with key management issues and problems associated with EUC.

Poor security, incompatible hardware and software, inadequate documentation, insufficient validation and testing, and ill-defined policies regarding access to corporate databases are but a few of the problems that can lead to excessive EUC costs for an organization. Although the list of problems is lengthy, most researchers seem to agree that the majority could be resolved or largely avoided through the careful development and enforcement of EUC policies [3,6,22,51,52,56].

The extent to which organizations have created EUC policy statements has been the subject of several investigations, but the results suggest that there is still a high degree of variability in the extent to which EUC policies and procedures have been formalized and the control procedures used to implement them $[25,43]$ . To date, the literature has provided little in the way of empirical evidence explaining this variability. It has, however, raised a number of interesting questions about the relationships that may exist among a variety of organizational context variables, the process by which EUC policy and procedures are developed and administered, and the content of these policy statements.

The purpose of this investigation is to begin exploring these relationships to develop a well-grounded theory of EUC policy. This study also seeks to add a new dimension to existing formulations by considering the nature of the relationship between EUC policies (process and content) and organizational compliance with these policies. Since policy measures can only be effective if organizational members actually adhere to them, the issue of compliance is extremely important to MIS researchers and practitioners alike.

## EUC policy defined

Although the term “EUC policy” is widely used in the MIS literature, a clear definition of it has not been stated. The acronym, EUC, refers to “the direct assumption of system development and data processing tasks by the user of the service for his own direct benefit” [27]. It includes not only the use of standalone personal computers, but also user-initiated processing activities using mainframe support.

The term “policy” is used in the business literature in two ways. Broadly viewed, policy refers to an organization’s grand plan or strategy which defines its overall goals and objectives. More narrowly viewed, it refers to specific statements that specify both desirable and undesirable management activities and, as a result, limit the discretion of employees [10]. This second definition most clearly conveys our concern in this study where the term “policy” refers to the rules, guidelines, and specific procedures that are developed to restrict organizational choices to a pre-specified range of possibilities consistent with the overall direction of the firm. Combining these definitions, "EUC policy" can be defined as:

## A model of EUC policy

The general research model, shown in Figure 1, depicts the relationships of interest here. The numbered links correspond to the general research questions to be discussed and explored:

Link 1: How does organizational context affect the process by which EUC policies and procedures are developed and administered?

Link 2: How does organizational context affect the actual content of EUC policies and procedures?

Link 3: How does the process by which EUC policy is developed and administered affect the content of stated EUC policies and procedures?

Link 4: How does the formality of policy-related processes affect organizational compliance with stated EUC policies and procedures?

Link 5: How does the content of the EUC policy statement affect organizational compliance with stated EUC policies and procedures?

## The effects of organizational context variables on process and content

There is a substantial body of management literature that examines the relationship between the internal structure of an organization, the content of its policies, and its management processes, arguing that the “fit” or congruence between them is crucial to the achievement of organizational objectives [11,33,42,46,57]. According to contingency theory, there is no universally appropriate approach to policy and control that applies to all organizations in all circumstances. Rather, the optimal approach (from the standpoint of organizational effectiveness) is one which matches policy and control structures with the characteristics of the firm.

A number of MIS researchers have drawn upon contingency theories in identifying organizational factors that affect the successful implementation of an MIS [15,16,17,18,19,20,35,38,39,40]. Others suggest that a close fit between organizational context, management processes, and policies may also be critical to the effective implementation of EUC [12], providing a clear and consistent sense of direction for decisions about the acquisition and use of EUC resources.

A variety of organizational variables have been studied by MIS researchers as possible determinants of MIS effectiveness. In our study, variables that describe a firm's structure and its strategic posture with respect to MIS in general, and EUC in particular, are postulated as key determinants of the process by which EUC policies are developed and administered and the content of such policies (Links 1 and 2). The variables investigated include (1) the degree to which organizational planning processes have been formalized, (2) the extent to which they are linked, (3) the degree of bureaucratization of the firm, (4) the placement of the MIS function within the organizational structure, (5) the level of interest in EUC, (6) the firm's EUC growth strategy, and (7) the experience the firm has in dealing with EUC-related issues (maturity). While the variables included in an empirical investigation necessarily represent only a subset of the possibilities, efforts were made to ensure the inclusion of those variables which had strong theoretical support in the literature.

Formalization of organizational planning processes. One way to develop and disseminate policy in an organization is through formal, well-structured planning efforts. Termed “synoptic-formal” approaches, these planning processes are systematic and analytical in nature, resulting in comprehensive policy statements that impose significant constraints on organizational choice [9].

Although much has been written about the benefits of such approaches $[31,36,55]$ , many organizations have no formal planning system $[34]$ .

Instead, they manage through an informal and unstructured process of adaptive responses that has been labeled “logical-incrementalism” [48]. These planning approaches are, by design, more limited in scope and more interactive. They tend to emerge in piecemeal fashion, evolving over time as new opportunities and needs are sensed and addressed. Furthermore, they attempt to take into account non-rational aspects of the organization, such as power, politics and social processes.

Given that companies which exhibit synoptic-formal planning patterns seek to define comprehensive policies and procedures for deploying organizational resources, we would expect these companies to have developed more formal processes for EUC policy development and administration (Link 1) and more comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements (Link 2) than companies with less formal planning orientations.

The link between strategic planning and MIS planning. A number of researchers have argued that the effective use of information resources depends on the strength of the linkage between an organization's strategic and information systems planning processes [30,32,45,49,54,58]. A tight link between these processes is considered important because, in the process of translating an organization's business strategy into an information resources strategy, questions regarding the role of information and the policies and standards governing information resources are likely to be addressed [30]. By implication, a weak link between these processes is likely to be associated with less well-defined information resource policies and standards.

Although the impetus to develop formal EUC policies can come from sources other than formal, tightly-linked planning processes, we would expect that companies with tight linkages are more likely to have also developed formal EUC policy-related processes (Link 1) and more comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements (Link 2) than companies with loose linkages.

Degree of bureaucratization. Organizational structures can be classified in a variety of ways, but the approaches that are most useful from a planning and control standpoint tend to be those that describe how responsibility and authority are shared by organization members [10]. The structure that most clearly defines the responsibility and authority of organizational members is a bureaucracy. Bureaucratic firms are primarily concerned with rational efficiency and control, achieving these ends through clearly defined task responsibilities and a system of procedures for handling recurring activities. As a result, bureaucratic decision making is generally highly centralized and formal [24].

In contrast to the bureaucracy is an organizational form that has been termed an adhocracy (because decisions are made in a seemingly ad hoc way). Here, decision making authority is widely distributed across the organization and coordination is achieved through social interchanges and mutual adjustment [35]. Formal rules and policies are few.

Because of their focus on rules and procedures, we would expect organizations exhibiting strong bureaucratic characteristics to have more formal EUC policy-making processes (Link 1) and more comprehensive and restrictive policies (Link 2) than less bureaucratic ones.

Organizational placement of the MIS function. Since policy-making is a key aspect of an organization's planning process, factors that influence the success of MIS planning are likely to influence the development and administration of EUC policies as well as their scope and comprehensiveness. One factor that may exert particular influence in this respect is the hierarchical placement of the MIS function. Pyburn [47] found that the status or organizational placement of the MIS manager can have a dramatic effect on the success of MIS planning. His results suggest that MIS is more likely to be perceived as supporting organizational goals when few levels exist between the top MIS manager and senior management. Ein-Dor and Segev [17] and Raghunathan and Raghunathan [50] provide additional empirical evidence linking rank of the MIS director with success of the MIS department.

The organizational placement of the MIS function may have an effect on EUC policy-making, whether or not the MIS manager is responsible for overall EUC implementation and management. In a survey of senior MIS managers, Alavi [1] found that EUC issues of greatest concern included: (1) enforcement of EUC standards and (2) development of policy guidelines defining the nature and scope of EUC activities. Since highly-placed MIS executives are likely to have a greater ability to influence organizational priorities in these directions, we expect that firms having few reporting levels between the CEO and the MIS executive will have more formal EUC policy-making processes (Link 1) and more comprehensive and restrictive policy statements (Link 2) than those with more reporting levels.

Interest, growth strategy and maturity. Munro et al. [43] presented a matrix model of EUC development that considers both the firm's EUC growth strategy and EUC maturity within the organization (see Figure 2). They identified two relatively independent forces which affect the direction of EUC development: expansion and control. In companies where expansion is emphasized and control is de-emphasized, steps are taken to speed up EUC diffusion by providing additional resources and/or management support. Where the emphasis is on control rather than expansion, the result is a strategy of containment which restricts EUC growth by limiting users' hardware and software choices, mainframe access, etc.

Munro et al. contend that all firms start out in the low-low cell of the matrix without any real strategy for expansion or control. As user interest grows, the firm begins to evolve a formal strategy for EUC, emphasizing either expansion or control. Eventually, a balance is reached and EUC maturity is achieved. Thus, the policy development process is dictated by the level of interest in EUC, the length of time that users have been exposed to it, and the overall EUC strategy of the firm.

Based on this model, we would expect to find more formal EUC policy-related processes (Link 1) and more comprehensive and restrictive EUC policies (Link 2) in companies where EUC facilities have been in place for some time, where EUC interest is high, and where the company's EUC strategy is one of containment rather than expansion.

## The relationship between process and content

Most research on EUC policy has focused on identifying specific substantive areas that are or are not addressed by EUC policy statements. Little has been written about the process by which EUC policy is developed and administered or the effects of such processes on policy content and policy-related behavior. Yet, the way in which policies and procedures are formulated can have a significant effect on employees' willingness to comply with both the letter and the spirit of organizational directives [8].

The management control literature distinguishes between formal and informal controls $[14]$ . Formal controls rely on impersonal mechanisms such as written policies and procedures to reflect the goals and plans of the organization and to substitute for direct personal supervision of employees. These controls are generally quite visible as they are administered through regular organizational channels $[4]$ . Informal controls depend on personal control by human participants within the firm to direct and correct the behavior of employees $[14]$ . They tend to be less visible than formal controls and may be erratic in their application, depending as they do on individual leadership skills and group norms [29].

Although few organizations rely solely on one or the other of these approaches, formal control mechanisms are typically associated with more bureaucratic organization structures as well as with more formalized planning processes $[44]$ . Thus, we would expect to find a strong relationship not only between the organizational context variables and the formality of policy-making process and content variables (Links 1 and 2), but also between the process and content variables themselves. Formal processes for developing and administering EUC policies are expected to result in more comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements (Link 3).

The relationship between process, content and compliance

Perhaps the most interesting aspects of our research model are links 4 and 5, which depict the relationship between the process and content variables, and compliance. These links focus on specific factors that may help to explain why some policies and procedures are more effective than others.

The content of documented EUC policies and procedures may be an important determinant of compliance because, as March [37] observes, most people in organizations tend to follow standard operating procedures, “even when it is not obviously in their self-interest to do so.” This can be viewed as part of an implicit contractual agreement whereby employees behave in prescribed ways in return for being treated in a consistent and appropriate fashion. Thus, the mere existence of a comprehensive EUC policy is likely to result in a high level of compliance since policies communicate what is expected of employees.

As Merchant [41] points out, however, rules and procedures are most effective if there is a formal mechanism for monitoring compliance and the consequences of deviating from the rules are clearly specified. In the absence of specific monitoring mechanisms, some employees may view firm policies as guidelines to be followed only when it is convenient to do so. The literature on taxpayer compliance provides additional evidence to support these assertions. Studies of taxpayer behavior indicate that voluntary compliance with published tax laws is generally high, but compliant behavior is significantly higher when taxpayers believe that there is a high probability of an audit [59].

Based on these arguments, we would expect to find high levels of compliance with EUC policy in firms that have comprehensive and restrictive EUC policy statements (Link 5) and formal processes for the development and administration of EUC policy (Link 4). Furthermore, we anticipate higher levels of compliance in organizations that employ formal monitoring mechanisms.

## Methodology

Considerable thought was given to the question of whether to test the model by surveying multiple end-users in an organization, by surveying only the top MIS executive, or by trying to combine the two. The MIS executive or CIO was selected as the focus of this preliminary study for several reasons. First, the study is concerned with planning processes that tend to occur at high levels in the organization. Although end users may have some representation in this decision making, the top MIS executive is more likely to be familiar with these processes than randomly selected end users. Second, the focus of the study is firm-wide policies and procedures rather than departmental strictures. We are interested in the compliant behavior of end users throughout the firm, rather than in one specific group. Third, as Goodhue and Straub [21] point out, the ideal person to ask about a topic is someone who is charged with and therefore intimately aware of the extent to which safeguards have been implemented. Since MIS managers view effective EUC management as among the more important issues facing businesses today [7,26], we believe they are most likely to keep themselves informed about matters relating to EUC policy and compliance. Additionally, survey results suggest that MIS has retained responsibility for managing both EUC hardware and software in most companies [1]. Unless the distribution of EUC management responsibilities has changed dramatically in the last few years, the top MIS executive may still be the best single source of information about EUC.

Our sample was drawn from the Fortune 500 directory of service companies and the Fortune 500 directory of manufacturers. (These companies are referred to collectively as the Fortune 1000). One hundred fifty companies were randomly selected from each list and a variety of secondary sources was consulted to identify the top MIS executive in each. Where secondary sources did not list an MIS executive's name, the company was contacted by phone to obtain it.

A 6-page questionnaire was mailed to 276 MIS executives identified in this way. Usable responses were received from 107 organizations (37% service firms, 45% manufacturing, and 18% other), representing a response rate of almost 39%. The survey approach appears to have been reasonably effective in reaching an executive audience as 44% of the actual respondents held the title of vice president or CIO and most of the remainder (49%) were directors or managers. To encourage a high response rate, respondents were not required to identify themselves or their companies. Since many executives opted not to do so, names of nonrespondents were not known for follow-up interviews. Thus, the possibility of nonresponse bias cannot be ruled out.

The survey instrument was pre-tested using a convenience sample of eight MIS vice presidents, directors and managers. All had been involved with EUC in some way. These executives were asked to complete our questionnaire and to explain their interpretations of the questions. Based on their input, minor adjustments were made to improve the understandability of the questionnaire.

In its final form, the survey instrument asked each MIS executive to provide information about the following organizational context variables:

(1) the existence of formal strategic and MIS planning processes,

(2) the linkage between these planning processes (no relationship, loosely connected or tightly linked).

(3) the location of MIS in the organization (highest title, number of reporting levels between the top MIS manager and the CEO),

(4) the degree of organizational interest in EUC (low, medium, high), and

(5) the organization's EUC growth strategy (restrictive, permissive or encouraging).

In addition, four dimensions that have been associated with the bureaucratic form of organization were adapted from Hall [24] and measured using 5-point Likert scales. Bureaucratic measures included the extent to which:

(1) task responsibilities are clearly assigned to individuals or groups (division of labor),

(2) department managers are granted decision making authority (centralization of authority),

(3) rights and responsibilities of organizational members are defined and enforced (system of rules), and

(4) procedures for handling routine activities are clearly documented (system of procedures).

Responses to these questions were combined to produce a mean score representing the degree of bureaucratization.

To determine the degree of formalization of EUC policy-related processes, questions were asked about:

(1) the existence of a formal planning process for EUC,

(2) the existence of a formal group charged with setting EUC policy,

(3) the linkage between MIS and EUC planning processes (no relationship, loosely connected or tightly linked),

(4) the existence of a formal mechanism for monitoring compliance with stated EUC policies, and

(5) the mechanisms used to communicate EUC policies to users, including policy manuals, formal training programs, memos or newsletters, and informal interactions between users and support personnel.

An overall measure of process formality was derived by aggregating responses to these questions.

A list of areas addressed by EUC policy was developed through a review of the literature and used to measure the comprehensiveness of each firm's EUC policy. This list included such items as restrictions on the type/brand of hardware acquired, requirements for cost justification, restrictions on the software used or types of applications developed, restrictions on uploading and downloading data, and documentation requirements. MIS executives responding in the affirmative to the question regarding the existence of a formal written policy were asked to indicate whether each of these areas is being addressed in their policy by a rule, a guideline, or not at all. A measure of comprehensiveness was calculated by counting the number of issues explicitly addressed. Policy restrictiveness was assessed by counting the number of areas addressed by a formal rule.

MIS executives were asked to judge the overall extent of compliance with stated rules and guidelines using the same list that was used to measure comprehensiveness. For each item included in their firm's formal written policies, respondents were asked to indicate whether users tend to comply in full, in part or to ignore the policy statement altogether. The measures of restrictiveness and policy compliance were computed by combining the respondent's answers across all areas addressed by policy and dividing by the number of areas. This procedure was used so that compliance scores would not be affected by the number of specific areas addressed by EUC policy.

## Results

## Profile of responding firms

Organizational context. Table 1 presents a profile of responses to the organizational context questions. As indicated, the majority of firms sampled have strong planning orientations, and most that do both strategic and MIS planning link them together in some way. The hierarchical placement of the top MIS executive is generally high. Not surprisingly, these large organizations also tend to be rather bureaucratic.

Almost all MIS executives rated the level of interest in EUC as mixed or high; the fact that only 3% assessed interest levels as low is perhaps indicative of nonresponse bias, but it may be that EUC is of significant interest to most Fortune 1000 firms. Although most firms are either permissive or encouraging in their approach to EUC growth, most have taken steps to control EUC through specific guidelines and procedures. On average, these firms have had more experience with mainframe supported terminals than with microcomputers.

EUC policy-related processes. As Table 2 indicates, most of the 39% of firms that plan for EUC link this activity to their MIS planning efforts in some way. One interesting observation that emerged from a review of the descriptive data was that 63% of these firms have a written EUC policy statement, yet only 44% of the 67 have a mechanism to monitor compliance. An earlier survey of EUC-related activities [1], yielded similar results: 20 of the 31 firms responding (63%) also had developed EUC standards, and roughly half of them were actively enforcing their policies. The similarity in results between these two studies suggests that, despite the attention to EUC policy in the literature, little change has occurred in practice, either in terms of the number of firms that have developed standards and procedures or the number that have taken steps to enforce existing ones.

EUC policy content and compliance. Table 3 provides a summary of the areas covered by EUC policy statements and the extent of compliance reported for each one. The typical EUC policy covers 11 out of the 14 listed areas. The five most frequently occurring policy areas involve acquisition approval for both hardware and software, and compliance with these policies is perceived by MIS executives to be fairly high. This is perhaps because acquisition is the easiest aspect of EUC to control since asset purchases are often covered by existing policies that are well-developed and internalized by managers in most companies. Uploading and downloading restrictions also appear in a majority of policy statements and user compliance is reported to be relatively high. This may be because mechanisms to control access to and updating of corporate databases can be readily implemented through most mainframe security packages.

Policy restrictions on actual user development activities are not only less widespread than either acquisition-related controls or upload and download restrictions, but also evidence somewhat lower compliance rates. This is not too surprising given the difficulty of monitoring user software development in large companies. Generally speaking, the compliance scores are lowest in areas where effective oversight is most difficult to achieve.

Surprisingly, firms with less experience managing EUC exhibit more formal EUC policy development processes. There are several possible explanations for this unexpected finding. Perhaps the well-publicized control problems encountered by firms that were early adopters of EUC technologies served as cautionary notices for later adopters, allowing them to be more proactive in managing and controlling the introduction of EUC. Firms with longer histories in EUC management may also find it more difficult to formalize the control process after users have become accustomed to operating with little restriction. Finally, it is possible that highly formalized policy-related processes are implemented early in the organizational learning process when firms are uncertain about the implications of a newly introduced technology and react by over-controlling it.

Correlations of the individual organizational context and process variables, shown in Table 4, indicate that formal EUC planning is more likely to be done in organizations with (1) relatively few reporting levels between the MIS function and the CEO, (2) formal MIS planning processes, and (3) tight linkages between MIS planning and strategic planning. Apparently, an orientation towards planning at the top of an organization percolates downward and is reflected in more formalized planning at lower levels, particularly when the MIS executive is part of the top management team.

The correlation results also suggest that formal processes for developing and administering EUC are more likely to be found in organizations where interest in EUC is high and management has actively encouraged EUC growth. Munro et al. [43] view interest and management support as expansionist forces that increase the pace of EUC development. In a large portion of our sample, rapid expansion is occurring in EUC, but not without a planned direction. Although it is difficult to say whether these firms implemented formal controls in response to increased interest and participation in EUC, or whether they developed the necessary control structures before introducing EUC, it is clear that many are currently employing a strategy of controlled growth.

As expected, organizations with the strongest bureaucratic orientations are most likely to have formal written EUC policy manuals and to coordinate interdepartmental activities through clearly specified rules and procedures. EUC policy manuals predominate in companies where EUC interest is highest, perhaps in response to the need to communicate clearly with a large number of prospective users throughout the organization.

Formal training programs are more likely to be used as a vehicle for EUC policy communication in companies with relatively little EUC experience. Firms with more experience may have encountered the need for policy dissemination at an earlier time and held training sessions for most of the affected employees long ago, or firms that have recently entered the EUC arena may be placing more emphasis on training in an attempt to avoid the EUC control problems reported in the business press.

Finally formal mechanisms for monitoring compliance with EUC policy and tightly linked MIS and EUC planning processes tend to predominate in firms where EUC interest is high and management has made deliberately efforts to encourage EUC growth. These results provide further evidence of a migration toward controlled growth.

## Link 2: Relationship between organizational context and policy content

The relationship between the organizational context variables and the content of EUC policy was examined using multiple linear regression. The model served to relate the set of organizational variables to two content measures: the number of areas covered by EUC policy and the degree of constraint imposed. Both regressions were significant at the 0.05 level, indicating that the content of EUC policy is a linear function of specific organizational context variables.

The number of areas covered by formal EUC policy is predicted by a model that includes only the organization's bureaucracy score ( $F = 5.88$ ; $p = 0.018$ ; 1,63 df). Firms which are more bureaucratic have the most comprehensive EUC policy statements. Approximately 9% of the variance in number of areas covered is explained by this single factor. The degree of constraint imposed by formal EUC policy is also predicted by a linear model that includes only the organization's bureaucracy score ( $F = 4.07$ ; $p = 0.048$ ; 1,63 df) and explains about 6% of the observed variance in the outcome measure. When viewed together, these results suggest that organizations which attempt to coordinate the behavior of organizational actors through the use of clearly stated rules and procedures are also most likely to implement formal policies to regulate EUC activities.

## Link 3: Relationship between EUC policy-related processes and content

Multiple regression models were also developed to test the relationships between process formality and both the number of areas covered and the degree of constraint imposed by formal EUC policy. Significant results were obtained when the number of areas covered was used as the dependent variable, however, the model included only one predictor variable: the existence of an EUC plan $F = 4.98; p = 0.029; 1.63 \text{ df}$ . This single process variable predicts slightly more than 7% of the variance in the number of areas addressed in the policy statements of responding firms. Thus, it appears that the EUC policy statements in organizations practicing formal EUC planning are somewhat more thorough, but not necessarily more restrictive, than the policy statements developed through less formalized processes. No significant linear model resulted when degree of constraint was used as the dependent variable.

## Link 4: Relationship between EUC policy-making processes and compliance

The extent to which organizational members actually comply with stated EUC policies was a variable of particular interest here, as the mere existence of policies relating to EUC may be irrelevant for control purposes if they are ignored. If we assume that firms implement specific policies and procedures in an attempt to limit EUC activities to a pre-specified range of acceptable choices, then the extent of compliance provides a crucial measure of policy effectiveness.

Stepwise regression of the process variables and the aggregate measure of compliance produced a model (F = 6.83; p = 0.011; 1,63 df) that included only one key process variable: the existence of a formal compliance monitoring mechanism. This single variable explains almost 10% of the variance in the compliance scores. Pearson's chi-square tests were used to determine whether there is a difference between firms that have a formal monitoring mechanism and those that do not, with respect to the level of reported compliance (full, partial, users ignore) for each of the 14 individual EUC policy areas. ## Link 5: Relationship between EUC policy content and compliance

A regression model that included aggregate measures of policy content (comprehensiveness and restrictiveness) was used to test the relationship between content and reported compliance. The model that resulted was significant and included both variables ( $F = 7.29$ ; $p < 0.002$ ; 2,64 df). Thus, comprehensiveness and restrictiveness together explain $19\%$ of the variance in compliance scores. This is somewhat surprising, given that the existence of a formal compliance monitoring mechanism was helpful in explaining slightly less than $10\%$ of the variance in compliance scores. This finding does, however, lend credence to March's assertion that many employees follow rules simply because they exist. The fact that more comprehensive and constraining policies were associated with higher compliance scores also suggests that guidelines regulating EUC may be considerably less effective than formal rules.

## Limitations and future research

Our research was an effort to begin developing and testing a model of EUC policy that considers the organizational antecedents of EUC policy as well as the effects of various aspects of policy and policy-making on compliance. The results, shown in Table 7, suggest that the model is useful in capturing some aspects of these relationships, but the limited amount of variance explained indicates a need for additional refinement of the model and further research.

This study also had several limitations which should be considered when interpreting the findings and planning future studies of EUC policy. First, although the items included on the survey instrument were largely drawn from published research, the exact wording of previously-used questions was generally not available. Furthermore, although the questionnaire was pretested, the convenience sample of pretest participants may not have been truly representative of the focal population, since several of the respondents were not the top MIS executives in their organizations. More thorough validation of the survey instrument is obviously in order. Second, reliance on a single informant in each firm may have biased the assessment of several key variables, most notably the level of interest in EUC and the extent of actual compliance with specific policy areas. Future studies may benefit from the inclusion of multiple respondents from each organization, including a representative sample of end users from different functional areas. Third, because many of the respondents chose to remain anonymous, non-response bias was not evaluated. Possibly many of the nonrespondents work in organizations where overall interest in EUC is currently low. Fourth, our organizational context variables included only internal characteristics of the firm. It is possible that key variables relating to the external environment (e.g., competitive strategy, level of industry rivalry, environmental uncertainty) also have an effect on the development of EUC policy. Future research efforts might include environmental characteristics, such as industry competitiveness, that could have an impact on EUC policy. Finally, this study considered the relationship between the existence of formal compliance monitoring mechanisms and compliance, as judged by the top MIS executive. No attempt was made to identify the specific monitoring activities that are most effective in gaining user compliance. The results of our analyses on Link 4 seem to suggest that this would be a particularly fruitful avenue to investigate.

## Summary and implications

As the EUC phenomenon gains momentum, many organizations have responded by implementing policies and procedures to control user-directed computing activities. Previous research has identified a variety of EUC concerns that can be addressed through written policies, but has provided little information about the results achieved. The objective of this investigation was to examine EUC policies in more depth by developing a model that relates organizational context to both the process involved in developing and administering policy and the content of resulting policies. In addition, the research framework extends existing conceptualizations of the EUC policy problem by examining the relationships between both process and content variables and the extent of organizational compliance with EUC policies. Contingency models such as ours are important to the development of theory in this area and can be useful in helping organizations understand and manage EUC activities.

This study provides partial support for the research model. Collectively, the organizational context variables proved to be reasonably good predictors of process formality, suggesting that many organizations are extending their existing planning and control processes to encompass the emerging EUC phenomenon. Although organizational context did not appear to be strongly associated with the policy content measures, the degree of bureaucratization did help to explain a small amount of the observed variation in both comprehensiveness and restrictiveness. In organizations where task responsibilities and decision making authority are clearly defined and recurring activities are proceduralized, EUC activities appear to be governed by more comprehensive, rule-oriented policies.

Organizational context has not been viewed as a key variable in previous EUC research. However, our results suggest that decisions about how to manage and control EUC are not made in isolation. Rather, they are influenced by the system of controls surrounding other aspects of an organization's operations. If this is the case, our understanding of the best way to manage EUC activities must necessarily take into account the context in which these activities occur.

Examination of the link between formal policy development processes and comprehensive/restrictive policy statements indicated that firms that engage in EUC planning develop more comprehensive, but not necessarily more restrictive, EUC policy statements. Thus, it appears that synoptic-formal planning activities may provide policy-makers with a more complete picture of the requisite policy areas to address, but they do not by definition result in overly-restrictive rules that discourage creativity and innovation.

Perhaps the most interesting results were the findings relating to organizational compliance. Compliance was chosen as the critical outcome measure because it is key to organizational effectiveness. Through formal policies, the actions necessary to achieve the goals and objectives of the organization are communicated to employees. Thus, the effectiveness of the organization is ultimately dependent on the action that is obtained, or the extent to which employees actually comply with stated policies.

Compliance with EUC policies was reported to be higher in companies with more comprehensive and restrictive policies, however, formal compliance monitoring was the only process-related variable that was helpful in predicting compliance scores. Formal compliance monitoring was associated with significantly higher compliance scores for five of the fourteen specific policy items, including restrictions on the type/brand of hardware purchased, requirements for MIS approval of hardware acquisitions, hardware vendor restrictions, documentation requirements and post-development audit requirements. For the nine remaining policy items, the MIS executives in this sample reported varying degrees of compliance, and compliance in these areas appears to be unrelated to monitoring activities.

Two important conclusions can be drawn from the compliance-related results: First, it appears that many employees will comply with stated policies and procedures simply because they have been asked to do so. Further research is needed to determine whether such rule-following behavior is an individual trait or a result of policies and procedures that are well-integrated into existing organizational control systems. Second, there is evidence to support the assertion that visible compliance monitoring activities can contribute substantially to the level of compliance. Compliance monitoring is an aspect of the control process that has received far too little attention in the EUC literature, but it is our hope that this preliminary investigation will stimulate others to examine this aspect of control in more depth.
