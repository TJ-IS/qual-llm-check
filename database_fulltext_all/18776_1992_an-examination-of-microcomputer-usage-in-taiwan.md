# An examination of microcomputer usage in Taiwan

Magid Igbaria \*

Department of Management, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

This study reports the results of a survey on the use of microcomputers in Taiwan. Eighty five users participated in this study. Microcomputer usage was represented by four indicators: frequency of use, time of use, number of business tasks, and number of software applications. The frequency of each of the indicators were shown and the relationships between the four indicators and demographic variables, computer experience, and user training were examined. We also examined the relationships between organizational support, task characteristics, and beliefs about microcomputers, and the four indicators of microcomputer usage. Results show that a significant positive correlation exists between computer experience, user training, management support, and attitudes and negative correlations between task complexity and all the four indicators of microcomputer usage. Results also show that information center support has a strong relationship with number of tasks and that user satisfaction is highly correlated with number of applications, frequency of use, and time of use.

Keywords: Microcomputer usage, Taiwan, Background factors.

## Introduction

The phenomenal growth in end-user computing represents a significant development in the management information systems (MIS) field in the 1980's (Benson, 1983; Guimaraes and Ramanujam, 1986; Igbaria et al., 1989). This phenomenon is driven by many forces: sharp decreases in the price of microcomputers, an increase in the power and simplicity of new software applications, the availability and capability of communication networks, and the MIS department's traditional systems development backlog. The combination of these and other forces has accelerated the proliferation of microcomputers: the major avenues of growth in end-user computing. Today, the microcomputer is being hailed as the driver of a revolution that will profoundly change the nature of professional work (Curley and Pyburn, 1983; Igbaria, 1990). Business professionals are increasingly using microcomputer based applications as viable alternatives to mini and mainframe systems. It is reported that the majority of organizations will continue to increase their microcomputer expenditures and it has been estimated that microcomputer shipments will increase steadily in the next few years, with microcomputers bringing in the most cash (Business Week, 1990; Computer World, 1990). The expanding role of microcomputers as components of departmental or corporate-wide data processing and decision support systems is another important reason for gaining a better understanding of usage levels and factors affecting it.

Microcomputers have also had worldwide impact. The economic development of the South Asian countries have forced entrepreneurs to give more emphasis to using computers in their offices in order to boost the speed and efficiency of operations as businesses grow. The GNP of Taiwan has grown 9.3% annually over the past decade and computer expenditure has recently grown by 10% (Wang, 1989). However, despite the increasing importance of the international computer industry, very few studies examining the usage of microcomputer applications in South East Asia have been reported.

The growth in the number of computer installations in Taiwan (from 646 in 1980 to 7963 in 1989) represents a significant development of computer-based information systems in Taiwan. Microcomputers make up an increasingly important component of the computing resources in Taiwan: it was estimated that private organizations own 61.5% of the total installed microcomputers and 48.3% of the minicomputer sets. Generally, the private sector has been more microcomputer oriented, while the public sector has been largely mainframe oriented (owning 40%).

This study reports the findings of a field study of microcomputer applications and identifies the current state of microcomputer usage in Taiwan; it also focuses on the relationships between microcomputer usage and demographic variables (e.g., age, gender and education), organizational level, user training, computer experience and knowledge, organizational support, and attitudes. Specifically, the objective of this study was to investigate microcomputer usage in Taiwan using indicators of microcomputer usage: (1) actual daily use of microcomputers; (2) frequency of use; (3)

the number of software packages used by the participants; and (4) the number of tasks for which the system was used.

## Methodology

## Sample and procedure

A questionnaire was distributed to managers and professionals who had microcomputers on their desks or had easy access to a microcomputer in their work place. The participants were drawn from a sample of 15 corporations in Taiwan. The number of employees in these corporation varied between 35 to 500; the corporation's capital ranged from \$200 thousand to \$20 million; and most of them were established in the last two decades (average age = 18.4 years, range = 8–32). The sample of 15 corporations was drawn from the trading industry, since this sector plays a major role in Taiwan's economy (25% of GNP).

A comprehensive survey was distributed through a contact person to each person who agreed to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and people were assured that their individual responses would be treated as confidential. Eighty six questionnaires were sent to the contact-persons and all questionnaires were completed and returned. The high response rate may be due to the fact that the top managers strongly encouraged participation, the survey was translated into the Chinese language, and the issue being examined was of current concern. Of the 86 participants, 37 were men and 49 were women. They held managerial and non-managerial positions in a wide range of functional areas, such as accounting, finance, marketing, general management, production and operation, information systems and research and development (R&D). Table 1 shows a profile of the participants.

## Operational measures of the study variables

System Usage: Based on several studies (Cheney and Dickson, 1982; Igbaria et al., 1989; Srinivasan, 1985), four indicators of microcomputer usage were included in this study: (1) actual daily use of the computer, which asked individuals to indicate the amount of time spent on the system per day; (2) frequency of use; (3) the number of packages used by the participants; and (4) the number of tasks the system is used for.

Following Lee (1986) individuals were asked to indicate the amount of time spent on the microcomputer per day, using a six-point scale ranging from “almost never” to “more than 3 hours per day”.

Frequency of use was proposed by Raymond (1985) for providing a perspective on use slightly different than time. Frequency of use was measured on a six point scale ranging from “less than once a month to “several times a day.”

In a microcomputer environment, users have a wide variety of packages to use. In such an environment, the number of different software packages can provide a good indication of overall usage and of the variety of tasks performed on the microcomputer. Respondents were asked to indicate which packages they use from a list of 10 generic packages (i.e., spreadsheet, data management, word processing, etc.). The number of packages used is another indicator of microcomputer usage. Last, the number of computer supported business tasks performed by the user is also an indicator of microcomputer usage. Eight tasks, such as making decisions, looking for trends, planning, and budgeting were defined and participants were asked to indicate whether they personally use the microcomputer to perform these tasks.

User Satisfaction was measured by a twelve-item scale developed by Doll and Torkzadeh (1988). This provides a measure of overall user satisfaction as well as an analysis of the degree of satisfaction in the way the computer applications meet the user's requirements for information content, accuracy, output, format, ease of use, and timeliness. Each item was measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from (1) almost never to (5) almost always. The mean of the 12 items was used as an overall index of user satisfaction. The alpha reliability coefficient of the 12-item scale was 0.92.

Attitudes toward Microcomputers: This measure assesses a person's attitudes toward hands-on usage of a microcomputer in accomplishing job-related tasks. The ten items used to construct the measure were adapted from prior research (Igbaria, 1990) with appropriate modifications to make them relevant to microcomputers. Individuals were asked to indicate the extent of their agreement or disagreement with each of the ten items on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. Sample items are: "Using a microcomputer could provide me with the information that would lead to better decisions," "Using a microcomputer improves my productivity on the job;" "Using a microcomputer can take up too much of my time in performing my tasks." The items were recoded such that high scores reflected favorable attitudes toward microcomputers. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) of the 10 items produced two factors with eigenvalues of one or higher that accounted for 61.3 percent of the total variance. "Perceived usefulness," accounted for 75.2 percent of the common variance and reflected its importance in the use of the system and its effect on individual attitudes toward microcomputers. "Perceived problems" explained 24.8 percent of the common variance and described problems and difficulties in using microcomputers due to hardware limitations and difficulty in accessing and using the system. However, since a second-order factor analysis revealed one global factor explained 72.4 percent of the variance, the 10 items were averaged to produce an overall index of attitudes toward microcomputers. The internal consistency reliability of the 10-item scale was 0.79.

Organizational Support: This measure incorporated two broad categories of support: application development, which is quite specific and includes the presence of an information center and availability of development assistance; and general, which includes top management encouragement, allocation of resources, and MIS staff support. A factor analysis of the 8 items was used to examine the existence of the two categories specified above. In this case, factor analysis was used to confirm rather than to discover factors (Nunnally, 1978). The factor analysis produced two factors with eigenvalues $\geq$ 1.0 that accounted for 65.1 percent of the total variance. The four items representing specific support were labeled “information center support” and the four items of general support were labeled “management support.” Both sets of factors are examined. The alpha reliability coefficients of the management support and information support were 0.84, and 0.77, respectively.

Task Uncertainty refers to the amount of structure and variety found in the job; i.e., the extent to which tasks are not “repetitive and routine” and have clearly defined procedures and methods (O’Reilly, 1982). Our previous experience in constructing a four-item task structure scale was used to operationalize this variable. Individuals were asked to indicate to what extent the task is repetitive, similar, varied, and unambiguous on a five-point Likert type scale ranging from (1) very little to (5) almost completely. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced two factors with eigenvalues $\geq$ 1 that accounted for 66.7% of the total variance. Two items describe to what extent the tasks are repetitive and routine, these were combined in a factor labeled task structure. The remaining two items, which describe to what extent the tasks are similar and routinized, were labeled task complexity.

Computer Experience and Training: These factors were assessed by asking respondents to indicate how long they had used different types of computer software, different computer languages, and developed computerized information systems. Participants who reported having experience in computer-based information systems were coded 1 and those who did not have experience in computer-based information systems were coded 0 (Igbaria and Nachman, 1990). The total number of categories in which respondents reported experience was used as an overall index of computer experience. Additionally, respondents were asked to report the degree of training received from four sources: college courses, vendor training, in-house training, and self-training. The items were anchored on a 4-point scale ranging from (1) none to (5) extremely extensive.

Single item questions were used to ascertain respondents' gender, education, organizational level, and age. Gender of respondents was coded (1) for men and (2) for women. Education consisted of three levels: (1) high school; (2) Bachelor's degree; and (3) graduate or professional degree. Age was measured in years. Level in the organizational hierarchy consisted of 2 tiers: (1) professional employee without supervisory responsibilities; and (4) management. Five divisions were used: marketing and sales, accounting and finance, computer and technical, general management, and production and operation. Since we selected a previously validated measures, it was not necessary to perform a field test of the instrument.

## Results and discussion

Descriptive analysis, crosstabulations, zero-order correlations, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine the microcomputer usage in Taiwan, and to test the factors affecting microcomputer usage and satisfaction.

Frequency of use: Participants used microcomputers very often. Figure 1C shows that more than 50 percent of the participants used the microcomputers several times a day and 94 percent used microcomputers at least a few times a week. Hence, the participants in this sample can be classified as “heavy users”.

Consistent with our expectation, the microcomputers were used more frequently by the technical division followed by production and operation and general management. We also found that management used microcomputers more frequently than professionals and that men used microcomputers more frequently than women. Interestingly, less educated people (high school) used microcomputers more often than the more educated participants. The less educated participants used microcomputers for a specific task: word processing.

Time of use: The frequencies of time of use is presented in Figure 1D. About 50 percent of the participants used microcomputers for two or more hours a day. These users can be classified as “heavy users”. It is interesting to note that while these users used the microcomputers more frequently than U.S. users, they used fewer packages for fewer tasks.

Across the functional areas, we found, as expected, the technical division used the microcomputers most, followed by accounting and finance, and general management. Participants are committed to use specific software packages very frequently and for a long periods of time, particularly in the accounting and the finance division, where they used spreadsheets and modeling systems several hours a week. The results also show that the non-managers used microcomputers more than managers. Also women used microcomputers more hours than men, mostly for word processing and spreadsheet applications. This, again, may be due to the women's role (secretaries).

Demographic characteristics and microcomputer usage

Microcomputer usage is determined by several factors, among them demographic characteristics. Education was found to be positively correlated with number of software packages and number of tasks (See Table 4). It appears that increased educational attainment may foster feelings of self-efficacy, that is, the belief that one can develop the skills necessary to use the microcomputers for different tasks and strengthen confidence in one's ability to master and use microcomputers.

Age was found to be negatively correlated only with time of use. The nonsignificant correlations between age and other usage dimensions are surprising and may be because the average age of the participants is relatively low. Gender was also found to have an effect on number of applications and the time of use. The results show that women used a fewer number of applications for a fewer number of hours. This is consistent with popular literature, since computers are widely perceived as belonging to the “male domain” of mathematics, electronics, and machinery (Kiesler et al., 1983). Since the female percentage of the master’s program in Computer Science has been increasing (Eerkes, 1991), this perception may be changed. Finally, organizational level was found to have no significant effects on microcomputer usage.

## Computer experience and user training and microcomputer usage

Computer experience and user training have been found to have strong effects on microcomputer usage (Cheney, Mann and Amoroso, 1986; Fuerst and Cheney). The importance of prior computer experience in promoting increased microcomputer usage was highlighted by the finding that computer experience is positively and strongly correlated with all the four dimensions. We also found that most of the participants had an experience only with software packages (84%) and third generation languages (44%). Fourth generation languages were found to be less popular; only 3.5 percent of the participants had used them. Organizations may need to provide educational opportunities to increase use of fourth generation languages.

The importance of user training has long been proposed as a critical component of MIS success, in general, and for microcomputer usage, in particular. It is interesting to find that 94% of the participants received training through self-study, 81% received training in college, 74% were trained in house, and lastly 64% were trained by the vendor.

## Organizational support and microcomputer usage

Organizational support was found to incorporate two broad categories: (1) management and (2) information center. This emphasizes the need to coordinate top management blessings with the efforts of the staff to provide training, information, and consulting on data access, system development, and operations. Top management support is positively correlated with all the four dimensions of microcomputer usage, whereas the information center support is positively correlated with number of tasks only. Note that the average score of the information center (2.80) is low compared to top management support (3.35). Organizations should create a supportive environment that is responsive to user concerns and needs and work collaboratively with individuals in utilizing new software packages (Gupta et al., 1989).

## Task characteristics and microcomputer usage

Task characteristics were also examined. They were found to be represented by two separate components: task complexity and structure. Business non-managers and managers show a tendency to use microcomputers for decisions that are relatively more complex and require more information. The data shows that task complexity influences all the four dimensions of microcomputer usage and that task structure promotes the acquisition of a wider variety of software packages and enhances user application of microcomputers to a wider variety of business tasks.

## Beliefs and microcomputer usage

Two variables were included: user satisfaction and attitudes toward microcomputers. Beliefs had found to have a strong effect on actual usage of microcomputers. This is based on Fishbein and Ajzen's (1975) model which post that beliefs about an object influence reactions (usage). User satisfaction was found to be positively correlated with a number of applications, frequency of use and time of use. Our results in Taiwan are consistent with the findings of Baroudi et al. (1986) that user satisfaction leads to usage. Furthermore, attitudes toward microcomputers were also found to be positively correlated with number of applications, number of tasks, time of use, and frequency of use. These results show that belief has an important role on whether or not users apply microcomputers to a variety of business tasks, use different type of software packages, and develop a computing infrastructure (usage). Mechanisms to prove training and information are most useful in this case. Other important factors are organizational support and computer experience.

## Summary and conclusion

The results of our study provide information about microcomputer usage in Taiwan. That, in turn, provides the basis for several conclusions and recommendations for the management of microcomputers in organizations and their usage. In several cases, we compare these results with a similar study conducted by us in North America.

Participants from Taiwan used fewer software applications for a longer period of time than the U.S. users. This may be due to the price of software packages in Taiwan, the availability of hardware and software and differences in the two cultures (Taiwan users may perceive the availability of software packages as an opportunity to use them more intensively and not to “play” with different applications). Furthermore, it is interesting to find that the percentage of the participants who used spreadsheets (61.2%) is less than that of the U.S. users (94%). This may be due to the popularity of the spreadsheet packages, in the U.S. companies and academic institutions and their low price in the U.S. market. The results also show that communication packages are not heavily used in Taiwan (8.2% comparing to 52% in the U.S.) The size of the country, the telephone system and cultural differences may contribute to these differences. Organizations should try to provide communication packages) to the users or install local area networks and encourage them to use them.

There are several factors within the control of organization that were found to influence microcomputer usage. Organizational support plays an important role in encouraging users in Taiwan. Organizational support incorporated two dimensions: top management and information center (IC). The data shows that top management support is the more important of the two. In terms of number of tasks, IC support seem to have more effect. Both are influential in helping users build a computing infrastructure by developing a wider selection of software tools potentially useful in the future, and in applying computer technology to support a wider variety of business tasks. IC support was found to be low, which indicates the non-existence of information centers in Taiwan and the fact that they may be a few years behind. They should try to create information centers to support and encourage the users using a variety of software packages for different business tasks.

The results also emphasize the importance of training programs. The percentage of the people self-trained is much higher among those participants than U.S. users. However, a strong correlation between training and microcomputer usage indicates that a lack of training could result in stagnation and waste of company resources: organizations must establish training programs. Such training can be provided by the organization or offered at the college level. Newsletters or short presentations can also help. Such training could avoid the dysfunctional and nonproductive effects occurring naturally in the diffusion process, specifically at the introduction of new software packages and new technology.

Microcomputers were used more often for tasks that are relatively more complex and unstructured. These results have some implications: while one should recognize that there are many menial, repetitive, and straightforward tasks which have benefited from computerization, organizations should prioritize the distribution of microcomputers to business non-managers and managers who deal with business tasks that are highly complex and unstructured.

Finally, organizations should try to improve the user perception about microcomputer systems, because beliefs were found to have a greater influence on microcomputer usage.
