# Facilitating the adoption of information technology in a developing country

Ugur Yavas

East Tennessee State University, Johnson City, TN 37614-0002, USA

Mushtaq Luqmani and Zahir A. Quraeshi

Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, MI 49008, USA

Many developing countries are ardently seeking ways to encourage greater computer usage. But results have been disappointing. The slow diffusion is partially attributed to social factors. This study, based on a survey of 187 professionals, examined the efficacy of opinion leadership approach in accelerating the acceptance of computers in Saudi Arabia. Findings indicate that the approach has promise. Opinion leaders, who express greater satisfaction with computers, can be crucial as change agents in helping to reduce anxieties and dissonant feelings towards computers. Marketing strategy implications of the findings are discussed.

Keywords: Diffusion of computers, Opinion leadership, Saudi Arabia, Developing countries, Computer adoption, Diffusion of innovations, Computer marketing

## 1. Introduction

Over the past twenty-five years, computers, more than any other innovation, can be credited with bringing about tremendous improvement in the performance of an organization's operational, tactical, and strategic activities [17]. Globally recognized as symbols of power, computers in developing countries are considered the Western technology most needed for rapid economic development [11,13,20,29,34]. Not surprisingly, authorities in many third world countries have embraced computers and attendant information technologies with unguarded enthusiasm. This is nowhere truer than Saudi Arabia, where this study was undertaken.

In the Kingdom, this initial drive towards computerization was therefore led by the public sector, which is still the most important end-user segment representing approximately 50 percent of the Saudi Arabian market [8]. The installation of mainframe computers in public sector organizations has been paralleled by a surge in the acquisition of mini and micro computers in the private sector [4]. In the early 1980s, informed observers of the Saudi computer scene were predicting that computers would be as widespread as calculators by the end of the decade [32]. Recent studies, however, not only prove these optimistic predictions wrong but also raise questions about the future diffusion of computers in the Kingdom. The problems appear to be two-fold. On one hand, utilization of computers among the current owners is low [8]; on the other, in some companies there is strong opposition to the concept of using computers [1] and at present, many Saudi organizations are still without any end user computing activities [2].

The lackluster success of the diffusion of computer technology in Saudi Arabia, as in other developing countries $[22]$ , can be partially attributed to social factors $[25]$ . Computers are highly discontinuous innovations creating a great degree of change for the user. They are not compatible with existing procedures, practices, or work habits. While computers offer a strong performance-to-price value compared with manual practice, most users have not been made aware of this or are unwilling to accept it. Such problems are compounded by fear of and alienation from computers; such feelings are rampant among some Saudi executives.

The high-context culture of Saudi Arabia appears to be another social barrier to widespread adoption and use of computers at both the intra- and inter-organizational levels. In high-context cultures information is implicit in the physical context or is internalized within the people. Establishing personal communication within an organization and among organizations is the dominant mode of operation. This is considered to be the key to organizational success. Computers undoubtedly reduce the need for personal contact and thus may be viewed as threatening the very existence of the organization. Thus, it appears that, unlike other developing countries where lack of financial resources hamper rapid adoption of computers [15,21], in Saudi Arabia, which is blessed with vast financial resources, social factors serve as primary impediments.

With this in mind, this study investigated the feasibility of utilizing interpersonal communication to facilitate the acceptance of computers in Saudi Arabia. A study addressing this issue is relevant and significant. On the theoretical side, there has been a growing interest in understanding the diffusion of innovations framework to predict and influence the adoption rate of new products, brands, ideas and practices $[14]$ . A common thread that runs through the contemporary behavioral writings is that dissemination of information via interpersonal communications has a major impact on an individual's adoption decision $[31]$ . This is because individuals associate varying degrees of risk with innovation and seek information capable of reducing that risk. It is claimed that communicating to the potential adopters through an opinion leader (a form of interpersonal communication) enhances the effectiveness of the message $[18]$ . Despite the attention paid to opinion leadership as a mechanism for facilitating the adoption of an innovation, its role in achieving this end with respect to diffusion of computers is overlooked.

On the practical side, the study findings may yield invaluable insights for scores of computer system marketers in Saudi Arabia. Being able to identify the characteristics of the opinion leaders, trend setters who accept the risks and uncertainty associated with these new products, they can design pointed strategies to better appeal to the Saudi market.

## 2. Method

## 2.1 Data gathering

Performing survey research in Saudi Arabia poses several unique problems. There is no official census of the population. Lists that can serve as sampling frames do not exist. Telephone directories tend to be incomplete and out of date. Mail service is unreliable. Because of these and other acute problems, use of biased samples becomes a necessary evil [35]. This study is no exception to other surveys conducted in Saudi Arabia [6,39] in employing a nonprobabilistic sample. Data for the study were collected by Saudi graduate students via personal interviews with the senior managers of various organizations located in the tri-cities of Dammam, Dhahran, and Al-Khobar. Of the 250 organizations contacted, usable responses were obtained from 187 people resulting in a response rate of 74.8%. This response rate compares favorably with other studies undertaken among businessmen and public and private sector managers in Saudi Arabia [3,5,7,10,33,36].

The questionnaire was written and administered in English. The use of English was deemed appropriate for two reasons. First, English is the business language in the Kingdom [23]. Second, many of the managerial posts in the country are held by expatriates and Saudis who have risen to managerial rank have a good command of English [9,39]. Use of English-language questionnaire eliminated the cross-linguistic equivalence problem associated with questionnaire translation [16].

## 2.2 Operationalization of opinion leadership

In measuring computer related opinion leadership, King and Summers' self-designating method and scale [24] were adapted for use. This particular method and scale were preferred over other sociometric and key informant procedures for several reasons. First, the self-designated technique measures a respondent's own perception; this is what actually affects the person's behavior. Second, the scale consists of a parsimonious set of seven straight-forward questions making it easy to administer. This is important, because Saudis not accustomed to survey research are reluctant to participate in lengthy studies. Third, the scale has been used and tested for its reliability in previous studies not only in the United States [30,37] where it was originally developed but also in other countries including Saudi Arabia [38,40]. Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient [28] computed on the data of this study was 0.80, further indicating that the scale demonstrates satisfactory reliability [27].

The opinion leadership score of a respondent was derived by adding the respondent's answers to seven items in an unweighted linear fashion following score reversals for four items. The mean and standard deviation values for the opinion leadership scores of the survey sample were 12.18 and 2.61 respectively. In dividing the respondents into dichotomous categories of opinion leaders and opinion recipients, opinion leadership scores are first rank ordered and then the upper 23 to 30 percent of the respondents are classified as opinion leaders [19]. After arraying the respondents' opinion leadership scores, about the first quartile of the respondents were labeled as opinion leaders and the remainder were classified as opinion recipients. This classification system resulted in 42 opinion leaders and 145 opinion recipients.

## 3.2 Organizational characteristics

To gain insights into the types of organizations where opinion leaders and recipients were more likely to be found, data were collected on six company-related characteristics. As shown in Table 2, distinct differences between the two groups surfaced on computer ownership, the formation of a data processing department, and the size of the company. Computer ownership was much more likely in companies with opinion leaders, and these companies were much more likely to have data processing departments. While both opinion leaders and recipients tended to work for smaller organizations, relatively higher percentages of opinion leaders were found in medium and large sized companies.

No significant differences emerged when opinions leaders and recipients were compared along other organizational characteristics, including national origin, date the company was established, and the type of industry.

## 3.3 Computer involvement and attitudes towards computers

Are opinion leaders and recipients equally involved with computers? Do they differ in their attitudes and opinions regarding computers? Some insights into these decidedly critical questions can be gleaned from the data summarized in Table 3. It appears that while both groups agree that computers may have wide applications, they find greater use in the opinion leaders' companies. The relatively low application level among the opinion recipients' companies may be partly due to the greater resistance exhibited towards computers in those companies. Additionally, the evidence suggests that a significantly greater proportion of opinion leaders are employed in companies that develop their own software. The greater degree of computer involvement among opinion leaders is also reflected in their relatively more favorable attitude toward certain aspects of computer use. For instance, leaders feel that computers are easy to learn and operate.

Regarding maintenance costs and security problems, opinion leaders and recipients essentially hold similar opinions. As shown in the table, about one-third of both opinion leaders and recipients find computer maintenance costs too high and also perceive security problems in computer operations.

## 3.4 Satisfaction

Respondents were finally asked to indicate, on a three-point satisfaction-dissatisfaction scale, their feelings about various dimensions of computers. Because of the ordinal nature of the data, differences between the opinion leader and recipient groups were analyzed via the non-parametric Mann-Whitney-U test. An examination of the results presented in Table 4 reveals that both groups were generally satisfied with the computer and available software in English. Some degree of dissatisfaction was expressed by both groups regarding the Arabic software. The opinion leaders and recipients, however, were significantly different in their satisfaction with the value of computers in improving managerial effectiveness. Clearly reflecting their greater involvement, opinion leaders believed that computers were instrumental in improving the effectiveness of their organization's managerial practices.

## 4. Discussion

Since the publication of a seminal report by the United Nations in 1971 stressing the critical role of computers in economic development, the slow progress of the diffusion of computers in developing countries has been widely discussed. The purpose of this study was to explore the feasibility of applying an opinion leadership approach in improving the adoption of computers in Saudi Arabia. The results of the study raise some hopes in the usefulness of the opinion leadership approach. First, it appears that opinion leaders and recipients are similar in terms of some demographic characteristics. This is important, since people with similar characteristics tend to interact more often, engaging in subject-matter related conversations $[26]$ . Second, opinion leaders are more involved with computers. Third, they are satisfied with the contributions of computers in bringing about improvement in the conduct of managerial activities in their organizations. These findings suggest that the expert credentials of opinion leaders enhance their credibility. When opinion recipients turn to leaders for advice and information, they usually do so because of their belief that opinion leaders are knowledgeable about the subject-matter. The fact that opinion leaders are satisfied with computers implies that when they disseminate information about computers, the word-of-mouth communications will be favorable.

Within this framework, some guidelines can be offered when putting opinion leadership into use. First, there is a need to stimulate word-of-mouth communication among the leaders and the recipients. This can be achieved by mass media advertising aimed at opinion leaders. Currently, advertisements by computer marketers in Saudi Arabia provide information only about the machines themselves. Although this is important, messages generally ignore the benefits of the utilization of computers and do not encourage people to talk about their experiences. Therefore, at least some of the messages in the media should be designed to prompt opinion leaders to talk to their peers. Word-of-mouth communication can also be stimulated by advertising designed to prompt opinion seeking through messages such as: “Ask your colleagues about computers.”

Campaigns sponsored by computer marketers could also use themes that simulate the opinion leadership process by developing advertisements that are high in conversational value. For instance, ads could feature scenes in which one person tells another about some aspect of computers and the two then discuss its value. Opinion leadership could also be simulated through a testimonial approach wherein someone who has recently purchased computers conveys the importance of that experience to others. Ads, additionally, could feature testimonials by prominent business people.

Such media based efforts can be complemented by training seminars that are currently offered by computer marketers and vendors in Saudi Arabia. In these, besides “teaching” how to use computers, seminar organizers should devote some time to discussion of case histories of successful firms: these may demonstrate benefits of change and be helpful in dealing with the inertia exhibited by those who do not make good use of computers. If managers are expected to change, they must be made aware of both the need to change and the rewards associated with such change. Additionally, seminar participants should be encouraged to share their experience with colleagues in their and other organizations.

Finally, it should be noted that invoking interpersonal communication to accelerate the adoption of computers in developing countries should not be viewed as a panacea. However, it certainly represents a step in the right direction. This study is a beginning, and it is hoped that it will lead to future investigations, but until then the conclusions must remain tentative.
