# Communication technologies and collaboration systems $^{1}$ Common domains, problems and solutions

Joline Morrison

University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA 55242, USA

Olivia R. Liu Sheng

University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

One of the main purposes of office communication is collaboration. Much is known about established office communication technologies such as electronic mail, videoconferencing, and computer conferencing. Collaboration systems such as group decision support systems (GDSSs) and computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) are a more recent phenomenon. The communication domains of collaborative systems can be shown to be similar to those of existing communication technologies. If the functions and strengths of these communication technologies are isolated and the communication requirements of collaborative systems are also identified, common goals and problems emerge. Knowledge about the underlying communication technologies can then be used to recommend improvements to existing collaboration systems. Emerging communication technologies suggest future collaboration system directions.

Keywords: Communication technologies, Collaboration systems, Emerging technologies

## Review of communication technologies

Three popular communication technologies will be reviewed to explain how these technologies can potentially support collaborative systems and identify knowledge that may be applicable to collaborative systems.

## Electronic Mail

Electronic Mail (e-mail) systems use computer text-processing and communication tools to allow high-speed information exchange. A complete email system should allow users to send and receive information in both soft- and hard-copy format, file incoming and outgoing messages, and browse incoming messages by sender name and subject. Other desirable features include interactive communication when both sender and receiver are on-line, the ability for senders to determine if their messages have been read, and features to provide on-line user directories, distribution lists, and multiple-addressing [30]. E-mail implementations and significant research are summarized in Figure 1.

## E-Mail implementations

The first large e-mail network was the ARPANET, established in 1966 by the Department of Defense. By 1986, it had hundreds of thousands of users at over 1300 locations. Electronic mail systems appeared in the corporate world during the late 1970's, and became widespread during the early 1980's. The Honeywell Technalysis survey in 1984 reported that 40% of managerial and professional employees surveyed had e-mail access.

Public mail systems that can be accessed via a telephone line and modem came into existence during the early 1980's. Examples include The Source, MCI Mail, and CompuServe. Cost-free communications between universities began in 1981 with the first BITNET link between Yale University and the City University of New York [26]. This was followed in 1983 by the Canadian NORTHNET and the European EARN (European Academic and Research Network).

An experimental system that allows users to create, edit, send, and receive multimedia images containing text and voice messages as well as graphic images is under development at the University of Southern California $[39]$ . A facsimile machine is used to convert off-line sources to data files or bit-mapped files at varying degrees of quality; these files are then converted to a format that may be transmitted over the network. Playing or recording voice data is done through a connection to a voice server. Voice data may be edited to adjust qualities such as volume and pitch.

## E-Mail research

A 1978 pilot study at Digital Equipment Corporation observed the use of a mail service by a diverse group of employees [6]. Very favorable results were reported; users were impressed with the speed and effectiveness of the system for broadcasting information, processing task assignments, and communicating status reports and information requests. A majority of users reported a 5–15% increase in productivity. Managers disliked the depersonalization of e-mail communication but appreciated the time savings resulting from its use for scheduling and task-delegation.

A 1985 study by an agency within the Canadian government observed one-hundred managers and professionals using a system that provided e-mail along with other automation features. Managers reported the most satisfaction with e-mail, mostly through time savings by elimination of telephone tag, ease of multiple messaging, and fewer telephone interruptions. Satisfaction among professional employees was not as pronounced, although they reported quicker managerial approval on documents. Studies at General Motors [16] and in the wholesale logistics arm of the U.S. Army [38] also reported increases in organizational efficiency, effective ness, and productivity after e-mail facilities were made available.

A field study of use of an electronic message system in a Fortune 500 company in the mid-1980's reported that while e-mail increased the amount and speed of information exchange within an organization, it also changed information distribution patterns. A reported 60% of all messages received contained information that the receiver believed would not have been obtained without e-mail. Individuals were also observed to be more likely to send e-mail messages to their managers than to their subordinates; it was hypothesized that this was because e-mail communication gave senders a feeling of equality and most preferred peer status with their managers rather than their secretaries. This observation interleaves with the observation of increased information, because managers tended to get information via e-mail that they otherwise would not have received. Other observations included: users preferred to deliver bad news electronically, and good news face-to-face; more socially-undesirable behavior (profanity, negative affects) was seen in e-mail than verbal communication; and, workers used e-mail more than verbal communication for non-work-related messages.

Another line of research addresses the question of whether the additional information made available through e-mail is a liability rather than an asset. In an editorial condemning “junk electronic mail,” Denning [7] suggested the need for restricted mailbox addresses, content filters, and the ability to specify who can (and cannot) send mail. Hiltz and Turoff [23] disagreed, arguing that restricting communication flow may destroy one of the greatest benefits of electronic communication: the opportunity to communicate. They believed that the problem should be alleviated through system organization and filtering tools. Examples of research in this area are Project LENS at MIT [32] and AIMail at the University of Arizona [35].

## Project LENS

A series of interviews with thirty e-mail users identified three possible mail filtering approaches: (1) cognitive, matching contents of messages with information needs of recipients through keyword-search techniques; (2) social, matching senders and receivers through social anti organizational relationships; and (3) economic, allowing a cost/benefit analysis of sending and receiving messages. Personalized messages are considered to have a high “cost” to the sender (due to time required to compose them); long messages are considered to have a high “cost” to the receiver (due to time required to read them).

To explore these approaches, a prototype system called Information Lens was developed. Its primary component is a semi-structured message template for specific message types. For example, for meeting scheduling, specific information fields such as time, place, and participants would be included, with access to a database to obtain other information. A set of rules may be included for processing the information. Templates are constructed in a hierarchical form with inheritance; for example, “LENS Meeting Announcement” would be a child of “Meeting Announcement”. It would inherit all of the fields of its parent, plus could have other fields specific to its application.

The LENS system also allows users to build their own production-system rules for finding, filtering, and sorting messages. Possible problems with this approach include the danger of excessive filtering and loss of information, and the problem of “imperfect finding” – the case where individuals do not know what they are looking for until they find it.

In one study [31], twenty-three “very active” e-mail users in a research lab were interviewed to discover their perceptions of mail use before the LENS filtering system was installed. Following interviews and examinations of mail folders, it was observed that users seemed to fall into three categories: “OK” (managing their mail successfully, reading as much as they want, and able to find messages when they need them); “overwhelmed” (having serious problems managing their mail, not reading as much as they want, and unable to find messages); and, “on the edge” (having some success, but not completely satisfied).

The “OK” users had the following common traits: (1) not trying to read all of their mail; (2) making an effort to remove themselves from voluntary distribution lists, and (3) keeping their inboxes and folders small. Conversely, the “overwhelmed” users tried to read all of their mail, kept hundreds of messages in their in-boxes (and never cleared them all out), wanted to save a lot of messages, and had a lot of folders. The “on the edge” users had the same traits as the “overwhelmed” users, except they had an intermediate number of mail folders. Interestingly, the actual volume of mail received did not seem to relate to the user’s category.

Users also fell into distinct categories regarding the type of help they wanted in managing their mail. “Prioritizers” were individuals to whom time management was very important; they desired organizing and filtering before mail was opened. “Archivers” perceived information gathering as an important facet of their job, and preferred to organize and filter themselves.

## AiMail

AiMail provides a framework, architecture, and prototype for a knowledge-based messaging system (KMS) that provides project coordination features, such as meeting scheduling, task management, document routing, and message dissemination and management within a messaging system coupled to a knowledge base/database. The knowledge base contains both user and organization knowledge. Each user can manipulate his or her own knowledge; the organizational knowledge can be accessed and modified by users with appropriate access privileges.

![](/api/attachments/K9W84X2P/fulltext/images/2a78ad7c7737a583c5c4f8719b981927ba20b680f09c1d6b5e0a582d040109fc.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Examples of videoconferencing implementations and research.

After personal and organizational knowledge is procured and entered into the database, the knowledge base structures this data into rules that are used by an inference engine in the KMS. Meeting scheduling is accomplished by querying the sender on the group name or meeting subject; the knowledge base generates a list of potential group members by querying the database for either their affiliations or personal interests. The KB then scans calendars, and proposes alternate meeting times. The sender may choose one or more times, and instruct the KMS to send a meeting announcement to all group members.

The KMS performs message management by ordering new incoming mail messages according to sender priority and subject priority as determined from organizational knowledge and user knowledge in the KB. As new messages are received, they are inserted in the user's mail box according to the priority the user has previously specified for the sender and subject. Therefore, high-priority messages are always at the top of the incoming mail, while “junk” messages tend to fall to the bottom.

A validation study of the message management feature was performed by asking groups of administrators, teaching faculty, and research faculty subjects to manually prioritize a list of ten incoming mail messages according to sender and subject header information. These manual lists were then compared to lists generated by the system, as prioritized by user and organizational knowledge. The system effectively prioritized the messages at a statistically significant level for all three groups.

## Videoconferencing

Videoconferencing allows groups at two (or more) geographically dispersed locations to conduct simultaneous interactive meetings with audio-visual support. An example of a modern two-way videoconferencing facility may consist of a room designed for 6-12 active participants and an “audience” of 12-24 [27]. The active participants sit around a conference table and are surveyed by a video camera. Microphones in front of the participants are voice-activated, as is the camera, which zooms in on the current speaker. A control panel, operated by the conference chairperson, can override the automatic controls. Other features may include wall monitors to show images of participants who are not speaking, and devices to send and receive blackboard drawings [21]. Sample implementations and research studies are summarized in Figure 2.

## Videoconferencing implementations

The concept of videoconferencing began during the 1950's at AT&T for broadcasting large corporate meetings [10]. The idea gained popularity during the 1970's as a result of rising business travel costs; corporations such as Boeing, Exxon, Xerox, 3M and IBM installed videoconferencing installations in the early 1980's when video digitizing and compressing techniques improved image quality and decreased transmission costs [37]. During this time period, public videoconferencing rooms for lease were established by hotel chains, such as Hilton, Holiday Inn, and Marriott. Such facilities are used primarily to allow decentralized individuals or groups to work together and for internal training. Videoconferencing has also been used for “coordination” of separate groups (enabling groups that are not working together directly to keep abreast of one another's activities) by NASA and American Airlines.

## Videoconferencing research

Studies conducted for Honeywell Technalysis in 1980 and by the Institute For The Future in 1982 indicated a lukewarm reception to videoconferencing by office workers. Other studies have concluded that people prefer to meet in person when possible, or otherwise use audio conferencing. Research has also indicated that access to videoconferencing facilities does not change travel plans. Despite early favorable forecasts, the total number of operational videoconferencing facilities was estimated to be only about one hundred in 1985. Possible reasons for this downturn may have been an original over-estimate of the number of potential users, and the question of whether a videoconference can really take the place of a face-to-face meeting. Studies have indicated that teleconferencing is best suited for “regularly occurring meetings aimed toward the presentation or exchange of neutral information between colleagues in different locations” [10]. It was reported that only 4% of all meetings fall into this category, thus implying a limited market. Also, videoconferencing cannot totally take the place of face-to-face meetings because the informal chats outside of meeting room are eliminated, and this is may be where important information is exchanged and real decisions are made.

On the positive side, a study sponsored by Satellite Business Systems in 1982 of videoconference users at ten U.S. firms reported that about half of all respondents believed that meeting effectiveness had increased as a result of video-conferencing due to better preparation and task orientation. Also, examples of successful applications were reported at Wang, Digital, and Kodak for training sales and technical personnel. Boeing officials believe the videoconferencing system between their airfield, engineering, and manufacturing facilities has enabled them to meet ambitious production schedules. J.C. Penney uses videoconferencing to allow junior executives to be included in executive meetings in spite of limited travel budgets.

## Computer conferencing

Computer conferencing allows communication between participants dispersed in space and time. It is usually accomplished through a terminal, modem, and telephone line. A conferencing system may support synchronous, on-line group interaction for completion of specific tasks [23], or an asynchronous bulletin-board that allows users to log on at their convenience, read the comments entered since their last session, and leave either community or private messages. Implementations and research are summarized in Figure 3.

## Computer conferencing implementations

The first computer conferencing system was created in 1970, and used a Delphi communication technique for examining policy options $[45]$ . Participants could enter only two types of comments: policy alternatives for the current issue and arguments stating reasons for accepting or rejecting the alternatives. Members could vote for accepting arguments at any time. The computer notified and introduced new comments, tabulated the votes, and informed group members when a consensus had been reached.

An early computer conferencing system was EMISARI, developed for crisis management at the Office of Emergency Preparedness in 1971. This system allowed a human monitor to assign a specific data item, such as a numeric estimate or an incomplete data table, to each participant. The participant could then enter new items or update the data; the computer accumulated the data in a database, and recorded who made the entries and when they were made. Messages and/or data items could also be exchanged between participants.

In 1977, the New Jersey Institute of Technology began operating the Electronic Information Exchange Service (EIES). This system allows individual messaging, group conferencing, personal notebooks, and special structures for specific needs. Paying customers include individuals from industry, government, and academia. An updated version of this system, TEIES, provides an object-oriented architecture and sophisticated database storage and retrieval features [46].

Numerous commercial systems and academic systems became available during the early 1980's. They include UNIX News/NotesFiles (for the UNIX operating system), the CONFER system at the University of Michigan, COM, a Swedish system widely used in Europe, PARTI (operating on The Source) [15], and CompuServe. An interesting system at Carnegie Mellon University is called Project Andrew [3]. Among many other features, the system allows transmission of graphic images, and supports “active messages” – messages which prompt a specific action from the reader. Its Advisor system is an electronic mail consulting service that allows users to submit specific queries. These queries are first routed to databases constructed from information circulated over the network of campus bulletin boards for an answer. If no answer is found, the question is then sent to the appropriate professional staff organization to be answered. Turn-around time is generally about 24-48 hours.

## Computer conferencing research

A review of the research on this topic through 1979 by Johansen, Vallee and Spangler indicated that, while some participants reported increased candor and flexibility through computer conferencing, others reported reluctance to make certain statements in writing. Some studies reported an increase in equality of participation over audio and face-to-face communication, and participants felt it beneficial to have a written record of communication. “Meetings” were reported to be easier to arrange, but were of longer duration.

A laboratory experiment comparing decision-making through synchronous computer-conferencing versus face-to-face (FTF), non-computer-supported communication indicated that FTF groups were more likely to reach a “total consensus” on problem solutions, dominant individuals were more likely to emerge, and communication was more intensive. The computerized groups showed more asking for and giving of opinions. Overall, the computerized groups used only about one-fourth of the number of words as the FTF groups in reaching a decision.

A fifteen-month field study in 1982 by Pieper observed a computer-conferencing system in West Germany consisting of fourteen individuals in five sub-groups. The emergence of a “gatekeeper” – an individual within each group who communicated with the other groups – was reported. More overall communication was seen in all sub-groups as a result of the system, but the distribution of this communication was not equal among the group members.

Hiltz reported in 1984 that the EIES system initially experienced a high user attrition rate, but the system eventually gained acceptance. An increase in total communication, reading of professional publications, and in social networks with other scientists was reported [22]. A study of the Swedish COM system by Palme in 1985 reported that users felt that 65% of the communications transmitted over the system would not have taken place otherwise.

In 1984 Hewlett-Packard installed a version of the CONFER system for use between multiple geographically-dispersed engineering design groups. Overall, the system was judged a success. An interesting result of their experience was the observation that the most important factor behind a successful computer conference was the activity level of the session organizer. Increased time spent on the system by the organizer resulted in increased time on the system by the participants, and high activity levels produced the most successful conferences.

A 1985 study on structuring conferencing systems reported that users of computer conferencing systems believed that flexibility and the ability to support communication were the greatest assets of the systems, while “junk messages” – not perceived as relevant or useful – were the greatest detractors. The paper proposed that systems should offer options for organizing and filtering messages according to the individual’s needs by such means as separate topic threads, allowing users to scan the subject and/or first few lines of a message and skipping it if not relevant, and imposing length limitations on all messages. Further, systems should obtain evaluation and feedback from their users.

## Review of collaborative systems

Dennis et al. [8] suggest that a GDSS is more communications-oriented (the end result is a completed task, plan of action or solution to a problem). In contrast, a CSCW is more task-oriented (the end result is a jointly-created document or design).

## Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS)

The basic components of a GDSS are a computer or terminal for each participant networked to the machines of the other participants, some sort of public viewing screen or public space on the individual screens, and support software and procedures to aid decision-making. Its purpose is to improve group decision-making by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for structuring decision analysis, and providing a structure for directing the pattern, timing, or content of a discussion $[9]$ . Sample implementations and research are summarized in Figure 4.

## GDSS implementations

Research using computers to aid group decision-making began in the late 1970's with interactive systems. The term “Group Decision Support System” was suggested during the 1980's to describe a system aiding groups in solving unstructured problems.

Sample implementations have been categorized according to participant proximity (centralized versus dispersed) and time frame of the dispersed groups network tools (synchronous versus asynchronous). Names for the configurations are listed in parentheses. Definitions of each category and descriptions of sample implementations and their communication forms are:

(1) Centralized / synchronous (decision room or collaboration laboratory). This configuration places all participants in a central location, like a boardroom, and combines face-to-face verbal communication with computer-supported presentation technology, DSS tools, or electronic group support tools, such as brainstorming or voting. Examples include:

Perceptronics group decision aid [43]. This early system aided construction of decision trees, and allowed users to input probability and utility values. Sensitivity analysis techniques identified the critical issues in the decision tree, and a conflict-resolution strategy attempted to isolate and thus resolve conflicts. The technology involved individual data-entry terminals and a front display screen. Communication support: text.

Colab. Colab is an experimental meeting room developed during the mid-1980's at Xerox PARC [44]. Designed for small groups consisting of two to six people, the room consists of a U-shaped table, networked workstations for all participants, and a large public display screen. All meeting participants see the same image on the public display screen and on the public portion of their individual screens. Private work space is also included on each workstation. Text and graphic image communication is available to participants through the public display screen or public portion on individual screens.

One Colab group-support tool is Argnoter, a task-specific tool used for presenting and evaluating research proposals. The first phase of the process involves proposing, or entering the proposal data – name, a short text description, and maybe a sketch – into the window set. Next, the arguing phase involves entering reasons for choosing or not choosing a specific proposal. Participants are encouraged to write something about all proposals; comments tend to be specific and focused, since they are in the public view. Proposals can be compared with auxiliary tables concerning compatibility, costs, etc., and can also be modified. Finally, during the evaluating phase, assumptions behind individual arguments are identified. From these, “belief sets” about various assumptions can be identified; they are intended to represent different generic points of view, such as liberal versus conservative, or marketing versus development. Next, evaluation criteria are entered and ranked. By explicitly stating these, it is hoped that consensus or compromise can be reached, and proposals can be ranked. Communication support: text, graphics.

Nick. The Nick meeting room at Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation (MCC) [11] supports face-to-face meetings with networked workstations with private and shared space, an electronic blackboard for recording and modifying information, and supporting experimental software. Communication support: text.

University of Arizona GroupSystems. Two facilities designed for large groups (over ten members) exist at the University of Arizona [8]. The software, Arizona GroupSystems, includes general-purpose tools for brainstorming, issue analysis, voting, stakeholder identification, etc. The facilities are used by both industrial and academic groups for planning, problem solving and collaborative group research; the software tools have been installed at several IBM corporate sites and at some universities. Communication support: text.

Other GDSS centralized/synchronous implementations exist at the University of Minnesota, Southern Methodist University, Georgia Institute of Technology, SUNY-Albany, and other sites. Most support text communication only.

(2) Distributed / synchronous (group network / computer conference / local area decision net /LADN]). Participants are physically dispersed, perhaps in their own offices at microcomputer workstations. They can communicate directly with other participants, either verbally through telephone links and/or through shared public displays at the workstation. Each workstation can have both public and private spaces, terminal linking, meeting scheduling, bit-map sharing, and shared applications. An example of this configuration is:

Co-oP. Designed for distributed users, Co-oP [4] uses a decision-modelling technique wherein the group collectively identifies the problem, the individual members evaluate alternatives, and a group consensus is reached through previously agreedupon weighting/aggregating criteria. Co-oP's authors believe that a successful distributed GDSS system must include a specific communications component, because in the distributed environment, the technology is the major means of communication. Communication support: text.

(3) Distributed / asynchronous. Examples of dedicated systems for distributed asynchronous group meeting support are rare, and such support is usually provided by computer conferencing systems.

## GDSS Research

Although Vogel and Nunamaker [47] suggest the applicability of multiple methodologies for GDSS investigation, most efforts to date have focused on laboratory and case/field studies. Dennis et al's survey [8] suggests that of ten studies done from 1981-1988, results are inconsistent, but the overall impression is that GDSS: (1) either has no effect on or does not improve decision quality; (2) either has no effect on or lengthens the time taken in decision making; (3) has no effect on or increases the equality of participation; (4) has inconsistent results on user satisfaction with the decision process. Experiments comparing decision speed, quality, and participant satisfaction of users on LADN's versus Decision Rooms were also inconclusive. One study [5] compared the use of the same GDSS system (Co-oP) in the group versus dispersed environment; more “correct” solutions (according to surveyed experts) and higher decision making speed were observed with the LADN, with no effect on satisfaction. Another experiment [17] compared groups solving the same task with and without GDSS-support in both dispersed and group environments. No effect was seen on decision quality; all of the GDSS groups took longer, and the dispersed non-GDSS group took longer than the centralized non-GDSS group. No satisfaction effect was seen from the use of the GDSS, but overall the dispersed groups were less satisfied.

The case and field studies showed markedly different results. Of five later case studies, all reported high levels of participant satisfaction and process effectiveness. Dennis et al. suggest that the differences between the experimental and case/field research may be explained by: (1)

the types of problem being considered (real-world, and important to the participants); (2) the types of group (actual working groups); and (3) group sizes (case/field groups were usually larger than the experimental groups).

Conversely, Pinsonneault and Kraemer's analysis [40] suggests that the GDSS results are consistent if the experiments are classified according to system technological support. Some systems support primarily group communication, while others target group decision-making.

## Computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW)

CSCW systems support organizational collaboration [14]. An example could be a conferencing system for collaborative work, such as calendar management and collaborative document editing [19]. Implementations and key research are presented in Figure 5.

## CSCW implementations

Like GDSS tools, CSCW systems can be classified according to the space and time frames of the participants: centralized or dispersed, and synchronous or asynchronous.

Fig. 5. Examples of CSCW implementations and research.

(1) Centralized / synchronous. Execucom. Execucom's planning laboratory [41] was an early example of a boardroom-style collaboration system. Executives were seated at a round table; video graphics terminals were available for each participant. There was also a large public screen. Each terminal could be used as a personal terminal or as a slave to the master screen. The personal mode could be used for sending and receiving messages, analyzing data, and examining private models, and the public mode could be used to display models and make additions, deletions, or modifications. Communication support: text, graphics.

Colab. Colab also has two CSCW tools: Broadnoter, which imitates a chalkboard and allows participants to electronically manipulate text and graphical images; and, Cognoter, used to prepare outlines for talks and papers. Cognoter begins with a brainstorming phase, allowing participants to insert free-form ideas anywhere on the public screen. The next step is for participants to link the ideas into subgroups and into presentation order, usually with a lot of verbal communication. Finally, the structure is reviewed to fill in details and eliminate irrelevant or redundant ideas. Communication support: text, graphics.

(2) Distributed / Synchronous. Bell Labs. A system was developed at Bell Laboratories during the late 1970's to support design and engineering of complex building plans by allowing a group of engineers to hold on-line design meetings simultaneously with a telephone conversation [42]. Similar design-type software became commercially-available in the early 1980's. Communication support: text, graphics.

MIT (RTCAL / MBLINK). RTCAL is a real-time calendar management/conferencing tool developed at the MIT Laboratory for Computer Science. It supports scheduling by displaying a filtered view of all participants' calendars on all workstations and allowing participants to view their complete calendars. A designated person may propose meeting times on the shared workspace, and participants may vote on the choice or suggest alternatives. Another interesting MIT tool is MBLINK, which allows a bitmapped image to be displayed at different workstations, and can track the position of each station's mouse to allow participants to point out different features within the image. Communication support: text, graphics.

System Concepts Laboratory (SCL). This system was established by Xerox PARC to provide an audio-video link between sites at Palo Alto and Portland, Oregon [18]. The link is used to study the effect of this technology on communication in both formal meetings and informal, unscheduled interactions between individuals or small groups. Communication form: voice, text, visual image, graphics.

GROVE. This real-time group text editor prototype [12] was developed at MCC to explore group interface issues involving group focus/distraction group dynamics, screen space management, and group protocols. When used in the distributed setting, users are advised of member participation through presentation of active members' photographs on the workstation screen. Communication form: text, visual image.

MERMAID. This is an experimental multi-media system developed to support geographically-distributed conferences [48]. The current prototype supports simultaneous individual and group-oriented tasks in a windowing environment. The system emphasizes communication through both audio and video channels as well as through text. Communication form: voice, text, visual image, graphics.

(3) Distributed / Asynchronous. NLS / AUGMENT. NLS, or On-Line System, was developed at the Augmentation Research Center (ARC) at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) in the mid-1960's; as development continued during the 1970's, the system was renamed AUGMENT [13]. This system was designed to support document creation for various types of knowledge workers. At a time when most computer users were using punch cards, the workstations were revolutionary, providing a typewriter-like keyboard, a five-finger keyset, and a new invention – a mouse for cursor control. Initially, screens were generated on CRT's in a central machine room and transmitted to participants via closed-circuit television. NLS files were structured with locks so that only one person could have write-access at a time, although other team members could read the previous file versions. Special tools were included for moving quickly through files. Programmers could modify portions of a file, test it in their own work environment, then place the tested file in the group environment.

Newer versions of the system allow on-line conferences, during which users can view and edit system files, pass control through a “gavel,” and jointly review and revise documents. All participants view a shared “blackboard,” and can manipulate an electronic “chalk and eraser.” Participants also have access to a private workspace. Communication support: text, graphics.

CES. This collaborative document editing system tool was developed at MIT to allow participants to work asynchronously on portions of documents. Authors of individual segments store their portions on their workstations, and a complete document outline is available at all workstations. Each portion of the document has a “lock,” so that two or more authors may not edit the same portion of the document simultaneously. Communication support: text.

Quilt. Quilt is a collaborative document production system developed at Bell Labs [29]. It provides structured configurations for annotating documents, either by text or voice. Revision suggestions, public comments, private comments, and other information can be represented as annotations; these annotations can be modified according to users preferences during the course of the project. Annotations can include various attributes, including creator and creator time. The system also provides activity monitoring, participant notification, and triggering mechanisms to notify group members when a deadline is approaching. An asynchronous computer conferencing feature is included to allow discussion of specific topics within a document; this feature provides direct access to the sections of the document that relate to the points being considered. Communication support: voice, text.

A variety of commercially-available systems appeared in the mid-to-late 1980's, including For-Comment (collaborative writing), Higgins (e-mail, scheduling, project tracking), The Coordinator (enhanced e-mail for work groups), Caucus (real-time conferencing), Office Works (computerized phone message slips, message handling, and calendar planning), and WordPerfect Office (calendaring integrated with other office applications) [36]. Most of these systems support text communication only, and are primarily aimed at e-mail and scheduling tasks. A notable exception is Lotus Notes, which concentrates on group data sharing and tracking [28].

## CSCW research

Little research can be found on the benefits or drawbacks of CSCW systems. This may be because CSCW is a relatively new area, and, as suggested by Grudin [20], it is difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of CSCW applications in the field. A 1987 case study of CSCW tools by Ehrlich reported a problem with electronic calendar systems that automatically schedule meetings during times when all group members are free: essentially, managers have no real free time. This leads to either rejection of such systems by managers, or control of the system by the manager (or his/her secretary). As a result, the manager benefits from the system, but other work-group members may resent the system because it forces them to maintain electronic personal calendars which they may not otherwise need or want to use. It was suggested that this problem may be overcome by either minimizing the extra work caused by the system or by designing systems to have features that will benefit all users.

Another study of a CSCW system installed at a hospital to aid nurses in sharing information reported that users liked the system and said it helped them, but they could not explain why $[1]$ . ## The role of existing communication technologies in collaborative systems

## Functions of communication technologies

Communication technologies support distributed users in different time relationships and communication forms. A framework to demonstrate this concept was constructed as follows: the time relationships and communication forms were placed on the horizontal axis, and the communication technologies were placed on the vertical axis. Time relationships are synchronous and asynchronous and communication forms include voice (speech only), text (on paper or a screen), visual image (the “hearer” can see the speaker), and graphic image (pictures, graphs, etc.). Each technology was rated according to the degree of support it offers in the time and form categories (Figure 6). The time frames and forms readily supported by many commercial applications are rated as having strong support, while those lessreadily supported or used by users of commercial applications are rated as having some support. Time frames and forms that are presently only supported by limited or experimental applications are rated as having weak support.

E-mail systems mainly support asynchronous communication of text, although some systems offer synchronous communication features, such as VAX Mail's interactive PHONE Utility. Voice and graphic image support is possible as illustrated by the University of Southern California's Multimedia System. Videoconferencing provides the only synchronous support of voice and visual images. The research done by Xerox PARC at SCL indicates that text and graphic image communication are also possible through video, but so far this has not been a primary use of the technology. Asynchronous communication through video has been used as a limited non-interactive teaching tool for corporate training.

Computer conferencing offers support identical to e-mail. This is because the underlying functionality of the two technologies is the same: the exchange of (usually) textual information between asynchronous users on a common computer system or local/wide area network. The classification difference has arisen due to the way the tools have been used: e-mail as a vehicle for communication about a specific topic among individuals or between an individual and a group, and computer conferencing as a medium for a broad exchange of ideas by more diverse groups. For the remainder of this paper these technologies will be combined and referred to as Electronic Messaging.

Communication support technologies for collaborative systems

A framework was constructed to determine how the communication technologies can support collaborative systems. The collaborative system space/time frames previously identified (centralized/synchronous, distributed/synchronous, and distributed/asynchronous) were placed on the horizontal axis and the communication mediums were placed on the vertical axis. The communication technologies were then mapped into this framework to suggest compatible communication technologies for each configuration; example implementations of the technology within the configuration are also included. The value of this is to identify how communication technologies are used or can be used in collaborative systems. We then consider if problems observed in the collaborative configurations are similar to those identified in the underlying communication technologies, and if similar solutions are applicable.

The framework (Figure 7) and problems/potential solutions are now discussed in terms of the collaborative system space/time configurations:

## Centralized / synchronous systems

Centralized/synchronous systems usually do not require voice and visual support because of close participant proximity. The Perceptronics, Colab, Nick, GroupSystems, and Execucom installations use electronic messaging for text communication for tasks such as idea and information exchange, conflict resolution/voting, and information recording: Colab and Execucom include graphic image support through electronic messaging for design work.

A disadvantage associated with centralized/synchronous GDSS systems is increased time to reach a decision or group consensus. This may in part be caused by a problem identified in e-mail and computer conferencing research: users are overwhelmed by large amounts of information, disorganized information, and “junk” comments. Although high volume and free flow of ideas is often the intent of collaborative sessions, information overload and the presence of distracting and inappropriate comments can be detrimental. Message filtering and organizing systems using artificial intelligence principles such as those being explored in e-mail and computer conferencing research should be considered in collaborative systems.

## Distributed / synchronous / asynchronous systems

The distributed categories have been grouped together for analysis because their communication technology support is identical, although their implementation examples are not. All communication forms are supported by videoconferencing in the SCL system. Digitized voice communication is supported through electronic messaging in the Quilt system. Text communication is supported through electronic messaging in the Co-oP, Bell Labs, MIT (RTCAL/MBLINK), NLS, CES, and Quilt systems; graphic image support is provided through electronic messaging in the Bell Labs, MIT (RTCAL/MBLINK), and NLS systems.

For distributed collaborative systems not requiring voice or visual imagery, electronic messaging will continue to be the primary communication technology. An aspect of electronic messaging research that may be applicable to collaborative systems is the importance of a strong group leader. Systems should be designed so session organizers can easily initiate sessions, keep track of group status and progress, and communicate with group members $[34]$ . This will be especially important in the asynchronous environment.

Videoconferencing appears to be the most versatile technology supporting distributed collaborative work, and the only technology supporting visual image communication. Although videoconferencing has had mixed success in the past, distributed collaborative work may be a successful application of this technology. Videoconferencing may also be a way to help further preserve group synergy and dynamics in GDSS and CSCW systems. Additionally, collaborative work provides the “single-office” setting shown preferable by the Xerox SCL System. Videoconferencing also has the advantage of being able to transmit all communication mediums.

## Emerging technologies and future collaborative systems

Emerging technologies promise to have a profound influence on future collaborative systems. Technologies that enable efficient transmission, storage, and retrieval of verbal, visual, and graphical images will enhance the value of electronic messaging; advances in video transmission technology will increase the viability of videoconferencing support. The following emerging communications technologies promise to play a profound role in future collaborative system development:

FAX. Advances in digitization and transmission techniques have resulted in the improved image qualities and transmission speeds that have brought this technology to the office automation forefront. FAX could also play a significant role in distributed collaborative system use by providing a means for rapid sharing of hard-copy graphic information.

Fiber optics. Optical technology developments have enabled transmission of data by pulses of light. Since visible light has a very high frequency, and the “fiber” (glass or fused silica) transmission media exhibits little power loss, it is possible to transmit vast amounts of data over long distances. This will enhance the feasibility of visual and graphical image transmission between users of distributed collaborative systems.

Hypermedia. Hypermedia is a method of storing and retrieving discrete pieces of data (text, sound, or graphics) in an associative manner. This technology is a potential means of logically organizing and retrieving data generated in collaborative sessions. Data objects can be comprised of text, pictures, or sounds, or a combination of these. Using this technology, users could potentially browse related verbal, textual and graphical ideas recorded during collaborative sessions.

Optical storage. Optical storage technology uses a laser beam to store vast amounts of data on disk cartridges. Erasable drives are being developed to provide the large amount of storage space required for visual and graphic images. This will increase the viability of using many different types of communication media in distributed asynchronous collaborative systems.

With the arrival of technologies enabling efficient transmission, storage, and retrieval of all forms of communication, new collaborative system configurations are being conceived with participants separated across cities, countries or even oceans. While task-directed CSCW systems have successfully supported distributed users working both synchronously and asynchronously, few of the more communications-oriented GDSS systems have been implemented to support distributed meetings. This is possibly because technology has limited the ability to effectively support the interactive communication required for communications-oriented group work. New collaborative system space/time configurations supported by advanced communications technologies are being explored; two possible configurations and their uses of emerging technologies are as follows: Mixed-mode meetings. Group productivity may be increased by structuring electronically-supported meetings in ways that optimize both individual and group performance. For example, tasks that rely heavily on group interaction, such as problem identification and goal setting, could be accomplished in traditional face-to-face, synchronous sessions. Tasks that require a minimal amount of group interaction, such as idea generation on a single topic or several related topics, may be successful in a synchronous distributed mode, since interaction is supported. but group distractions are removed. Tasks relying primarily on individual processes, such as idea organization and voting, may be best accomplished in a distributed asynchronous mode.

Since meeting participants could potentially be separated by large distances, advanced videoconferencing technology will support face-to-face sessions; high-speed fiber-optic, ISDN facilities are necessary for distributed synchronous sessions. Efficient high-volume transmission, storage, and retrieval facilities provided by fiber optics and ISDN as well as optical storage and hypermedia filing systems will be used for distributed asynchronous sessions.

"Virtual" meetings. Research is under way at the University of Arizona to develop a futuristic meeting environment where centralized electronic meeting facilities are connected to other similar facilities through communication channels that include text, voice, and graphic and visual image. The project intends to concentrate on making the technology unobtrusive and instantly responsive so participants can concentrate on tasks instead of processes. The necessary digital processing, transmission and storage capabilities will be provided in part through fiber optic, ISDN, and optical storage technology.

## Conclusions

The communication technologies used in collaborative systems are the same as those that have been used and studied for many years in the individual environment. Electronic messaging has successfully supported text communication for messaging and conferencing in both individual and group environments. However, the demand for more complex communication systems that include voice and graphic support is increasing, and electronic messaging applications are limited because of problems inherent in transmitting, storing, and retrieving the large amounts of data generated by voice and graphics. New technologies such as fiber optics, hypermedia, ISDN development, and optical storage hold promise in overcoming these difficulties.

Videoconferencing has had mixed success in the past as an alternative to group meetings. It has achieved greater success supporting meetings between individuals or small workgroups involved in design work than among larger groups with non-design agendas. Distributed collaborative work, with its emphasis on small workgroups and group design, may be a successful videoconferencing application. Advances in fiber optics and optical disk storage make it a viable future collaboration technology.

Ideas for improving group systems can also be gained by looking at past and current research in basic communication technologies. Expert systems and hypertext should be investigated for organizing information generated by group collaboration, and knowledge about the importance of strong leadership should not be forgotten as collaboration moves toward more distributed and/or asynchronous implementations.

The advent of improved information storage devices and transmission media increase the viability of mixed-mode meetings or virtual meetings where participants are separated by time and/or space. By applying existing and emerging technologies in innovative ways, collaborative system configurations can be created to increase group and organizational productivity.
