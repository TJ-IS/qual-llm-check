# Capital budgeting in information systems development

Kar Yan Tam

The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong

The results of an empirical study on the current usage of capital budgeting techniques for evaluating, terminating, and auditing information system investments are presented. Findings based on 134 senior MIS personnel and management executives indicate that capital budgeting has little impact on IS investment, and simple techniques such as payback period and cost benefit ratio are preferred over more sophisticated discount cash-flow models. Problems with cost and return estimations are shown to be the key factors that limit their use. It is suggested that the decision authority varies according to the project value and the type of decisions being undertaken.

Keywords: Capital budgeting; Information system investment; Cost of capital; Corporate planning and strategy

## 1. Introduction

The use of information technology to increase the competitiveness of a firm has been a major area of interest in information systems (IS) literature. As information technology continues to diffuse into every level of a firm, making major impacts on the way it operates, corporate managers are becoming more responsive to the need for integrating the IS functions into the firm's strategic plan. A number of frameworks have been proposed to address their strategic implications. Porter's value chain is one that illustrates how IS can provide competitive advantage in each phase of the chain [23,24]. An alternative approach, called value-based planning, has also received considerable attention by corporate planners in recent years [1]. Rockart and Scott-Morton [27] go even further to postulate that IS is driving corporate strategy. Their views are supported by a recent BusinessWeek report [4] in which the security industry is described as undergoing structural changes which to a large extent are driven by information technologies.

It is important that the strategic implications of an IS investment can be realized as planned. Approaches such as value chain deal with planning at the corporate level and are limited in offering a systematic way to derive an IS plan. To bridge the gap between business and IS plans, a number of strategic IS planning methodologies and process models have been suggested [11,12,14,15,16,22,25,31,32]. They share the same objective of providing a set of methodological and operational guidelines to develop an IS plan that is in line with the firm's overall business plan.

A major outcome of the planning process is to select the right IS projects. A crucial step in this is to compare the costs of a project with its potential payoffs. The guidelines suggested by the various planning methodologies are mainly qualitative in nature, with a focus on the strategic and intangible payoffs of an IS investment. They fall short of providing a formal model to evaluate and rank IS projects in financial terms that facilitate cross comparison with competitive investments. Furthermore, these planning tools are not adequate nor intended to function as a control mechanism that provide feedback in each stage of the investment.

The various contingencies considered in the plan must be carried over to the development and operational stages. This is necessary for two reasons. First, the business environment may have changed since the plan was finalized. These changes must be accounted for in making decisions at each milestone. For example, the environment may have changed to such an extent that there is a need to consider abandoning the project even though it is partially completed. Second, after the project is completed, it is still worthwhile to check whether the actual costs/benefits of the investment are in line with original expectations. To gather the necessary information, management should conduct an audit on the finished project. As suggested by Barnes and Bollinger [2], such practice is of great value, since it expands the notion of re-use from the lower coding level to the higher decision-making level.

The investment decision should be based on the economic benefits it provides. A firm should be indifferent in its decision whether to invest in a new computer system or to acquire another company if their expected returns and risks are the same. Both investments are driven by the same set of financial incentives, which are to maximize shareholders' wealth. Given this objective, a firm should employ a formal and consistent method of selecting and ranking projects, particularly under limited capital conditions. An IS project is only one of the many investments in a firm's portfolio. The returns it generates are not independent of other investments. The interaction must be accounted for in estimating the overall return. Unfortunately, existing IS planning models do not include this interaction.

Capital budgeting theory provides a foundation for the evaluation and ranking of a firm's investments, both individually and as a portfolio. Numerous budgeting techniques have been developed, ranging from straightforward payback to complex mathematical programming formulations [3]. Guimaraes and Paxton [9] have suggested the use of capital budgeting techniques to evaluate IS projects; however they have received little attention from IS researchers, due largely to problems in quantifying the intangible payoffs. At the same time, a large body of work has been performed on the use of capital budgeting techniques in research and development (R&D) projects [5,17]. These consist of empirical and methodological studies that generate substantial insights into the process by which R&D projects are selected, supervised, terminated, and evaluated. While R&D projects are at least as ‘strategic’ and their outcomes as difficult to estimate as IS investments, little research has been done along the same line in IS investments.

Our study is motivated by the need to understand the use of capital budgeting techniques in IS investment. Its objective is to investigate the extent to which capital budgeting techniques are used in the selection, termination, and post-audit of IS projects. Unlike previous work which focuses largely on project selection $[5]$ and emphasizes on the validation of an IS plan $[10]$ , the study looks at the various decisions regarding an IS investment and investigates the extent financial incentives play in the decision-making process. Contributions of this study are twofold: First, this is a study devoted to addressing the financial incentives of IS investments. It provides empirical evidence of the discrepancy between what the theory suggests and what is actually done. Second, it identifies factors that are perceived to be important by practitioners in selecting, terminating, and post-audit of an IS project. The current study is not intended to make rigorous statistical inferences about the various phenomena observed in the data sample; rather, it serves to suggest what is happening in the industry and reflects the views of IS practitioners. To this end, it may be regarded as an exploratory study that identifies research areas regarding IS investment in general.

## 2. Capital budgeting in IS investments

Capital investment in information technologies is important in a firm. It not only involves long-term resource commitment, but also ties with other potential investments. Capital budgeting prescribes a set of normative rules for making investment decisions. The techniques typically generate a single index which can be used to evaluate and rank potential projects. Four simple techniques which can readily be found in standard finance texts are described below:

Payback period (PP). PP calculates how long it takes for a project to generate enough return to cover its costs. The planner decides on a time period within which the full cost of the project must be recovered: If it is less than the calculated payback period, then the project is launched.

Cost benefit ratio (CBR). CBR is defined as the ratio of the costs of a project to its benefits (measured in monetary terms). The decision to invest is contingent upon the value of the cost benefit ratio. Only when the ratio is less than a threshold value, usually less than 1, will the planner initialize the project.

Neither payback period nor cost benefit ratio takes into consideration the time cost of money. The following two techniques explicitly incorporate this parameter into their calculations. They are based on the discount cash flow model in which net cash flows are discounted by a factor indicating the opportunity cost of the project.

Net present value (NPV). The NPV of a project is calculated by the discount cash flow model

$$
\mathrm{NPV} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \frac {I _ {t} - O _ {t}}{(1 + r)},
$$

where NPV is the net present value of the project; T the planning horizon; t the time period; r the opportunity cost of capital (or “hurdle rate”); $I_{t}$ the expected incoming cash flow in period t; $O_{t}$ the expected outgoing cash flow in period t.

There are a number of ways to determine the discount rate r. The firm's cost of capital is commonly adopted as this. Gitman and Mercurio [8] surveyed a sample of Fortune 1000 firms and reported that unadjusted cost of capital was used in a considerable number of projects. They also found that, depending on the risk class of the project, the cost of capital was slightly adjusted to reflect the difference in risk exposure. However, such an adjustment was argued to create biases against high-risk projects by Reimann [26]. He contends that risk should be incorporated in the estimation of $I_{t}$ and $O_{t}$ ; this has a more theoretical appeal. Other commonly adopted values for r include the US Treasury bond, fixed rate security, bank's borrowing rate, rate of return based on the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) and the arbitrage pricing theory (APT). The decision rule for NPV is straightforward: if the calculated NPV is greater than a threshold value (at least zero), then the project is accepted; otherwise it is rejected.

Internal rate of return (IRR). IRR uses the same discount cash flow model as in NPV. The difference between NPV and IRR is that, while NPV fixes the hurdle rate r to compute the discount cash flow value, IRR fixes the discount value to zero to derive r, the internal rate of return. The rationale is to determine what rate is needed for the project to break even. A company may set different IRRs for different classes of projects. For example, a project with substantial risk is only accepted if the calculated IRR is above a relatively high threshold value.

The above techniques are by no means complete. They represent the basic and widely adopt techniques in financial management. More advanced models, such as multi-period capital budgeting under constraints, require solving large-scale mathematical programming problems. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in using the option pricing framework to incorporate the option to delay an investment decision $[19,21]$ . This approach has tremendous ramifications, because the option to offer new services and to enter new markets is contingent on the decision of the initial investment. Very often, the opportunity to expand or develop new products is described as strategic and having an intangible payoff in the IS literature. The availability of a formal model derived from option pricing theory will provide a valuable means to quantify opportunities in a more explicit way.

Capital budgeting techniques provide normative guidelines to aid management in selecting from a set of investment alternatives the one that benefits shareholders the most. In fact, their applications are not restricted to the selection of projects; they can also be applied to project abandonments and post-audits. Prescriptive rules for project termination are relevant to IS projects, as cost overruns and schedule slippages occur frequently in information system projects. From a behavior finance perspective, Statman and Caldwell [29] found that managers tend to continue to commit resources to a failing project, even when there is little chance for it to succeed. They suggest that project failures and their adverse effects can be minimized by following the normative guidelines set forth by budgeting techniques.

Once the project is completed and the system is in operation, it is important to perform a post-audit. While IS should give an edge to adopting firms, little post-audit has been done. Existing literature tends to focus on the operation and maintenance aspects of an information system after deployment. Performing a final audit provides feedback that can be integrated into the firm's organizational learning process. Singhvi [28] suggests three reasons: (1) it serves as a basis for corrective actions when deviation from the original plan is observed; (2) it holds the planner to continued responsibility; and (3) management recognizes the importance of investment decisions and the studies that underlie them.

While capital budgeting techniques provide an economic rationale in decision making at various stages of a project, some studies argue that other important factors must be considered. They posit that power and politics in organization play a major role in IS investments $[18,30]$ .

As an initial step towards resolving the two conflicting views, an empirical study was conducted to provide information on the practice of capital budgeting techniques.

## 3. Methodology

We have developed a structured questionnaire addressing the issues and mailed to 1000 senior IS executives who were members of the US Data Processing Management Association (DPMA). The questionnaire had been field-tested by a small group of IS graduate students and faculty to ensure that it was unambiguous and readable. We received 166 returns. Of these, only 134 could be used, as the rest were incomplete. Of the useful questionnaires, about 10% were responses of CEO or company president, 15% by senior executives (vice presidents and general managers), and the rest by senior MIS personnel (project managers, system analysts, specialists, and consultants actively involved in all phases of IS development). A fairly uniform demographic distribution and industrial composition was observed. No explicit control was exercised on industrial composition. Since our study is aimed at providing a general idea of the current practice of capital budgeting in IS investments, the lack of a rigorous control measure is inconsequential.

The questionnaire consists of 12 close-ended questions. Of these, the first 7 were scaled on a five-point Likert scale (1 - very important or always to 4 - not important or seldom and 5 - never); responses to the rest of the questionnaire needed only a check (tick).

## 4. Data analysis

Depending on their responses to question 1, the respondents were divided into two groups. Those who stated “always” or “very often” were categorized as the using group, while those stated “sometimes”, “seldom” or “never” fell into the non-using group. The proportions of each group were roughly the same: 51.5% using and 48.5% non-using. Figure 1 summarizes the responses to this question in a pie chart.

![](/api/attachments/W5AFPH9V/fulltext/images/9348fefe346ff15e639ec9147f11ecc56ac77a25f52fbaf8d554ad8df4fecd6a.jpg)  
Fig. 3. IS project selection criteria.

The rest of the analysis is discussed in terms of these two groups. Responses to the use of the four budgeting techniques are illustrated in Figure 2. Responses were aggregated by weighing each response on a level from 1–5. This gave an overall weighted response. As shown in Figure 2, there were differences between the two groups. While all four techniques were frequently used by the using group, non-using respondents seldom performed capital budgeting. An average disparity of 1 was observed in their weighted responses. On the other hand, both groups appear to employ less sophisticated techniques, such as payback period and cost benefit ratio in evaluating IS projects.

When asked what criteria were used in selecting IS projects, both groups indicated strategic value as most important. Improving operational efficiency was another criterion perceived to be of great value. Financial incentive was at the bottom of the list in both groups. About 57% of the respondents in both groups ranked strategic value as the most important factor, while only 9% felt that financial performance was relevant. It seems that capital budgeting techniques were used to reconcile the strategic values of projects, rather than to screen investments.

As suggested in the literature, quantifying the strategic value of an information system is difficult. In fact, there is still a problem in relating improvement in operation efficiency to economic benefit. This is illustrated in Figure 3, which shows a difference of 0.43 and 1.44 between operation efficiency and financial incentive in the using and non-using groups, respectively. This phenomenon may be caused by the lack of financial skills of the planning group.

We asked the respondents to state the usefulness of capital budgeting techniques in making termination decisions. Results are depicted in Figure 4. Like project evaluation, the two groups had divergent perceptions. The using group generally thought that capital budgeting techniques were somewhat useful, but the non-using ones had reservations; in fact, the difference in weighted responses between the two groups for PP, CBR, NPV, and IRR were 1.03, 1.06, 0.84, and 1.01, respectively. Again, simple techniques were more popular than discount cash-flow methods, with CBR identified as the most popular one among the respondents.

Both groups ranked priority change and technical infeasibility as major causes for terminating projects. Cost overruns and schedule slippages came next, followed by financial incentive based on capital budgeting techniques.

The results seem to suggest that IS projects were not evaluated entirely in financial terms but based on priorities assigned by top management. There are numerous reasons for changing a project's priority. One possible reason is the change in the firm's political structure. Our findings seem to corroborate with a recent study on project abandonment [7] which reported a dominant role play be stakeholders, particularly top management, in terminating IS projects.

![](/api/attachments/W5AFPH9V/fulltext/images/27ec9890c4c1429167f574036320ff98424005d5289dddd3abb5b0d29c4848bb.jpg)

A failing project will also lead to termination. Given the current state of the art in software engineering, technical infeasibility, cost overruns, and schedule slippages cannot be clearly separated in reasoning about the failure of a project. Indeed, they are the end results of poor planning and management $[13,20]$ .

To determine the extent budgeting techniques were used in conducting post-audits of completed projects, we asked the respondents two questions. The first applied to the usefulness of each of the four techniques in auditing the performance of IS projects. Results are presented in Figure 6. When compared with project selection and termination decisions, the usefulness of budgeting techniques had declined. However, this aggregated finding did not apply to the using group separately. As shown in Figure 6, respondents in this group ranked each technique slightly more important than in their responses to project termination. On the other hand, the non-using group seldom used the techniques. For the latter group, little was done to compare the actual performance with the initial plan in financial terms.

The second question asked respondents to compare financial incentive with other criteria in conducting post-audits. It was found that both groups were more concerned with meeting schedules and budget constraints than the deviation between actual financial payoffs and theoretical projections. Evidence suggested that a project was evaluated by how well project managers were able to produce a deliverable within the allowed budget and given deadline. Results of this question are shown in Figure 7.

To assess the difficulties in using capital budgeting techniques, we asked the respondents to identify their problems with applications. As Figure 8 illustrates, the majority of the respondents had problems with parameters estimation. It was more difficult to estimate costs and returns of investment then to select an appropriate discount rate. Furthermore, respondents were more familiar with simple models, such as payback period and cost benefit ratio than NPV and IRR. About 13% stated they they were not familiar with budgeting techniques. This percentage is consistent with responses to the first question, which stated that 10.8% of the respondents did not use them.

Those who used a discount cash-flow model were asked to identify the way the discount rate was calculated. A significant number of respondents stated that they used the cost of capital provided by the their companies: such information is usually available in large firm. The rate typically varies according to the firm's cost of capital and the risk class of the project. Contrary to finance theory, subjective assessment was also frequently used. As shown in Figure 9, the borrowing rate was also reported as a common way to discount cash flows generated from the investment.

We have made a number of observations by comparing responses to each type of decision. Since these were made in a chronological order, findings would also be applicable to the stages of the entire development effort.

First, there was a shift of criteria from project evaluation and termination to post-audit. While strategic value and operation efficiency were identified as dominating factors in evaluating an IS project, a different set of criteria were adopted to determine whether the project should be continued once it was launched. Apparently, practitioners found it very difficult to estimate the duration and cost of an IS project. Methods developed in software cost estimation seem to provide little help.

Second, both groups showed similar responses to criteria for project evaluation and terminations. The using group seems to perform a more rigorous post-audit, indicating that capital budgeting techniques were part of an overall approach to IS investment.

Third, capital budgeting techniques played a minor role in IS investment. All respondents identified financial incentive as the least important factor among those we solicited in IS development.

In addition to the techniques and criteria used to evaluate an IS investment, we also asked the respondents to identify the decision-making authority. IS investments were divided into categories according to the amount of capital involved: (1) < \$10K; (2) \$10K–\$100K; (3) \$100K–\$1 million; (4) > \$1 million. The results are summarized in Table 1. When the dollar value of a project is small, the person in charge of the IS department was the final decision maker, responsible for evaluating, terminating and auditing IS projects. This was observed in both the using and non-using groups. About 50% of the respondents reported that the head of the MIS department was the person who evaluated, terminated, and audited IS projects. As the value of the investment increased, the authority switched from the IS manager to higher level management or collective decision making bodies.

In the \$10K–\$100K category, project committee was identified as the most popular authority. Termination decisions at this level were typically made by corporate staff, which scored 27.3% in the overall response. This was closely followed by project committee (23.8%) and steering committee (20.9%). Interestingly, the responsibility for auditing an IS project after it was completed went back to MIS head in the using group (32.8%) and to project committee (32.7%) in the non-using group. The offload of post-audit tasks to IS managers indicated that experiences learned from projects at this level were best directed back to those who prepared the plan.

Responses to the \$100K–\$1 million category showed that corporate planner, representing 33.8% of the sample, was the primary authority. Responses from the using group indicated that steering committee (31.9%) was at least as likely as corporate planner (29.2%) to evaluate IS investments in this category.

When the investment is more than 1 million, the role of corporate staff had become more distinct in making all three decisions. About 39.5% of all respondents identified corporate planner as the single most important authority in evaluating IS projects. Furthermore, corporate staff were responsible for over 40% of the termination decisions and performed more than one third of the post-audits.

When comparing the groups across the four categories, both groups showed similar responses for low capital projects. However, as the project value increases, the results revealed that the non-using group relied more and more on top management (corporate staff) to evaluate and terminate IS projects. For example, in the \$100K–\$1 million category, 39.3% in the non-using group identified corporate staff as the decision authority in evaluating IS projects, as compared with only 29.2% in the using group. Similarly, the percentages were 45.1 (non-using) and 35.6 (using) for investments over \$1 million. This could be explained by the fact that the using group was more familiar with capital budgeting techniques and therefore had a better understanding of the financial impacts of an IS investment. The using group therefore could interpret the financial implications of a project without handing it over to corporate management. An interesting question that may be worth further study is whether this phenomenon is related to the decentralization of the decision-making process within an organization; unfortunately, the current study does not provide enough data to address this question properly.

## 5. Summary and conclusion

Our findings corroborate existing IS and financial management literature in general; yet they provide additional insights into the usefulness of capital budgeting techniques in IS investments. While previous studies focus on the evaluation and ranking of IS projects, we have included termination and post-audit considerations. These are important aspects that have received little attention by IS researchers. The major findings are:

\- Capital budgeting techniques are used but have minor impact on the evaluation, termination, and post-audit of IS projects;

\- Simple budgeting techniques, such as payback period and cost benefit ratio are preferred over complex discount cash-flow methods;

\- the major problems with capital budgeting techniques are estimation of cost and return;

\- the firm's cost of capital is used as the discount rate;

\- strategic value is reported as the most important criterion in evaluating IS projects;

\- project priority change and technical problems are the primary cause of most termination decisions;

\- schedule and budget constraints are the most important criteria in post-audit of IS projects;

\- there is a shift of decision-making authority from the MIS head to corporate staff as the value of a project increase;

\- the more familiar MIS personnel are with capital budgeting techniques, the less they rely on top management to evaluate IS projects;

The current study is exploratory in nature and has some limitations that prevent us from stating the above findings in rigorous statistical terms. First, the return rate is not high and may cause non-response bias. Second, the sample was not carefully controlled for possible interfering factors. We are well aware of the potential biases introduced into our sample. However, the intent of this study is to provide a feel of the use of budgeting techniques in IS investments; thus potential biases are inconsequential.

Contrary to finance theory, the study finds empirical evidence to the effect that capital budgeting techniques are not much in use in IS investment. It also finds that the decision authority in selecting, terminating, and auditing IS projects varies according to the project value and the type of decisions being undertaken. To understand the gap between capital budgeting theory and practice requires more indepth empirical and case studies.
