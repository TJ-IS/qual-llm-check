# Factors affecting decision support system benefits \*

Godwin J. Udo and J. Steve Davis
Clemson University, Clemson, SC, USA

Almost every DSS researcher has identified evaluation of DSS as an important research direction. Most previous studies of DSS benefits have failed to address the intangible benefits. This study investigated the relationship of selected predictor variables with the tangible and intangible benefits of DSS utilization in the financial services industry. A mail survey involving 121 DSS users from this industry was used to examine benefits of DSS and the factors determining them. Results indicated that benefits are affected by four groups of factors: Industry, organization, system, and user attributes.

Keywords: DSS; System effectiveness; DSS benefits; Industry attributes; Organization attributes; Intangible benefits Evaluation; Multivariate analysis.

## 1. Introduction

Anyone familiar with decision support systems (DSS) research will agree that research gaps exist, especially in the area of evaluation. Little progress has been made, possibly because DSS are a relatively new kind of information system. Hopple has rightly described the current research state of DSS evaluation as the following [9]: “A final high priority research item is the need to construct, operationalize, and test models of DSS and other determinants of outputs and outcomes of the decision-making process (...) such models feature four sets of interacting variable clusters: Decision-maker characteristics; information (DSS) system attributes; aspects of the decision environment; and the dependent variable of decision output characteristics (especially effectiveness). Special mention should be made on the decision-maker characteristics variable area because of the extensive and inconclusive empirical work on this set of factors.”

The evaluation of DSS is a research direction mentioned by almost every author in this field, but measuring the effectiveness of information systems is a difficult task [15].

Due to the potential benefits of DSS, organizations are willing to spend a lot of money and time to install, operate, and maintain a system. Edelman [6] estimated that at least 1% of total revenue of organizations is spent on information systems, much of it on DSS. Since information system budgets have tended to grow in recent years, the need has increased for reliable methods to assess information system effectiveness both in absolute terms and relative to competitive firms.

Most of the benefits derived from DSS utilization are qualitative in nature. Indirect (qualitative) benefits are often overlooked because they are difficult to measure or are assessed via an indicator, and some of these are not convincingly linked to actual benefit. For example, previous studies have measured information system benefits in terms of frequency of use $[7]$ .

This paper proposes a comprehensive and realistic model for evaluating the benefits of DSS. This model accounts for both the direct and the intangible benefits of DSS utilization, considering the user's perspective to identify the significant benefits (or effectiveness measures) of DSS and the factors determining them.

Surveys of DSS utilization have concluded that the literature provides little guidance on evaluation of benefits $[8]$ . One of the few frameworks for general evaluation of DSS, upon which this study is partly based, has been reported by Adelman and Donnell $[1]$ . They defined three alternative types of evaluation procedures: Objective measurement, expert observation, and subjective judgement. For practical reasons we selected subjective judgement for this research. It requires fewer resources and is less time consuming than other methods. Subjective judgement requires DSS users to score their experiences by answering a questionnaire. They identified three phases of DSS evaluation: Technical, subjective, and empirical. The technical phase evaluates the algorithms and input/output procedures, in order to identify potential problems prior to actual use. The subjective evaluation phase determines the user's perception of the system in terms of effectiveness, efficiency, user-friendliness, and so forth. The empirical phase focuses on measuring DSS performance, such as determining whether users make significantly better or faster decisions. This study takes place in the empirical phase, the one most concerned with measuring the benefits.

## 2. Model for evaluating DSS benefits

We developed a DSS evaluation model to correct for the existing shortcomings of previous studies, taking into account other research findings and suggestions in the DSS literature. In particular, industry environmental factors are included in the model and more relevant measures of the success of DSS are explored. A search of the DSS literature revealed that four main attributes may affect DSS benefits, namely those of the industry, the organization, the system, and the user. The following identifies each and describes related previous studies.

1. Industry attributes Vincent [23] pointed out that industry type affects the level of DSS benefits. The information-driven industries such as financial services, marketing, etc., were identified as having a higher return on investment (ROI) than the manufacturing industries. Strassman [21] concluded that the organization's strategic position influences the effectiveness of information systems. Where strategic position is superior, information systems yield an increased management productivity, but where strategic position is poor, management productivity declines or remains unchanged. He measured strategic position in terms of market share, product quality or utilization of assets and ROI. McFarlan [14] indicated that the type of generic strategy (price leadership, product differentiation, or market focus) significantly influences the effectiveness of information systems. Porter and Millar [16] listed industrial structure as one of the three ways that information systems affect strategy. Industrial structure can be expressed in terms of industry growth, stability, and type of industry.

In effect, five variables believed to constitute the industry attributes had, in one way or the other, been investigated by other researchers and were believed to influence the effectiveness of information systems. These variables include: Strategic position, generic strategy, industry growth, degree of competition, and type of industry. However, none of these variables has been investigated directly in connection with DSS.

2. Organization attributes Many studies have investigated the influence of organizational attributes on the effectiveness of information systems in general and DSS in particular. Watson et al. [24] investigated the effect of organization support for DSS. They found that the more the organizational support, the more effective the DSS, and others found that the level of top management support, user training, and length of DSS use were correlated with DSS success [19]. Cheney et al. [4] identified organizational factors that appeared to affect the success of end-user computing, including user training, task structure, and psychological climate. The organization variables found to be significant in one or more previous studies include: Size, user training, frequency of use, organizational support, vendor support, and task structuredness.

3. System attributes Previous studies have found that certain DSS characteristics seem to have important influences on the effectiveness of the system: Friendliness; length of use; size of DSS; range of alternatives; timeliness, accuracy and relevancy of output. Some have concluded that accuracy of output and its relevancy are important. Others have found that length of use and user-friendliness are positively correlated with overall satisfaction with the system. Timeliness has also shown a positive association with user satisfaction [10,17]. Strassman [21] concluded that the size (cost) of the information system is not proportional to the benefits derived when measured in terms of management productivity.

4. User attributes The variables associated with this group of attributes are: Age, educational level, years of experience, user expectations, attitude toward DSS, technical knowledge of the system, and the number of years of experience the user has with DSS. There seems to be a positive relationship between years of experience and effectiveness of DSS. The greater the experience in the current position, the more managers tend to use the information system. One study reported a situation in which a DSS did not improve upon manual techniques, because users were reluctant to accept it (thus their attitude toward DSS was not favorable) [5]. Barki and Huff [3] studied 32 DSS in 9 organizations and noticed that user expectations (willingness to change) strongly influenced user satisfaction. Zmud [25] indicated that the more educated users are, the less they tend to use MIS. He also concluded that the highly educated and the highly experienced users are less satisfied with MIS. Cheney et al. [4] concluded a positive association between user's attitude and expectation and system effectiveness. Shara et al. [20] found the user's knowledge to be correlated with system effectiveness.

Our review of the DSS literature revealed that the benefits of DSS include direct benefits (cost reduction, increased productivity, time saving, and overall cost effectiveness) and intangible benefits (enhanced competitive edge, decision quality, improved communication, and overall satisfaction with the system).

## 3. Hypotheses

To test the model, we formed the following null hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. There is no significant relationship between the benefits derived from DSS and the attributes of the industry in the presence of other factors.

Hypothesis 2. There is no significant relationship between the benefits derived from DSS and the attributes of the organization in the presence of other factors.

Hypothesis 3. There is no significant relationship between the benefits derived from DSS and the attributes of the system in the presence of other factors.

Hypothesis 4. There is no significant relationship between the benefits derived from DSS and the attributes of the user in the presence of other factors.

For each of these four main hypotheses, we generated eight sub-hypotheses which corresponded to the eight benefits mentioned earlier. The full regression model for these hypotheses is:

$$

To test the significance of each of the four groups of factors, the full model is compared with a restricted model using the procedure described in the SPSS manual [18]. A restricted model is the full model less the factors of the group of interest. For instance, to test the significance of industry attributes, the restricted model is the full model less the industry factors. The full and the restricted models are compared using the difference in “sums of squares of errors” of the models. If the difference is significant for at least one of the benefit measures, as indicated by its F-statistic, the null hypothesis would be rejected with the conclusion that the particular group of factors is significant in explaining the variability in benefits.

## 7. Discussion

The following discussion suggests an interpretation for some of the more interesting relationships between various factors and DSS benefits based on the regression coefficients.

Industry variables: Consistent with McFarlan's finding, the type of strategy played an important role in determining most of the benefits [14]. Price leadership and product differentiation were generally more positively associated with benefits than were market focus or any of the combined strategies.

The participating firms were from different industries such as banking, insurance, and real estate. Generally, organizations from industries which are at the infant or growing stages of growth reported less intangible benefits than organizations from industries in the stable or declining stages. Since infant or growing industries generally experience a more dynamic environment, this finding may result from the greater difficulty in adapting DSS to the changing situation. Generally the better she strategic position of the firm the higher benefit levels were reported by DSS users, a finding consistent with Strassman [21].

Organization variables Task unstructuredness, quality of training and organizational support were all positively associated with at least two benefits and were not negatively associated with any. In keeping with well-accepted DSS theory, users who applied DSS to unstructured tasks experienced greater benefits than those who applied DSS to routine tasks. Frequency of use was associated with better decision quality, production cost reduction, and improved productivity, but was not a significant predictor of the other benefits. In particular, frequency of use was not linked to overall cost-effectiveness. This finding suggests that frequency of use is not necessarily an indicator of system effectiveness, which casts doubt on previous studies that have employed it as an indicator.

The larger the organization, in terms of sales volume and number of employees, the greater the benefits achieved in improved communication. Intuitively, the potential for improving communication increases with the size of the firm. Users who received superior support from the organization experienced greater benefits in competitive edge, improved communication, production cost reduction, increased productivity and overall satisfaction than users who did not.

System variables Only three of the system variables were positively associated with most of the benefits: Timeliness of output, the length of time of use, and the range of alternatives. The longer the system had been in use, the greater were most of the benefits. Intuitively, the benefits of a newly installed system will increase over time, as the organization becomes accustomed to it. DSS that provided a greater range of alternatives were generally associated with greater benefits. Other system variables were not positively associated with most of the benefits, and were negatively associated with some, e.g., the cost of the system was inversely proportional to four of the benefits, and was positively associated with only one. In achieving DSS benefits, perhaps the attributes of the system itself are not as important as the environment in which the system operates (the industry, organization and user). User-friendliness had a significant, fairly strong negative impact on the time saving benefit. This finding is consistent with certain guidelines for designing human-computer interfaces for information systems. Generally there is a trade-off between user-friendliness and efficiency of use. For example, an extensive menu system might help a beginner, but could impede the more experienced user, who could more efficiently enter commands directly.

User variables Consistent with Larcker and Lessig's [12] finding, the more positive the user's attitude about using a computer-based system, the greater the benefits achieved. Supporting studies by Shara et al. [20], we found that the user's DSS experience was positively related to the overall cost effectiveness; however, it was negatively associated with the other benefits.

Age of the respondents (ranging from 20–60) was positively related to most of the benefits and was not negatively associated with any. Apparently the older users were comfortable with the relatively new DSS technology.

Implications of the study Results of this study provide suggestions for researchers and practitioners. Those conducting research into DSS benefits should consider four groups of attributes: Industry, organization, system, and user, to be potential predictors of DSS benefits. Potential benefits include the eight measured in this study. Since frequency of use may not necessarily be an indicator of benefit, it should be considered a predictor (an independent variable), rather than a measure of benefit.

Practitioners want to answer this question: How can my firm get the maximum possible benefit from using DSS? The most useful suggestions in response to this question are those involving variables over which a manager can exercise control. Generally a manager has more control over system, organization, and user variables than industry variables, because a single manager can do little to alter the characteristics of an entire industry. With respect to system variables, a manager can influence the design of an in-house system or the acquisition of an off-the-shelf DSS. Management can control most organization variables, such as organization support and quality of training, and can influence most user variables through hiring and assignment activities. Since the various types of benefits are associated with different predictors, the appropriate management approach depends on which benefits are to be maximized.

Potential for improving decision quality appears greater when the prospective users are relatively inexperienced in their jobs, and are inexperienced with DSS but have a positive attitude toward computer-based systems and are trained well in using the DSS.

To foster improvement in the competitive edge, one should select user-friendly DSS whose output is timely, and should emphasize organization support. Overall, an effective person-machine interaction seems likely to achieve the desired improvement.

To enhance communication in the organization, managers should: Make sure that the DSS provides timely output, emphasize organization support, and select users who have a positive attitude toward computer-based systems.

The likelihood of achieving production cost reduction is increased by selecting DSS which consider a wide range of alternatives, providing quality training in their use, and encouraging frequent use. DSS should be introduced to users having high expectations of success with DSS, whose tasks are relatively unstructured. Managers interested in developing a positive climate for introducing a new DSS should review the results of research into user resistance $[13]$ .

To increase productivity, managers should take advantage of opportunities to employ DSS on relatively unstructured tasks, and should provide quality training and organization support. Users should have a positive attitude toward computer-based systems.

Time savings can be achieved by selecting users with a positive attitude and providing them quality training.

Overall satisfaction can be fostered by applying DSS to less structured tasks which are performed by people with favorable attitudes toward computer-based systems, and by providing good training and organization support.

Overall cost effectiveness can be enhanced by selecting experienced DSS users who have favorable attitudes toward computer-based systems, and by providing good training in the use of DSS.

## 8. Concluding remarks

Since the findings of this study are based on the subjective perception of the respondents, strong claims of a general nature cannot be made without further investigation. We assumed that respondents were accurate and consistent in evaluating the DSS with which they were most familiar, but different respondents might have provided different evaluations. Since we didn't have the freedom to actually select specific respondents, it is possible there exists a sampling bias (e.g., managers might have reacted to our request this way: "Give the questionnaire to Charlie; he has used a DSS and seemed to like it.") It would be interesting to conduct a study using a more objective approach to DSS evaluation. In one such approach, the researcher would directly observe use of the DSS, and would employ consistent criteria to obtain the data. This kind of data collection is expensive and requires a lengthy period of observation. The value analysis method might be particularly useful in accounting for the intangible benefits of a DSS [11].

The survey instrument developed for this study could be adapted for investigating benefits of group decision support systems, a relatively new form of DSS used by two or more decision makers in a joint decision-making process. Also, this study could be repeated for other industries and results compared to those of this project.
