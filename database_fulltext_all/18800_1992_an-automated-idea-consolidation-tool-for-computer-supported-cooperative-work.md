# An automated idea consolidation tool for computer supported cooperative work

Milam Aiken and Judith Carlisle

University of Arizona, Tucson AZ, USA

The development and application of an idea consolidation tool used in conjunction with idea generation software at the University of Arizona is described. The tool groups comments generated by meeting participants, based upon identical stemmed content keywords in each comment or idea. Results show that the tool groups comments in less than five percent of the time required by the current manual process and has nearly perfect recall and precision compared to the 82.6% recall and 73.1% precision of manual groupings.

Keywords: Electronic meeting systems, Electronic brainstorming, Pattern matching, Nearest neighbor problem, Idea consolidation, Stemming, Text retrieval

## 1. Introduction

Information technology has been used increasingly in recent years for the support of group cooperative work $[53,65]$ . Computer-based systems for group support, also known as group decision support systems (GDSSs) or electronic meeting systems (EMSs), can be described as “... a set of software, hardware, language components, and procedures that support a group of people in a decision related meeting” $[24]$ or integrated computer-based systems which facilitate solution of semi- or unstructured problems by a group that has joint responsibility for making the decision $[12,13]$ . A broader definition is stated in $[11]$ : “An information technology-based environment that supports group meetings, which may be distributed geographically and temporally. The information technology environment includes, but is not limited to, distributed facilities, computer hardware and software, audio and video technologies, facilitation, and applicable group data. Group tasks include, but are not limited to, communication, planning, idea generation, problem solving, issue discussion, negotiation, conflict resolution, systems analysis and design, and collaborative group activities such as document preparation and sharing.”

Group decision support systems have been shown to provide enormous benefits for many group tasks including time and cost savings of up to 56% [45]. In addition, GDSSs have been shown to foster collaboration, communication, deliberation, and negotiation [4,30,50,59,63,69]. However, there is still potential for improvement of efficiency and effectiveness with many of these systems. For example, during a typical GDSS session using the University of Arizona's GroupSystems software, participants must manually collect or group comments that have been generated during the meeting. This process is extremely tedious and unrewarding and is one of the most significant bottlenecks in electronic group meetings [25].

In this paper, we describe a tool that significantly reduces this bottleneck. This is one of many pseudo-intelligent tools that can be used for assisting group meetings $[3]$ and is a further step in the direction of a transformation to “softer software” $[28]$ to simplify use and operation of GDSSs.

## 2. Idea consolidation

Idea consolidation is a process in which comments from an idea generation session are examined, cross-compared, and synthesized so that salient points are integrated with less redundancy. Idea consolidation groups together comments with a common focus or theme in order to gain understanding of the set.

## 2.1. The current approach

Many GroupSystems sessions consist of four stages with different levels of satisfaction as shown in Figure 1:

(1) Generating ideas (electronic brainstorming) Group members usually gain a great deal of satisfaction when they are generating comments, because they believe they are contributing to a solution and are glad to express their ideas.

(2) Consolidating ideas (idea organizer) Satisfaction plunges in the following stage, as participants feel that organizing the ideas is drudgery. Group members are often confused about grouping, and they feel that the session is 'stalling'. Some comments don't reach the group list, people have problems identifying issues, and they argue over issue titles due to misunderstanding.

The technique is largely an interactive, manual process with group participants individually aggregating comments under identified issues and then discussing which issues should be in a consolidated list in the following manner [1]:

(a) Each individual identifies key issues in the generated comments. The chances are that many participants will identify similar issues using different wording. This complicates the consolidation process.

(b) Each individual then goes through all (or if the comment file is very big, a subset) of the comments and groups each idea under one or more items in the list of issues. The grouping of comments is subjective, depending upon each individual's understanding of the meaning of each issue and each comment. In addition, participants frequently do not have enough time to go through the whole list, searching for ideas to group under a particular issue. Many relevant comments may be omitted or, irrelevant comments may be erroneously grouped.

![](/api/attachments/QPTMHM9N/fulltext/images/c097a0c2f39f987df276ef02b22dbd60625ae63497ce37eda6378d6e8a4e177e.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Satisfaction with the current process.

(c) The group then tries to consolidate individual lists into one consolidated list. Individuals discuss openly whether certain issues are identical and whether they are important enough to be included. A participant must sometimes convince other members to keep an identified issue in the consolidated list. Lists of issues are frequently reduced from 40 or 50 to less than 18.

(3) Voting on the list of issues (vote) Satisfaction picks up again when group members vote and see concrete results from the meeting.

(4) Adding more information (topic commenter) Satisfaction continues to rise as group members add comments to the issues.

Nunamaker et al. [46] report that GroupSystems is in use in more than 70 facilities worldwide and more than 30 000 individuals from more than 200 organizations have used the software. Idea Organizer is used in approximately 72% of the GroupSystems meetings and is used on average 84 minutes in each meeting that the tool was used (in some meetings, the tool was used multiple times) as shown in Figure 2 [2]. Thus, a great deal of time is being spent on manually consolidating ideas. This time could be better spent in more productive ways, such as acquiring more knowledge from the group through generating additional comments or voting on other topics.

## 2.2. Keyword search

Another way of implementing idea consolidation is the use of specific keyword searches. However, such a method requires rigorous understanding of the issues prior to the idea generation phase of the session. Keywords must be established before generating ideas – and thus may take as long as consolidating the comments without the defined keywords. Other shortcomings include the side effect of ignoring comments that do not fall into predetermined subjects or headings, and the need to impose limits on the group comments during the idea generation session.

Finally, if keywords or issues can be defined prior to the session, then the group may want to consider using Topic Commenter in which participants enter new ideas directly into the predefined, structured list of issues or categories. Thus, topic commenter precludes the use of idea organizer. However, in most cases, the topics cannot be categorized a priori, in which case Electronic Brainstorming and Idea Organizer are better alternatives.

## 2.3. Intelligent support

One way to ameliorate the tedium of consolidating comments is to further automate the process through artificially-intelligent ‘agents’ which are capable of acting on behalf of humans. “Mailer Demons” and “News Agents” are examples of such agents that redirect mail and search out relevant news for a particular user. A similar agent may be developed to organize the ideas generated.

Some initial research has been conducted in the area of applying intelligent support to GDSSs including an automated group conflict resolution system $[64]$ , a system which actively filters and structures information exchange $[23]$ , an intelligent agent which monitors session activity $[16,20]$ , and intelligent electronic mail management systems to filter messages $[9,37,48]$ . In addition, many attempts have been made over the years to implement automated natural language processing.

However, most of these implementations have been restricted to a particular domain (such as the understanding of legal terminology or toy block movements) [7,8,21,27,38,39,42,61,62,68,72]. Various language translators and other programs have been developed [52], but no program has been constructed to ‘understand’ unrestricted natural language.

Ideally, an intelligent idea organizer should be able to ‘understand’ the meaning of each comment in order to find matches with other comments. Pattern matching is an alternate approach to consolidating comments, and this does not rely on knowledge bases, dictionaries, thesauruses, or other guides to extract ‘meaning’ from text $[10,29,36,55,67]$ . Several pattern matching applications and algorithms have been developed that are similar in approach to the idea consolidation technique presented here. The “nearest neighbor problem” $[60]$ or the “closest point problem” $[57]$ tries to find objects in a file which are most similar or dissimilar to a given target. These objects can refer to strings $[26,31,33]$ , documents $[58,70,71]$ , or records $[73]$ , for example. This research is unique in that it applies pattern matching to comments generated during an electronic meeting with the subsequent increase in group productivity.

## 3. The idea consolidation tool

Since the goal of the computer ‘understanding’ the comments generated in a brainstorming session is largely unobtainable without restricting the vocabulary that can be used, a more limited approach of keyword matching is used to group comments. This keyword matching and grouping adequately approximates the identification of issues or categories while eliminating redundant comments, including all relevant ones, leaving out irrelevant comments, and taking much less time than the manual process, while increasing user satisfaction as shown in Figure 3.

## 3.1. Condensing the text

In order to reduce the number of keywords in the text needing matching, it is necessary first to eliminate words with very little meaning. In the English language, these words with little meaning are called “function” words. They have little to do with the semantic significance of language, rather, their use is grammatical. Function words account for only about 6.4% of the total English vocabulary while written samples of English text will contain as much as 59% function words [40]. The rest of the text consists of “content” words (nouns, verbs, adverbs, and adjectives) which make up the semantic gist of the sample’s meaning. For this reason, content words are the only ones necessary to retain for applications such as idea consolidation. When creating a structure for idea consolidation, this same approach is taken: function words are removed and content words are retained to use in comparison.

The amount of textual information which must be stored and processed during idea consolidation can be further reduced by “stemming” the content words $[21,49,56]$ . Stemming a word is the process by which either the prefix or suffix, or both, are removed from a word in such a manner that only the root of the word remains. Stemming words reduces the number of morphological forms in which it may occur.

The type of stemming algorithm used can have a profound effect on the possible types of applications. English words may be composed of three parts: The root, the prefix, and the suffix. Addition of a suffix to a root, will change the form of the word, but rarely the meaning. Conversely, addition of prefixes changes the meaning of words, though rarely the form. Removing a word's suffix will result in the re-creation of the word's root, or stem; stripping the prefix from a word will often result in a stem more general in meaning than necessary for issue consolidation purposes.

Stemming algorithms vary not only in their method but also their result. The “strength” of a stemming algorithm is measured by the type of stem that the algorithm produces. For instance, a very strong algorithm will remove both the prefix and suffix of a word, so that only the root remains. Other, less strong stemming algorithms, work by removing as much of the suffix as possible. The idea consolidation tool uses the Lovins algorithm [34], a relatively weak algorithm. This algorithm removes only the suffix of a word. Suffix removal correlates more to historical and phonological word formations than to syntactical rules. For example, consider the following three sentences:

(1) The revolution of the engine has increased.

(2) The amount of time it takes to revolve has increased.

(3) This time, the revolutionary had a revolver.

Sentences 1 and 2 are somewhat similar in meaning, they both refer to the concept of revolution, as it is derived from the stem “revolut”. The function words in these two sentences are “the”, “of”, “has”, “it”, and “to”. These words give structure to the sentences rather than meaning. After the function words are removed, the words which remain suggest that the sentences have similarities, as both sentences contain one word which is derived from an identical stem, i.e. “revolut”. Sentence 3 is highly different in semantic content. The removal of the function words “this”, “the”, “had”, and “a” leaves the words “time”, “revolutionary”, and “revolver” to be recognized as content words. The stem “revolut” does not result from the stemming of any of these words. “Revolutionary” when stemmed, results in “revolution”. The Lovins algorithm does not recognize “-ionary” as a systematically occurring English stem. Instead, the suffix “-ary” will be identified as the longest occurring suffix; only the “-ary” will be removed. No recoding rule exists for the ending “-ion”, so it is left as is with the stem “revolution”. The noun “revolver” is derived by adding the suffix “-er” to the verb “revolve”. Formation of the noun “revolver” may also be attributed to the process of backformation: When the agentive suffix “-er” is added to the verb, the result is a descriptive noun which identifies some thing based on one (or more) of its characteristics. Such a word is dissimilar, both semantically, and also morphologically, from its apparently similar counterparts. A stronger algorithm could result in all three words, revolution, revolve, and revolutionary, stemming to “revolut”. Though such strength may be relevant for some applications, the weaker algorithm may be better-suited to idea consolidation.

Larsen [32] proposes a “surrogate database” of condensed text for storage of the stemmed content keywords. This conceptual database structure can be used for term matching, locating all occurrences of a particular stem with the original document, or as an inverted file to provide an index to documents that have been stored within the surrogate database. We have stored the conceptual database of keywords in a matrix. The keywords are then grouped using cluster analysis.

## 3.2. Best-match grouping

Various techniques for matching have been developed including the use of inverted files $[35,43,47]$ , bounding procedures $[6,17,19,41]$ , hashing $[14,44]$ , the Voronoi diagram, Euclidean space $[5,54]$ , and dimensional feature spaces $[15,18]$ . The technique used here is a somewhat simpler approach that involves the identification of clusters in the stemmed content keyword matrix. The clustering algorithm takes into account the relative frequency of keywords (for example, the same keyword may appear several times in the same comment), the number of unique keywords in each comment (and thus, the span of its content), and limitations on the “relatedness” of similar comments.

The use of the idea consolidation tool can be illustrated in an example involving some trivial comments that represent results from a brainstorming session:

(1) This is my first comment.

(2) This is another comment. Group with first.

(3) I think computers are productive.

(4) Computers and printers are productive.

(5) Computers can help you.

The idea consolidation tool automatically parses out the keywords from this list of comments (by eliminating function words and stemming other words) and generates the keyword matrix shown in Table 1. The comments are then grouped as (1,2) (3,4,5) (6,7) since these have similar keywords, and a new file of grouped comments is generated.

Actual electronic brainstorming files are much larger and complex. Some may contain up to 1500 comments with 900 identified keywords. More importantly, most comments are not likely to be as clearly grouped as in the example. Many clusters are likely to overlap, as in the following:

(1) Our product sales are up.

(2) Sales and profits are up.

(3) Profits at Acme and XYZ are also up.

(4) Acme and XYZ have more personnel and funding.

(5) More funding allows XYZ to hire more personnel.

(6) Who decides about hiring and firing?

In the example above, it is not as clear what issues or topics are involved, since there is more overlap of keywords in the matrix (Table 2). There are issues of sales, profits, Acme, XYZ, personnel, hiring, and funding. In the manual process, different people would have differing opinions on the issues, and they would have to argue among themselves when consolidating their own lists of issues into the final group list. The idea consolidation tool would cluster the comments as (1,2) (3,4,5,6).

In addition to these limitations of grouping, this automated consolidation process also suffers from the problem of occasional misspellings. For example, the tool will not recognize that “computer” and “compter” were meant to be the same thing though one is not spelled correctly. Also, there is the problem of synonyms and related topics. Without defining these beforehand, the idea consolidation tool does not recognize that “automobile” is the same thing as “car”, and consequently, comments referring to the same topic may not be grouped. This is the price that must be paid, however, to process a virtually unlimited vocabulary of common English words, proper names, and jargon, without defining comprehensive knowledge bases prior to the meeting.

## 3.3. Implementation

The GroupSystems software is implemented on IBM-PC AT compatible microcomputers connected via a local area network in a decision room environment with the group members seated in a semi-circle around a facilitator at the front of the room. The electronic brainstorming tool collects each group participant's file of comments into one file on the file server at the end of the idea generation phase. The group facilitator (at the front of the room) then sends this file or parts of it out again to each participant for manual grouping of comments using the Idea Organizer in the subsequent phase with the problems of decreased satisfaction and productivity.

Alternatively, the idea consolidation tool can be used by the facilitator on this file of comments, obviating the need to send the file out to the participants for manual grouping. The meeting may adjourn for a short break, or, since the tool takes so little time, participants may wait at their terminals. They may then either accept the groupings of the idea consolidater, or they may modify the file as desired. In either case, the efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction of the meeting participants is increased considerably.

## 4. Experimental study

Experiments were conducted on eight randomly-chosen historical cases of GroupSystems electronic brainstorming sessions to determine the efficiency and effectiveness of the Idea Consolidater (IC). In these studies, the tool was used to group EBS comments, and then these groupings were compared with the actual grouping of comments by the participants using the Idea Organizer (IO) tool.

[51]. Recall was defined as the number of relevant comments in each group divided by the total number of comments in the group. The recall for the case as a whole was defined as the average recall of all the groups in the file of comments. Similarly, precision was defined as the number of irrelevant comments in each group divided by the total number of comments in the group. This percentage was then subtracted from 100%. High percentages for both recall and precision were desirable.

Results of the eight case comparisons show that the idea consolidation tool took on average 1.12 minutes with 100% recall and 100% precision of groupings while the manual process took on average 42.5 minutes with only 82.6% recall and 73.1% precision on average (Table 3). The idea consolidation tool saves on average 97.6% of the time spent manually grouping comments with a maximum savings of 99.2%. The minimum saving of time was 95.9%. The variance in time is dependent upon the number of comments and identified stemmed keywords in the file.

In all cases, idea consolidater produced fewer groupings: this is probably due to the fact the participants using Idea Organizer have widely-varying ideas about the issues, consequently increasing the number of comment groups.

In similar experiments comparing related and unrelated documents, precision rates of up to 95% and recall rates of up to 50% were found [10,66]. When knowledge bases of related terms are defined before querying textbases, almost perfect recall and precision rates have been reported [38]. Idea Consolidater also has nearly perfect recall and precision without predefined knowledge bases (excluding the lists of function words, suffixes, and prefixes). The same standards of relevance and irrelevance were used with both the manual and automated groupings. Much stricter standards of what ideas are relevant would consequently lower the recall and precision rates for both the manual and automated groupings.

## 5. Conclusion

This paper details the development and application of an idea consolidation tool used in conjunction with idea generation software at the

University of Arizona. Idea Consolidater groups ideas generated by meeting participants in less than 5% of the time required by the current manual process using Idea Organizer with nearly perfect recall and precision.

The University of Arizona's GroupSystems software is used at dozens of sites throughout the world by thousands of people, and experience has shown that idea generation and organization occur in the vast majority of these meetings. The current process for consolidating the ideas generated is slow, unproductive, inefficient, error-prone, and unsatisfying. Idea Consolidater should alleviate this significant bottleneck in group meetings.
