# An examination of end-user types

Stephen J. Schiffman

Babson College, Wellesley, MA, USA

Larry C. Meile

Babson College, Wellesley, MA, USA

Magid Igbaria

Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, USA

An empirical study is presented; it verifies that types of computer end users differ significantly with respect to system usage and dependency. The differences are shown to exist even when controlling for differences in attitudes towards EUC, organizational support, end-user training, and demographics. The results suggest that existing structural models for end-user computing should be refined to take these differences among end users into account.

Keywords: End-user computing, User types, System usage, System dependency, User attitudes, Organizational support, User training, End-user management.

These types, they observed, “differed significantly from each other in computer skills; method of computer use; application focus; education and training requirements; support needed; and other variables....” The focus of our paper is to examine differences among them.

Amoroso [2] and Sipior and Sanders [25] have pointed out the lack of a consistent definition of end-user computing (EUC). The latter, in reviewing the literature, integrated the various definitions into a statement which highlights “the development and use activities associated with the employment of computer resources by one or more non-DP professionals in functional areas to perform and facilitate job related tasks and responsibilities....” For our purposes, we accept this definition but allow for the inclusion of MIS professionals.

System usage and user satisfaction are the primary measures of system success used in the study of EUC $[10,11]$ . System usage has been measured by the number of tasks performed using the system, the number of different packages employed, how often the system is used, and/or how much time is spent using the system. Ives, Olson, and Baroudi $[16]$ argue that system usage is a valid measure provided usage is voluntary, which is generally the case in the EUC milieu. Satisfaction has also been used as a surrogate for system utility (success).

Differences in system usage and user satisfaction can be due to many factors, not just end-user type. The range of factors that have been studied is quite broad. Igbaria, Pavri, and Huff [15] showed that microcomputer usage was related to computer experience. Lee [17] reported microcomputer usage to be correlated with prior computer knowledge. Nelson and Cheney [18] found computer-related training to be correlated with computer-related ability and this ability in end users correlates with acceptance of information systems technologies. Furthermore, certain demographic characteristics such as age and gender may be related to use.

It has also been found that attitudes mediate system usage. Trice and Treacy [27] discuss the “theory of reasoned action” which lies behind these attitudes. Igbaria [14] showed that user training and top management support directly affect attitudes and microcomputer usage, and that computer experience, training, organizational support, and demographic variables have indirect effects through attitudes.

It should be noted that system usage and user satisfaction are employed by researchers as surrogates for more direct but problematic measures such as value $[3,12]$ or utility in decision making $[6,9]$ . However, because of questions regarding the reliability and validity of user satisfaction measures $[7]$ and the importance of investigating actual end-user behavior, system usage has been chosen as our measure of information systems success.

The major hypothesis we test is that system usage varies by end-user type. As we move through their range from nonprogrammers to professionals, we expect to find increasing use. This hypothesis, if supported, will point to the need to include these differences when managing and supporting EUC systems and when modeling EUC effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction. Additionally, we are interested in how the user types differ in their attitudes toward and dependency on EUC.

## 2. Method

## Sample and procedure

A regional engineering company was chosen for this study. EUC had been used by this firm for several years and was continuing to grow. Management recognized the existence and value of EUC and recently had become more interested in controlling, supporting, and planning for it because it had become a significant part of the IS structure.

The user services manager of the company's MIS department administered the survey. Out of approximately 650 employees at the company's main office, 300 were identified as computer end users, based on lists of employees who had received personal computers or had mainframe/minicomputer accounts. Each computer end user received a written questionnaire which was distributed by company mail. A cover letter signed by two company vice presidents accompanied the survey. Out of the 300 questionnaires distributed, 212 were completed and returned representing a response rate of 71 percent. The exclusion of these incomplete questionnaires resulted in a final sample of 209 end users.

Education levels are high with 80% of the respondents having bachelor's degrees or higher and 46% having master's degrees or higher. Ninety percent of the sample arc between the ages of 25 and 55, with 50% being in their 30s. Only 14% of the respondents were female, but this is in line with the company's end-user population according to the user services manager. Forty-two percent of the respondents manage or supervise other employees. The number of years an individual has been employed by the company has a median of 8 years, a mean of 8.7 years and a standard deviation of 5.5 years. The length of time in current job has a median of 3.5 years, a mean of 4.3 years and a standard deviation of 3.4 years. Job types spanned from secretarial to professional and managerial. The majority of the sample, 68%, are engineers and scientists; 12% are computer professionals; 11% have management or supervisory job descriptions; 5% are secretarial or public relations; and 4% are financial, accounting, or purchasing personnel.

## Measures

Type of end user. Respondents were asked to identify themselves as one of the five particular end-user types: nonprogramming end user, command level end user, end-user programmer, functional support person, or professional MIS person. The first four of these types match Rockart and Flannery's types; the last is a collapsing of two Rockart and Flannery categories: EUC support personnel and DP programmers. Respondents were provided with short descriptions of end-user types to help them identify into which category they best fit. The descriptions had been externally validated in preliminary research using a similar survey instrument $[24]$ .

System usage. Based on several studies $[4,8,20,26]$ , four indicators of EUC usage were included in this study: (1) number of business tasks; (2) number of packages used by the participants; (3) frequency of use; and (4) time of use. Participants were asked to describe all business tasks they accomplish with the help of EUC. The number of tasks was recorded as an indicator of usage. In an interactive computing environment, users have a wide choice of software packages. The number of different ones used can provide a good indication of overall usage and of the variety of tasks performed on the microcomputer. Respondents were asked to list all the software packages they use; this number became the second variable recorded. Frequency of use, measured as the number of times per day, week, month, or year, provided another perspective on use. The participants were finally asked to indicate the amount of time they used the system per day, week, month, or year. The two latter measures were standardized by converting them to number of times or amount of time per year.

Dependency. Another measure distinct from but related to system usage is “dependency”. Nolan [19] suggests that an unsuccessful information system will cause the user to abandon or reduce use of the system. Although dependency as a measure does not seem to be established in the literature, we chose to use it here. Respondents were asked to assess on a five-point Likert scale, from totally dependent (1) to not at all dependent (5), the extent to which they depend or rely on EUC to carry out their tasks.

Attitudes and beliefs. This assesses a particular end-user's attitudes and beliefs about EUC systems. The eight items used to construct the measure were adapted from Sanders [23], with minor modifications to make them relevant to the study of EUC. For each of the eight items, individuals were asked to indicate the extent of their agreement using a five-point Likert scale from strongly agree (1) to strongly disagree (5). The internal consistency/reliability (alpha) of the eight-item scale was 0.82.

Organizational support. Management's encouragement to use computers and provision of access to computer equipment were the two dimensions of organizational support. In the literature these activities sometimes fall under the rubric of the organizational environment as described by Amoroso. Rivard [21], however, views support as a technological factor, being easier to control than some organizational factors such as management's readiness for change. Management support was measured using a five-point Likert scale by asking the participants to indicate their agreement or disagreement with the following item: "My manager encourages me to use computers."

Accessibility and adequacy. The workers' access to a computer was measured by asking the respondents to indicate: "How convenient is it for you to get access to a computer?" using a five-point Likert scale from always (1) to rarely (5). In addition, we asked the participants to indicate whether: "The equipment and its availability are adequate for current needs." The average of the two items was used to represent accessibility and adequacy (alpha = 0.72).

End-user computer training. Respondents were asked to list relevant classes they have taken. The number was used as an indicator of training. Respondents were also asked to identify other training materials they use, including computer-aided instruction and software documentation. The number of material sources identified was used as another indicator of end-user training.

Demographics. The respondents' gender, age, education, organizational level, organizational tenure, and job tenure were also recorded. Age included 10 groups ranging from (1) under 25 to (10) over 64 in a range of 5 years each. The highest level of education completed included 6 levels: (1) grade school; (2) high school; (3) associate degree; (4) bachelor's degree; (5) master's degree; and (6) doctoral degree. Organizational level consisted of two tiers: (1) worker without supervisory responsibilities and (2) supervisor. Organizational tenure was indicated by the length of time with the company; job tenure by the length of time in the present job.

## Data analysis

One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) by user type was performed on all the variables to determine whether different types of users differed in demographic variables, organizational support, user training, attitudes toward the EUC systems, system usage, and dependency. If differences were found, it would be necessary to control for these variables. These differences were examined with a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) with type of end user as the independent variable, the predictors of system usage were the covariates, and the four system usage dimensions (frequency of use, time of use, number of applications, and number of tasks) were the dependent variables.

Since dependency is affected by system usage, differences among types of end users with respect to dependency were also examined using MAN-COVA. Type of end user was the independent variable, the predictors of dependency, including system usage indicators, were the covariates, and dependency was the dependent variable.

Because attitudes toward EUC have been reported to be affected by demographic variables, end-user training, and organizational support, we controlled for these covariate variables as well. Intercorrelations were used to examine the relations between system usage and demographic variables, organizational support, end-user training, attitudes toward EUC, and dependency.

## 3. Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and matrix of intercorrelations. The matrix shows that among the demographic variables, education is positively correlated with attitudes toward EUC, frequency of use, and time of use. Among the environmental variables, we found that management support, accessibility and adequacy, and the two dimensions of end-user training are also positively correlated with attitudes toward EUC. Additionally, Table 1 shows that frequency and time of use are negatively correlated with age, organizational level, and organizational tenure.

Analysis of variance shows that females used the system less frequently, for less time, and for a fewer number of applications and business tasks than males. Frequency and time of use were also found to be positively correlated with management support, accessibility and adequacy, number of training courses, and number of material sources for training. Number of tasks and number of applications were found to be affected more by training than by support. Consistent with our expectation, attitude toward EUC was found to be positively correlated with all four dimensions of system usage. Finally, dependency is positively correlated with education, management support, accessibility and adequacy, training, attitudes toward EUC, and the four dimensions of system usage, but is negatively correlated with age.

The results of analysis of variance are presented in Table 2, which shows that there are significant differences between the five types of end users in their age, management support, number of training courses, number of training material sources, attitudes toward EUC, the four dimensions of system usage, and dependency. These indicate that the command level end users are the oldest and the functional support persons are the youngest. As expected, the more knowledgeable the users, the more training courses taken and the more material sources used.

Organizational support was also found to be correlated with type of end user, where MIS professionals reported the highest level of encouragement. We found that the more knowledgeable user types had the most favorable attitudes toward EUC and the nonprogramming end users had the least favorable attitudes toward EUC. We also found that MIS professionals are much more dependent on computers, use them more frequently and for more time. Interestingly, end-user programmers were found to use the largest number of applications and perform the greatest number of business tasks on a computer.

## 4. Summary and conclusions

This study examined differences between types of end users, as reported by Rockart and Flannery. The results showed that their classification is empirically valid. Four indicators of system usage (frequency of use, time of use, number of software applications, and number of business tasks) as well as the degree of dependency were used to examine differences in the end-user types. In addition, we examined differences in the factors affecting system usage including attitudes toward EUC, training, demographic variables, and organizational support. We recognize the limitations that obtaining data from only one regional firm places upon the interpretation of the findings. Additional research will be necessary to verify how universally they will apply.

The major finding was that end-user types differed significantly in their computer usage. Frequency and time of system usage were both monotonic with respect to user type. MIS professionals used computer systems most frequently and for a longer period of time than other groups.

End-user types also differed in their number of software applications as well as the number of business tasks. Interestingly, neither of these relationships was monotonic. End-user programmers were found to use the largest number of software applications as well as perform the largest number of business tasks. This is encouraging since this group represents the level at which EUC can make the biggest contribution. The tasks are complex enough to be significantly assisted by computer technology and these users are knowledgeable enough to take advantage of it.

Apparently end users differed in their dependency on computers. MIS professionals were found to be most dependent: this group is in charge of developing applications and aiding end users and is responsible for computer operations and development. The least computer-dependent group is composed of non-programmer end users who perform relatively few tasks on computers. Dependence has resource implications. The more dependent a worker is on a technology, the more important it is that the organization provide requests for upgrades in technology. It is a managerial cost/benefit decision to support those requests; this can have significant productivity and morale implications.

Examination of the relation between individual and organizational characteristics to system usage and dependency shows that system usage is correlated with management support as well as system accessibility and adequacy. Organizational support as represented by management support and information center support are considered to be influential in helping end users build a computing infrastructure. Support can be given by providing a selection of potentially useful hardware and software tools and offering assistance and encouragement in applying computer technology to a wide variety of business tasks.

End-user attitudes toward the system also differ significantly. MIS professionals had the most favorable attitudes toward EUC, followed by functional support, end-user programmers, command-level, and non-programmer end users. We found that MIS professionals were encouraged to use computers more than any other groups; they also reported that new technology is more accessible and available to them than other groups.

They had also taken more training courses and used a wider variety of training materials.

All end-user types tended to use computer aided instruction and tutorials equally but only end-user programmers and MIS professionals tended to make substantial use of software documentation: most reference documentation requires substantial understanding of the software package and is primarily designed to give technical, syntactic, or functional details.

Figure 1 shows a model of the network of linkages between all variables in this study. The links represent correlations; it is not a path-analytic model.

Since existing causal models only consider users as a single group, it would be appropriate to reframe them incorporating user type. The relationships would have more predictive power if user type were considered. It may also be useful to expand these models to include dependency as an outcome. The results could provide useful insight to the management of EUC.

One set of relationships that needs to be further investigated is the effect of individual characteristics, organizational support, and knowledge (training) on attitudes and usage. Individual characteristics are not controllable by the individual and the organization has only moderate selection over them. There may be some preferable set of characteristics that a manager should seek in an end-user employee: it would be useful to determine them.

The nature of organizational support has been studied quite extensively $[1,5,13]$ , but directing them to the effects on each user type would be constructive.

The relationship between knowledge gained through experience or training and attitudes and usage for each user type needs clarification. Does increased knowledge expand the range of attractive opportunities to which EUC can be applied? Does the need to use the technology motivate the person to gain knowledge? Does increased knowledge improve attitude? Does a more favorable attitude lead to a willingness to work at improving knowledge? Knowing how each of these relationships operates for each type of end user would help management decide the best way to expend resources.
