# Effects of information system education and training on user satisfaction

# An empirical evaluation

Gayle J. Yaverbaum

Penn State Harrisburg, Middletown, PA, USA

John Nosek

Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, USA

This study links end-user computer education to user satisfaction. A questionnaire was designed and employed to evaluate the impact of education and training on user information satisfaction. The subjects, who are employed in a variety of organizations, were queried prior to and after having completed a specially designed course in the management of information systems. After the educational experience, an increase in total user satisfaction was perceived by the subjects. The study demonstrates, however, that the connection between total satisfaction and education must be qualified by the caliber of the services rendered by Information Systems (IS) and the quality of the information product.

Keywords: Information systems education, User information satisfaction, End-user.

This end-user phenomena has brought with it the challenge to provide computer related skills to allow a wide assortment of employees to employ it efficiently and productively [17].

Experts laud the value of computer related education. Moran [24] proposed that a user cannot complete a task without effective knowledge of it, just as a game cannot be played unless the rules are understood. Bronsema and Keen [8] discussed implementation education as a vehicle for change and suggested that the success of any information system implementation effort increases substantially if there is a strong commitment to education. To alleviate this predicament, the goals of our research were to:

(1) increase knowledge about computer education and training, and

(2) recommend actions that will lead to better education and training.

To achieve these goals, we studied user satisfaction and posed the following questions:

1. Does computer system education and training impact the overall satisfaction of the user?

2. Does it change one's attitude toward the Information Systems (IS) staff and services?

3. Does it change the manner in which a user views the information product?

4. Does it increase the end-user's perception of computer knowledge and lead to more involvement with systems?

## The research

## End-user training and education

Davis and Olson [11] differentiated the person who makes decisions based upon system output from one who develops systems. Rockart and Flannery [30] and McLean [23] defined the end-user as one who directly interacts with a computer. Our research uses a broader definition of end-user, as defined in Yaverbaum [33]: all people within the organization who interact with computer systems but are not systems analysts or programmers. This includes users of computer output.

Nelson and Cheney [26] distinguished between training and education; both imply formal transfer of knowledge, but education involves understanding abstract theory while training involves the accretion of skills necessary to perform a task.

Bronsema and Keen [8] described a taxonomy of education. Concrete experience is reflected in actual hands-on training. Abstract conceptualization refers to the theoretical issues which underlie IS, while reflective observation implies reviewing cases with the objective of reflecting upon issues. Finally, active experimentation occurs as people actually participate in activities related to the learned concepts.

This taxonomy combines the concepts of both education and training. We posit that this merging leads to quality education and that both education and training are important for successful implementation of IS. Education comprises a sequence of activities which focus on abstract conceptualization and reflective observation. Training, on the other hand, emphasizes concrete experience and active experimentation.

Based upon a review of the literature, we ask why educate? Apparently, education is needed because:

1. Lack of training results in less successful implementation [1,20].

2. Above average training is highly correlated to above average use of the system [14].

3. Education and training promote the three necessary stages of organizational change: unfreezing, moving, and refreezing [8].

4. Users who feel better trained have more positive attitudes towards the system and the data processing staff [20,22].

## End-user satisfaction

The link between user satisfaction, i.e. the user's attitude towards the system, and system utilization is well documented by research $[3,16,28]$ . Brady's $[7]$ conclusion that the computer has a more significant impact on an organization if the organization attends to the educational needs of management implies that education and training are critical to system utilization and, thus, satisfaction with the system.

In spite of the wealth of literature that extols user satisfaction as important in the measurement of IS products and services, Conrath and Mignen [9] found few organizations that measure user satisfaction. In fact, a study of 163 organizations found only 42 indicating that they had procedures in place to measure it.

Compounding the shortage of measurement procedures, the difficulty in understanding the relationship between education and training and user satisfaction is the questionable effect of user satisfaction on productivity. While researchers have found no reliable link between satisfaction and productivity $[2,31]$ , we found several arguments for the study of user information satisfaction (UIS).

First, employee satisfaction is an inherently worthwhile criterion because: (1) people's feelings about the circumstances and outcomes of work are tied to their general mental health and satisfaction [29], (2) links have been established between commitment to the organization and satisfaction [15], employee satisfaction, absenteeism, turnover [27], organizational image and employee satisfaction [18], and 3) a relationship exists between behavior and attitude; for example, behavior may give rise to attitudes, or at least to changes in attitude [19]. Second, motivation to use a system is an important outcome of IS education and training [6].

Finally, and perhaps most relevant to MIS managers, the study of the effects of education and training on user satisfaction is important due to their impact on discretionary use of IS: i.e., employees who choose to use the system instead of an alternative.

As a person ascends the managerial ladder, the use of a system increases at the discretion of that person. Indeed, systems developed to support semi-structured problems for senior managers may fail due to lack of use and not due to apparent quality of the IS. Therefore, it is critical to understand any and all factors that influence IS use.

## The UIS survey

As measures of satisfaction, attitude surveys are utilized to collect information pertaining to one's feelings about something or someone. Doll and Torkzadeh [12] reported on the development of an instrument to measure user computing satisfaction, i.e., of those users who program. A UIS survey designed by Bailey and Pearson [3] and later modified by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [16] focuses on general satisfaction.

To explore the relationship between user satisfaction and education and training, a form of the UIS survey was selected. It focuses on general satisfaction; this was deemed the most appropriate survey for our purposes, based upon our broad definition of user. It can also be argued that change in satisfaction can be an indicator of change in users' motivation and that the component questions should be important concerns of all IS managers.

The Bailey and Pearson survey contained 39 scales, each with 4 items. Ives, Olson, and Baroudi ranked the scales and eliminated a group based upon reliability, content validity, and construct validity, with two items from each scale. Although this produced an acceptable instrument, the team still believed it to be too lengthy. A final survey, therefore, was produced.

A shortened form, evaluated by Baroudi and Orlikowski [4], was used for our research. The survey measured thirteen variables related to user satisfaction of computer-based information services and products. A semantic differential technique evaluated two adjective pairs for each variable. The questionnaire analyzed subject attitudes relative to any one adjective pair by means of a seven interval Likert scale.

In addition to the measurements, it is possible to calculate a “total satisfaction” score and average score for three components: “EDP staff and services,” “information product,” and “knowledge and involvement.” Variables can thus be analyzed independently or within the categories aforementioned. Although Baroudi and Orlikowski employed the term EDP to describe the information systems within a firm, we perceive IS to be more descriptive of modern systems. Therefore, unless directly quoted, the term, IS, is applied in this paper. The groupings are:

IS Staff and Services: The respondents' assessment of the attitude and responsiveness of the IS staff and the quality of the relationship with the IS staff.

Information Product: The respondents' assessment of the quality of the output delivered by the IS.

Knowledge and Involvement: The respondents' assessment of the quality of the training, their understanding of the system, and their participation in its development.

## The experiment

MBA students enrolled in an introductory Management Information Systems (MIS) course at Temple University were used to test the value of education and training as it relates to end-user satisfaction. This setting was chosen because:

(1) The course was designed to include the four sequences of events: concrete experience, abstract conceptualization, reflective observation, and active experimentation.

(2) The course was designed to adhere to sound principles of education and training: the course is of high quality.

(3) Most students enrolled in this course are part-time students who are employed elsewhere. Only those students who could be classified as users employed in organizations participated in the study.

(4) The only common influence on all participants was the education and training.

Seventy-three students volunteered to participate in the study. All were college graduates with varying amounts of computer system experience. For anonymity, students were not asked to name their organizational affiliation. Few were from the same organization, and even where the employer was identical the actual jobs differed.

The experimental group, for the most part, spent less than 25% of their time each day actually using the computer. Although some reported no direct computer contact, they did utilize computer generated material. Fifty-six percent reported using a PC and twenty-two percent actually owned a computer. Assorted software was used; approximately one third of the participants reported the use of spreadsheet, database, or word processing software.

The attributes of the participant group are summarized in Table 1; it shows that our sample was diverse in computer experience, computer background, and age. The common link was their classroom experience.

A questionnaire was administered during the first week of the course. Its purpose was to establish a baseline of the participants' satisfaction with their data processing services and products.

The results of a second questionnaire, completed during the final week of the course, were compared with the first results; however, we must admit that such longitudinal studies may be influenced by other factors in the participant's organization. However, this study involves employees across so many organizations that the breadth of the study should reduce such effects.

We compared results of the UIS survey using two measures of statistical significance: analysis of variance, calculated using the SAS GLM procedure $[13]$ and the Chi Square test. The GLM procedure was chosen to adjust for drops, absenteeism, or other miscellaneous causes of a non-equal sample size; the Chi Square test was used to determine if relative differences in satisfaction means occur by chance or depart from it significantly. By assessing relative change, we were able to observe the significance of distribution along the interval scale as opposed to the differences in average means. Moreover, we believe that relative frequencies are more realistic than nominal frequencies. Therefore, the Chi Square figures represent cell frequencies as percentages of the total number of students who completed the questionnaire.

The meaningful positive changes are indicated by “processing of change requests” and “time required for system development.” A noticeable shift in the distribution was observed by examining the actual data. Cautious interpretation of these results suggests that users acquired a better appreciation of the difficulty in developing systems and the problems involved in processing change requests.

However, expectations of what constitutes good relations between users and the DP department may have risen, accounting for the downward shift in perceptions of “communication with EDP staff” and “attitude of EDP staff.” Reviewing the actual data, we observed a marked increase in persons ranking these variables in the four range. Previously, a cluster of participants classifying the variables used to interpret the above perceptions chose six.

The “information product,” i.e. system output, is measured by its “relevance,” “accuracy,” “reliability,” and “precision.” Shifts revealed significant change in relative cell frequencies over the semester. The group perceived output to be more precise after completing the course. The major change in actual distribution was away from the average or slightly below to a ranking of six. The participants appeared to be more critical of “reliability,” “relevance,” and “accuracy” of output. A greater tendency to choose four rather than lower numbered choices was observed.

An increased perception about “user’s understanding of systems” supports the fact that the group as a whole had an improved understanding of the product. A greater proportion of the participants were inclined to select six rather than the previous propensity toward three.

The other significant variable in “knowledge and involvement” is the perception of “training provided users.” After taking this course, there was a significant downward shift of opinion concerning effectiveness of their organization’s efforts to educate and train. On this instance, however, the range of rankings narrowed and shifted towards four. Some persons perceived the training to be better while others perceived it as inadequate. In other words, there was a trend towards the ambivalent.

## Conclusions and future directions

The results indicate that education and training will lead to changes in user satisfaction and changes in user perceptions of IS staff and services, the end product, and knowledge and involvement with systems. The changes, however, are not necessarily positive. In some cases, the impacts on satisfaction are negative, indicating that improved knowledge leads to a better understanding of the computer and, consequently, less tolerance for the current information product and services. We offer the following as important observations from this study:

1. The experimental group, after receiving more education, had less tolerance for the poor attitudes of IS staff toward the user and for the deficiencies in user communication. A similar observation relating to communication was noticed, i.e. more people shifted to the more ambiguous “4” category. We attribute these results to increased knowledge about systems and thus greater expectations.

2. There was improved understanding of change processing and development time after the course had been completed. The students were more understanding of the functions of IS and, therefore, exhibited more tolerance for these processes. However, we note that the shift in distribution was again toward the more ambiguous four rather than a marked shift toward a high ranking. These values for “processing of change requests,” “time required for system development,” and “training provided to end users” indicate critical areas for MIS managers.

3. The information product was perceived in a more critical light after course completion, but the perceived “precision of output” was significantly more positive due to an increased ability to interpret it.

4. There was a shift in perceptions of the quality of education and training that organizations provide to users. Although there is little difference in the mean score, participants were more ambivalent about the quality of the training. We attribute this to the fact that the students perceived the current education to be valuable. Also, because people took this course voluntarily, they most likely value education and training. Therefore, they become either critical of organizational efforts in this area or more positive to organizational efforts.

5. As a consequence of a the educational experience, users have a better understanding of the computer system and its capabilities.

This study provides interesting implications about IS training and education. Most important is the value of the educational experience and the delegation of funds to support it and measure its impact.

As already noted, there are always likely to be problems with longitudinal studies: we do not know what other variables were present in the environment of each organization. Additionally, there may be some bias caused by the fact that our participants could have a predisposition towards the importance of education.

Finally, we need to study education and training in a more controlled way and to assess previous knowledge. Our education was within a university curriculum. Further study of organizational effects in general are needed.

## Future directions

Our study suggests many important observations and questions for future research:

1. Based upon the significant change in the distribution of cell frequencies pertaining to users' attitudes toward the processing of change requests and time required for system development with education and training, MIS managers may be able to alter the perceptions of these areas. A greater effort to educate users about the reason for these delays may be more cost effective and possible than an actual reduction.

2. Will more education and training suffice? Or, should we look at the quality of our existing programs?

3. We must seek better understanding about the underlying perceptions of user satisfaction components that were ranked at mediocre levels or less.
