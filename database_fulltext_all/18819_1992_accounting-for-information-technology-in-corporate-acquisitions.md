# Accounting for information technology in corporate acquisitions \*

Maylun Buck-Lew

Northeastern University, Boston, MA, USA

Caroline E. Wardle \*\*

Boston University, Boston, MA, USA

Nava Pliskin \*\*\*

Suffolk University, Boston, MA, USA

Corporate acquisitions have become key elements in strategic planning for many companies. Historically, analysts of corporate acquisitions have emphasized strategic and organizational factors in evaluating candidates for friendly takeovers. Since company data and information technology (IT) are as much a management resource as are the financial and human resources for the combined firm, the authors propose that IT fit should be explicitly considered in analysis of corporate acquisitions. An assessment of IT fit will refer to the IT environments of the two joining firms, the IT contribution each firm can bring to the combined firm, and the role that IT should play both in negotiating the acquisition price and in integrating the joining firms.

Keywords: Information technology, Corporate acquisition, Information technology fit, Strategic planning.

There is a growing recognition in the business community that integration has been quite difficult and that rates of success have been lower than expected. As we enter the 1990s, the complexity of the business environment is not expected to diminish and the question of how best to manage corporate acquisitions remains of great interest. This article addresses the important, yet often neglected, issue of Information Technology (IT) in friendly takeovers. (Note that in the context of mergers and acquisitions, an acquisition refers to buying a company. However, in this paper, we also use the term corporate acquisition to avoid confusion with IT acquisition in an information systems context).

When the acquiring firm intends to integrate the operations of the target firm with its own, a critical dimension of managing the acquisition is the assessment of “fit” between the two firms. Jemison and Sitkin $[7,8]$ consider two dimensions, strategic and organizational fit, to be important determinants of acquisition success. Strategic fit reflects the extent to which the acquired firm can contribute to, augment, or complement the strategy of the acquiring firm. Organizational fit considers how the administrative systems, corporate cultures, or personnel characteristics of the acquiring firm match those of the acquired firm.

Assessment of strategic and organizational fit is important in analysis of corporate acquisitions before the deal is struck, since the extent of fit can impact the value of the deal. It is also relevant to achieving a smooth integration afterwards, since the planning for and the actual integration can benefit from early recognition of the extent of fit.

While assessing fit offers important insights throughout the lifecycle of a corporate acquisition, the question arises: is a two-dimensional view of fit sufficient? To shed light on this question, we searched the literature and studied recent corporate acquisitions. This led us to believe that the current concept of fit is incomplete: IT, in the modern business world, is embedded in administrative systems (and vice versa) and is inexorably entwined with business activities. Most firms nowadays rely on IT for routine operational procedures. For many, IT is also important in gaining strategic advantage. This dependence of business organizations on IT is growing steadily.

Still, in the role of acquirers, many companies emphasize strategic and organizational factors in evaluating candidates for friendly acquisitions, but only implicitly account for IT, if at all. We propose that IT fit be explicitly considered in analysis of corporate acquisitions, because it can lend robustness to valuation of the deal and improve the integration process that follows. An assessment of IT fit will consider the IT environments of the two joining firms, the IT contribution each firm can bring to the combined firm, and the role that IT should play in negotiating the acquisition price and in merging the two firms.

## 2. Complexities of corporate acquisitions

Both academics and practitioners agree that acquisitions are strategic endeavors pursued by more and more players $[12]$ . However, Linder $[10]$ has found that reports in both the trade and the academic literature place acquisition success rates at no more than 50%: either expectations were not met, divestitures followed, or failure was acknowledged.

None of the foreseeable trends are expected to help improve the prospects of corporate acquisitions. Among the complex dynamics of the modern business environment that make matters more difficult are globalization, fierce local competition, shorter product lifecycles, rapid technological progress, environmental constraints, and governmental regulation.

While recognizing future complexities, it is instructive to understand the present dynamics of corporate acquisitions. The parties involved do not completely control the process and may face unavoidable situations because of time, secrecy, and the diversified backgrounds or interests of people involved [6].

These issues can have a negative impact at different points. Analysis and planning for integration cannot receive proper attention if the timetable or confidentiality guidelines are too severe. Decision makers and their consultants bring into acquisition negotiations diverse perspectives, agendas, and goals, which interact with time pressures to diminish the quality of analysis and planning. Objective integration of varying perspectives is difficult within a short time and decision makers tolerate ambiguities, allowing the more tangible and the more quantitative perspectives to dominate. As a result of poor analysis and planning, the integration process itself may suffer. While it is possible to agree to disagree early in the acquisition process, disagreements tend to surface during integration and very often reach unmanageable proportions and turn to irreversible mistrust. There is also a change of guard after the deal is struck: the integration task is usually delegated to line managers who are rarely invited to contribute to pre-acquisition deliberations. Their ability to implement the integration is inhibited by being required to cope with disagreements and mistrust created by others.

Our concern is with the ability of decision makers to engage in an effective assessment of fit during analysis and planning. In addition to the limitations (in terms of time, secrecy, and participant mix) which interact in a way that may limit the depth of analysis, the assessment of organizational fit is usually more superficial than that of strategic fit for two reasons. First, since decision makers dealing with strategic fit are accustomed to strategic planning and the standardized analytical approaches it involves, they find it easier and quicker to discuss issues concerning strategic (rather than organizational) fit. Second, negotiators are relatively remote from actual operating experiences in the two firms. Hence, they tend to focus on strategic issues rather than on organizational or operational issues (in the domain of lower-level line managers).

## 3. Information technology and the assessment of fit

In the conventional two-dimensional assessment of fit, accounting for IT is at best superficial. First, since the IT environment of any firm is closely meshed with administrative systems, corporate cultures, and personnel characteristics, assessment of organizational fit should provide the most natural opportunity to consider IT. However, as long as assessment of organizational fit receives less attention than assessment of strategic fit, so will implicit assessment of IT fit. Second, within the context of strategic fit, consideration of IT is likely only when the acquiring firm considers IT critical to its strategic goals [11]. For many firms, however, IT is not yet strategic. Thus, in the majority of cases IT is likely to be overlooked on both dimensions of fit assessment.

Outside of the corporate acquisition arena, accounting for IT is more explicit [13,14]. Walton [15] suggests that there are three components to a firm's vision: business strategy, organization, and IT. Thus, it is essential for a firm to incorporate all three perspectives into one vision and to consider how each of the three perspectives may drive or be driven by the other perspective. It is no longer sufficient to focus on the strategic and organizational components of the vision and on the connection between them. As management research has evolved over the past two decades, it has become clear that the IT perspective must be explicitly considered alongside the strategic and organizational perspectives.

A consolidation of the two previous paragraphs suggests that assessment of IT fit will lend robustness to decision making prior to the acquisition (e.g., in negotiating the acquisition price) and will contribute to smooth operational integration afterwards (e.g., in focusing on types of information needed from the acquired firm).

Basically, assessment of IT fit concerns the match between the information environments of the acquiring firm and the acquired firm. The assessment also aims at capturing the IT contribution that each firm can bring to the combined firm and to the role that IT should play in developing a preferred strategy for IT integration.

The assessment will encompass not only the respective information infrastructures, but will also refer to the quality of information. The characteristics pertaining to the IT infrastructure (e.g., hardware, system and application software, telecommunications, emerging technologies, data administration, heterogeneity, effectiveness, efficiency, cost, cost/profit orientation, personnel quality) are relevant to exploiting opportunities and to dealing with threats associated with the acquisition. The rest, pertaining to the quality of information (e.g., availability, consistency, integrity, relevance, timeliness, and accuracy) are relevant to valuation of the deal, determination of risk, and planning the integration process. The rating of each characteristic will involve comparative evaluation of the strengths and weaknesses possessed by either the acquiring or the acquired firm.

Under the best IT fit, the acquiring company will possess the IT strengths to take advantage of attractive IT features of the acquired company, and it will be possible to rely on high quality information from both firms before and after the transaction. If the deal is struck, it might be most sensible to take the best each firm can offer. At the other end of the IT fit spectrum and under a significantly less desirable degree of IT fit, it will be highly probable that the acquirer be misinformed, overpay for the acquired firm, or fail to integrate the joining firms. If the deal is struck despite lack of IT fit, how it might be best to integrate IT would depend, for example, on company size. A very small acquirer might follow a strategy of total replacement, while a large acquirer might choose a strategy of incremental replacement with its IT environment dominating initially. Other strategies of IT integration, such as conversion to one of the IT environments or co-existence of both environments, might be more suitable for more intermediate degrees of IT fit.

Our proposal for assessment of IT fit does not imply that IT compatibility or complementarity are necessary for acquisition success. These are not essential, for example, if the acquirer wishes to implement a strategy of conversion to its own IT environment. However, if IT consolidation is a critical goal of the acquisition, then either a coexistence or a take-the-best integration strategy can benefit from compatibility and/or complementarity.

## 4. A detailed case study

A detailed discussion of an actual corporate acquisition will allow us to walk through our proposal for assessment of IT fit. The names of all firms discussed hereinafter are not included. To preserve anonymity we relate the process as “Company A bought Company B”.

The business strategy of Company A, a firm in the retail industry, includes growth and geographical diversity through acquisition of other retailers. Company B, was the first chain of department stores acquired by Company A in the north-east. This acquisition took place in the late 1980s, after Company A had successfully acquired several other retailers in the US.

There was a good strategic fit between the firms, in that they were both retailers reaching the market via department stores. However, organizational fit was not as good. The holding company that had owned Company B previously was very decentralized and allowed top executives of Company B to set the company's direction. Company A is very centralized, and from the outset of the acquisition it was clear that Company B's autonomy would be taken away.

IT is very important to Company A. In comparison with other large retailers, the IT environment of Company A is strong and includes regional data centers, each supporting several divisions housing IBM mainframes for batch work and Tandem computers for online work. According to Company A's IT strategy, all divisions use common systems and share regional data centers. Therefore, Company A's ultimate goal for IT at Company B, as with previously acquired companies, was to convert the systems of Company B over to the online systems of Company A.

Company A did account for IT during the acquisition process. IT at Company B was carefully examined before the deal was signed. Company B was just completing a 5-year upgrade from an antiquated batch systems running on IBM mainframes with DOS to online financial and merchandising applications running on an IBM 3090 with ISDF, CICS, TSO, and IMS. The new systems had produced a growth of the IT organization in status, size, and budget. The improved status of IT within Company B was also reflected by the fact that the vice president of IT reported directly to the chief executive officer.

Because the business policies and procedures at Company B differed markedly from Company A's, the systems at Company B and the systems at Company A initially ran in parallel. Within a few months, however, the data center at Company B was phased out, and Company B began using the online applications of Company A by running from the nearest data center. In addition, unique Company B applications were moved to the same regional data center.

From Company A's perspective, the advanced IT environment at Company B was very attractive, despite the ultimate intention to convert Company B's systems on to Company A's online systems. First, during the negotiation process, Company A requested and received quality information about Company B as well as a detailed report about Company B's IT environment. Both helped determining the value of the deal for Company A and also in planning the integration process.

Second, Company A harnessed the IT environment of Company B not only to help in the IT conversion but also in coping with the eventual centralization. In order to run Company B from Company A's corporate headquarters, Company A needed large quantities of detailed data early in the integration period. Indeed, within a few weeks after buying Company B, Company A's corporate management had access to all information necessary for the transition from decentralized to centralized management. Thus, the upgraded systems of Company B were able to capture the data at a low level, and Company A took advantage of this ability in their effort to provide a smooth operational integration.

Third, although Company A's practice of always converting online systems of the acquired firms to Company A's IT environment was followed, the IT sophistication of Company B not only enabled a smooth conversion under an extremely aggressive schedule but also allowed Company A access to advanced applications that Company B was running at the time of the acquisition. Thus, although the IT integration strategy was eventual conversion to the systems of company A, assessment of the degree of IT fit made an important contribution to effective management of the corporate acquisition and subsequent conversion.

## 5. Other examples of corporate acquisitions

Several banking examples suggest that companies involved in acquisitions simply cannot afford to overlook IT in assessment of fit. The banking industry ranks first among all industrial categories in the number of mergers and acquisitions from 1983 to 1987. The dismantling of the barriers against interstate banking in the early 1980s triggered this. Since small banks were at risk of being consumed by larger banks, banks sought to grow, through acquisitions, in order to survive and remain independent. Acquisitions across state boundaries were often justified to regulators and stockholders by the projected economies of servicing large branch networks with a single set of information systems [16].

In a recent study of two superregional banks, systems consolidation was unanimously recognized as an appropriate vehicle for reducing expenses $[10]$ . The bank executives looked to gain efficiencies through combining operations and all agreed that IT resources would be consolidated after the transaction. Yet IT was not considered in the pre-deal discussions and the bank executives had no interest in the task of IT consolidation. One of the banks had not yet completed IT integration, and it is now more than 3 years after the acquisition. Assessment of IT, along the line of this discussion, could have made a difference.

In contrast, Fleet Norstar is recognized as a superstar in successfully consolidating IT operations. In 1988 Fleet Norstar collapsed its five-state operations into one new super data center, saving \$10 million. The new super data center aids in absorption of the acquired business. Top management there views this IT flexibility, nicknamed the “acquisition machine”, as enabling aggressive acquisition [3]. By developing a strong IT environment, Fleet Norstar is able to pursue a strategy of converting the information systems of acquired banks to its super data center. Assessment of IT fit can thus be helpful in effectively pursuing integration.

The situation is similar in non-banking organizations. Black and Decker Corporation engaged in a friendly acquisition of Emhart Corp. IT at the two companies was studied, prior to signing the contract, and the IT environments were found to be compatible (the same IBM mainframes, same DBMS for corporate applications, etc.). This helped the chairman oversee a successful integration which has taken “the best systems and people from both companies” [2]. Assessment of IT fit, in this case, has helped in effective implementation of an IT integration strategy that takes the best that each IT environment can offer.

Incompatible technological environments in two joining firms can result in an expensive integration effort because “technical obstacles caused by incompatible information systems can negate some of the benefits from the merger or acquisition” [9]. When Ultramar Canada Inc. (Toronto, Ontario) acquired Gulf Canada Inc.’s Eastern retail business in 1986, it faced the problem of reconciling two distinct environments: Ultramar's six Wang VS systems and one IBM 3031, and Gulf's IBM-dominated environment. Eventually, Ultramar decided to adopt Gulf's systems, because the firm's business decision support systems were more developed. To get the systems in-house and running, Ultramar signed a 5-year, C\$20-million facilities management contract with The BST Group Inc. [5]. This is another example of the need to pay attention to the IT environments of both firms in order to optimize their integration.

Careful assessment of IT fit and detailed planning can often ameliorate problems arising in an integration effort. At National Semiconductor Corp., which acquired Fairchild Semiconductor Corp. in 1987, IT integration was recognized as an important element. National's first step was to integrate Fairchild's IT operations into its own by decentralizing Fairchild's worldwide distribution and customer service group, (while moving from IBM System/38's to National Advanced Systems mainframes). The next step was to bring Fairchild from DEC's Vaxmail to IBM's Professional Office System, ensuring that orders entered anywhere go into a common database under IDMS/R. The IT organization at Fairchild faced technical and sociological barriers because they had to decentralize both physically and managerially. Nevertheless, National Semiconductor met its deadlines for systems conversions [4].

In summary, the experience from past acquisitions in a variety of industries illustrates the importance of assessment of IT fit in corporate acquisitions both for valuation of the deal as well as for integration planning and implementation.

## 6. Conclusion

The IT environment is so interrelated with many key aspects of the business that as much attention should be paid to IT fit as to strategic and organizational fit. In this paper we proposed to expand the two-dimensional assessment of fit and to pursue an assessment of IT fit.

Each acquisition is unique and requires a customized and individual assessment of IT fit. IT fit reflects the extent of match between the IT environments of the two joining firms. The IT environment includes the technology infrastructure (computer hardware and software, communication hardware and software, database systems, etc.), the people and organization behind the technology (systems analysts and designers, application programmers, database administrators, network engineers, structure of the IT organization, etc.), and the quality information used to support the operations and strategy of the organization (type, volume, etc.).

Assessment of IT fit means taking a broad but not superficial look at what exists. The assessment for each company's IT environment should consider the quality of information and include the technology infrastructure, the IT organization, and a sizing-up of the use, support, administration, organization, and distribution of information and IT. The fit between the companies' IT environments is then determined.

The assessment of IT fit should show:

1. The IT contribution each firm brings to the combined firm.

2. The ways in which the IT environment of the acquired firm complements, augments, diminishes, or conflicts with the IT environment of the acquiring firm.

3. The role, if any, that IT should play in the negotiation of the acquisition price.

4. What lies ahead in the integration effort in terms of IT.

Some acquirers have a fixed integration strategy that does not depend on the IT environment of the acquired company. For example, acquiring firms that wish to convert the IT operations of the acquired firm to their IT environment. In this case, assessment of IT fit might be less relevant for pre-acquisition discussion, but it is still very important in achieving smooth operational integration. For acquiring companies entertaining a wider variety of options with respect to IT integration (e.g., to mesh the IT environments by taking the best from each or to let the IT environments co-exist with interfaces between them), assessment of IT fit is also important for making an informed choice.

In conclusion, assessment of IT fit is an important factor in the evaluation of an acquisition. Such an assessment can help create realistic expectations about the acquisition and provide insights into the complexities of the integration effort.
