# AUTOREF

# A deductive database for automatic referee selection

Amitava Dutta

University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA, USA

A central function in processing papers submitted for publication is that of finding suitable referees. Referees should be knowledgeable, have sufficient time, and be thorough and prompt in their reviews. Sometimes, specific knowledge, such as a known conflict of interest, will have a bearing on the process. While the record-keeping associated with submissions is often automated, the referee selection process is still done manually. We describe a prototype deductive database named AUTOREF, implemented in PROLOG, that can provide automated assistance in this selection process. AUTOREF strives to find the best referees, failing which, it progressively relaxes requirements to find the most suitable available ones. As a last resort, it goes into manual mode. The editorial activities supported by AUTOREF are generic, and could be customized for most specific requirements. Automated assistance for referee selection might further improve quality and efficiency of the editorial process.

Keywords: Referee selection, Editorial function, Deductive database, Logic, Prolog.

## 1. Introduction

A major task performed during the processing of a paper submitted for possible publication is that of finding “suitable” referees to evaluate the paper. All other processing activities, such as acknowledgements, recordings, mailings, etc., are really ancillary. For purposes of this paper, the entity to which a paper is submitted will be termed the journal, and the person(s) responsible for selecting referees, evaluating reviews, and making a final decision on whether or not to accept the paper is called the editor. Informally, selection of a referee requires a determination of the subject areas of the paper, followed by identification of a few individuals who are knowledgeable in those or closely related areas. However, additional factors play an important role. A potential referee should not already be overloaded with pending reviews (at least from this journal) and should be careful, thorough, and timely in producing reviews. Not infrequently, fragments of information are available to the editor, such as knowledge that one of the authors and a potential referee are bitter competitors, or are from the same “camp”. All such factors should be taken into account to maintain the integrity and efficiency of the refereeing process. While most journals and editors have automated much of the record-keeping associated with submissions, the difficult task of selecting referees is, by and large, still performed manually and is potentially time consuming. Here, we propose to provide automated assistance for this selection process. The use of deductive techniques is particularly appropriate for this purpose, because the manual selection process invokes similar activities in combining different pieces of information about a referee. Our prototype system, called AUTOREF, is written in Turbo PROLOG $^{®}$ [1], with the data being stored as a “dynamic database”.

Informally, AUTOREF proceeds as follows. It attempts to find three good referees for a submission, failing which, it automatically tries to find two good referees; two good referees being assumed to be better than several mediocre ones. Failure of the second alternative prompts further relaxation of selection requirements. When it is not able to find any referees, AUTOREF goes into a manual mode, where the user essentially finds a referee.

## 2. Structure of the database

The database structure is shown in Figure 1. It stores generic items recorded by any journal, such as referee addresses, submission data, and referee reports. There has been no attempt to customize the database for any specific journal. A snapshot of a specific instance of this database appears in the appendix. Certain nuances of the editorial process, such as special handling of a revision, could be easily incorporated, but have been ignored in the prototype. Most of the relations are self-explanatory. The REF\_AREA relation records information on areas which the referee is knowledgeable. The closed world assumption [4] is made: if the database does not show a referee as having a particular interest, s/he is assumed not to be interested in it. The REF\_PERFSMRY relation is progressively updated for each referee as each review is returned. It is examined when judging whether a referee is chronically late or otherwise incompetent.

A variety of conclusions, not explicitly stored, can informally be deduced from these database relations. These deductions play a significant part in the referee selection process. For instance, subject areas overlap and many are related, an example being physical and logical database design. Such facts are helpful in selecting referees, since one with expertise in a related area is a candidate for evaluating a paper. An examination of raw data can indicate if a referee is often chronically late, or makes cursory or careless reviews. ## 3. The deductive knowledge base

Deductive databases combine general knowledge with existing facts in a database to generate new information not explicitly stored in the database [3]. The reader is referred to [5] for a summary of key concepts and recent developments in the area. The heart of our deductive knowledge base has the tree-like structure shown in Figure 2. Execution of rules is controlled by PROLOG's depth first search mechanism. They have been shown in somewhat sanitized form and do not conform strictly to PROLOG syntax, solely for expository purposes.

![](/api/attachments/MS7KX9DB/fulltext/images/49b130781bd363380d3715dbd5443b2ecf554748b400302d5ea1e1c048e7e868.jpg)  
Fig. 2. AND-OR tree for deductive component of AUTOREF.

In Figure 2, “x” is the identification number of a submission. Predicates that denote database access are in italics. The process of finding appropriate referees for a submission is represented as an AND-OR tree. Following normally accepted notation, AND nodes have their outgoing edges connected by an arc. The tree indicates that, upon submission, a check is first made whether the submission should be held up due to some exception conditions. For example, if one or more of the authors have been poor referees, their submission could be flagged and processing held up. Assuming no holdup, the search process continues; it seeks to find as qualified a set of referees as possible by gradually relaxing requirements.

"Find\_3\_good" occurs when three good referees are found; they are then processed and the SUBMISSION relation is updated to reflect that it is now under review. Finding a good referee requires identification of an eligible referee, determining that s/he has a good performance record, is knowledgeable in the subject matter of the paper, and is "otherwise" acceptable. Eligibility requires that the referee no be one of the authors, and that his/her areas of expertise overlap that of the paper. Database accesses determine if an eligible referee has good performance. Currently, the check “otherwiseok” looks to see if one of the authors is a close friend of the referee; if so, the referee is disqualified. Additional constraints, such as author and referee being required to be from different organizations, can easily be included in this check. “Find\_2\_good” is similar to the task of finding three good referees, except that we are now satisfied with only two good referees.

“Find\_3\_soso” attempts to find three referees who, while not the most knowledgeable in the paper’s area, are reasonably suited for reviewing a paper. A “soso” referee is not one of the authors, and is knowledgeable in the paper’s area or a related area. There is no attempt to check if s/he has a good performance record or his/her level of expertise. This is a relaxation of the requirements for a good referee. Obviously, further relaxations are possible.

If AUTOREF cannot find three “soso” referees, it gives up and goes into manual mode, where it simply facilitates some querying on the part of the editor. Frequent causes of AUTOREF’s failure include referee overload, or incomplete knowledge about areas of expertise. The actions taken as a result of “giveup” are user controlled and we do not discuss them further. Next, we present example transactions that are supported by AUTOREF. Special emphasis is given to the transaction used for selecting referees for a submission.

## 5. Evolution of database and knowledge base

The most important part of the database, the collection of referees and subject areas, will grow over time. One possibility is to add to the pool of referees, authors of papers that gain final acceptance. This can easily be automated as part of the “Final Decision” transaction. Isolated facts, such as certain individuals being close friends or antagonists will perhaps only get recorded slowly over time. Obviously, AUTOREF cannot resolve controversies involving such subjective judgments as frequently have to be made by editors.

AUTOREF's structuring as a collection of rules, facilitates refinement of the deductive component over time. For instance, suppose a new gradation of referees was desirable, between "three\_good" and "three\_soso"; such referees should be quite knowledgeable, but need not have very good performance records. The corresponding rule can be simply added at the appropriate position in Figure 2, which would be just to the right of "find\_3\_good". Alternatively, we may want not to send a paper to a referee at the same organization. Again, a very simple new rule needs to ba added to the knowledge base. Thus, the reasoning based on which selection is made can become increasingly sophisticated over time.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Due to the complexity of criteria that determine acceptability of a referee, and the volume or raw data to be considered, manual execution of the selection task is time consuming and error-prone. Some degree of automated assistance can therefore improve both the efficiency and quality of the editorial process. Clearly, many of the problems that arise in managing the reviewing process lie completely outside the scope of a system such as AUTOREF. Several improvements to AUTOREF can be made. For instance, further sophistication can be achieved if the relationships between subject areas were deduced in a more elaborate manner, to include the strength of such relationships. Analysis of a referee's performance could be expanded to examine if s/he has a very high rate of rejections or acceptances. At present, addition of new rules to the deductive component requires direct PROLOG programming. A simple front end could be designed, wherein many of these new rules are automatically generated through simple interactions with a user. Thus the deductive component could incrementally become more sophisticated in its selection task.
