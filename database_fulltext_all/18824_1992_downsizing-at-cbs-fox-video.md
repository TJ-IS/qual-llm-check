# Downsizing at CBS/FOX Video

William J. Doll

University of Toledo, Toledo, OH, USA

Mark W. Doll

Doll & Associates, Toledo, OH, USA

Driven by top management interest in cost reduction, IS downsizing is rapidly emerging as one of the most significant phenomena in information resource management in the 1990's. Downsizing is a process of (1) migrating applications from a mainframe architecture to a mini- and/or microcomputer architecture, and (2) transferring responsibility for IS functions to line management. Firms recently downsizing their IS function report significant budget reductions and other benefits. This exploratory case study focuses on CBS/FOX Video, a half billion dollar a year firm in the home entertainment industry, that has received significant attention for the magnitude of its IS downsizing efforts. CBS/FOX Video has reported steadily reducing its annual IS budget and staff. To implement its downsizing strategy, this firm: (1) migrated applications from a large mainframe to a mini-and three microcomputer networks; (2) transferred many information processing functions to user departments; and (3) outsourced application development. The objectives of this case study were to describe and explore a phenomenon (downsizing) that is not well understood and to verify reported cost savings.

Keywords: Downsizing, IS downsizing, Microcomputer, Local area networks.

## 1. Introduction

In today's increasingly competitive business environment, corporate downsizing has emerged as a standard weapon in the chief executive's arsenal of techniques for improving cost effectiveness [15]. The target of this activity is white-collar employees – up to and including middle managers – in the administrative or overhead components of the business [32]. At first, the IS department was largely exempt from downsizing pressures. Efforts to improve productivity often required new systems. Now many firms, especially firms such as Eastman Kodak [20] whose IS expenditures as a percent of sales were twice that of their competitors, are beginning to question their choice of computer architecture and the way they manage information resources. IS downsizing is rapidly emerging as one of the most significant phenomenon in information resource management in the 1990's.

Until recently, MIS managers regarded downsizing as ripping the heart and soul out of their department – a move backwards to a less capable architecture. As MIS managers improve their understanding of the economics of downsizing, the concept is moving into the mainstream of IS practice. In firms like of TRW [14], Eastman Kodak [31], Consolidated Insurance Group [16], American Sterilizer [12], Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital [23], Home Mutual Life Insurance [7], Borg Warner [4], Rank Organization PLC [7], Playboy Enterprises [26], Schreiber Foods [9] and Echlin [17] who have re-sized their computer architecture to better fit their application portfolio, downsizing is considered an clement of prudent and decisive stewardship.

![](/api/attachments/Y7NUA4BT/fulltext/images/5399e65b4c9e13f1e53b97b0633f204ee2dc84d4b00cdcde94ead0d9b71a1ea0.jpg)

Downsizing is a process that involves (1) the migration of existing applications from a mainframe architecture to a mini and/or a microcomputer architecture and (2) the transfer of IS functions to end-users. It also includes efforts to design new applications so that they can be implemented on less costly platforms. Specifically, applications can be downsized from: (1) a mainframe to minicomputers; (2) a mainframe to minicomputers and microcomputer networks; (3) a mainframe to microcomputers or microcomputer networks; or (4) a mini to microcomputers or microcomputer networks.

Downsizing requires careful management of a firm's overall application portfolio structure [22]. At the application level, Scherr (1978) identifies price/performance, application complexity and the structure of an application as variables affecting the choice of architecture. Structured analysis and design concepts have gained wide acceptance; these top-down application design concepts call for the creation of programs that are cohesive within modules and loosely coupled between modules. Downsizing extends these concepts of cohesiveness and loose coupling, to the management of the firm's overall application portfolio structure. The goal is the decomposition of a firm's entire application portfolio into loosely coupled clusters of applications that facilitate the use of lower cost minis and microcomputer networks.

The downsizing process results in a distributed [1] and/or decentralized [19] computing environment. Thus, downsized firms share many of the benefits and hazards of distributed or decentralized computing. The benefits [13] include increased reliability, reduced interactive response time, expansion of capacity in small increments, increased motivation and involvement of users, and decreased cost due to relative simplicity and lack of frills. Potential hazards include control problems, undesirable duplication, incompatibility, and incompetent design and implementation. Should computing be centralized or decentralized? Organizational structure [5], politics, and technology trade-offs [28] affect the choice of computer architecture.

Despite growing top management interest and significant IS implications, little is known about the causes, the process, or the consequences of downsizing. A case of IS initiated downsizing at American Sterilizer $[12]$ has been reported. The CBS/FOX Video case involves a different implementation process. At CBS/FOX Video, top management initiated IS downsizing, an outsourcing strategy was used, and significant IS functions were transferred to end-users. Other reports of downsizing have been “applications descriptions” $[3]$ written by key participants or reporters who interviewed the key participants but had no independent knowledge of events. There is a need for carefully conducted case studies by independent researchers.

## 2. Case study methods

For several reasons, case research is particularly appropriate for the study of the downsizing process. First, the phenomenon is a recent trend that does not have an established theoretical base. Second, research and theory on this topic is in the early formative stages $[25]$ where: (1) we have little a prior knowledge of which variables will be of interest; and (2) the boundaries of the phenomenon are not evident. Third, downsizing is a practice based phenomenon where the experience of the actors is important, the process is influenced by events that happen unexpectedly, and the context of action is critical.

To identify leading edge firms in this area, the last two years of magazines such as Computerworld, Network World, Datamation, Telecommunications, PC Week, Network News, and LAN Magazine were reviewed. The researchers also conducted a computerized library search for articles on “downsizing” or “downsized” in Information Access’s Computer Database (CMPT) that indexes 204 computer magazines and journals. This computer search revealed 210 articles on downsizing that have been published since 1983. These articles were obtained and reviewed to identify revelatory cases. The researchers also contacted consultants knowledgeable about downsizing efforts.

CBS/FOX Video of New York City, was identified by an article in Network World entitled “CBS/FOX Video Abandons Mainframe for Local Nets” [33]. It is a revelatory and unique case. Of the cases identified, it was one the largest firms, had one of the largest mainframes (IBM 3081K) before it downsized, and reported [34] the largest reductions in IS budget (from \$5.2 million to \$800,000) and staff (from 58 to 5). Furthermore, the company’s willingness to participate in the case study and provide documentary evidence of estimated savings made them ideal for our research.

Data were gathered via structured personal interviews at the company's data center in Livonia, Michigan and corporate headquarters in New York City. The interviews were conducted using structured formats to ensure that critical aspects of the case could be verified by two or more respondents. Interviews were conducted with the V.P. of MIS, Mr. Howard Tatem, and his three managers – Director of Systems Management, Director of International Information Processing, and Director of Technical Support. The researchers also interviewed Mr. Pete Barker, Senior Vice-President and Chief Financial Officer, and eight managers in the firm's New York offices including: Vice-President and Worldwide Controller; Vice-President and Controller, North America; Vice-President of Marketing; Manager of Sales Administration; Manager of Operations; Director of Acquisition Planning; Manager of Royalty Accounting; and Manager of Product Tracking & Research. These managers were interviewed after their applications had been downsized.

In addition to these personal interviews, the researchers asked for documentary evidence. Of the over 1500 pages of original documents, 78 major exhibits (782 pages) were selected by the researchers. These major exhibits include: the IS strategic plan to downsize including estimates of costs and benefits, the IS department's budgeted and actual expenses from 1986 to 1990, top management correspondence concerning the downsizing effort, a series of organizational charts prepared after events precipitated changes, correspondence between the project leaders of the downsizing effort and the director of MIS, consultant reports, and system architecture diagrams before and after.

These procedures provided a rich data base while allowing us to examine the phenomenon in its natural setting, employing multiple methods of data collection to focus on managerial and organizational questions concerning how the context of the situation and the innovation interacted. The first step in data analysis was the preparation of a time-line of major events and the verification of the dates using documentary evidence. Next, the home video industry was examined to identify contextual factors influencing the downsizing decision. Finally, to ensure an accurate description of the situation, the events, and the thinking of key participants at CBS/FOX Video, Mr. Barker, Senior V.P. of Finance, and Mr. Tatem, V.P. of MIS at the time of the downsizing effort, were asked to review and comment on this manuscript.

## 3. The home video industry

The home video industry is a downstream stage in the movie industry's value chain. The upstream activities of producing and marketing a major movie can require huge sums of cash with little guarantee that the public is going to like the product. An average movie cost \$15 to \$20 million to produce and an additional \$70 million to advertise and distribute [30]. The major movie companies (e.g., Paramount, Warner, Universal) compete vigorously to create films for the movie theaters, but the later (downstream) sales of video cassettes to retail video stores is not a competitive business. The major movie companies prefer to handle their own distribution to the home video market.

Film entertainment companies are something of an anomaly in the business world. Unlike other industries, where changes in fashion or technology can quickly result in product obsolescence, movie and television assets have experienced rapid appreciation as VCRs stimulated rapid growth in the home video market during the early and mid 1980's. In 1981, 2.5 million households owned a VCR (3.1% of homes with a TV) and 5.5 million prerecorded video cassettes were sold to retail video stores. By 1988, there were more than 56 million households with VCRs (62.2% of homes with a TV) and over 135 million prerecorded video cassettes were sold in 1988 to approximately 25,000 specialty video retail stores in the U.S.

This expanding market has made home video the single largest revenue source for movie companies. In 1988, film distributors generated close to \$3 billion from tape sales in the U.S. domestic market, compared to roughly 1.9 billion earned from supplying films to U.S. and Canadian theaters. Most recent theatrical releases are initially priced high, \$80-90, with the expectation that consumers will rent rather than buy. Demand for these tapes comes primarily from specialty video retailers, who then rent them to VCR owners at \$2 to \$3 per night. Distributors like CBS/FOX Video sell prerecorded cassettes for an average of 62% of the suggested list price; a \$90 tape aimed at the rental market nets the supplier about \$56. Distributors of videocassette generally do not share in the retailers' rental fees, as they do when supplying movie theaters.

By 1988 the home video industry was making a quick transition from growth to maturity. As VCR sales declined 5% in 1987, the pace of new retail store openings slowed (fewer orders were generated by new retailers building inventories) and the industry experienced a consolidation towards fewer and larger retail stores. The problems faced by distributors shifted rapidly from “how to expand capacity to keep up with sales” to “how to reduce overhead costs”.

## 4. CBS / FOX Video

CBS/FOX Video, a joint venture of CBS and Twentieth Century Fox, is a half billion dollar a year world-wide distributor in the home entertainment industry. It acquires, markets, and distributes film rights for home viewing in the video-cassette and video disc format. The partnership was created in the early 1980's when the home video market started to expand. With a commitment from both CBS and Fox to channel all existing and future homevideo-bound products through the venture (plus 250 existing and future United Artists films) CBS/FOX Video was created to exploit the strength of the two allied companies in a nascent industry. A tape-duplicating (manufacturing) facility was built in Livonia, Michigan and the firm's corporate staff was located close to the partners in New York City.

The CBS/FOX board, consisting of equal representation from CBS and Twentieth Century

Fox, tended to be creative entertainment industry executives that viewed upstream activities in the value chain (i.e., the creation of the film itself) as the major value added work where competitive advantage is achieved. Despite its enormous revenue, CBS/FOX Video was viewed by its partners as a distribution mechanism, rather than a source of competitive advantage. A high percentage of the revenue generated in the home video market was returned to the partners as royalty fees earned on each film. Like many distributors, it operated on a narrow profit margin.

In May of 1987, CBS/FOX Video's board of directors approved the sale of its tape-duplicating (manufacturing) facilities and recording technology to Technicolor. CBS/FOX Video received cash that was diverted back upstream to the creation of new movies by the partners and favorable terms on a long-term contract for prerecorded video cassettes.

The CBS/FOX Video's board did not particularly want to be in the manufacturing business, but the manufacturing facility had been necessary during the home video market's rapid growth in the early 80's. During this time, manufacturing capacity in the industry lagged demand and it needed a capability for strategic reasons (i.e., to insure a source of supply). By 1987, total industry capacity had caught up with demand, a supply of prerecorded video cassettes could be insured via long-term contractual arrangements, and the return on investment of a new film was higher than the return available from manufacturing prerecorded video cassettes. The sale of the manufacturing division to Technicolor dramatically reduced the size and complexity of the company. This sale and growing cost reduction pressures stimulated the company's efforts to downsize their information systems function.

## 5. Computing at CBS / FOX Video

In the first half of the 1980's computing expenditures grew rapidly. Sales of video cassettes were growing rapidly and investment in information technology was viewed as a way of achieving economies of scale. The corporate staff viewed themselves as a manufacturer as well as a distributor. Apparently, without recognizing the relative simplicity of their business compared to other manufacturers, computer expenditures for equipment and staff were budgeted as a percentage of sales. As sales increased, so did the IS budget.

By late 1984, the firm had a large mainframe computer system – an IBM 3081K (see Figure 1) – in its Livonia, Michigan plant, an authorized IS staff of 58, and an IS budget of \$5.2 million. Despite this impressive array of manpower and equipment, the IS staff lacked the experience, training, and leadership necessary to keep pace with the rapidly growing information processing needs. Senior management became increasingly concerned with the lack of progress, especially problems and delays in developing the manufacturing and distribution systems.

Throughout the early 1980's, a management consultant, Ms. Anne Payne, worked closely with Mr. Barker, Senior VP of Finance, on many management consulting and information system projects; she enjoyed his confidence. In late 1984, when the previous VP of MIS left, she became Acting V.P. of MIS. Ms. Payne was an experienced management consultant with an IS background, but her IS operating experience was limited. Most of her efforts focused on improving management practices within the department: enhancing the technical skills of the IS staff through training, establishing proper information system controls, and assisting in the search for a permanent V.P. of MIS.

As she became familiar with the firm's information processing needs, she realized that except for manufacturing, most of the information processing needs were departmental in nature and could be met more appropriately via microcomputer networks. The departments periodically exchanged information necessary to conduct their business. However, for operational purposes, these departments enjoyed considerable autonomy. After Ms. Payne left the MS position in July 1986, she continued to work for CBS/FOX Video as a senior consultant in New York City with Mr. Barker and Mr. Jim Fifield, President of CBS/FOX Video. According to Mr. Barker, Ms. Payne felt strongly that microcomputers and networks were a viable option for their firm's application portfolio and continued to advise him and the President accordingly.

In July 1986, Mr. Tatem was hired as V.P. of MIS. He was, according to the MIS staff, the first really well trained IS operating professional to direct the firm's IS department. Mr. Tatem came from the Levi Strauss Corp. where he had built a reputation as a competent and cost conscious IS executive with extensive experience in managing system development efforts in a large mainframe environment. This made him an ideal candidate for directing CBS/FOX Video's lagging efforts to develop manufacturing and distribution systems. When he started in July of 1986, the MIS organization was centralized in Livonia, Michigan with an authorized staff of 58. Over $60\%$ of the staff were responsible for maintenance, operations, and support with the rest responsible for new systems development. At this time, except for two support positions in New York, the IS department provided little support for microcomputer systems or applications. The info center positions supported end-users who wanted access to data from the mainframe.

According to Mr. Tatem, “any MIS shop can reduce expenses by 20% to 30% or more based on how well the shop is run.” Within a year, Mr. Tatem had reduced the IS staff to 44 (including himself). In all, 14 positions (24 percent) were eliminated and duties were reassigned. Some development projects were canceled or modified. By January 1987, the mainframe was downsized from a 3081K to a 3083J. Mr. Tatem explained, “Some systems were not worth the investment.” One was a bar coding system for the firm’s distributors; they would be able to determine whether tapes in the retail stores in their territories were purchased through them or via other distributors. The system would have tracked every tape’s current location, but it “served no perceived need of the organization and cost tens of thousand of dollars in disk storage and computer processing power.” By the summer of 1987, the elimination of unnecessary work had reduced staffing levels to create a lean IS organization.

## 5.1 The sale of manufacturing

The sale of manufacturing in July of 1987 had an immense impact; the manufacturing facility had utilized 40 percent of computer resources.

\- The sale transferred all manufacturing equipment and systems, the manufacturing systems development staff (9 people), and the year-to-year mainframe lease to Technicolor. CBS/FOX Video leased back partial use of the mainframe system until September 1988.

The MIS organizational charts before and after the sale of manufacturing are depicted in Figure 2. The MIS staff was reduced by 21 positions. Nine of these were in manufacturing. Reflecting the transfer of the mainframe, ten positions in operations (“Information Processing”) were also eliminated. Thus, the MS budget was reduced to \$3 million before major changes in computer architecture were implemented.

The sale significantly reduced the size and complexity of information processing. The manufacturing software was a tightly linked set of application programs designed for the firm's mainframe. This set was complex and, because it was interfaced with distribution, order entry, and inventory management systems (which were linked to the financial systems), upgrading or modifying any portion of the software was extremely difficult. Mr. Tatem said, "We were integrated in such a way that we couldn't change anything without considerable cost."

Mr. Tatem felt it was unlikely that CBS/FOX Video would have considered downsizing if they had not sold the manufacturing division. After removing this large cluster of applications, the remaining ones could be more easily decomposed into loosely coupled clusters, permitting the use of lower cost minis and microcomputer networks. Mr. Tatem said, "We just assumed that the manufacturing systems required a mainframe. In retrospect, I believe we could have downsized manufacturing to a mini or a microcomputer network." Concurrent with the sale, Mr. Barker and Mr. Tatem began rethinking CBS/FOX Video's approach to information systems management. Discussion focused on the departmental nature of many of the unmet data processing needs and the rapid improvements occurring in the capabilities and cost/performance ratios of microcomputer networks. They felt that many applications could now be handled by local area networks. They argued that this would allow substantial reduction in hardware and software costs, and place the access and control of important data in the hands of users. This was particularly important because many of the users were already using micro based software and were continually asking for better access to their data.

Mr. Tatem realized that microcomputer networks were cost-effective for many of the firm's needs, especially unmet departmental ones. However, he and most of his MIS staff tended to favor downsizing critical transaction processing systems to a minicomputer platform. According to Mr. Tatem, “the real issue was moving off the mainframe to a technology that they (the MIS staff) viewed as beneath their skill level and would negatively affect their careers.”

Mr. Barker, Senior Vice-President of Finance, strongly advocated the use of microcomputer networks for all applications, even critical ones such as order processing and distribution. He did not consider himself a “computer person.” Nevertheless, he became the driving force behind efforts to downsize. Although Ms. Payne did not directly participate in IS planning, she appeared to play an important role in reinforcing Mr. Barker’s thinking and in building top management commitment to a downsized architecture.

Although previously planned mainframe development work continued on applications such as order processing and distribution control, in September 1987 Mr. Tatem presented top management with a proposed computer architecture based upon LAN technology. The management approach was to evaluate each project on a case-by-case basis to determine the best solution (LAN, mini, or mainframe). Users and MIS management would review and jointly make a decision whether a host or micro, or a combination, would best meet the business requirements. The plan called for the MIS staff to install new network based systems that would then be turned over to the user to operate and manage. To the extent permitted by local expertise, many functions would be transferred to user departments. This case-by-case policy for platform selection was first applied in the product acquisition department.

In October 1987, CBS/FOX Video hired a consultant to evaluate the requirements of the product acquisition department which had users who were among the most computer sophisticated in the company. At that time, the department was using a Wang for word processing and microcomputers for decision support (e.g., to model financial aspects of potential acquisitions).

The consultant recommended a microcomputer network with work stations connected via IBM token-ring network adapters running over shielded twisted pair wiring in a modified ring (standard IBM Multiple Access Unit configuration) topology. Netware 2.1 2b by Novell was used as the network operating system. This was the firm's first test project using a microcomputer network. By February the Product Acquisition Network was physically installed and users had been trained to operate and manage the network. By April the consultant had completed development of key applications and implementation was "nearing completion without problems."

In April 1988, encouraged by the success of the product acquisition network, CBS/FOX Video installed another network (the 47th Street Network) to be shared by the International Division and the Legal and Business Affairs department. Consultants began development work on key applications. Although the MIS staff was just beginning to work with users to implement new mainframe order processing and distribution systems that would not be fully operational until July 1988, in April the systems staff began to look for microcomputer network based systems to replace those systems as well as the other mainframe systems (i.e., sales analysis, purchasing, inventory management, accounts receivable and payable, and general ledger systems).

## 5.2 The downsizing plan

On April 28, 1988, Mr. Pete Barker and Mr. George Kreiger, the new president of CBS/FOX Video, met with the partners to discuss plans for cost reduction. The partners were demanding that overhead costs be reduced by \$5 million. Mr. Barker had known about these cuts since mid-March and had been quietly working with the top management team to determine where savings could be achieved. Cutting overhead costs is difficult in a world-wide distribution network with a sales and distribution capability in each country. Attention focused on the centralized MIS budget (\$3 million) which represented one of the firm's major budget line items. The requirement to reduce overhead costs implied downsizing of the information systems function.

At this meeting, Mr. Tatem presented the tentative plans he and Mr. Barker had developed.

These called for: (1) downsizing of all applications, even critical transaction processing systems, to a microcomputer network architecture; (2) transferring responsibility for many information processing functions to user departments; (3) dramatically reducing the MIS staff; and (4) developing new microcomputer network applications for functions not covered by existing systems.

The MIS budget was to be reduced from \$2.8 million to \$800,000 and MIS head count from 23 to 5 by transferring all applications to microcomputer networks by mid to late 1989 at a one-time cost of \$1 million. All development of the new microcomputer network applications was to be outsourced to consultants but internal MIS staff would set development standards and coordinate activities. Where users had the expertise (e.g., in the product acquisition department), they would work with the consultants to specify requirements. To meet mainframe computing needs, CBS/FOX Video leased a mainframe (IBM 4341) for one year (until September 1989) until applications could be converted.

The tentative plans described each project, its implementation status as of April 1988, and target dates for completing the migration (see Figure 3). On May 6, 1988, the MIS staff presented these tentative plans to management staff in New York. The following concerns/ reactions were expressed by user managers:

"The real goal is to save \$2 million in MIS expense but to do this more responsibility is placed on the users. It seems like an attempt to make MIS and the administrative side look good at the expense of the users."

"Is MIS really going to help us, or is this all some trick to dump their responsibilities on us? Look at the recent reduction of support in New York, no one is supporting the Wang...we lost that...document so we had to stay up all night recovering it, and no one is around to help us."

"Why should I listen to these people (MIS) anyhow, they never deliver what they say they are going to deliver anyhow."

“MIS has done very little that was innovative or that met the users’ needs over the last few years, how all of a sudden do they plan to do major systems over the next 18 months.”

Despite these concerns, the plans for downsizing were formalized in a May 20, 1988 letter that was distributed to all employees. Mr. Kreiger set a July 1, 1989 deadline for the completion of the downsizing (i.e., to have all applications off the mainframe and operational).

Morale among the MIS staff was understandably low; no one working on domestic data processing was an enthusiastic supporter or champion of microcomputer networks. The downsizing was viewed as “de-skilling” and transferring many of their responsibilities to users. With the application development work outsourced to consultants, a small internal staff would concentrate on providing technical support (e.g., setting development standards for consultants).

Eight days after announcing the plans for downsizing, Mr. Barker expressed concern about increased turnover in MIS staff: “We have lost several key people and will lose the rest soon. As a stop-gap, I have approved some attractive ‘staying bonuses’ – payable only if the individuals stay through a given date. The cost of the consultants to replace lost skills would exceed these payments by several times.”

Despite this, MIS staff was committed to looking first for a microcomputer solution. However, they had serious reservations about its ability to handle the necessary volume of transactions. The MIS staff also doubted that they would provide adequate controls or audit trails for the order processing and general accounting applications on the Consumer Products and Finance network. Therefore, the MIS staff developed an extensive procedure, including user evaluations and “stress testing,” for evaluating packages.

In early September 1988, consultants Coopers & Lybrand were engaged to evaluate the feasibility of downsizing its consumer products and finance systems, including the adequacy of microcomputer packages from a control and auditability perspective. Coopers & Lybrand reported that both of the microcomputer based accounting systems (Platinum and Macola) were feasible solutions and could provide “adequate levels of control and auditability through system features and strong user control”. However, they noted that microcomputer based accounting systems transfer some control responsibility from MIS to the users, requiring user controls outside of the application system.

Starting in mid October and continuing a month, users evaluated these two microcomputer packages (Platinum and Macola) and a minicomputer software package (GABA) developed for the entertainment industry on the Wang computer. Test systems were set up in Livonia and New York. An extensive set of test data (source documents) was created for the evaluation. The evaluation was estimated to require half of a user's time over a two week period. As part of the evaluation, each user was to become familiar with documentation for the systems, enter transactions to set up master files, process two months of transactions, and conduct two end-of-month closings. Users expressed a preference for the GABA/Wang system because it was tailored to the entertainment industry and similar in "look and feel" to the current mainframe system. To assess the ability of a LAN to handle order processing and financial system requirements, the MIS staff developed a limited stress test to load the system with the volume of data that would normally occur when fully installed. This made it possible to test the response time and processing time for each module and for alternative system configurations. The stress tests revealed that the microcomputer system (using Platinum) outperformed the Wang system (using GABA) and provided greater flexibility.

During this testing period, Mr. Barker became increasingly frustrated by the lack of progress on the consumer products and finance network and increasingly concerned about meeting the July 1 deadline. Missing this July 1 deadline was considered “unacceptable”. Mr. Barker felt that the process had to move forward more aggressively and became directly involved in project details, including direct supervision of key systems personnel. Mr. Barker also played an important role in overcoming user resistance to the changes. He described how he handled resistance in the user organization, “There were pockets of resistance.

The resistance had names.” User resistance ceased after Mr. Barker personally explained the urgency of the project.

Reluctantly, Mr. Barker agreed on November 28, 1988 to recommendations from his MIS staff to select the GABA/Wang minicomputer system for the Consumer Products and Finance network. Mr. Barker was convinced that a microcomputer network was less costly, more flexible, and better met long-term needs. However, MIS staff estimated that the migration of order processing and accounting systems to a microcomputer network would require extensive software modifications and could not be completed until early 1990, six months beyond the deadline. Mr. Barker said that the GABA/Wang system “minimized the risks of missing the July 1 deadline or causing massive and destructive disruptions to operations”.

## 5.3 Downsizing: Implications and results

As they proceeded with the development and installation of the networks, the location of the departments that used these networks became a very important factor influencing communications costs. Most of the personnel for North American operations (royalty accounting, order processing and distribution accounting) were located in Livonia. In December 1988, the company decided to close the Livonia facility and consolidate all operations, computer systems, and MIS staff in New York. The major considerations were:

1. the sale of the manufacturing facility in Livonia and the possibility of saving an additional \$500,000 per year in lease expense;

2. the planned move from the computer mainframe to microcomputer networks would increase communications costs if offices were not consolidated; and

3. the growing importance of international business operations required that systems and support services be located closer to International Headquarters.

CBS/FOX Video estimated that the cost of the remote link from New York to Livonia would run \$10,000 to \$20,000 per month or possibly more. CBS/FOX Vidco was spending less than \$30,000 per year for their domestic data communications costs. The result of leaving the staff in Livonia, using systems in New York, would have been substantially increased communications costs. The one-time cost of the move was estimated at approximately \$750,000.

By March 1989, the royalty accounting, rights tracking and international standards, and master tape tracking networks were operational. By April, the Wang mini and the GABA software for the consumer products and finance applications was installed in New York being tested. By July, all systems were operational. CBS/FOX Video had successfully downsized all mainframe applications to a mini and three microcomputer networks and reduced the annual MIS budget to \$800,000, including an allowance for consultants to make continuing system enhancements. By the end of August, all MIS personnel involved in the downsizing effort had left. A new MIS director and a four person MIS staff were hired in New York.

The downsized computer architecture (CBS/FOX Video System Network Plan, 1989) is illustrated in Figure 4. A Wang VS 7150 is located in New York to handle the majority of North American transaction processing; this system supports over 30 sessions and has 3.8 gigabytes of disk storage. One Wang VS 5000 is located in Livonia to handle the Canadian office and another is located in the Chicago sales office to handle order entry functions. Leased communication lines connect the three. The three LANs in New York (Corporate Finance Network, Program Acquisition Network, and 47th Street Network) operate under Novell Netware 386 version 3.1a.

The bulk of North American processing (the Consumer Products and Finance applications) is handled by the Wang minicomputer using GABA software for order entry, purchasing, inventory management, accounts receivable, accounts payable, and general ledger. This Wang/GABA system transmits sales orders to Technicolor and Agincort (the Canadian duplicator) and receives inventory information from these suppliers. This Wang/GABA system performs title tracking from budget through actual performance in North America. It contains all the detailed transaction history for each title. Summary information for each title is transferred to the Corporate Finance Network which issues North American and consolidated financial reports.

The Corporate Finance Network performs the following business functions: royalty accounting; fixed asset management; corporate reporting/Pyramid; MIS development and testing; and payroll/human resources. The new royalty accounting system (developed in Paradox) receives North American sales information from the order entry portion of the Wang/GABA system and international sales information from the subsidiaries through the 47th Street Network via GE Mail. On a monthly basis, the system prepares royalty and trademark license fee statements for each licensor and/or partner, by title.

As corporate reporting is implemented, the international subsidiaries prepare financial reports such as income statements and balance sheets on microcomputer systems at their locations. These files are then transmitted to the 47th Street Network. The information is reviewed and sent to the Corporate Finance Network for consolidation into the corporate financial statements. The Pyramid software package is used to consolidate financial data.

The 47th Street Network performs: collection of financial information from the international subsidiaries; maintenance of the film rights database (PDS); and distribution of marketing and rights data to the international subsidiaries. Data is gathered from each of the subsidiaries (France, West Germany, UK, Spain, Japan, and Australia) and transmitted via US Robotics V.32 modems through a point-to-point connection to GE electronic mail. At the corporate offices, data are received through eight modems that are connected by a J&L Systems' Chatterbox, a gateway allowing up to eight simultaneous users to communicate with subsidiaries. Data is received in Lotus 1-2-3, Paradox, Wordperfect and Pyramid formats and disseminated to the area that requires the information.

The Product Data System (PDS) is an on-line database of worldwide information about titles that CBS/FOX Video can license. Information can be found about the rights, the titles and territories, and release and expiration data by title and territory. The system was designed to provide information to meet business objectives such as: developing marketing strategies for the exploitation of expiring product; evaluating potential sublicensing agreements; and answering requests for information from the partners. The menu driven system consists of on-line screens for users to ask fundamental business questions. Paradox enables users to play “What if?” with the information in the system and answer urgent ad hoc requests. This system consists of three components: contract summary; worldwide dates tracking; and movie synopsis. Using PDS, marketing information is gathered and sent to each of the subsidiary offices on an as-needed basis.

Finally, the Program Acquisition Network hosts the “Tracking” and “Deal Track” systems, both developed in Paradox. In the initial portions of the product life cycle, the “Tracking” system is used to trace the process of obtaining the video rights to a product from the initial contact through negotiations. Staff members update the status of any deal as developments occur. This provides management with the opportunity to review the status of negotiations instantly. The “Deal Track” system provides a summary of sales, history, and performance information about a title, from inception to date.

The researchers interviewed eight user managers at the New York offices in September 1989 after the applications were operational. Their comments on the new systems varied by the system (mini or micro) they used and their level of computer expertise. The MIS staff reductions meant less user support. Users preparing reports from data in the minicomputer were particularly dissatisfied. They had to download data from the mini to microcomputers where they used Lotus and Paradox to prepare reports. They had experienced substantial difficulty in downloading data. The more experienced users whose data resided on microcomputer networks enjoyed the greater control and ownership that accompanied lower levels of MIS support. In contrast, less experienced users were frustrated by their new responsibilities. Users assumed new responsibilities on both the mini and microcomputer systems, but the transfer of functions from the MIS staff to end-users was greater for applications on the microcomputer networks.

For applications on microcomputer networks such as product acquisitions and royalty accounting, responsibility for the custody and distribution of data has been transferred to the department manager. For example, Royalty accounting has become the most accurate source of historical data on sales and royalties by title. When other managers need this information, they directly contact the Director of Royalty Accounting. Thus, the royalty accounting network functions like a “focused” [29] data center with the Director of Royalty Accounting responsible for a portion of the total corporate data set. Custody and distribution of data on the mini system remained primarily the responsibility of the MIS staff.

## 6. Discussion

Downsizing is a growing trend that has significant implications for information resource management. The advantages and disadvantages appear to be similar to those associated with the migration to minicomputers in the mid to late 70's. However, the relative simplicity, low cost, and ease of use of microcomputers, greatly enhance the potential to transfer responsibility for information processing to line management. This transfer of functions and the risks [2] associated with it creates a qualitatively different phenomenon. Without new management strategies, the full potential of downsizing is not likely to be achieved.

This case study suggests that downsizing is primarily a managerial rather than a technical issue. Downsizing is an architectural decision that distributes and decentralizes application responsibility. Line management has increased autonomy to manage not only their people but their systems and information. Thus, downsizing is more appropriate for firms with decentralized structures and information processing needs of a departmental nature. In the case of CBS/FOX Video, operating departments enjoyed considerable autonomy and only periodically exchanged information necessary to conduct their business.

The CBS/FOX Video case suggests that industry structure may be an important determinant of downsizing. It influences: (1) the degree of cost pressure in an industry [24]; and (2) the opportunities for competitive advantage through information technology [18]. Where firms are under severe cost reduction pressures and information technology has few competitive implications, downsizing may be an attractive cost reduction strategy. In this case, the home video industry was a non-competitive “downstream” stage in the movie industry’s value chain. In this stage, the cost of information technology was more important than its potential for achieving competitive advantage.

At CBS/FOX Video, downsizing was primarily driven by a desire for cost savings. When CBS/FOX Video began to consider downsizing in the summer of 1987, it focused on a case-by-case approach with two objectives: (1) placing access and control of data in the hands of users; and (2) reducing hardware and software costs. However, the actual decision to migrate all applications to smaller platforms was not made until the spring of 1989 when the firm was forced to make major budget reductions.

This case study suggests that downsizing savings may be overstated. The actual MIS budget reduction achieved was substantial (\$2 million annual MIS budget reduction), but not as much as reported in the trade literature (from \$5.2 million to \$800,000). The MIS budget was reduced to \$2.8 million before the downsizing effort began via improved IS management and the sale of manufacturing.

A firm's application portfolio structure is a major factor determining whether a firm can downsize. Does it contain clusters of applications that are, or can be, loosely coupled and moved to smaller platforms? The complexity of the application set (or sub-set) and the volume of transactions determine the required platform. In the CBS/FOX Video case, the firm's largest cluster of programs were in manufacturing. The sale of manufacturing initiated downsizing discussions.

Certainly, not all applications are sensible candidates for downsizing. Very large applications that can not be decomposed into smaller modules still need to be run on large systems. But the typical profile includes a large number of small and medium-size applications that could be run more economically on smaller systems $[35]$ .

Downsizing does not mean a return to the “islands of automation.” Developing integrated systems across hardware platforms may be more difficult, but it is not impossible. The clusters of applications should be loosely coupled, not un-integrated. At CBS/FOX Video, the degree of integration across the major applications (order entry, general ledger, royalty accounting, international finance, etc.) has not diminished.

Top management support is often considered critical to the successful implementation of information systems $[11]$ $[21]$ ; downsizing is no exception. In the case of CBS/FOX Video, top management's involvement went beyond mere support. The chief financial officer's vision was the driving force behind the downsizing effort. The CBS/FOX Video case suggests that top management championship may be essential where no one within the MIS staff is a strong advocate for the use of microcomputer networks. This is consistent with the corporate downsizing literature $[32]$ that calls for strong top management direction.

Downsizing often involves the transfer of systems ownership and control from the IS staff to end-user departments. This transfer involves risks that the MIS staff may find difficult to accept. Strong user controls are necessary. Line managers must be responsible for insuring system integrity by training users and supervising information processing activities.

When top management rather than the MIS director drives the downsizing effort, there may be a greater transfer of functions to outside consultants or user departments. At CBS/FOX Video, line management drove the downsizing effort and the MIS staff was substantially reduced. Operations functions were transferred to users and systems development was outsourced to consultants. Only a small IS staff was maintained to deal with network maintenance, standards, and security.

Downsizing has significant implications for the IS profession. In the decade of the 90's, the economics of downsizing may drive similar changes in other organizations. Downsizing alters the skill requirements and employment patterns of IS professionals. These changes have been described by Deardon (1987) as the “withering away of the IS department.” Opportunities for employment in a central IS department will decline. Increasingly, systems development and support will be outsourced. IS professionals with business and entrepreneurial skills may start their own consulting firms. IS professionals without these skills will find employment opportunities in firms that specialize in the systems development and maintenance needs of particular industries. Downsizing will accelerate these trends.

## 7. Conclusions

As the underlying economics become better known, downsizing is likely to emerge as one of the most significant phenomenon in information resource management in the 1990's. Increasingly, MIS directors will be required to respond to (1) top management demands for significant IS budget reductions, and (2) the need for line management to have more control over their own systems and information.

At CBS/FOX Video, a \$2 million dollar reduction (70%) in the MIS budget was achieved, the MIS staff was cut from 23 to 5 (78%), and there was a significant transfer of IS functions to end-users. These short-term results seem impressive. However, without new strategies for line management of information resources, the full potential of downsizing is not likely to be realized. The “focused” data center concept may be one such strategy. Finally, the long-term implications of IS downsizing are unclear. Will costs rise again as users demand more service? Is the organization less capable of responding to business changes?
