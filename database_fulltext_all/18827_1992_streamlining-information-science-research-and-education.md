# Streamlining information science research and education

Guisseppi A. Forgionne

University of Maryland Baltimore County, Catonsville, MD 21228, USA

Information is needed by a variety of users for various purposes in private and public enterprises. Colleges and universities help meet this need by conducting research and by educating people in information science. The relevant education and research is provided in a fragmented manner by these institutions through business, computer science, and other programs (including library science, communication, and engineering). Each program provides partial, but not complete and integrated, information science education and research. This paper examines the various programs, defines the educational and research gaps, and proposes organizational changes that may streamline the educational and research process.

Keywords: Decision technology systems, Decision support systems, Information reporting systems, Information science, Information science education, Information systems, Management information systems, Management communication systems, Transaction processing systems Professional support systems.

## Introduction

The future of the global economy lies in producing high technology goods and services within an information society.

Computer science programs have provided much of the basic research and technical education needed by information science. Applied research and systems education have evolved through computer and management information systems programs in business schools. Additional applied research and application-specific education has been delivered through library science, information and software engineering, systems engineering, communications, medical informatics, and legal informatics programs.

## Information age

Information, knowledge, and wisdom are crucial to a variety of people in modern society $[22,28]$ . Organizations utilize employees, vendors, and professionals to generate goods and services sought by consumers. For the process to succeed, there must be efficient and effective communication between all affected individuals and groups within and outside the organization. These individuals and groups seek information, knowledge, and wisdom to help accomplish their tasks and the organizational objectives and policies. Information systems facilitate communication and deliver the desired information and knowledge.

## Information systems

A number of information systems have been developed to support the communication and delivery roles. These systems can be classified as follows [10];

(1) Decision Technology Systems (DTS) - that support all phases of the decision making process.

(2) Information Reporting Systems (IFRS) – that provide information for the management of the day-to-day, routine operations of the organization.

(3) Management Communication Systems (MCS) – that provide an effective link between users and computer technology and between various forms of computer technology.

(4) Professional Systems (PRS) – that support the individual and unique needs of physicians, lawyers, certified public accountants, and other professionals.

Decision technology systems include individual and group DSS, executive information and executive support systems, expert systems, and idea processing systems. These systems focus mainly on providing the knowledge and wisdom needed by upper-middle and top-level managers to make tactical and strategic decisions $[23\ and\ 24]$ . Knowledge and wisdom may be in the form of problem elements and structures, status reports on conditions that affect the problem, forecasts of problem parameters and decision outcomes, recommended actions and strategies, and explanations for the recommendations and problem outcomes.

Networking and telecommunications systems, human-computer interface technologies, office automation systems, and work group support systems are the primary management communication systems. They focus primarily on supplying or supporting the communications among individuals and groups. Support may include networking one computer system with another or providing an effectively-designed user interface, while the communications may be a series of documents generated by an office automation system or a multimedia presentation from a work group system.

Transaction processing systems (TPS) and MIS are the primary information reporting systems. They focus mainly on processing routine transactions efficiently and supplying the organization-wide information needed by first-line and middle managers to run the day-to-day operations of the enterprise $[1,16,25]$ . The information may be status reports from TPS and MIS or automated decisions from MIS.

## Information science

Basic and applied research is needed to identify the information, knowledge, and wisdom requirements of people, find effective ways to generate the desired requirements, and develop systems that provide the necessary support. This research must be based on scientific reasoning, analysis, and evaluation. Otherwise, it will be difficult to generalize the research results and establish sound principles.

The discipline that provides the required basic and applied research can be called information science. This involves the study of $[14,17,20,26]$ :

(1) information, knowledge, and wisdom attributes and characteristics,

(2) the processes that successively transfer the information, knowledge, and wisdom to the human mind, and

(3) the concepts, methodologies, and technologies that lead to the effective utilization of information, knowledge, and wisdom.

The field is largely concerned with the development of effective systems for the storage and dissemination of information, knowledge, and wisdom and with the scientific evaluation of these systems in terms of their ability to serve and to be operated by the wide variety of people pursuing the solution of complex societal problems.

## Educational programs

Enterprises could perform the basic research needed to develop information science and technology. Indeed, some government agencies (especially the U.S. Department of Defense) and private research laboratories (such as Bell and IBM) have been involved in such research. However, resource limitations, operational demands, and the shortage of available talent make basic information science research infeasible or impractical for most organizations.

Colleges and universities have recognized the need and have offered leadership in directing economic energies by performing the necessary cutting-edge research, providing high-quality education, and transferring the results to industry, government, and professionals. Most of the information science education and research takes place in graduate programs.

## International characteristics

According to the latest Peterson's Guides to Graduate Programs, less than fifty United States institutions provide graduate education through the doctoral degree in information science. Additional research reveals that many other North American (mainly Canadian) and European educational institutions also offer information science programs.

An examination of the relevant catalogs indicates that most of the North American institutions offer information science in conjunction with a computer science program, while some provide information science, library science, and sometimes communications through the same department or school. There are also many universities that offer masters and doctoral education in CIS or MIS through their business programs. Some of these universities have both masters and doctoral level CIS/MIS, computer science, and information science programs.

European institutions are somewhat different than North American universities. In Europe, there is a strong tradition of offering arts and science, business and economics, and science and engineering education in separate universities. Graduate education in computer and information systems typically is found in the universities dedicated to business and economics. Information science, frequently called informatics in Europe, is usually located in technical institutes or universities committed to science and engineering education.

## Computer science programs

Early in the computer age, leaders in business, government, and science recognized that large scale problems could be solved by a computer executing a procedure. Educational programs in computer science have evolved to meet this prob-- lem-solving need. Typically, such programs have focused on the development of procedures that are efficient and effective, languages suitable for stating the procedures, physical systems for executing the procedures, and the analysis of the social setting within which the physical systems are used [2 and 5].

There are a variety of specialization areas, including but not limited to $[3,9,18]$ : (1) artificial intelligence, (2) computation, (3) computer system architecture and organization, (4) computer graphics, (5) database systems, and (6) programming. Throughout these areas, the orientation has been on the use of the computer as a problem-solving tool and the effects of its adoption on procedure and data representation. The primary emphasis has been on the flow of information within computational and technological systems structures.

Typical computer science programs offer incomplete (or no) coverage of information systems $[7,13,19]$ . They also spend little time examining the attributes and characteristics of information, knowledge, and wisdom. In addition, there is brief (if any) treatment of the concepts, methodologies, and technologies that lead to their effective utilization within and between organizations.

## Business programs

Educational programs in CIS/MIS have evolved to address the organizational and information system aspects of information science. In these programs, the emphasis is on the flow of information and the global impact of information on management decisions and organizational structures and operations [21]. Students learn how to analyze the information needs of an organization, design and implement systems that gather, store, transmit, manipulate, and present data to various users, and manage the information flow and systems. The study of this discipline takes place typically in business schools.

There are several areas of specialization, including but not limited to [6,8]: (1) computer security management, (2) database management, (3) data communications management, (4) information resource management, (5) management support systems, (6) office information systems, (7) systems analysis and design, and (8) transaction processing systems. While some of these areas (such as artificial intelligence and databases) are also found in computer science programs, the CIS/MIS programs offer a different perspective. For example, artificial intelligence has an algorithm and physical device slant within computer science but a management and decision-making focus within business programs.

Standard CIS/MIS programs offer an incomplete view of the technologies that lead to the effective utilization of information, knowledge, and wisdom. By focusing on business, these programs also risk ignoring the specific needs of nonbusiness users in government, engineering, the professions (such as law and medicine), and other areas. In addition, CIS/MIS programs spend little time examining the attributes and characteristics of information, knowledge, and wisdom.

## Other programs

Other programs have been developed to provide information science education not found in either computer science or business programs. These include professional informatics, arts and science informatics, engineering, communication, and library science. They are usually housed in the school, college, or department containing the program.

Professional informatics apply the concepts, methodologies, and technologies that lead to the effective utilization of information, knowledge, and wisdom to health, law, public policy, education, and other professions $[12,15,27]$ . Arts and science informatics perform a similar function for assorted artists and scientists, and engineering informatics play an analogous role for engineers. Other (most notably electrical, computer, and systems) engineering programs focus on some physical processes that transfer information, knowledge, and wisdom to individuals and groups. Additional transfer processes are supported through the emerging discipline of information and software engineering. Communication and library science programs are dedicated to the study of information, knowledge, and wisdom attributes and characteristics and the processes that transfer these attributes and characteristics to the human mind $[4\ and\ 11]$ .

## College of information science

Information science is highly interdisciplinary in nature, drawing on the fields of: art, accounting, economics, and management, communication, computer science, computer, electrical, information and software, and systems engineering, library science, linguistics, mathematics and operations research, sociology, philosophy, psychology, and statistics. Some of the expertise is provided through business programs, portions are found in computer science, and elements are offered in other educational programs. However, none of these furnishes the full range of interdisciplinary skills needed for complete information science education. Moreover, important relationships between the disciplines may be uncovered in the existing programs.

The gaps can be reduced by following the business and engineering models of integrating the required programs into a school within a college or a university. The specific arrangement will depend on the overall organizational structure of the college or university. To be complete and integrated, this arrangement should include undergraduate and graduate curricula.

## School / college benefits

Offering information science within a school or college, rather than through a scattered department, framework can lead to the following benefits:

(1) Clarity. A school/college would help clear up much of the current confusion among prospective students and potential employers and funders concerning the differences between computer science, information systems, information and software engineering, and informatics.

(2) Maturity and growth. By bringing together appropriate teachers, researchers, and students, a school/college would provide a way for information science programs to grow and mature more effectively and efficiently.

(3) Resources. The school/college concept would avoid much of the duplication of effort and resources that occurs when information science education is fragmented among multiple departments in a university.

(4) Service. A school/college would provide the vehicle to service existing arts, humanities, science, and professional (business, communication, education, engineering, health, law, and library) programs more effectively.

(5) Synergy. There are synergistic effects (such as the creation of new applications and fields, improved concepts and methodologies, and innovative approaches to societal problems) that can be realized by integrating the various information science fields within a school/college.

Similar benefits already have been realized by business, engineering, and the few information science programs in North America and Europe that have adopted a school/college approach. Universities using such an approach also have the vehicle to meet industry and government information science educational needs in their regions and thereby gain program advantages over the competition. The leaders will be in a better position to set standards for the discipline.

## School / College framework

Some universities have implemented a college/school of information science. Each of the existing ones has a different organization, and none cover all of the information science disciplines.

Nevertheless, it is possible to have a standard college/school framework that will build on existing organizations while filling the gaps in information science coverage. One potential framework adopts a cube philosophy involving fields, concepts/tools/techniques, and applications. It offers the flexibility to design an application of a field, a new or emerging field, or an application across fields.

Fields. Despite its interdisciplinary nature, information science consists of unique fields, each with its own well-defined career paths, avenues of research, and tools. Departments within a college/school of information science, then, could be organized by fields. Such an approach would lead to few disruptions of existing programs and would require minimal new resources. It also would assemble related areas of research and emulate the evolution of information science theory and practice.

Currently, the major fields of information science are communication, computer engineering, computer science, information systems, information and software engineering, and library science. Each of these fields could be a separate department in the formed college/school, or related fields could be grouped into departments. For example, computer engineering and computer science (with physical systems as the common basis) might make one department, communication and library science (with information theory as the central core) might fashion a second, information and software engineering might constitute a third, and information systems might be a fourth department.

Core concepts / tools / techniques. There are concepts, tools, and techniques that cut across the fields. These are drawn from the many disciplines that support information science and form the core of education for all college/school students. The core might include:

(1) communication and information theory,

(2) computer hardware, software, and programming,

(3) database development and management,

(4) modeling and problem-solving,

(5) scientific research design and methodology,

(6) systems theory, analysis, and design,

(7) management theory and practice.

To provide the desired perspective, these core courses should be broad and interdisciplinary in nature. For example, the database course should include data structure (as in computer science), file storage, retrieval, and manipulation (as in library science), and management (as in information systems) perspectives. Similarly, the modeling course should incorporate numerical and symbolic representations, qualitative as well as quantitative analyses, and simulation as well as the optimization approach.

Applications. Information science is applicable to many problems in business, engineering, government, and the professions. Many of these, such as strategic planning and enterprise modeling, draw on several information science fields and thereby are best covered through courses within the college/school. Applications that require special or technical expertise, such as medical informatics and concurrent engineering, might best be offered through courses jointly sponsored by the college/school of information science and other programs.

## Department framework

Departments within information science can be organized around the same cube philosophy of fields, aspects common to the fields, and then applications. Theories and methodologies drawn from several fields can be used to form new or emerging disciplines. Consider an information systems department.

Information systems is largely concerned with the design, development, and implementation of systems. Thus, one way to organize its fields is by the four major system types of decision technology systems (DTS), information reporting systems (IFRS), management communications systems (MCS), and professional systems (PRS). Subjects within a field can be offered in separate courses, or related subjects can be integrated into a single class.

Concepts, tools, and techniques normally covered in one information systems field will be useful in others. To ensure complete coverage, the department core can have separate courses that broadly survey each of the four major systems. An additional course on research design and methodology can provide the desired scientific foundation for all students.

Some information systems applications cut across fields, while others can be offered effectively within a single field of information systems. With the cube philosophy, students can design concentrations that address unique applications requirements. A single field can be utilized on a variety of applications, a single application can cut across several fields, or a single application can draw from a single field.

## Center and institutes

Communication, computer science, computer information systems, and library academicians have lived in different social and professional worlds. Forming a college or school of information science can induce a political battle between these groups for constituents and initiate ideological arguments over intellectual autonomy. Centers and institutes can alleviate the battles and arguments while easing the transition to a college/school framework. They also can serve as a vehicle for education and research jointly sponsored by departments within an established college/school of information science and between the college/school of information science and other colleges, schools, or departments.

Internal centers or institutes bring together faculty, students, and practitioners from the disciplines. External centers or institutes join the same individuals and groups from information science with relevant counterparts in other disciplines. Through team teaching, segmented courses, practitioner presentations, and other innovative educational approaches, center/institute faculty offer core and specialized information science education and conduct joint research.

Such centers and institutes will necessitate some rethinking of teaching and service loads. However, they can facilitate the sharing of ideas, contribute to the professional growth of the participants, and expedite the development of information science and other participating disciplines.

## Summary and conclusions

Information is needed by a variety of users for various purposes in private and public enterprises. Colleges and universities help meet this need by conducting research and educating people in information science. The relevant education and research is provided by these institutions through business, computer science, and other programs.

None of the individual programs furnishes the full range of interdisciplinary skills needed for complete information science education. Moreover, important relationships between the disciplines may be uncovered in the existing programs.

The gaps can be reduced by following the business and engineering models of integrating the required programs into a school within a college or a college within a university. This college/school can be effectively and efficiently organized around the three educational dimensions of fields, concepts/tools/techniques, and applications. Such an organization provides the university with the flexibility to design an application of a field, a new or emerging field, or an application across fields. Departments within the college/school can be organized around the same cube philosophy.

Centers and institutes can ease the transition to a college/school framework. They also can serve as a vehicle for education and research jointly sponsored within the college/school of information science and between the college/school of information science and other colleges, schools, or departments.
