# Patterns of structural control in high and low computer user organizations

Rachid Zeffane

University of Newcastle, Newcastle, NSW, Australia

This paper examines patterns of structural control in organizations concomitant with variations in the extent of their functional use of computers. It draws on a random sample of 149 Australian enterprises. Based on computer-use in fourteen different functions, cluster analysis produced two main groups: i.e., organizations with high and low computer-usage profiles. Analysis of variance between these groups revealed that organizations with high usage profile tend to be larger and privately owned. Structurally, these organizations tend to be more formalized, more departmentalized and flatter in management levels. They tend, however, to be relatively less centralized. Multiple regressions within each of these groups revealed that contextual characteristics (such as size and ownership) and the varying extent of computer use in different functions had different impact on structure within each group.

Keywords: Centralization, Control, Computer-use, Departmentalization, Formalization, Hierarchy, Management levels, Organization, Ownership, Size, Structure.

## Introduction

There has been much academic concern, in recent years, on the structural implications of the adoption of computer technology in industry (Zeffane, 1989). This concern spreads from the managerial implications of computers (Burlingame, 1961; Reif, 1968; Burn, 1989) to the more strategic concerns involving the choices in organization design (Pfeffer and Leblebici, 1977; Child, 1987; Bodi, 1987). Despite this concern, current knowledge of the precise relationships between the extent of computer use and organization structure remains slim. There are two basic reasons:

(a) In most studies, the concept of computer-use has been considered in a global sense, hence overlooking the specific functional or managerial applications to which computers may be put; and

(b) Few studies have attempted to isolate the computer-use effect by means of sub-sampling methods allowing for the identification of specific groupings.

These deficiencies call for research approaches that cater for the relevance of different functional applications; (Grinyer and Yasai-Ardekani, 1984; Dastmalchian and Javidan, 1987). Such approaches should provide better grounds for evaluating the effect of computer-use on the structure of an organization.

This paper reports on the results of an empirical, exploratory, study involving 149 Australian Enterprises. It addresses the question of whether varying levels of computer-use impact on the types and degrees of organizational structuring and the way in which aspects of structuring (such as the loci of decision-making and the extent of formalized or departmentalized structuring) are in turn affected by contextual aspects (such as size, ownership, and the specific functional usage of computers).

## Previous research

The role of computers in shaping various means and strategies of structural control has gained an important place in the debates on the impact of computing technology on organizations (Child, 1984). Three main characteristics of organization structure appear to have attracted the main research attention (1) the implication of computer technology on structural patterns of decision-making (e.g., Blau et al, 1976; Robey, 1976, 1981; Moynihan, 1985). (2) the impact of computers on patterns of structural departmentalization (e.g., Reif, 1968) and (3) the relationship between computers and the use of formalized (i.e. procedural or rule-based) means of structural control (Storey, 1987).

Views and analyses differ widely on whether greater centralization or decentralization could result from the use of computers. Indeed, the empirical evidence is scattered and [remains] conflicting. Some researchers contend that computers cause centralization through their power to facilitate the availability of information to higher hierarchical echelons (Leavitt and Whisler, 1958; Whisler, 1970). However, others suggest that computers would take over routine decision-making at lower and middle levels, thereby increasing the capacity of these levels to handle less routine decisions (Robey, 1977). Carter (1984) made the observation that studies conducted prior to the year 1970 have, by and large, tended to support the view that computer use increases the degree of centralization while those studies carried out since then have tended to espouse the opposite view. One explanation is that, prior to 1970, organizations might have been (on average) less familiar with innovative technology and the potential applications that would impact the spread of its usage. Also, the availability and use of micro and mini computers was minimal, with batch operated main frames as the predominant mode (Rule and Attewell, 1989). It was also argued that, beyond this threshold time-period, organizations have become more familiar with the computer which in turn has effected greater degrees of adoption at sub-unit levels. The lowering of the levels at which implementation takes place does speak for a tendency towards decentralization. However, downward trends do not always go hand in hand with a reduction in the actual loci of decision-making.

Opposite views contend that computer use tends to increase centralization, however, few are supported by systematic empirical evidence. The argument is that computers will actually strengthen decentralization because of the ability of computer processed data to enhance the lower level manager's hold on decision-making. In her attempt to relate the extent of computer usage to the degree of centralization in 53 state employment agencies, Klatzky (1970) found that decentralization was clearly and positively related to computer use (in terms of the number of computers and the number of 'input-output unit' employed by each agency). These findings have been strongly supported by more recent studies (Tavakolian, 1989; Zeffane, 1989).

In contrast, research on the impacts of computers on the degree of use of written documentation, rules and procedures remains slim. Speculations have generally been equated with effects pertaining to the extent to which the control process is standardized. Computers could be used to enhance consistencies in recording and assessing performance. They may also be streamlined towards the provision of instant information on current rules and existing procedures (Markus and Pfeffer, 1983). They could also be targeted at providing greater formality in guiding operational activities under various circumstances (O'Reilly, 1982; Morden, 1985), where formalization extends from the mere transmission of information to organization members and outsiders, to the specification of roles and operational activities. These roles may also be extended to facilitating the communication of policies to relevant internal and external constituencies. March and Simon (1958) place a strong emphasis on these roles in terms of their contribution to the process of coordination and control. On the empirical front, the introduction of computer systems has been found to increase the use of formal channels of communication and control. Robey's (1981) empirical observations in the hospitality industry suggest that the effect of the computer system, in that context, was to formalize relations among departments. Prior to the introduction of the system many informal ‘hallway conferences’ were used to coordinate the activities of purchasing, materials management, sales and accounting, afterwards these conferences were no longer necessary, because the system provided the same information automatically. In their study of thirty-eight US manufacturing units, Pfeffer and Leblebici (1977) found and argued that information technology was strongly and positively associated with the use of formal written reports and negatively related to the use of oral evaluation of performance. The assumption is that formal rules and procedures facilitate evaluation. The use of advanced information systems will enhance the process of record keeping while reducing the exercise of personal (informal) control.

While a great deal of literature dealing with computers and organizations has dealt with vertical authority and control issues, there has been relatively less concern with horizontal differentiation, or the lateral complexity of organizations. As in the case of centralization, the body of evidence is extremely varied and opinions range from the assumption that enhanced use of computers is associated with increased lateral differentiation to those that purport the opposite. Database proponents contended that one of the main effects of information technology on departmentalization is aggregation. The process is assumed to result in the consolidation of departments with reduced size. However, this assumption overlooks the facilitation of specialized tasks at operational levels. This may be more conducive to functional differentiation at unit and sub-unit levels. More recent research supports this view and shows that computers are associated with greater horizontal differentiation (Bjorn-Anderson and Pedersen, 1980; Carter, 1984). In this context, computers are viewed as alternative strategies for tighter lateral relations needed as a result of increases in the number of functional units and sub-units.

Overall, consideration of the body of literature points to conflicting views, and existing empirical evidence remains parsimonious. For instance, few attempts have been made to substantiate the computer-use effect by isolating and comparing organizations with high and low use profile. However, the desirability of research strategies scrutinizing functional distinctions has often been advocated.

In this light, this paper explores structural differences (and their likely determinants) by examining and comparing different computer-user organizations. The study is exploratory and makes use of clustering (statistical classifications) techniques (Aldenderfer and Blashfield 1984).

## Data and method

## Sample

Data was gathered by means of a mail-questionnaire addressed to Chief Executives of five hundred organizations operating in Australia; Specific executive names, drawn from the Kompass Directory of Industry, were used. The sample of work-units was selected in a 'loosely randomized' basis, since the target-sample needed to have representative quotas of size ranges and industrial sectors had to be catered for. Because the addressing system used was personalized, a number of organizations simply returned the questionnaire (uncompleted) on the grounds of recent changes in personnel. Thirty-four uncompleted questionnaires were received from organizations in this category. Overall, there were 149 usable questionnaires, (i.e., about a $30\%$ response rate). Despite the low percentage, the responses did not bear sampling bias. A comparison of the matrix (i.e., by size and industry) of the 500 organizations against the responses revealed that each cell was represented in the final sample. The responses for each cell were sufficiently consistent and representative of the target-sample.

The final sample has 92% private-sector organizations (i.e., wholly private organizations). In terms of status, 43% are independent organizations; 27% are wholly Australian owned subsidiaries and 28% are branch-units or subsidiaries of parent companies outside Australia. A distribution of the sample by size and industry is provided in Appendix A.

## Scales

The variables are (1) Organization Size; (2) Type of Ownership (i.e., degree of public ownership); (3) The Extent of Computer Use; and (4) Structural Characteristics Assessing the Degree of Centralization of Decisions; the Extent of Formalization, the Degree of Departmentalization and the number of hierarchical levels.

## Size

Size was measured in terms of the number of full-time employees. The size-bands were selected in a mode that allows for curvilinearity (Pugh and Hickson, 1976): from the <250 employees category (Scoring 1) through 250–500 employees (Scoring 2); 501–1000 employees (Scoring 3); 1001–2000 employees (Scoring 4),...etc, to >64,000 employees (Scoring 10).

## Ownership

The degree of public ownership was measured by percentage of privately owned capital asset within each organization. A four-category scale was used for this purpose: 1 for wholly private organization; 2 if partly state-owned (50% of capital); 3 for predominantly state-owned (>50% of Capital) and 4 when wholly state-owned (100% of capital).

## Computer-use

In previous research, the extent of use was measured by counting the number of sub-units or functional departments making use of computers (Carter, 1984). This approach corresponds to a multi-item comparative measure made up of binary scales. Each item taps a specific function where computers may be used. While this method has produced acceptable results it does not provide a precise evaluation of the 'extent' of use. For instance, a function with a minimal use of computing facilities will be given the same score as a function with more extensive use. One way of overcoming this is to devise a measure close enough to a vector quantity. The early introduction of computers were crystallized essentially around very few core functions such as production and stock control. More recently, however, computers have become involved in organization-wide functions. Such wide involvement is mainly attributable to the universal function of information-processing incorporated in advanced computer systems. To cater for this spread of use and in line with previous research the concept of computer use was measured in terms of the number of terminals (including personal computers) used in each of fourteen functional areas. [A listing is provided in Table 1]. A six-point categorized scale was used: 0 for no computer terminals; 1 for one or two computer terminals; 2 for three to six; 4 for seven to fourteen; 5 for fifteen to thirty and 6 if over thirty terminals]

## Structural variables

As fundamental measures of structural control, both centralization and formalization were measured on the basis of itemized vector-scales. In keeping with previous research, centralization was measured by asking respondents to consider the hierarchical structure of their company/organization and indicate the level at which each of 17 types of decisions is normally taken. A seven-point standard hierarchical scale was provided (see Appendix C). Respondents were asked to base their answers on this scale or be as close to it as possible. They were asked, specifically, to refer to the hierarchical level of the last person (or level of the person of the lowest status for collective decisions) whose consent is necessary for the execution of each of seventeen types of decisions. The list of decision-types was partly borrowed from the operational dimensions developed by the University of Aston researchers (see Zeffane, 1989).

Formalization was measured by the amount of written documentation initiated and circulated between individuals and work sub-units for the purpose of maintaining control. It denotes the extent of use of written documentation (rules and procedures) for the purpose of control (Mansfield and Zeffane, 1983). This was measured by asking respondents to consider a list of ten types of documents and indicate the approximate proportion of the workforce that these are normally circulated to. A four-point categorized scale ranging from not available/applicable (Scoring 0), through less than 10% of the workforce (Scoring 1), 10–50% of the workforce (Scoring 2), 51–75% of the workforce (Scoring 3), to above 75% of the workforce (Scoring 4).

Departmentalization refers to the number of functional activities represented by a unit, section or department. If provides an indication of the extent of division of labour within the organization. Unlike centralization and formalization, the adoption of a composite (itemized) vector-scale was not plausible. Using counts of the relative number of employees in each departmentalized functional area (for the entire organization In focus) would have led to an aggregate measure more closely related to size than to the intended notion of functional division of labour. Hence, this variable was assessed on a scale of binary-items indicating that a function is either departmentalized (Scoring 1) or not (Scoring 0). Sixteen items of functional specialisms were used in this perspective.

Following Pugh and Hickson (1976), the number of hierarchical levels was measured by asking respondents to use their organization chart and count the number of hierarchical levels (a) within their organization and (b) in the total corporation, if it is a subsidiary or a branch unit of a larger corporation.

The scales used in measuring Centralization, Formalization, Departmentalization and the number of hierarchical levels are shown in Appendix C.

## Reliability tests

Tests of reliability were performed on all composite scales. Assessments of internal consistency were performed for each item. The criterion considered for accepting or rejecting items was that each one should have a correlation with its relevant scale (including that item) sufficiently greater than “the reciprocal of the square root of the number of items in the scale” (i.e. r > root of N - 1) (see Payne and Mansfield, 1977). According to this criterion the above constructs showed significant coverage. The mean correlation coefficients obtained were: 0.62 for Formalization, 0.59 for Centralization, 0.46 for Departmentalization and 0.66 for Computer Use. The equivalent Alpha Cronbach for these were 0.75, 0.67, 0.58 and 0.78 respectively.

## Analysis

In previous studies, the potential impacts of computers have been examined using global correlational and regressional analyses on the overall sample (Blau et al, 1976, Carter, 1984; Zeffane, 1989). While these analyses suggest relationships between the dependent and independent variables, they may mask specifics and distinctions across different sub-groups of organizations in the sample. This is more important when the sub-groups are defined in terms of differences in the independent variable (in this case the extent of computer use). To overcome this shortcoming we adopted the following approach: First, we tested for potential groupings on the basis of the extent of computer use. For this purpose, cluster analysis was performed (Everitt, 1974). This mode of analysis allows the classification of cases (in this case organizations) into meaningful sets. A one-unit split method, after maximum iterations, was used for this purpose (Wilkinson, 1986). Secondly, we tested for contextual and structural differences that might differentiate the clusters. This was achieved by (1) performing one way analysis of variance on all the dependent variables across clusters and (2) testing for and comparing regressional impacts across clusters. To conform with previous research, we ran correlations across all the study variables. The results of the correlational analyses are reported in Appendix B.

## Results

In order to test for potential clusters the K means method was used (Cormarck, 1971; Hartigan and Wong, 1979). This method splits the cases into a number of groups by maximizing between (relative to within) Cluster variation. In simple terms, it is like performing a one-way analysis of variance where the groups are unknown and the largest F-value is sought by reassigning cases to each group. This method assumes normal distribution. Hence, the K-means (Cluster Analysis) was based on the standardized scores of each item scale of computer use. These denote the extent of computer use in each of fourteen different managerial functions. Table 1 shows the results of this analysis.

The direction and strength of these differences are also justified by the magnitude of the statistical F-ratios obtained for each functional use and by their concomitant levels of confidence (ie: P = 0.000 for each type of functional use). Hence, the difference in the number of organizations in each cluster, depends on the statistical significance and strength of the K-means clusters. In other words, while a priori judgements on the lack of balance in the number of organizations across the two clusters may seem prohibitive, the fact that they have been tested on the entire number of subscales (i.e., the entire range of functional use of computers) caters for such imbalance and reiterates the need to investigate these differing types of organization on a comparative basis. This may, in fact, reflect some of the realities involving the extent of functional use of computers by different types of organizations. Further scrutiny of these groupings was conducted in terms of the industry and size profiles of organizations that they comprise. This produced a distribution of profiles of low and high computer-user organizations based on organization size as well as the type of industry. (This distribution is shown in Appendix A). This distribution does not provide any distinct indication of differences between low and high computer-users based on the type of industry or on the size of organizations. In fact, comparisons of the distributions of organizations on this basis points to comparable spreads in size and type of industry between the two groups. This is substantiated by comparable loadings across the range of industries. Neither the size of the organizations nor the predominant type of industry in which they operate supersede the extent of use of computers in justifying the distinct nature of the organizations in each group.

To further investigate the profile of these clusters, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The results are shown in Table 2.

Variance (in size, type of ownership as well as the variables denoting organization structure) is explained by these two computer-use discriminating groups.

The size differences do not seem to support the lack of discrimination (based on size profiles). This is unclear, though it may be worth noting the statistics on size (for each cluster) in comparison with the actual sample-ranges (i.e., 1–10). By and large, organizations with low computer-use tend to be comparatively smaller. This supports the intervening effects of size, reported by Carter, 1984 and Zeffane, 1989. It also seems that on average, these latter organizations tend to have less government involvement in the ownership. Hence, the high computer-user organizations are more likely to be found in the private sector.

In terms of differences in degrees of structuring, organizations with high computer-use tend to be less centralized, but more formalized and more departmentalized with taller hierarchical shapes. These lend strong support to recent findings associating computer introduction and use with the vested managerial need for standardization. However, they do not support the view that greater computer use underlies the tendency to centralize information and thereby power in decision making. These results are further verified by the relational analyses reported in Appendix C. These, however, show that the patterns are stable across the two clusters.

To further investigate determinants of characteristics of organization structure within each of the two groups, stepwise multiple regressions were performed on the data. The three main structural variables (centralization, formalization, and departmentalization) were hypothesised as dependent variables. The regressional estimates were made by setting size, ownership, and the fourteen types of functional use of computers as independent variables in the equations.

The purpose of these tests was to explore the predictive strength of these variables on the structural characteristics of organizations within each cluster. Further, these tests allow for more minute consideration of predictive differences that might be masked by consideration of the overall sample and/or the overall computer-use scale. The results of these tests are shown in Table 3.

Consideration of the likely predictors of centralization for low and high computer-use organizations reveals marked differences. In low computer-user organizations the functional use of computers in public relations seems to have a positive impact on the degree of centralization. While the use of stock-control seems to have a negative impact on the loci of decision-making, coupled with findings indicating greater prediction of formalization in organizations with greater government involvement (ownership). In contrast, for high computer-use organizations, the extent of computer-use in stock-control seems to be a better predictor of centralization. The predominantly private organizations with high computer-use profile might consider the essential use of computers in terms of improving the decision-making mechanisms to be more on the efficiencies in controlling inputs and outputs (ie Stocks). In contrast, the predominantly government-owned organizations tend to emphasise the need for improved computer-aided decision-making, mainly for improved public relations. This finding provides some support to studies that have considered both privately and publicly owned organizations, mainly in terms of different emphases in their computer use.

High computer-use organizations tend to enhance the use of computers in production, primarily, for the purpose of reducing paperwork and procedural delays. The low use organizations tend to standardize and formalize means of achieving high research and development output.

## Conclusion and implications

The potential impact of technology on the structuring of organizations remains a topic of extreme pertinence and keen interest in organizational analyses. While the debate on the importance of size compared with technology in effecting organization structure goes on (Child and Mansfield, 1972; Marsh and Mannari, 1981; Fry, 1982; Montanari and Morgan, 1983; Lincoln et al, 1986), considerations of the impacts of computers have added new dimensions to this controversy. Major studies focussing on aspects of 'production technology' have been rather pessimistic over the possibilities of the direct relationships between technology and structure but the more recent studies incorporating aspects of 'computing technology' have pointed to significant associations. Blau et al. (1976) in fact considered both production and computer aspects of technology and found that these aspects related more strongly to structure. It appears that aspects pertaining to the use of computers are more significantly related to characteristics of administrative control, portrayed in structural characteristics, than the conventional production technology measures. Indeed, computer technology and other microelectronics technologies have become pertinent to the question of managerial practices and ways of organizing. One of the central issues is that of the difference that computers may make to the way structural control is undertaken in organizations.

Academic interest in the underlying connections between organizational strategies of control and the role of computing technology in shaping such strategies is increasing. Prior research seems to suggest that the new computer technology does have a strong impact on organizations in general and in the way in which they tend to structure their activities in particular. While there remains considerable disagreement about the nature and degree of this impact, the findings of this study point to encouraging grounds upon which this impact might be uncovered. Our findings strongly suggest that increased computer use goes hand in hand with decentralized, formalized and more departmentalized means of structural control. The scrutiny of sub-measures denoting specific functional use and activities proved more exacting in highlighting the nature of these relationships. It has been argued and demonstrated that decentralization is often coupled with increased formalization in the orchestration of structural strategies of control (Child, 1973). By increasing formalization and reducing centralization, computer use may be seen as a chief tool in the strategy of administrative control.

In support of previous research, the results of this study suggest that significant associations between the extent of computer-use and organization structure do exist. These may speak for the revival of the technological imperative on a Fresh tone. Greater computer-use is associated with increased decentralization, formalization and departmentalization. Consideration of functional specificities coupled with the identification of groupings based on extent of computer-use, proved useful. The regression analysis performed within each of the groups is also quite revealing. It appears that the ownership characteristic plays an important role in moderating the effects on structural control within each group, but more so for the extent of centralization. It is a priori difficult to ascertain the reasons for this moderating effect; but variations in emphases in the use-of-computers within specific functions provide some indications. The emphasis that the predominantly government-owned organization tend to use computers to improve their Public relations function is one of the many indicators. The predominantly privately owned organizations (with high computer-use) have a tendency to influence structure by means of implementation of computer-use in the production and market related functions.

This study is fundamentally exploratory. It is hence difficult to isolate universal patterns from the more country-specific ones. However, recently expressed concerns on the effective implementation of computers in Australia are strongly shared. Our view is that these concerns should focus on both the individual-level managerial roles and related aspects (such as competence and adaptation) and on the appropriateness of structural control mechanisms within which these roles might be appropriately distributed.

We support recent calls for more theoretically ingrained research in the area of information technology (Markus and Robey, 1988). However we do not support the view that future research should establish a strict segregation between studies conducted at the individual level and studies focusing on the organizational level. In fact, we would stress that research could benefit and advance more rapidly if these two levels of analysis were combined and integrated to form a single platform of investigation. In addition we suggest that future research makes room for the organizational implications of the varying managerial (or functional) applications of computers.
