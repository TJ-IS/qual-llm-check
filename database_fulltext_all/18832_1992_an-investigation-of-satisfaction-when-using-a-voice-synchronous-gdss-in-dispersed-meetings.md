# An investigation of satisfaction when using a voice-synchronous GDSS in dispersed meetings

Kimberly Cass, Timothy J. Heintz and Kate M. Kaiser

Marquette University, Milwaukee, WI, USA

This paper reports on a laboratory experiment which assesses the effect of a group decision support system (GDSS) on synchronous face-to-face and dispersed meetings with subjects linked via a voice connection. Location and GDSS-use form the basis for a $2 \times 2$ research design of 134 groups. Members solved a preference allocation task and reported their satisfaction with the meeting process and its outcome. Both GDSS and location effects were significant for discussion quality, with face-to-face non-GDSS group members reporting the highest level of satisfaction. The GDSS effect alone was significant for discussion effectiveness and outcome, with non-GDSS groups reporting the highest satisfaction. The solution satisfaction measure had a significant interaction between effects. Members of the dispersed GDSS groups appreciated the concept of this setting but not its implementation. Dispersed subjects without GDSS technology were more satisfied with both the process and the outcome of their meeting. When a GDSS was available, face-to-face subjects reported higher satisfaction with the meeting outcome than dispersed subjects.

Keywords: Collaborative work, Group decision support systems, Groupware, Dispersed meetings, Electronic meetings, Group process, Meeting outcome, Face-to-face meetings, Remote meetings, Quality teams.

## 2. Literature review

GDSS research efforts have increased rapidly over the past five years $[2,13,19,6]$ . Early work focusing on how technology can mediate group processes encouraged the development of a number of GDSS products. Most of these systems help in collecting ideas and arriving at a consensus among individuals during face-to-face meetings. They typically provide support for agenda formulation, brainstorming on problems and solutions, and consensus formulation via voting.

## 2.1. Face-to-face GDSS

DeSanctis and Gallupe [3] developed a three-level categorization for GDSS. Their Level-1 systems enable the entry, sharing, and group evaluation of ideas. At Level-2 the system provides specific tools to support decision making, and at Level-3 systems further facilitate the decision making process by acting as ‘intelligent mediator’. They further classified GDSS as being implemented within a decision room (small group, face-to-face), a local-area decision network (small group, dispersed), a legislative session (large group, face-to-face), and a computer-mediated conference (large group, dispersed).

Empirical studies have primarily examined Level-1: decision room environments. Their results are limited and appear conflicting. Joyner and Tunstall [18] indicate that the computer itself has no significant effect on the quality of team decisions, although subjects already trained in the decision process suggested by the GDSS performed better than those without instruction. Watson et al. [29] support this premise by demonstrating that teams using GDSS fared no better than those that received group structure training. Performance, measured by the increase of consensus from pre- to post-meeting consensus, is significantly better in groups trained with meeting structure than in groups with no training. Consensus implies that the team members own the outcome. For this reason, consensus is valued.

Other studies suggest that GDSS can help improve the quality of decisions, especially those involving more complex tasks $[8]$ or larger groups composed of business professionals $[21]$ . The same results hold for pre-existing teams in a field setting $[14]$ or when the technology is used over time $[30]$ . Examination of variables other than consensus or performance indicates that GDSS may decrease satisfaction with group decisions and reduce the number of ideas generated. However, team members whose contributions are made anonymously generate more comments, are more critical and probing, and embellish other members' ideas more than those whose contributions are identified $[15]$ . Some systems report very positive results during brainstorming. Nunamaker et al. $[20]$ argue that such results reflect the use of larger groups of professionals, the application of more realistic tasks, and the development of more mature software implemented in an environment specifically designed for GDSS meetings. Given the facilitator dependency in group systems, the use of a facilitator in these sessions would probably have some impact on results.

## 2.2. Dispersed GDSS

Although often cited as an important consideration $[16,17]$ , only a few empirical synchronous GDSS studies look at location as a key variable. The dispersed groups in these studies, however, did not have voice communication available and thus, had to rely on keyboarding and screen displays as their only way of communicating.

In one of the few dispersed group studies, Gallupe and McKeen [7] suggest that dispersed groups are less satisfied with the decision process than face-to-face groups. Their subjects in

GDSS-dispersed settings took longer to decide than those in non-GDSS face-to-face meetings. This study also found an interaction effect with 'choice shift'. This measure, which estimates the degree to which team members changed their decision preferences as a result of the group process, is positively affected by GDSS in dispersed settings but negatively influenced in face-to-face meetings. This result requires replication and enhancement. No significant results were found with other dependent variables such as decision confidence or decision quality.

Valacich et al. [27] report that dispersed groups are more productive than face-to-face groups, as measured by the number of alternatives generated. These dispersed groups are more task focused and less distracted. A meeting facilitator assisted groups in this study.

Videoconferencing facilitates another type of dispersed meeting. It has visual cues, rarely involves more than two locations, is estimated to be considerably more expensive [22], and involves more elaborate production and accessibility.

The majority of GDSS research focuses on situations where the group members are in the same physical location. Research dealing with groups whose individual members are not physically proximate has concentrated on the effects of asynchronous group communication using electronic mail $[12]$ or on synchronous meetings without voice communication.

## 2.3. Process and outcome

Reder and Conklin's [24] model cites the importance of the communication media and distinguishes between process and decision outcomes. George et al. [9] have categorized satisfaction and performance measures which address both process and decision outcomes. Characteristics of process outcomes include member participation, time to decision, and the satisfaction with the process, while characteristics of decision outcome include the number of alternatives generated, the quality of the decision, consensus, performance, and satisfaction with the decision.

## 2.4. Satisfaction

Prior research has yielded conflicting results regarding the impacts of GDSS on satisfaction.

For both meeting process and outcome, Steeb and Johnson [26] report that groups who used a GDSS in decision making were more satisfied than those who did not, whereas Gallupe et al. [8] reported that GDSS groups had lower satisfaction. Watson et al. [29] reported that manual groups who were instructed in decision structuring techniques had higher process satisfaction. Easton [5] reported that GDSS groups had a higher outcome satisfaction. Such wide differences may be attributed to experimenters using different GDSS technology, the presence or absence of facilitators, and inconsistent measuring techniques.

## 3. Hypotheses

The hypotheses concern the attitudes and self-reported satisfaction of team participants solving a preference allocation task. We assume that both location of group members and the availability of GDSS will affect the satisfaction of system users in both the decision making process and the outcome of the final decision. These are dependent measures similar to those used by George et al. [9].

The availability of a GDSS provides certain features unavailable in ordinary meetings. It provides alternative communications media, in addition to traditional use of handwritten notes, flip charts, blackboards and other manual tools, that may occur in enhanced group communications. By having ideas and evaluations stored within the computer, team members can quickly recall information compiled earlier in the meeting process. The menus generated by software suggest a structure for conducting meetings. Furthermore, GDSS allows individuals to enter and share ideas anonymously.

All these factors suggest that GDSS software has the potential for groups to process information more smoothly and effectively. The research reported here did not employ facilitators. Members had the option of using technology. Training did not encourage its use. We suggest the following null hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. GDSS group members will not differ in their satisfaction with meeting process from members of non-GDSS groups.

Hypothesis 2. GDSS group members will not differ in their satisfaction with meeting outcome from members of non-GDSS groups.

We also examine physical proximity of team members. People who meet face-to-face have the advantage of using non-verbal cues to enhance their communications process. They also have the advantage of being able to view a common physical medium, such as a blackboard or flipchart; this opens more channels of communication. In dispersed settings, members lack this common visual arena and physical cues about other members. They can attribute entirely different characteristics to other members than if they had met face-to-face.

Face-to-face groups should be more satisfied with their meeting process and outcome than those in dispersed locations. This suggests the following null hypotheses.

Hypothesis 3. Members of dispersed groups will not differ in satisfaction with meeting process from members of face-to-face groups.

Hypothesis 4. Members of dispersed groups will not differ in satisfaction with meeting outcome from members of face-to-face groups.

What impact will GDSS have on dispersed and face-to-face groups? Since dispersed groups lack communication channels available to face-to-face groups, they may rely more on a GDSS for communication. They cannot write down ideas on a blackboard or note pad; they must rely on the technology. Therefore, dispersed groups should benefit more from GDSS than face-to-face groups. If location does not matter, then both dispersed and face-to-face groups should proceed through their meetings with the same facility and experience similar levels of satisfaction. We argue that technology can compensate for some of the impediments due to dispersion.

This produces a set of null hypotheses concerning the interaction of location and use of GDSS.

Hypothesis 5. The effects of GDSS on meeting process satisfaction will not differ with meeting proximity.

Hypothesis 6. The effects of GDSS on meeting outcome satisfaction will not differ with meeting proximity.

## 4. Research design

The independent variables are location and use of a GDSS. Subjects were either in a common meeting room or in distinct locations. The physically proximate groups are termed “face-to-face” groups, while those in different locations are referred to as “dispersed” groups. The GDSS variable indicates whether groups had a GDSS available for their meeting. The subjects with a GDSS are referred to as “GDSS” groups while the others are referred to as “non-GDSS”. All group meetings were held at the same time. The GDSS-dispersed subjects communicated with one another via phone link and public screen. The non-GDSS dispersed members used a phone link.

## 4.1. Sample

The 134 groups of 502 upper division undergraduate and MBA students enrolled in business and communications classes were randomly composed, based on the subjects' availability. Four-member groups and a mix of graduate and undergraduate students were the goal. The mix should simulate the mix of quality project teams encountered in field settings. We attempted to insure a range of experience, maturity, and diversity and in order to generalize the trend of today's businesses to empower lower management. The nature of the task requires no experience. The values pervade all walks of life. By drawing out people's personal values, it elicits commitment across a broad base of individuals.

Table 1 presents the number of groups by treatment. The individuals who composed each team did not work together in a group setting prior to the experiment and their group affiliation ended at the time that their group completed the experimental task. Groups who have no history offer the advantages that they focus on solving the task at hand and that they have no preconceived ideas about individual group members. However, they have no built a social rapport nor have they established a team dynamic. Subjects participated voluntarily in the experiment as one of several options for five percent of their course grade as used by Watson et al. [29].

## 4.2. The GDSS environment

Software aided meeting management (SAMM) running on a Northern Telecom Meridian DV-1 network system, a Level-1 GDSS, was used in this experiment. This system integrates a LAN environment with phone switching hardware so that both voice and data communication are allowed over the same network. SAMM offers features such as: agenda setting, displaying the problem, selecting criteria, assigning weights and ranks to alternatives, and voting on the alternatives in order to make a decision. The package enables multiple users connected via a conference call to share computer screens.

SAMM has two screens: A private screen where participants enter ideas and evaluations visible only to that person; and a public screen where group information is compiled and displayed to all group members. Each workstation has a full-screen window running the private screen. For dispersed GDSS groups, a second window allows users to toggle back and forth between the private and the public screens.

Instead of two full-screen windows, face-to-face group members view the public screen overhead. Users can send private entries to the public screen. Thus, the GDSS facilitates anonymity.

## 4.3. Treatments

The experiments represented four decision-making environments. In the face-to-face-GDSS treatment, team members met in the same room and could use a GDSS. This environment is equivalent to a GDSS meeting held in a decision room. In the dispersed-GDSS treatment, each group member worked in a separate room, connected to other group members by a voice link and had a GDSS to help in reaching a group decision. In the dispersed-non-GDSS treatment, members were in different locations and connected only by voice. This environment resembles conference call meetings. In the face-to-face-non-GDSS treatment group members were in the same room and could use blackboards to communicate group ideas. This environment resembles traditional meetings, where participants have no electronic support. In this treatment, subjects usually talk among themselves and can perceive body language cues from others. The unstructured flow of informal information in face-to-face groups may be diminished when groups employ GDSS technology; team members' attention is diverted from fellow participants to the GDSS operation.

All groups received training in meeting techniques, including: creating an agenda, identifying decision criteria, generating alternatives, rating, ranking and voting on alternatives, and making the final decision. After the training, subjects participated in a practice task where they used the meeting techniques to make a decision. A document outlining the training techniques was available to each participant during the decision making task. Subjects had pencils and paper throughout the meeting and subjects in the face-to-face-non-GDSS treatment had access to a blackboard and chalk.

All GDSS groups received a thirty-minute training session that included instruction on using SAMM and the Meridian system. The pilot study showed that a thirty-minute training session on SAMM was sufficient to familiarize subjects with its functions and operations.

## 4.4. The task

The subjects used the GDSS to allocate money to various community projects from a philanthropic trust foundation $[29]$ . Each project reflects one of Spranger's $[25]$ categorization of six basic human values: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious. The task presents a situation where resolving conflicts necessitates a group decision and elicits personal commitment.

## 4.5. Experimental procedure

Subjects were assigned to one of the four treatments. Researchers introduced the experiment in a university class as one of several options for credit. Those who chose to participate in this option then completed a consent form, a survey of background information, and a pre-test allocation instrument.

At the time of the experiment each group received training, performed a practice task, and conducted the experimental task. Before starting this task, subjects were told to arrive at a consensus and that their discussion would be recorded. After completion of the experiment, one group member recorded the group's solution. Each subject then completed a post-meeting questionnaire measuring perception of the meeting process and outcome and satisfaction with the hardware and software. A later meeting provided debriefing to all subjects.

## 4.6. Measures

With the exception of additional items concerning dispersed groups, the study used instruments validated in other studies $[10,11]$ . The dependent variables consisted of single-item self-report satisfaction measures of meeting process and outcome from the post-test questionnaire.

Three Likert 7-point scale items focused on group discussion and may be interpreted as group process variables:

(1) discussion quality-(1 = poor to 7 = good);

(2) discussion effectiveness-(1 = ineffective to 7 = effective);

(3) discussion outcome-(1 = unsatisfactory to 7 = satisfactory).

## Results

Table 2 displays results of the analysis of variance on each of the four satisfaction measures. A substantial number of subjects did not answer the solution satisfaction question. Its placement on the questionnaire may have led to subjects missing it. No such problem occurred during the pilot study when questionnaire format differed.

In debriefing sessions some subjects using GDSS reported two difficulties: slow response disrupted their group process and the SAMM interface was awkward and cumbersome to use. A common complaint concerned the difficulty in transferring information from a subject's private screen to the public screen. SAMM's user interface is not as sophisticated as the user interfaces found in many popular software packages. Subjects seemed to expect more user-friendly features.

The most striking general trend is that non-GDSS team members reported much higher levels of satisfaction than members of GDSS teams across the four satisfaction measures. Subjects in face-to-face teams generally reported slightly higher levels of satisfaction than subjects in dispersed teams, with the differences largest for the decision quality satisfaction measure. The only case in which the trend does not hold is for satisfaction with decision effectiveness in GDSS teams. Here the dispersed-GDSS teams reported slightly higher satisfaction than face-to-face-GDSS teams.

Attempts at four-person groups did not always succeed. Of the 134 groups only 34 contained three persons. Analysis was performed to determine whether three and four person groups performed similarly. Group number was significant only for discussion outcome. Overall, subjects in three person groups had an average discussion outcome satisfaction 0.24 greater than subjects in four person groups ( $\alpha = 0.009$ , calculated using separate variances within groups). This contradicts the results of Watson et al. [29] and Valacich et al. [27].

Non-GDSS group members reported significantly higher levels of satisfaction ( $\alpha = 0.001$ ) on three of the four satisfaction measures. Solution satisfaction, had a significant interaction effect ( $\alpha = 0.066$ ). Location also produces a significant effect on the discussion quality ( $\alpha = 0.013$ ).

The results are not what we expected. GDSS groups consistently reported lower satisfaction on all measures. The interaction effect of solution satisfaction further confirms the negative influence of GDSS; dispersed-GDSS groups reported the lowest results. With dispersed participants the GDSS produces negative results although its effect is not significant for the face-to-face groups.

Location appears to have a negative influence with dispersed groups, but only has statistical significance for discussion quality.

## 6. Discussion

Hypothesis 1. Effect of a GDSS on meeting process satisfaction

GDSS groups are significantly less satisfied with the meeting process than non-GDSS groups despite arguments that GDSS adds an additional communication channel, helps to structure the decision process, provides anonymity, and produces a convenient means to recall earlier meeting results. Subjects found the process of typing ideas into a computer slow and cumbersome and many groups did not follow the decision structure suggested by the GDSS.

Groups shared their ideas freely, indicating that anonymity is not an issue for them. Qualitative data analysis of transcripts indicate that no GDSS groups went back to review results of earlier discussions as they proceeded with the process. GDSS subjects do not appear to realize the GDSS benefits touted by proponents. All GDSS groups were instructed that computer use was optional. Some groups abandoned the system. Generally the group members viewed the GDSS implementation as a hindrance rather than as an aid. Slow response time, difficulty navigating through menus, and diverting attention from the meeting to the technical details of using GDSS, interrupted the meeting process.

The issue of the members' commitment to the task was investigated. We present two pieces of evidence that suggest that members were invested in the decision: the amount of time they took to complete the task and the level of interest at the debriefing sessions. Groups took a significant amount of time in completing the task.

Analysis of recorded tapes present conversations of heated debates. Although groups were instructed that they had no maximum time limit, average time was 27.2 minutes and going as long as one hour and 22 minutes. Informal observation of the participants and post-task discussions provide anecdotal support for the above assertions. Audio tapes provide data suggesting that subjects spent a lot of time providing technical support in the GDSS groups. This appears to divert attention from the meeting purpose, thus resulting in lower satisfaction with the meeting process. In the debriefing sessions where we relayed results of the allocation, discussion and arguments arose again about where money should be allocated. Somehow the GDSS may have detracted from or negatively influenced the familiar voice link among members.

Many subjects from the dispersed-GDSS teams reported that users in remote locations would find the technology very useful in spite of contrary satisfaction results. They like the idea of GDSS but not its implementation. Regular use of a GDSS would familiarize users with its functions and the tool would become less prominent in the meeting process.

## Hypothesis 2. Effect of GDSS on satisfaction within meeting outcome

Groups in the non-GDSS treatment express more satisfaction with the discussion outcome than groups using GDSS. Subjects in the GDSS groups felt that the technology inhibited their discussion, and hence they perceived the outcome of their discussion as less than optimal. Some studies consider factors, such as the number of different alternatives generated and the quality of discussion, as outcome measures. Individuals with GDSS may have been paying less attention to the meeting process and thus may not notice the number of alternative solutions generated or the steps which led to their group's consensus. Some groups would just start typing information into the computer with little use of the voice connection. They did not even discuss how to proceed. Introduction of a GDSS into the meeting process may have caused such a radical redefinition of the term “discussion” that subjects did not find oral discussion useful.

## Hypothesis 3. Effect of location on meeting process satisfaction

Face-to-face and dispersed groups did not report significantly different levels of process satisfaction as measured by discussion effectiveness. However, the face-to-face groups did report significantly higher satisfaction as measured by discussion quality.

Location did impact subjects' perception of discussion quality. Of the four treatments, the face-to-face-non-GDSS subjects reported the highest satisfaction while the dispersed-GDSS subjects reported the lowest. Subjects found the most familiar meeting setting to be the most satisfying. Dispersed GDSS is the least familiar and maybe that is why it is the least satisfying. Expectations about meetings may affect this result. The lack of visual and social cues available to face-to-face groups may partially explain these location effects. This may also reflect the absence of a facilitator or leader to get the group on track when they became distracted. However, Dickson et al. [4] suggest that facilitated groups were less satisfied about their group decision. Distraction can be more nefarious in a dispersed setting where there is no visible peer pressure to get back to work. Observing others focussed on the task provides the social cue to conform.

## Hypothesis 4. Effect of location on meeting outcome satisfaction

Face-to-face and dispersed groups did not report significantly different satisfaction levels on the discussion outcome measure. Obvious distinctions between dispersed and face-to-face settings – such as access to non-verbal cues, visual information about meeting participants, shared proximity, and greater ability to socialize with other group members – might be categorized as process variables, as they seem not to impact meeting outcome perceptions. Pinnoseault and Kraemer [23] present a model which relates meeting process and outcome. From this model, it appears that meeting process influences the outcome of a meeting. However, we detected that location significantly affects process while it has no significant effect on meeting outcome. This refutes their model if one assumes no intervening variables.

Valacich et al. [27] report that dispersed groups 'outperformed' face-to-face groups in a problem-solving task. They suggest that dispersed groups are more task focussed. This increased task focus could counterbalance the reported dissatisfaction with meeting process; thus, masking out location effects with regard to outcome. The relationship between meeting process and meeting outcome needs to be more fully explored so as to clarify which aspects of a meeting a GDSS supports.

Hypothesis 5. Effect of GDSS/location interaction on the meeting process

Our results suggest no interaction effect between the GDSS and Location variables. The data show stronger GDSS effects than location effects with respect to meeting process. Maybe the main GDSS effect overshadowed any possible interaction effects.

Hypothesis 6. Effect of GDSS/location interaction on the meeting process

An interaction effect was significant ( $\alpha = 0.066$ ) for solution satisfaction. This supports the conjecture that GDSS could have a stronger effect, albeit in this experiment a negative one, in dispersed settings than it does in proximate settings. The lack of other communication channels than voice appears to produce a higher dependence on the additional communication channel. The fact that the GDSS was cumbersome may have magnified this effect. We would expect that if the subjects perceived the GDSS as an aid that we would see a positive rather than negative interaction. Dispersed GDSS groups, in this case, would value the GDSS more highly than face-to-face GDSS groups.

In summary, we find that although GDSS can be used in dispersed settings, there may be serious impediments to user acceptance. To overcome these impediments, we must better understand how to design the technology, how to train people to use it, and how to better support different types of groups with it. Practitioners must carefully identify which groups will benefit from groupware, which functions of groupware are useful to a particular team, and whether a dispersed team can meet effectively. Only some groupware features may assist some teams; none of its features may suit any other teams.

Our study implies that dispersed groups can effectively make decisions. The overhead and support necessary to achieve long-term productivity with dispersed teams assisted by groupware may be prohibitive for small and medium sized firms. Until costs are reduced or shared, these firms may benefit only from face-to-face electronic meetings. The time is ripe for the linking of videoconferencing and groupware which is possible only for large firms and as an outsourcing service. Another implication is one of staffing. We expect that new roles will emerge. Whether these roles develop in-house or are contracted must be evaluated before an organization makes a groupware commitment. Considerable work needs to be done before GDSS realize widespread user acceptance.
