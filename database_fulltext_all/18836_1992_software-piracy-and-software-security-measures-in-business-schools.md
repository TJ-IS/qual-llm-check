# Software piracy and software security measures in business schools

Jin H. Im and Pamela D. Van Epps

University of New Orleans, New Orleans LA, USA

Many business schools have become heavily dependent on microcomputers for educational purposes. This has exposed them to a new type of risk involving unauthorized software copying by faculty, staff, and students. An analysis of the results of a field survey on software piracy and software security measures is presented here. Participants included 241 member schools of the American Assembly of Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB). Some suggestions are then made on software security measures designed to reduce legal consequences for copyright infringements by faculty, staff, and students.

Keywords: Software piracy; Software security; Software policy; Microcomputers; Computer ethics; Business schools.

## 1. Introduction

Microcomputers are nowadays becoming indispensable educational tools. The sixth annual UCLA survey $[4]$ shows a remarkable growth in computer usage in core business courses. For instance, the computer usage rate for undergraduate core courses increased from 59.5% in 1985 to 72% in 1989. Two major reasons for the proliferation of microcomputers over the traditional computing facilities like mainframes and mini-computers are affordability and availability of user-friendly software. Microcomputers allow instructors to show students what they would otherwise only explain conceptually. Also, students can easily practice what they have learned. Because of this, instructors can shift their focus from tedious problem solving processes to more practical issues, like interpretation and implementation of the results.

In recent years, the main concern of business school administrators has been funding the explosive demand for microcomputers. The second annual UCLA survey $[5]$ identified funding and space limitation as the two most important constraints in expansion of computing facilities. Heavy dependence on microcomputers has caused several problems in maintenance and security of hardware and software. One issue is unauthorized software copying – software piracy – by faculty, staff, and students $[1,6,9,13]$ . Software piracy is a bigger problem in the academic community than in the business community $[7,10,11]$ . A field survey was conducted to study three major issues concerning software piracy:

(1) how much does unauthorized software copying occur in business schools?

(2) how much are the schools aware of their legal responsibility for preventing unauthorized copying?

(3) what security procedures do business schools use to protect their software?

The type of purchase method does not significantly affect any of the three dependent variables, as evidenced by the chi-square values of 6.10, 4.29, and 5.96, respectively (see Figure 5). Although purchasing activities are ancillary in nature for managers, these results surprised us somewhat. If computer facility directors habitually purchase only a few copies of the software used in their organizations and there is likely to be widespread use of that software within the organization, there is probably illegal copying [13]. On the other hand, the purchase of multiple copies or a site license should lead to less piracy, increased awareness of legal responsibility, and a greater degree of confidence in security measures. We can only speculate that if an institution's purchasing position (whether to buy multiple copies, a few copies, or a site license) were an integral part of their software policy, these statistical relationships would be clearer. It is also possible that facility managers do not have much to say about how the department heads within the school of business handle software purchasing. Finally, many respondents engaged in more than one type of purchasing method, and this probably served to confuse the results.

The type of software access method does not significantly affect any of the three dependent variables. The chi-square values are 3.80, 0.72, and 0.95 (see Figure 6) for the three dependent variables: Degree of software piracy, degree of awareness of legal responsibility, and degree of confidence in security measures, respectively. Regardless of access method (floppy disk, hard disk, or local area networks-LAN) most respondents reported a medium to high range for degree of piracy, degree of awareness, and degree of confidence.

The implication is that the method of access to the legal copy is irrelevant once an employee has decided to pirate software. Admittedly, it is easiest to make illegal copies from floppy disks – given the decline in the use of protection schemes. It is harder to pirate from hard disks and most difficult to violate the security of a LAN. Nevertheless, none of these access methods would daunt an experienced, unrepentant pirate.

The type of software security measures provides some interesting effects on all three dependent variables. The statistically significant results as reported in Figure 7: degree of software piracy - chi-square $= 17.9$ ( $p < 0.10$ ); degree of awareness of legal responsibility - chi-square $= 49.2$ ( $p < 0.01$ ), and degree of confidence in security measures - chi-square $= 68.8$ ( $p < 0.01$ ). All three are functions of the types of security procedures used within an organization.

Posted warnings in the computer facility is the most frequently used security method. Following the posting method, in order of declining frequencies, the arrangement of procedures is education, policing, the use of on-screen messages, signing a form, and no procedures. Other than to note that a “no procedure” response suggests that some schools may consider software pirating a non-issue or have not operationalized their concern about the issue, we do not address this category.

More interestingly, the remaining list ranges from those methods that are very impersonal, requiring a passive response, to those that are very personal, requiring an active response. Certainly the request for employees to attach their signatures to a document kept on file is more likely to elicit an awareness of legal responsibility for software piracy than a sign hanging on the wall that eventually becomes ‘invisible’ to regular visitors. The on-screen message is also more personal and active, to the point that a user must turn on the computer or call up a program, than policing or education, which are both rather impersonal and require a more passive response.

## 4. Discussion

Some respondents questioned the need for a school to spend its time and money to protect software vendors' interests. They feel that software vendors themselves should take care of the matter. Software vendors used to depend heavily upon technical protection schemes. The consequence was, users had difficulty legitimately backing up protected software, copying them into hard disks, or sharing them through local area networks. Because of the users' outcry over the protection schemes, most major software vendors have dropped them. Instead, software vendors have focused more on legal protection against copyright infringement $[8,12]$ . They also formed special industry trade associations, for example, Association of Data Processing Service Organization (ADAPSO), Software Publishers Association (SPA), and Business Software Alliance (BSA). SPA even set up a piracy hot line for informants to report suspected piracy.

In spite of the precedent-setting UCLA case, where the court ruled that state agencies do not have to pay damages for pirated software $[2,3]$ , educational institutions are responsible for the pirating activities of their employees – faculty and staff. When students engage in software piracy, legal liability does not extend to the organization, because there is no employment relationship. The university could, however, be held negligent if it fails to carry out proper safeguards or inform students of the obligations created by copyright law. Thus, the reason schools take efforts to prevent employees and students from pirating software is to protect the institution against the legal consequences of copyright infringements, not to protect software vendors' interests.

## 5. Conclusion

Business schools have become increasingly dependent on computers to carry out educational and research activities. Unfortunately, they have not adequately dealt with the issue of software piracy. This study presents the results of a survey returned by 241 AACSB member schools. Regardless of its size, software piracy occurs with equal frequency. Within schools, the directors of microcomputer facilities are most aware of their legal responsibility for software piracy activities.

One of the more interesting results relates to organizational policies. By themselves, policies have limited value. To be effective, consequences such as swift punishment of offenders need to back up well defined policies. Some of the strongest relationships found here involve the types of software security measures. Measures that require the most personal and active involvement (e.g., signing a form and calling up an on-screen message by logging on a computer or accessing a program) create an environment more sensitive to the piracy issue than do more passive measures (e.g., posting warnings, education, and policing). Finally, it appears that the problem of software piracy in institutions of higher education will continue until those in authority recognize that taking a strong stand against piracy is ultimately in their best interest – legally, ethically, and financially.
