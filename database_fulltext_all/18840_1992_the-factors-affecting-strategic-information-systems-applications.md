# The factors affecting strategic information systems applications

# An empirical assessment

William R. King

University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh PA, USA

Rajiv Sabherwal \*

Florida International University, Miami, FL, USA

The strategic potential of information systems (IS) is now well accepted. This paper examines some key characteristics of strategic IS applications and investigates their relationship with contextual factors representing the industry environment, the organization structure, and the IS function. Empirical analysis of 81 strategic IS applications in large companies shows that these applications contribute mainly to functions involving customers or competitors, primarily to reduce cost or provide some differentiation. They have limited effect on suppliers. However, the effect on suppliers is greater in decentralized organizations and in heterogeneous environments. Moreover, a heterogeneous environment and a mature IS function enhance the role of information storage. The implications for future research and for IS practice are examined.

Keywords: Information technology; Strategic utilization; Competitive advantage; Industry environment; Organization structure; Information systems maturity.

## 1. Introduction

Success stories about strategic applications of information systems (IS) frequently appear in business periodicals. The strategic applications differ in several ways, as reflected by the numerous frameworks proposed for classifying them [e.g., 23,46]. For example, some companies have used them for differentiation, and others to reduce cost [52,61]. Some IS applications have contributed at the competitive strategy level, while others have contributed at the internal strategy level [2]. Little research has however been conducted on the factors that influence the characteristics of the strategic IS applications. This study examines some key characteristics and their relationships with contextual factors – the industry environment, the organization structure, and the IS function. It addresses such questions as: How do large companies strategically use IS? To what extent are the strategic IS applications directed toward customers, and to what extent are they targeted at the other competitive forces? And how does the nature of these applications relate to such factors as the maturity of the IS function, organizational centralization, and industry dynamism?

## 2. Background

Strategic IS applications help an organization realize its goals and objectives. Following previous research on strategic IS use $[28,57]$ , a strategic IS application is considered to be one having a profound effect on a company's success and destiny, either by influencing or 'shaping' the company's strategy, or by playing a direct role in the implementation or support of the company's strategy. For example, an inventory control system is strategic only if it plays a direct role in the implementation of the business strategy and the achievement of competitive advantage. This might be the case if product availability is a critical success factor for the business or if inventory costs are a major cost element for a firm seeking to be a low cost producer.

The literature on strategic use of IS has been strongly influenced by Porter's work [44,45] based in industrial organization (IO) economics. The earlier perspective was primarily deterministic, with industry structure seen as determining firm performance, but Porter has incorporated the reverse effect: the influence of company strategy on industry structure. Several researchers in strategy and IO economics have also examined the relationship between a company's context, including the environment and the organization structure, and strategy [5,53,60]. They have indicated, for example, that innovation is more important in dynamic and uncertain environments, while a cost leadership strategy is associated with stable and predictable environments and with organizational structures characterized by a high degree of control. Some recent empirical studies have examined strategy in terms of Porter's generic strategies of low cost, differentiation, and focus. Thus, Miller [38] studied the relationships of Porter's strategies to the structures and environments in a study of 89 undiversified firms, and Kim and Lim [27] related strategies resembling Porter's generic strategies to the environment in a study of 54 high growth electronics firms.

Several authors on strategic IS use [e.g., 6] have utilized Porter's concepts of generic strategies and key competitive forces. Ways to search for such applications have been proposed [35], and modifications to Porter's concepts have been suggested [51]. The few empirical studies in this area indicate customer service and cost competitiveness to be the major areas of strategic IS use [29]. Strategic IS applications have been shown to be facilitated by a top management that participates in IS activities, a high-pressure environment, and an information intensive industry [25]. Information intensity has also been associated with the use of IS at higher levels of strategy, and in a greater variety of ways, rather than concentrating only on customers.

Empirical research on strategic IS use has emphasized the IS-related factors, but it has devoted much less attention to the effects of the organizational structure and the industry environment. Moreover, little systematic research based on large samples of strategic IS applications has been done to relate the nature of these applications to the contextual factors. This is a serious limitation, especially because strategy researchers have found a relationship between strategy and context.

This paper addresses this limitation of research on strategic IS use by attempting to relate some key characteristics of the strategic IS applications to the contextual factors, including factors characterizing the external environment, the organizational context, and the IS function.

## 3. Characteristics of strategic IS applications

Five key characteristics of the strategic IS applications were selected based on an extensive literature review [e.g., 10,11,49,51,62]. These characteristics include the IS skill utilized in the strategic application, the level of corporate strategy at which the application is based, the key competitive force at which the application is targeted, the form of competitive advantage it provides, and the degree of its success.

Several authors have examined the IS skill utilized in the strategic IS applications. They present these applications as requiring one or more of three generic skills: Information processing, storage, and transmission. Information storage, characterized by large databases, has two key aspects, namely the volume of information stored, and the time for which this information is stored [19]. Information processing activity may be associated with a specific product or service, with parts of the organization, or be at the organizational level. Information transmission skill may be utilized for information flows within the organization, or in inter-organizational systems involving linkages with customers or suppliers [13].

The strategic IS applications are believed to concentrate at one of several levels. Parsons' [43] three-level framework for examining information technology impact included the industry level, the firm level, and strategy level effects. Porter and Millar [46] similarly discussed the role information technology can play in spanning new businesses, altering industry structures, and supporting generic strategies. More recently, the opportunities arising from IS are viewed as applying to one of three levels of corporate strategy: The internal strategy, the competitive strategy, and the business portfolio strategy levels. The internal strategy level concerns the development of efficient and effective organization structures and processes, the competitive strategy level focuses on the moves within the industry in which the organization does business, and the business portfolio strategy level pertains to the choice of industries in which to compete and the positioning of the organization into them.

Porter's discussion of the key competitive forces in a company's industry environment has been widely applied to the strategic use of IS [e.g., 35,56]. IS applications can potentially alter the balance with respect to any of the five key competitive forces: customers, suppliers, competitors, new entrants, and substitutes. IS applications may be used to introduce switching costs, making it more costly for customers to change suppliers. They may be used to create links with suppliers of raw materials or other inputs to get benefits from just-in-time delivery systems. They may be used to alter the balance of power among the existing competitors by providing the firm with some advantages, and to influence the buyer's decision to substitute or not, by lowering cost, improving the perceived performance, or increasing the functionality of a product, etc. They may also be used to create entry barriers for entrants from other industries, based on costs, service, technology, etc.

Porter's generic strategies of low cost, differentiation, and focus have had a considerable influence on research on strategy and strategic IS use. They have also been modified by some researchers. Miller [37] has argued that differentiation may be achieved either through innovation, involving the creation of most attractive and up-to-date products by leading the competitors in quality, design innovations, etc., or through intensive marketing, involving the creation of a unique image for the product using prestige pricing, advertising, market segmentation, etc. Miller also included “strategic breadth”, which is the opposite of Porter's focus strategy, in his four generic strategies – cost leadership, product innovation, marketing differentiation, and strategic breadth.

In the context of strategic IS applications, Wiseman modified Porter's generic strategies in proposing five strategic thrusts, which may be considered as five different forms of competitive advantage that may result from a strategic IS application [62]: (a) differentiation advantage, by distinguishing the company's products and services from competitors, or by reducing the differentiation advantage of rivals; (b) cost advantage, by reducing the company's costs, its supplier's costs, or its customer's costs, or by raising the costs of its competitors; (c) innovation advantage, by introducing a product or process change that results in a fundamental transformation in the way business is conducted in the industry; (d) growth advantage, by volume or geographical expansions, backward or forward integration, product-line or entry diversification; and (e) alliance advantage, by forging marketing agreement, forming joint ventures, or making acquisitions.

The success of the strategic IS application has been discussed in terms of: (a) the size of the competitive advantage or the perceived contribution to the company's performance [12]; and (b) the sustainability of this advantage including the time period for which the advantage may be expected to last, and the difficulty faced by the competitors in copying it [10]. In this study, the strategic IS application's success indicates its overall contribution to the company, incorporating both size and sustainability of competitive advantage.

## 4. The contextual factors

In their framework for IS research, Ives et al. [24] included the external environment, the organizational environment, and IS environment due to their influence on information systems. These three environments may also be used to characterize the context for strategic IS applications. The IS function is a subunit of a firm, and the characteristics of the host organization as well as the business environment may be expected to influence the strategic IS application [18,33]. Moreover, since IS is a unique subunit, its characteristics should be also considered in examining applications that are centered around it [4].

The IS application's context is thus considered as comprising three components: (a) the environmental context, representing the industry in which the company operates, competitors, customers, suppliers etc., as well as the government, trade unions etc.; (b) the organizational context, characterizing the intra-organizational environment outside the IS function; and (c) the IS functional context. These contextual factors are now discussed.

## 4.1. The environmental context

Woo and Cooper [63] found that effective businesses in dissimilar environments pursue different strategies. Hambrick [22] suggested that different strategies do not lead to equal success within an industry. Some strategies were more successful, depending on the environment. The environment has also been highlighted in studies of generic strategies. Its importance has been recognized in some research on strategic IS use as well.

This study uses three characteristics of the environment – heterogeneity, dynamism, and hostility [39,40] – to assess its influence on strategic IS use. Environmental heterogeneity represents variations among the company's markets that require diversity in production and marketing orientations. Environmental dynamism reflects the rate of change and innovation in the industry and the unpredictability of the actions of competitors and customers. Thus, heterogeneity indicates the number and diversity of external factors, while dynamism indicates the rate and unpredictability of change in these factors. They both represent an information processing approach $[15,30]$ . In contrast, environmental hostility indicates the degree of threat posed by the multifacetedness and intensity of the competition and the downswings of the company's principal industry, reflecting a resource dependence perspective focusing on the restrictiveness of the environment $[26]$ .

## 4.2. The organizational context

Organization structure is regarded as an important antecedent in the literature on strategy and innovation adoption [e.g., 20,41]. Some studies on strategic IS use [e.g., 47] have also incorporated characteristics of organization structure.

In this study, two characteristics – centralization and formalization – were included to represent the effect of organization structure on the strategic use of IS. Centralization represents “the degree to which the right to make decisions and evaluate activities is concentrated,” while formalization indicates “the extent to which an organization uses rules and procedures to prescribe behavior” [18].

## 4.3. The information systems functional context

Several authors [31,32,48,55] have considered the influence of IS-related factors on IS planning and strategic use. The role of IS-related variables has also been considered in other contexts, such as the performance of computer-based information systems [8,34]. Moreover, Benbasat et al. [4] have developed an instrument to determine the 'maturity' of a company's IS function. IS maturity incorporates the various attributes of IS considered critical to the identification of strategic IS applications, such as top management's knowledge of IS and involvement of senior IS executives in the company's strategic planning. It thus represents the progress of the IS function from the era of data processing and management information systems into the strategic IS era [58].

## 5. The research hypotheses

The relationship of the characteristics of the strategic IS application with the environmental context is now examined, followed by a discussion of their relationships with the organizational context and the IS functional context.

## 5.1. Relationship of environmental context with strategic IS application characteristics

Recent research has shown the strategic use of IS to be considerably influenced by industry dynamism and competitive pressures [25]. Such environmental dynamism is likely to have the greatest impact at the competitive strategy level. Internal strategy applications, whose effect is primarily within the company, and business portfolio strategy applications, which concentrate on moves into other industries, are less influenced by the dynamism of the company's current industry environment.

Stable environments enable organizations to pursue a defensive strategy, based on high efficiency and cost-effectiveness $[36]$ . However, a rapidly changing environment necessitates frequent alterations, inhibiting efficiency, the foundation of cost leadership. It thus creates severe diseconomies for companies pursuing a low cost strategy. Innovation is more prevalent in such environments. It benefits companies in dynamic environments through more competitive products. Without innovation, organizations in dynamic environments would fall behind the competition. On the other hand, innovation is a costly luxury in stable environments. A dynamic environment may therefore be expected to facilitate strategic IS use for innovation advantage but inhibit strategic IS use for cost advantage. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Environmental dynamism is positively associated with the extent to which the strategic IS application: (a) contributes at the competitive strategy level; and (b) provides an innovation advantage, but negatively associated with the extent to which it (c) provides a cost advantage.

A heterogeneous environment implies many differences in customer, supplier, and competitor behavior in the company's target markets. This increases the breadth of relevant information. Since strategic use of information has been shown to be facilitated by information storage skill, we may expect this skill to play a more strategic role in heterogeneous environments. For example, databases containing delivery time, quality, price, and other performance information for alternative suppliers is more likely to be strategic when suppliers are numerous, and widely different in several ways.

If a company with significantly different target markets employs a focus strategy, it might spread itself too thin. A broad strategy, aimed at growth and alliance, is recommended under such environments. Conversely, a company pursuing growth and alliance is likely to perceive its environment as very heterogeneous. Environmental heterogeneity may thus be expected to be positively associated with the levels of growth advantage and alliance advantage resulting from the strategic IS application.

Furthermore, in attempting growth, a company with dissimilar products and markets may attempt to form linkages with suppliers. Such supplier links are initiated more commonly in unpredictable environments. For example, IS applications that support databases containing delivery time, quality, price, another performance information for alternative suppliers are more likely to be strategic in heterogeneous environments characterized by numerous and widely different suppliers.

Hypothesis 2. Environmental heterogeneity is positively associated with the extent to which the strategic IS application (a) utilizes information storage, contributes with respect to (b) the suppliers, and provides (c) a growth advantage and (d) an alliance advantage.

A hostile industry environment makes strategic IS applications more urgent. The pressure due to sharply declining profit margins, decreased demand for the company's products, deregulation, etc., which characterize such environments often sets off IS applications at the competitive strategy level as the company attempts to outperform its competitors.

Hypothesis 3. Environmental hostility is positively associated with the extent to which the strategic

IS application contributes at the competitive strategy level.

## 5.2. Relationship of organizational context with strategic IS application characteristics

A high degree of centralization implies that the decision-making authority is concentrated in the hands of very few people. As a result, IS managers may not be able to influence the important decisions concerning strategic IS-based alliances. Alliances with suppliers would be less likely in centralized organizations.

Moreover, when pursuing growth, authority must be delegated to reduce the burden of top managers. Therefore, strategic IS applications that contribute at the business portfolio strategy level and provide growth or alliance advantage would benefit from the administrative task being divided, as is the case with decentralized organizations. When lacking such decentralization, organizations are more likely to pursue strategic IS applications at the internal or competitive strategy levels.

Hypothesis 4. Centralization of organization structure is negatively associated with the extent to which the strategic IS application (a) contributes at the business portfolio strategy level, (b) provides an advantage with respect to suppliers, and provides (c) a growth advantage and (d) alliance advantage.

Cost strategies have a short feedback loop – the time between decisions, actions, and results – and tangible results. In companies pursuing a low cost strategy, there is usually a strong emphasis on formal profit and budget controls, in order to keep costs, and thus prices, at a minimum. Furthermore, a high degree of formalization emphasizes cost-effectiveness, since such benefits can be more easily quantified and formally evaluated. Formalization may be therefore expected to be positively associated with cost leadership.

Hypothesis 5. Formalization of organization structure is positively associated with the extent to which the strategic IS application provides a cost advantage.

## 5.3. Relationship of IS functional context with strategic IS application characteristics

Factors related to IS maturity, such as the IS managers' knowledge of the firm's business objectives, the top management's involvement in IS planning, and the integration of the IS and strategy functions have been consistently shown to facilitate strategic IS use. An increase in IS maturity implies an enhanced importance of information technology. As the IS function gains experience with designing and developing important information systems, and as the top management and users recognize the strategic potential of information technology, a shift may be expected from internally oriented, cost-saving applications toward applications that differentiate the company's products/services from those of its competitors and toward applications that help the company to compete in its current industry environment or move into other industries. Moreover, the strategic IS applications can potentially make a greater contribution in such companies.

Hypothesis 6. IS maturity is negatively associated with the extent to which the strategic IS application (a) contributes at the internal strategy level and (b) provides a cost advantage, but positively associated with (c) the application's success.

## 6. Data collection

The data were collected using a questionnaire that was first tested with six senior IS executives in large companies. They were given the questionnaire and requested to go through it for meaningfulness, relevance, and clarity. A personal interview, about an hour long, was conducted with each executive three to four days later. Their suggestions were very helpful in arriving at the final questionnaire. Changes made as a result of one executive's comments were accommodated before the next interview. This resulted in a continuous improvement in the questionnaire. The process was terminated after six such interviews, since the last two executives did not suggest any significant changes.

Following the pilot-test, the questionnaire was mailed to 447 IS executives from the Corporate 1000 [14], a directory of the 1000 largest manufacturing and service companies in the US. Only very senior executives are listed in this directory. The senior IS executive was considered to be the most informed about the strategic IS applications based on past research [e.g., 50]. For example, Johnston and Carrico [25] found that half of the active decision-makers and evaluators/influencers in strategic IS applications were IS executives.

Of the 447 executives to whom the questionnaire was mailed, 85 responded. These responses resulted from the initial mailing followed by two reminders. Eighty-one responses were complete; each briefly described a strategic IS application and the resulting benefits, and provided data about the nature of the application as well as the contextual factors. These applications are quite diverse and seem to represent a broad range of strategic IS applications. Moreover, the responding companies appear to have obtained considerable benefits from these applications such that they may appropriately be termed strategic. A sample of eight and their benefits is provided in Appendix A.

## 6.1. Non-response analysis

The 85 returned questionnaires represent a 19% response rate. This relatively low response rate necessitated non-response analysis to ensure that respondents did not systematically differ from non-respondents.

In contrast, the respondent was requested to split 100 points into five choices for the form of competitive advantage and the key competitive force. Since the number of choices among which 100 points were to be split were greater here, a slightly different coding scheme was employed. All ten items for these two aspects were coded as follows: (1) 0–5%; (2) 5–10%; (3) 10–15%; (4) 15–25%; (5) 25–50%; (6) 50–75%; and (7) 75% and above.

Though the need for a measure of success or size and sustainability of competitive advantage has been widely discussed, we could not find any existing measure. Based on the literature, we developed a measure consisting of six items: (a) the application's contribution to the company's market share; and (b) its contribution to the company's return on investment; (c) the perceived difficulty for a competitor in copying the application; (d) the duration for which the company expected to have an advantage over its competitors, due to this application; (e) the innovativeness of the application; and (f) the proactiveness of the application. All the items used 7-point scales. For the innovativeness and proactiveness items, brief definitions of the anchors of the 7-point scales (incremental and radical innovations, and proactive and reactive applications, respectively) were provided. Principal components analysis (with varimax rotation) was conducted to examine whether 'success' needed to be divided into two constructs – size and sustainability of competitive advantage. The results of this analysis supported considering success of the strategic IS application as one unidimensional construct. The reliability (standardized item-alpha) of this scale was 0.60, considered satisfactory for new scales. Moreover, all the corrected item-total correlations were significant at the 0.05 level.

## 7. Data analysis and results

The means and standard deviations for the research variables are provided in Table 2. Based on these, the following observations may be made:

Observation 1. Strategic IS applications were found: (a) in highly centralized companies; (b) operating in hostile industry environments, and (c) with a mature IS function.

Strategic IS applications were more commonly found in centralized companies. This suggests that strategic applications are more common in companies where sponsorship by a high-level executive (a “champion”) would be most effective. Moreover, the more common occurrence of strategic IS applications in hostile industry environments suggests that they might play an important role when the industry environment poses a major threat to the company. Finally, the strategic IS applications being more prevalent in companies with a mature IS function supports results from past research and should be encouraging to the IS professionals.

Observation 2. Strategic IS applications: (a) contribute mainly at internal strategy and competitive strategy levels, with much lower contribution at the business portfolio strategy level; (b) are commonly targeted at customers or competitors, with little contribution with respect to suppliers and substitutes; (c) provide considerable differentiation or low cost advantage but little alliance advantage.

These findings resemble our previous results $[52]$ that IS applications infrequently contribute at the business portfolio strategy level, while delivering often at competitive and internal strategy levels. The earlier study had also found that these applications provide an advantage mainly with respect to customers and competitors. Rarely were IS applications found to yield a strategic advantage concerning suppliers or substitutes. A third study also found a similar predominance of customer-oriented applications [29].

Our finding that strategic IS applications provide differentiation and cost advantage, with alliance advantage being quite rare, also occurs in previous research. Differentiation and low cost represent the two generic strategies emphasized by Porter and Millar. This suggests that strategic IS applications have been used mainly for these generic strategies, and to a much less degree for the other forms (innovation, growth, and alliance) proposed by Wiseman.

Observation 3. These strategic IS applications are highly successful.

The respondents consider the applications to have made a major contribution to the company's performance indicating that these applications they indeed view them as “strategic.” Providing the definition of “strategic” IS applications in the questionnaire appears to have been effective, despite its adverse effect on the response rate.

## 7.1. Analysis of research hypotheses

The research hypotheses were tested using correlational and multiple regression analyses. The correlations (Table 2) present the simple relationships among the contextual factors and the characteristics of the application, while the multiple regressions simultaneously control for the other contextual factors and disclose the collective variance explained. Table 3 shows the results of the regressions for the characteristics of the application, with the contextual factors as the independent variables.

## 7.1.1. The IS skill utilized

The regression for use of information storage skill showed 14.69% of its variance to be explained by the contextual factors. The F-value was significant (at 0.10 level). Support was found for Hypothesis 2a proposing that use of information storage is positively related to environmental heterogeneity. Apparently, database technology and other skills associated with information storage can make a greater contribution in companies operating in heterogeneous environments. This may be because such environments involve a multitude of elements that the company needs to track. The regression analysis also showed the use of information storage skill to be greater in companies with more mature IS functions. A mature IS function probably implies that the company is more likely to have stored large amounts of useful information, which it may deploy for strategic purposes.

The regressions for the use of information processing and information transmission provided low $R^{2}$ values (3.27 and 2.86%, respectively). They did not show any context variable to be associated with the use of these skills. However, this is not surprising: No hypotheses had been proposed for the use of information processing and information transmission.

## 7.1.2. The level of corporate strategy affected

The three regressions for the contribution at the internal strategy level, the competitive strategy level, and the business portfolio strategy level produced poor results. The $R^{2}$ values were very low (6.81, 5.82 and 5.40%, respectively), and no beta was significant (at the 0.05 level, 1-tailed). The correlation analyses confirmed the poor results of the regression analysis: No contextual factor was significantly associated with contribution at any of the three strategy levels. These results are very surprising. We had proposed the contribution at the internal strategy level to be negatively associated with IS maturity (Hypothesis 6a), the contribution at the competitive strategy level to be positively related to environmental dynamism (Hypothesis 1a) and environmental hostility (Hypothesis 3), and the contribution at the business portfolio strategy level to be negatively associated with centralization (Hypothesis 4a). All these hypotheses were rejected.

## 7.1.3. The key competitive force affected

The regression for the effect on suppliers produced excellent results: The $R^{2}$ was high (24.45%) and the F-value was significant (at the 0.01 level). We had proposed the strategic IS application's effect on suppliers to be associated positively with environmental heterogeneity (Hypothesis 2b) and negatively with centralization (Hypothesis 4b). Both these hypotheses were strongly supported.

The regressions for the effects on the other four competitive forces – competitors, customers, new entrants, and substitutes – produced non-significant results. As shown in Table 3, the $R^{2}$ values for these regressions were low (4.49, 8.14, 3.26 and 5.47%, respectively).

We did find the effect on customers to be negatively associated with environmental dynamism. This relationship had not been hypothesized, and the result is thus unexpected. Considering that the strategic IS applications in this study were commonly targeted at customers, this result has interesting implications. It seems that strategic IS applications are primarily used to affect the relationship with customers, but when the environment becomes increasingly dynamic, companies start looking for other kinds of strategic IS applications. This behavior is supported by our finding the use of IS applications with respect to suppliers to be greater in heterogeneous environments. It also relates to Johnston and Carrico's finding competitive pressure in the industry environment to "set off serious attempts to find ways to gain advantage from IT".

## 7.1.4. The form of competitive advantage provided

The regression for differentiation advantage provided an $R^{2}$ of 9.42%. Surprisingly, it showed differentiation advantage to be negatively associated with dynamism. This contradicts the earlier results indicating that a differentiation strategy can benefit a company in dynamic environment by helping create customer loyalty. Along with the negative relationship between dynamism and the effect on customers, it suggests that customer-oriented systems are commonly developed in environments where customer preferences are perceived to be more stable and predictable.

The regression for cost advantage produced a low $R^{2}$ (6.86%). It did not provide support for Hypothesis 1c proposing cost advantage to be negatively associated with environmental dynamism, Hypothesis 5 proposing it to be positively associated with formalization, or Hypothesis 6b proposing it to be negatively associated with IS maturity. The correlations for these relationships were not significant either. These hypotheses were therefore rejected. The regression for innovation advantage also produced a low $R^{2}$ (3.11%). Hypothesis 1b proposing it to be positively associated with dynamism was rejected.

The regressions for growth advantage and alliance advantage resulted in $R^{2}$ values of 13.34 and 9.42%, respectively. They supported Hypothesis 2c, proposing growth advantage to be positively associated with heterogeneity, and Hypothesis 4c, proposing it to be negatively associated with centralization. Hypothesis 4d proposing alliance advantage to be negatively associated with centralization was also supported, but Hypothesis 2d proposing it to be negatively associated with heterogeneity was not.

## 7.1.5. Success of the strategic IS application

The regression for the IS application's success produced an $\mathbb{R}^2$ of 10.2%. It supported Hypothesis 6c, proposing success to be positively associated with IS maturity. This intuitively appealing finding shows that the companies with more mature IS functions are able to derive greater benefits from strategic IS applications.

## 8. Summary

Table 4 summarizes the research hypotheses and the associated results. Overall, the results make our research hypotheses about the relationship between the contextual variables and the application characteristics appear in poor light, especially considering that statistical power was quite high at 0.84 [3]. This value of statistical power assumed an effect size of 0.30, as recommended for medium effects [59]. Support for only seven of sixteen hypothesized relationships, along with the low $R^{2}$ values for all regressions except for the effect on suppliers and the use of information storage, suggests the need to identify and examine other factors that may influence the nature of strategic IS applications, such as the company's business strategy.

This study builds on the past research on strategic use of IS. It confirms some of the findings of these studies (such as strategic applications being targeted more commonly at customers than at the other competitive forces), and also raises some interesting questions. For example, what is the reason for the rare occurrence of supplier-oriented applications?

A number of hypotheses, based on past research in information systems and strategic planning were proposed and tested. Future research needs to look further into these hypotheses using different samples.

This study concentrated on large companies with a mature IS department. This population was used because such companies are more likely to have developed and benefited from strategic IS applications. While this approach did yield the desired sample of more than 80 strategic IS applications, it necessarily reduced the variation in the size of the companies and in their IS environment. These limitations must be taken into account in interpreting the results.

The study yields useful results for top-management and information systems executives.

## 8.1. How have the successful companies strategically used IS?

Strategic IS applications seem to have been used mainly at internal and competitive strategy level. They have been largely targeted at customers or competitors, and used primarily to attain low cost or differentiation. These results indicate the potential contribution of IS applications toward corporate success at a higher level than their current contribution. Considerable scope exists for employing IS in newer ways – with respect to suppliers and substitutes – as the few examples demonstrate. Viewed together, the relative lack of IS applications at the business portfolio strategy level, applications targeted at suppliers, and applications which provide alliance advantage, suggests that strategic alliances with suppliers have not been much used. Definite possibilities exist in this area, and we should witness an increase in such IS use in the future.

## 8.2. How does the context affect the characteristics of the strategic IS applications?

The study showed the use of IS applications with respect to suppliers to be facilitated by a decentralized organization, and by a heterogeneous industry environment. This suggests that supplier-oriented applications are more common under adverse industry conditions. Supplier-oriented applications require greater cooperation from a second organization (the supplier) than is required in customer links. It appears that organizations therefore resort to this approach only under considerable industry pressure. Moreover, the necessary long-term commitment from the suppliers may be easier to obtain in decentralized organizations which permit lower level executives to play a greater role.

Growth advantage applications are more likely in heterogeneous environments and IS applications are used more for growth advantage in decentralized companies. These results probably reflect the greater likelihood for growth that exists in decentralized conglomerates operating in heterogeneous environments – through acquisitions, mergers, and links with suppliers – as compared to centralized companies in relatively homogeneous environments.

Information storage was found to make a greater contribution in heterogeneous environments. This may be because a heterogeneous environment enhances the breadth of relevant information, enabling information storage to play a more strategic role. The use of information storage was also found to be associated with IS maturity. A mature IS function probably increases the likelihood of the company having stored large amounts of useful information, which it may deploy for a strategic advantage.

The strategic IS applications were found to be more successful in companies with mature IS departments. This result should be encouraging to the IS executives, and should be useful to them in building a case with their top management for greater commitment to IS.

Numerous companies have successfully utilized information resources for strategic purposes demonstrating the importance of the area “strategic use of IS”. Several prescriptive models and exploratory frameworks have been proposed to assist companies in looking for strategic IS applications. However, little empirical research has been conducted. This study is the first large sample investigation of the effects of the industry environment, the organizational characteristics, and the IS function on the strategic utilization of IS.
