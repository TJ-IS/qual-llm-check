# Creativity software and DSS Cautionary findings

Douglas E. Durand \* and Susie H. VanHuss

University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

Contrary to expectations, a study of creativity enhancing software showed that user creativity declined when using the software on an analysis task. Software increased the number of alternatives, but independent judges determined that these were less original or inventive than those produced without the software. These findings were consistent for both 'highly creative' and 'less creative' groups. Computer technology is an added variable that can interact to inhibit creativity in a moderately complex case analysis task. Creativity inducing software appears to need further development before it can contribute meaningfully to improving decision support systems.

Keywords: Creativity, Decision support systems, Decision aids, Creativity enhancing software.

## Introduction

Recent interest in using software for creativity improvement has emphasized the need for enhanced non-analytical or right-brain activity. Much of the interest surrounds a desire to increase performance in decision and problem solving processes. Elam and Mead [4] conceptualized the role of creativity within DSS models; they point out that models of the creative process look similar to those of decision making (problem identification, information gathering, alternative generation, assessment, and selection). Interest in creativity, as it applies to DSS, coincides with Young's [17] more recent call for support of the “creativity process” as part of what he terms “worker support systems.”

## What is creativity?

There are several definitions of creativity; most include the idea of uniqueness and adaptation. Here, we use the following definition:

A combination of the flexibility, originality, and sensitivity to ideas that enables the learner to break away from usual sequences of thought into different and productive sequence [7].

Such a view of creativity has important links to improving the decision process. This seems particularly true during early stages (e.g., problem definition and generation of alternatives).

## Why increase creativity?

Creativity includes various positive connotations, such as innovation, inspiration, individuality, and imagination. Over the years, it has been credited with providing the impetus for many pace-setting discoveries and technical advances. Creativity improves the ability to adjust to new events and to develop unique approaches to problems. It is vital to improved decision making by groups or individuals.

The decision sciences are a creative field. As Evans states, “The future success and contributions of the decision sciences lie in dealing effectively with semi-structured and ill-structured problems. In order to do this, creative thinking is essential” [6].

## Approaches to creativity enhancement

In the late 1950's, several studies supported the positive effects of various techniques to enhance problem solving. Osborn [9] popularized the brainstorming concept. A positive correlation between quantity and quality of ideas was demonstrated [10]. Deferred judgment during the idea production stage had positive effects on decision quality. Extended effort to produce ideas showed high quality ideas could be generated [2,3]. Further, the best ideas were found to occur in the final one-third of an idea list or session.

When these results were joined by the report that training had a positive effect on generating “good ideas”, the stage was set for a broad-based adoption of the training-to-increase-creativity concept. It has been decades since this surge of interest, but recent technology has sparked new attention.

We agree with Evans that “creative behavior is not the property of a select few. … We all have culturally produced blocks to the use of this ability.” Research indicates two general approaches to enhance an individual’s creativity. First, remove the internal blocks by understanding the unconscious influence of background, experience, and habit on creativity. Second, encourage creative thinking by removing external blocks (e.g., analyze problems, learn new skills, practice methods to release imagination, avoid premature judgment of ideas, etc.).

Most approaches emphasize removing external blocks; this was the case in our study. We tested the efficacy of a software program designed to reduce external blocks among beginning business people.

## Hypothesis statement

To guide the research, the study was guided by the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis: Use of creativity enhancing software will increase creative responses as measured by: (a) quantity of alternatives reported, (b) uniqueness of alternatives reported.

## Method

Subjects. Eighty-eight MBA students served as subjects, with an average work experience of 1.8 years. Their average age was 26 years (sd. 2.4); 73% were male. They represented white collar entry-level employees of the near future.

Software. The Idea Generator [13] (a software package) is designed to increase brainstorming and generating alternatives. its basic functions are easily learned. The software assists the user to generate many alternatives, to defer judgement about them, and to select the most advantageous ones. Using a computerized format, it faithfully follows general practices for brainstorming and problem solving.

Procedure. Two short case analyses served to test the effects of the software. As part of a separate activity, the latent creativity of the subjects was measured using a test of creativity. It measured their creativity prior to the introduction of the software. Subjects were not told that creativity was a focus of the study.

The subjects were randomly assigned to two equal size groups. Each received one of the two cases for analysis. Each person wrote a three page executive report; no enhancement software was available for this portion of the study.

After submitting the first report, the second case (the alternative to that already analyzed) was assigned and the Idea Generator was introduced. A major design objective of this software is to increase the number and quality of alternatives considered when making a decision (e.g., case study recommendation). Each subject was instructed to perform the second analysis using the software tool. Subjects submitted a written case analysis after using the software. This procedure reduced variance resulting from individual differences since subjects analyzed both cases and served as their own control group.

## Scoring of responses

Three classifications (twenty-eight items in all) were used to score the writing samples. The three were: creativity (five items), organization and structure of the composition (ten items), and quality of communication (ten items). The instrument is included in Appendix A.

Using the coding categories, two persons independently scored the anonymous written case analyses. The raters had extensive experience at analyzing and judging written compositions for style, content, written coherence, etc. Neither was told the goal or purpose of the study; they were unaware that the two cases were produced using any different aid, software, instructions, etc. Nor did they know that creativity was the prime research focus. The inter-scorer reliability for the coding of the twenty-five coding categories was r = 0.78.

By contrast, when using the software, 93% of the variance was explained by “detail of ideas” and “quantity of ideas generated.”

This suggests that the subjects used the program properly; it prodded them to develop many alternatives before making a final choice. Such advice is common in the literature. When no prodding was present, the subjects presented fewer alternatives. For both cases, only two independent variables were needed to explain over 90 percent of the variance.

Table 2 shows the results of two cases. Surprisingly, in each of the twenty-eight tests, use of the creativity software resulted in lower creativity scores. Eight of these tests were significant: besides creativity (p = 0.043), writing style and effectiveness declined (i.e., effective sentence structure (p = 0.021), conclusions supported by facts (p = 0.034), quality of communication (p = 0.028), specific language (p = 0.048), appropriate vocabulary (p = 0.016), report was complete (p = 0.016), and “reader gets the intended message” (p = 0.025)).

Analysis of differences between male and female subjects showed similar patterns. Consistent with earlier studies [14], females scored higher on various creativity measures than males in both conditions. These differences were significant for the quantity of ideas generated (p = 0.033), idea originality (p = 0.038), idea detail (p = 0.039), or when no software was used (total creativity (p = 0.032)).

To determine whether the subjects' latent or prior creativity had an effect on the value of the software, creativity test scores were used. Subjects were divided into “high creative” and “low creative” groups based on the original creativity scores. Then t-tests of differences between “with software” and “without software” cases analyses were conducted. It was hypothesized that persons with low creativity test scores might benefit more from the software. Results of this analysis failed to show differences between “creative” and “non-creative” subjects; use of the software decreased the case analysis creativity across the spectrum.

When scores of the two cases were compared, no differences emerged in the “without software” condition. Yet, differences were noted “with software” for three of the six categories (detail of ideas $p = 0.020$ ), depth of ideas (pool), and reader given flexibility $p = 0.042$ ). This suggests that the software has differing effects, depending upon the case stimuli. Exploration of this remains for future study.

## Discussion

The effects of creativity enhancing software was clear and unexpected. Although the subjects appeared to follow the instructions carefully, the findings were contrary to our hypotheses. We measured clear differences when persons used the program and when they worked without it. When using software, the number of reported alternatives increased. This supported Hypothesis 1a, consistent with expectations regarding increased creativity and quality of decisions. A correlation between quantity of ideas and quality of alternatives had been widely reported in earlier studies (e.g. [16]). However, findings generally consistent to ours were reported by Elam and Mead. They found mixed results using a different creativity enhancing package in a very small sample.

Surprisingly, solutions developed with the aid of the software were judged less creative. Thus, the software had the opposite effect of that expected. Hypothesis 1b was rejected. This appeared to be the result of following the requirements of the software (form) but not its intention (function). Further, the software had a negative effect among both “high creative” and “low creative” groups.

These findings were puzzling since the Idea Generator faithfully follows the conventional wisdom regarding methods to increase creative problem solving. Since there was no control over session length and personal effort when using the software, the previous findings regarding extended effort or longer session may not be sustained; low quality alternatives could be produced, since no external pressure was present to require extended effort. This contrasts with previous findings that the generation of ideas was “simply related to the proportion of time spent working alone” [11]. Interestingly, performance when using software is similar to that when working in a group (see, [12]).

Computer technology is an added variable that can interact to inhibit creativity in a moderately complex case analysis task. Creativity inducing software appears to need further development before it can contribute meaningfully to improving Decision Support Systems.
