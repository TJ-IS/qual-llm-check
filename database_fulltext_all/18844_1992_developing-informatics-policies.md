# Developing informatics policies

Víctor L. Pérez

Universidad de Chile, Santiago, Chile

It is important to consider the motives of countries that are developing their informatics policies. For even though the politics of informatics may result in mandatory regulation of all activities associated with this and related fields or just in a set of recommendations, national informatics policies tend to develop common attitudes to those situations that arise when a country faces the challenge of developing computing systems and their applications. This paper discusses some typical situations and presents the problems and questions that arise when developing a national policy on informatics.

Keywords: National policies on informatics, Information technology, National informatics planning.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of scientific and technological knowledge in computing has resulted in new theories, methodologies, and techniques in computer science and related fields. At the same time, new methodologies, techniques, and products are being generated in almost every other discipline using or affected by computing products and their applications.

The effect of these developments on economic and social factors has not been uniformly felt in and applied by all countries. Some have seen these developments as new competitive weapons to be used in their international markets. The financial, productive, and commercial efforts that have been invested in these developments have produced, in turn, a change in the economic and political relationships of these countries.

The impact of new international relationships on the economic and social growth of non-participating countries has led some to identify the obstacles to their development $[6,10]$ .

The reactions of non-participating countries have not all been the same. Some have allowed market forces to orient towards developing new knowledge in computing and in finding how this may be employed in the manufacturing and service sector [1,8]. Some have stated guidelines for the private sector [1], while other countries have reserved for themselves the main role in the process [1,9,11]. Others have decided that their processes cannot be attacked in an isolated way and have promoted the formation of regional efforts for specific purposes in the computing area (e.g., standardizing communication protocols, grouping research and development activities, and using the State's purchasing power as a means of increasing markets for their own products) [1,3,4,5,7].

No matter what are the declared reasons (e.g., sovereignty over the transfer of national data, countering the threat of economic dependency, need for improved productivity in other industrial sectors, economics and social telecommunications, and individual considerations, such as privacy) almost every country is trying to develop, either explicitly or implicitly, a policy in a wide variety of situations, such as how to:

(a) Decide which industrial sectors have priority in the use of informatics (i.e., computing and computer based) technology.

(b) Establish financial and tax oriented incentives to accelerate technological innovations in areas considered fundamental for the country's development.

(c) Foment research in computing and related areas in order to adapt technology to the country's needs.

(d) Assign responsibility for the resources and applications of informatics technology in the public sector.

Several countries (e.g., Brazil, Mexico, Singapore, Malaysia, India, and Argentine) have developed national policies on informatics $[1,2,3,4,8,9,11]$ . At the same time, international organizations have sponsored the formation of regional efforts in the informatics field; examples are The Club de Cali in Latin America $[4]$ and The Yamoussoukro Conference in Africa $[6]$ .

Even though each country faces different conditions and visualizes itself at a different stage of the scientific and technological development and application of informatics, its national policies on informatics reflect a similarity of ideas, hopes, and fears.

Here we present a formalization of what appears to be the argument that most developing countries have been using when discussing the formulation of national policies on informatics and while developing such policies. It is presented in the form of a set of ideas and questions to be considered while carrying out the effort leading to a national plan for informatics. We do not pretend to provide answers to questions that almost every developing country and international organization is formulating. We do intend, however, to point out what we believe are the questions that should be formulated: the considerations of a country formulating national policies on informatics.

## 2. Developing national policies on informatics

2.1. A framework for a national plan for informatics

A national plan for informatics, as any plan formulated for the development of a specific social and economic sector of a country, should:

(a) serve the national objectives for the social and economic sectors of the country.

(b) attempt to predict the future social, political, economic, scientific, and technological development of the country.

(c) anticipate the nature and future behavior of variables involved in such development.

(d) unite participants in generating possible courses of action and selecting and carrying out the national policy.

(e) be flexible, in order to remain compatible with new scientific and technological developments.

If a country wants to play an active role in the “information society”, its national policies on informatics must provide a framework that will assure a long-run stability of the social, political, economic, scientific, and technological agents. Thus, the formulation of this type of national policy requires that the country adopt an attitude in which geographic frontiers shall not be an obstacle for interaction between individuals, economies, and cultures. It means wanting to have, within the desired timeframe, a role for the country and its inhabitants and being able to define and build that desired future.

These are the reasons, we believe, why it is so difficult to formulate a national plan for informatics, inasmuch as the future cannot be accurately predicted, nor will it depend on neither the hardware and software products that can be produced nor the economic franchises that could be established. All these elements may create the conditions and provide the stimulus, but the process of predicting and affecting the future must be carried out by the inhabitants of the country.

Therefore, the difficulty lies in finding out how to motivate people to become involved and participate in the process, as well as implementing the mechanisms permitting them to develop their creativity and efforts.

Even though a country does not have to utilize the national policies on informatics developed by another, we believe that once a country has formulated a policy, its most important objective has already been met; i.e., formulating a process of thinking about the informatics challenge at the national level.

For a country, the experience of formulating a national policy on informatics and the knowledge gained by the political authorities in surveying the national scientific and technological development are the major achievements.

## 2.2. Defining the objectives of a national plan

Countries differ in the size of their internal markets, the treatment given to capital and foreign technology transfer, access to the national market by foreign suppliers, the role of the State in both national and foreign investment in the field, the scientific and technological level of the universities, and the capacity and skills of the businessmen, professionals, academics, and workers. However, they tend to be alike when it comes to defining the goals they expect to attain with a national plan for informatics, because they all try to become industrialized and autonomous countries. They believe this is the way to assure social and economic development for their inhabitants in the information society.

In order to accomplish these objectives, several countries have already developed their own national policies on informatics. No matter how different their basis, almost every country proposes:

(a) To produce or assemble hardware products requiring relatively small capital or technological investment.

(b) To negotiate investments and technological transfers with foreign companies under favorable conditions.

(c) To benefit from the comparative advantages said to be obtained vis-à-vis other countries, through improved higher education.

(d) To develop a national software industry.

(e) To promote the formation of economic joint ventures with other countries of the region, especially in order to widen the market for their products.

## 2.3. Instances of national policies on informatics

It is difficult to assemble and understand almost any country's policies on informatics. The legislation and the administrative procedures governing it, as well as the changes, are often scattered and not easily available. Here is a somewhat terse and incomplete description of several Latin American countries' policies.

(a) The Program for Encouraging the Manufacturing of Computer Equipment in Mexico, and the Strategy for Stimulating the Informatics Development in Mexico attempts to $[1,9,12]$ :

(i) develop a national electronic industry that substitutes imports and increases exports: high priority was given to the manufacturing electronics components and parts, computers, telecommunications facilities, and entertainment oriented electronics.

(ii) favor that part of the electronic industry with more strategic content.

(iii) encourage the horizontal integration of the industry, generating an infrastructure of local suppliers.

(iv) stimulate the integration of large national firms with medium and small national firms and with foreign firms: agreements with foreign firms to ensure, as a condition, transfer of technology.

(v) support the formation and training of human resources in this field.

(vi) support the establishment of quality control standards for the national industry.

(vii) reserve the microcomputer market for Mexican owned firms: the rest of the informatics market was to be open to foreign companies, provided that they agree to integrate their products with locally manufactured components.

(b) The National Informatics Policy of Brazil pursues: (1) the technological development of the electronics industry, (2) the development of an assembly capacity for computer manufacturing, and (3) the development of a technological know-how [1,8,11]. Three stages can be seen in the development of Brazil's policy:

(i) 1971–1975: The State coordinates and participates in the computer market by:

(1) searching for foreign technological sources,

(2) establishing CAPRE (the Commission for the Coordination of Electronic Processing Activities), a State agency having no executive power but responsible for promoting the use of the informatics technology in the public sector, and

(3) establishing COBRA, a minicomputer manufacturing firm, working in conjunction with foreign suppliers under technological transfer agreements.

(ii) 1975–1979: The internal market is reserved for the national computing manufacturers by:

(1) establishing imports quotas,

(2) opening the country only to those technologies enabling the development of new substitution technologies,

(3) developing “know-how” in negotiating with foreign firms,

(4) defining approval criteria for projects in which national manufacturing firms are to work under agreement with foreign firms, e.g., how much technology is imported; up to what extent the project employs locally manufactured components; which part of the internal market is reserved for the new firm; what is the degree of participation of local industry; how much foreign exchange is needed, and what is the export value of the project?

(5) developing an industrial sector in the informatics field, and

(b) assigning to CAPRE the responsibility for further developing the guidelines for a national informatics policy.

(iii) Since 1979: the Special Secretary for Informatics, SEI, has been entrusted with the necessary administrative and financial autonomy for establishing and implementing the national policy on informatics; this involves:

(1) orienting, planning, and controlling such an implementation,

(2) stimulating the scientific and technological development of the informatics sector,

(3) supervising the informatics activities being carried out by any State agency,

(4) approving university educational and research programs in this field,

(5) establishing the areas for technological research,

(6) approving national manufacturing projects,

(7) approving the Informatics Plans proposed by any State agency.

(c) in Chile, from its foundation in 1968 to 1975, the State owned National Computing Center (ECOM) was responsible for the introduction of informatics: encouraging the use of informatics technology in the public sector, training specialists in the field, providing the necessary computer processing capacity for the public sector, and approving the projects requiring to import computing equipments. The National Informatics Policy was established in 1975, and now the State participates in the informatics market as any other user. Public and private institutions are free to make their own decisions about how they employ informatics technology: informatics will reach that development level required by the different economic factors. Through this framework the State encourages and facilitates the use of informatics in the public and private sectors:

(i) the State imposes neither administrative nor standards oriented guidelines affecting the use of informatics technology;

(ii) State agencies are free to develop the information systems they believe are necessaries for supporting their activities;

(iii) there are neither imports quotas, nor discriminatory import duties for informatics products;

(iv) a tax reduction scheme favors the training of human resources; and

(v) in several Agencies, the State leaves all informatics related activities to the private sector.

## 2.4. A brief comparison of informatics policies

Several similarities and differences may be observed in these three instances of national policies on informatics.

They are similar when they consider informatics as an strategic means for:

(a) increasing industrial and service productivity, (b) making efficient use of the available national resources,

(c) implementing communication related technologies, and

(d) reducing the deficit in their balance of payments.

They differ, however, in the way they insert themselves into the international marketplace of informatics technologies, and in the role they assign to the State in developing the informatics sector:

(a) Some countries have established central guidelines for the development of the sector. The State establishes the environment that allows national technological autonomy and plays an activate role in developing a national informatics industry. The State negotiates directly with foreign firms the terms of agreements involving technology transfer.

(b) Other countries have entirely left the international market in order to control and regulate the development of the informatics sector. The public and private agents move within a framework in which their decisions about informatics are based upon their own specific needs within the nation.

The approaches being considered for and the results being obtained from implementing these specific instances of informatics policies are not only different from one country to another. Even inside any of these countries, there is no consensus about the approach being followed, the objectives being pursued, and the manner in which the results have to be understood and evaluated. Only in the long run can the social, economics, educational, and political changes induced for these policies be assessed and the effectiveness of the policies known.

## 2.5. Expected outcomes from a national plan for informatics

These differences and similarities give rise to the questions:

What are the results that a country wishes to obtain when formulating and implementing a national plan for informatics?

Is it the creation of better industrial facilities, with information technology making the country's products and services more competitive, especially those destined for foreign markets?

Is it to sensitize the population on the social consequences of the development of informatics? This may appear to affect the country's relative position with respect to:

(a) The comparative advantage enjoyed by its natural resources.

(b) The technical innovations, quality, and price of foreign products that compete with its products in international markets.

(c) The foreign products and services offered inside the country.

(d) The generation and maintenance of high employment levels.

(e) The quality of its university and technical students.

(f) Access to public information.

(g) People's privacy.

Finally, is it to create the necessary conditions and stimulus for business to increase their use of science and technology, to reduce problems of education, health, justice, housing, food, etc., making the country's economy more effective, thereby allowing a fairer and more efficient assignment of resources? It is not easy to choose a mixture of objectives when formulating a national policy on informatics.

## 2.6. Facing competition from other countries

Several other questions arise when a country wants to develop a national plan for informatics and thus has to face the competition resulting from the implementation of other countries' policies:

(a) What is the best legislation favoring entry into a highly competitive international market with aggressive commercial practice?

(b) Whose experience should be imitated: e.g., Japan, the U.S.A., Brazil, Korea, Mexico?

(c) What impediments exist in repeating their formula?

(d) How much political and legislative good-will and what management, professional, labor and academic skills, and internal savings efforts are needed to make this successful?

(e) What are the real factors that are open to negotiation with large national and international companies, and who are the prime generators of current scientific and technological know-how in this field?

(f) How sensitive are the government, university authorities, corporate officers, and different interests groups (e.g., workers, scientists, and professionals) to the fact that lack of interest or investment in informatics in the strategic areas of the economy may have very grave long term social consequences? To accomplish the objectives, adequate and often difficult allocation of resources must be made.

## 2.7. Types of informatics industries

Other questions relate to the type of industry that the country wishes to develop,

If a hardware industry is to be encouraged, capital and scientific and technological knowledge are essential. Indeed, for a hardware industry to remain competitive, high quality, low prices, aggressive marketing strategies, good rapport with clients, and continuous technological innovation are the basic needs. Therefore, the hardware industry is continuously looking for more effective and efficient mass production and distribution systems, and at new developments in the design, production, and materials for hardware components. This implies the capacity to make frequent investment to remain competitive.

It is important to observe the size of international companies that manufacture computers, and note that they are gradually taking over the manufacturing of basic components (chips) and are now participating in the development of large informatics services, such as telecommunication systems. What is left for other countries? Within a global economy they may end up fighting to be selected as an assembly site because of low labor costs. How long will it take to the robot-based hardware manufacturer to return these assembly plants to the original sites?

The development of a software industry requires less capital than that of a hardware industry, but it requires more than intelligence, creativity and skillful managerial capacities. Experience reveals that it also requires keeping in touch with leaders in the international development of hardware and hard-software, and following and understanding progress in the fields of conceptualization, design, construction, and commercialization of software. In this sense, new questions arise when a country wants its national policy to be software oriented. How many of these factors does the country possess? Moreover, if it lacks some, how long will it take to acquire them?

## 3. Institutional framework

The implementation of a national policy implies the existence of an institutional framework with a political interest in the informatics sector, and also in the scientific and technological development of the country.

Some countries that have developed this institutional framework have done so through a State Agency for Science and Technology Development and Centers of Academic Excellence.

3.1. A state agency for science and technology development

An Agency for Science and Technology Development generally consists of a small, highly qualified, and autonomous group of people. It has the authority to demand, from other national institutions, the improvements necessary for the development of new scientific and technological knowledge and for its effective application in all social and economic activities.

It is responsible for searching for and negotiating with national and foreign institutions to obtain and channel resources and facilities for scientific research and technological innovation, working closely with the universities and other research and development (R&D) groups. Its purpose is not to promote the formation of bureaucracies administering and controlling R&D activities that they do not understand.

The institution promotes higher levels of scientific and technological research, both at the universities and in strategic organizations, encouraging collaboration between them. Communication and collaboration rather than competition between academic and R&D institutions is encouraged.

This Agency must prepare the country for Tomorrow's industry, when the creation and utilization of knowledge will be more important than the manufacturing of microcomputers. Thus, the State openly creates the conditions for scientific and technological development and encourage its utilization in the economic and social sector. The existence of this Agency acknowledges the fact that economic and social challenges cannot be separated from scientific and technological ones. And this implies that they have to find political answers to questions such as: What informatics for which development?, or What science and technology for which development?

## 3.2. Centers of academic excellence

Centers of Academic Excellence are established inside universities or academies for those disciplines of high priority within the national policies on informatics. They are supplied with the necessary resources to retain highly qualified specialists with modern equipment. They have close contact with the international scientific and technological community and with institutions entrusted with the search for solutions to national problems. They also look for alternative uses of the country's natural resources.

These centers must provide an environment in which academics and researchers flourish, can collaborate as consultants, work with national engineering companies, participate in large projects in the country, and help in the development of other national universities and higher educational institutions. Research performed in these centers is a complement to and the highest level of activity of all academic endeavors. Research in informatics is necessary to allow public institutions to analyze technological options, to define and delimit power as a tool for the industrial and technological development of the country.

## 4. Conclusions

Current literature in the informatics field suggests that government authorities, corporate officers, international organizations, academics, and professionals are increasingly concerned with the problems and questions that arise when countries face the challenge of developing computing systems. Many reports ask what advantages a country obtains from formulating a national policy on informatics $[1,3,4,7,10]$ . Others suggest specific courses of action in order to implement such a policy $[1,2,8,9,11]$ . Still others provide a framework for scientific and technological development $[12]$ .

No matter how different, one of the most frequent recommendations is that the process of formulating and implementing a national policy on informatics should be attacked by groups formed by government officials in conjunction with representatives of business, professionals, workers, and universities. In this manner, the risk could be reduced, through coordinated efforts and rapid actions of the major economic agents dealing with all economic factors.

Experience shows that developing countries can apply informatics technology as long as the proper questions are asked when facing the challenges of an “information society”.

The answers to the many questions posed here depend upon each country's economic and social expectations and driving forces: if a national policy on informatics is to succeed, the answers should make an honest appraisal of both the country's comparative advantages and disadvantages (vis-à-vis similar countries) and the areas generating the most value-added within the country's strategic industries (e.g., application software development, information systems applications, training, communication technology applications).
