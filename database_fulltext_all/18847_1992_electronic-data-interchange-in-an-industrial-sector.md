Research

# Electronic data interchange in an industrial sector

# The case of The Netherlands' building industry \*

Wil A.H. Thissen and Wim J. Stam

Delft University of Technology, 2600 GA Delft, Netherlands

Strategies for development and implementation of standards for electronic data interchange in an entire industry are investigated, using the Netherlands' building industry as a vehicle. The problem is tackled by analysis of the present situation and structuring and analysis of strategies for innovation, standardization options, and strategies for achieving standardization. It is concluded that a balanced combination of approaches is needed, including development of process and product description standards at the semantic and classification level, coordination of research and development, education, and stimulation of leading parties as an instrument of change. Rather than a completed set of standards, the major results of a three-year research program include an increased awareness of the relevance of EDI standardization in the industry, and partial process and data models that may provide the basis for standardization in the future.

Keywords: Electronic data interchange, Industrial sector, Innovation strategies, Strategic information planning, Information infrastructure, Standardization, Interorganisational information systems, Building industry.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background

In the mid-eighties, information technology started to penetrate into the Netherlands' building industry. Various parties in the process, such as town and country planners, architects, building engineers, and contractors increasingly used electronic data storage and manipulation for supporting their work. But it was generally felt that the pace at which the new technology was being introduced in the building trade lagged behind other sectors of industrial activity. As a result, opportunities for increasing the competitiveness of the industry might be lost.

He is one of the founders of the new School of Systems Engineering and Policy Analysis at Delft University.

Wim J. Stam is an economist, specialized in regional studies. Fields of interest are transport (including electronic data transport), innovation processes and strategy development. After his study he spent several years at the Ministry of Transport and Public Works, working as a staff adviser in policy analysis studies. At the University he has been involved in several studies, notably in the field of innovation processes in transport and urban development.

Therefore, in 1985 the Netherlands' Government decided to set up a research program to stimulate a major breakthrough in the application of information technology in the building sector. A steering committee, composed of representatives of government, universities, research institutions, and industry, as well as independent advisers, developed a three-year research program, aimed at developing standards that would facilitate electronic data interchange (EDI) among the parties in the building process.

## 1.2 Scope of paper

This paper primarily reports on a research project that was part of the program. Its objective was to specify an operational approach to development and implementation of a set of standards for EDI in the Netherlands' building industry. The focus here is on the general approach taken to the problem, including its analysis and structuring, identification and analysis of strategies for solution, classification and selection of standards, and discussion of strategies to achieve standardization.

Although the study relates specifically to the Netherlands' building industry, many of the problems are representative of a broader class of problems: the need to develop coordination among independent agencies and specialists in order to gain maximum benefits of emerging information and communication technology in other sectors. Examples include commerce, health care, transportation and logistics, agriculture, and manufacturing. Strategies and techniques need to be developed to solve the problems related to EDI among independent organisations.

## 2. Outline of research program

## 2.1 Problem outline

The state of utilization of information technology in the Netherlands' building industry around 1985 is best characterised as “pioneering” and “island-automation”. Depending on the specific branch of the trade and the size of the company, information technology was not used at all (small contractors), used for administrative support only (e.g., for order and account administration), or for specific engineering functions in the building process, such as computer-aided drawing or design by architects, and calculations for design of buildings. Applications were often implemented by enthusiastic pioneers. Integration of various applications, so that results from one application could be transferred electronically and used directly as inputs to another, had (and still has) only been achieved for a limited set of applications, typically administrative and project planning tasks.

## 2.2 Objectives of research program

The relatively backward position of the building industry with respect to innovation and information technology gave rise to concern about the future of the sector: the development of a European market will result in both increased competition in the home market and new opportunities abroad; similar developments may be expected at the level of the global market. Only if the Netherlands' industry can operate at the forefront of innovation and efficiency will it be able to meet the challenges at home and exploit the opportunities abroad. Therefore, the steering committee of IOP, the Netherlands' innovative research program, developed an agenda for research and action. The program's main objectives were:

\- to enhance the use of information technology and its innovative use in the sector;

\- to develop and implement an “Infrastructure for Information Technology” (IIT) for the building trade. This infrastructure was defined as “A set of agreed-upon standards that will facilitate communication among parties in the building process using electronic information systems, such that computerization of this multi-party process will be stimulated. The standards will have to deal with both technical and procedural aspects” [3].

The basic idea of the program was to exploit the lessons learned in the introduction of information technology in other sectors; generally the need for standards was realized only after extensive proliferation and development of automation. By bypassing that stage and guiding new developments directly to greater harmony, the relatively backward position of the building industry was to be turned into an advantage. Then, multiple developments of the same or similar standards (followed by tedious and time-consuming reconciliation negotiations), and problems and inefficiencies at the various information interfaces among the stakeholder parties might be prevented.

A side-objective was to improve coordination and cooperation in the research community of the building sector. Research efforts had been fragmented and competition among (small) research groups had predominated.

## 3. Exploration of problem situation

The objectives of the project discussed here were to:

\- identify the nature and type of existing problems with respect to information interchange in the building sector;

\- identify the standards needed to provide solutions to these problems; and

– develop a specific plan for development and implementation of those standards.

The project specifically addressed the house-building sub-sector.

## 3.1 Method of approach

The problem situation is too complicated to be tackled using only straightforward information analysis and system design techniques. Factors such as the (perceived) interests and mutual competition of the various parties involved in the building process play an important part. In addition, standardization requires participation of building process parties and industries developing information technology tools (both hard- and software). Moreover, regulatory authorities and important clients of the industry (such as the government) may play an important part.

Rather than rushing towards partial (technical) solutions, a multiple research strategy was defined, aimed at:

• identifying problems rather than solutions;

\- structuring the problem situation into more specific components, while maintaining a view of the relations among them;

\- analyzing the underlying reasons for existing problems.

The initial research effort was divided into a number of exploratory activities. The major topics were to study:

– previous efforts to establish standards in the building industry;

\- actual use of information technology in the sector, including statement of user needs and plans for new development;

\- development of information technology use for construction in other countries, notably Japan and the U.S.;

– experience with the resolution of similar or related problems in other industries, and in information resource management for complex organizations;

\- analysis and decomposition of the building process in order to provide a basis for definition of the major interfaces between parties in the process, of the information being transferred, and of the standards needed to facilitate communication.

## 3.2 Findings

Results are now summarized, in as far as they are important to the rest of the discussion.

Structure of the building industry: innovation and standardization

The building industry has a particular structure, characterized by:

\- Involvement of a large number of independent parties in the development and production of output units (houses, office buildings, etc.). Typically, these include the commissioner of the project, various government institutions setting and controlling regulations, utility companies, the municipal public works agency, the functional designer or architect, a specialist in installation design, a contractor and various sub-contractors (parts suppliers, plumbers, painters, etc.) and, in later phases, the users of the building, the person or institution responsible for maintenance, and maintenance firms.

\- Significant variation in the product, not only with respect to construction properties, but also with respect to size and location.

\- Significant variation in the production process.

\- Frequent alteration in the parties with which each individual or firm cooperates; typically, the coalition is unique for every building project. As a result, there is little interest in feedback and standardization.

\- Dominance of small-scale firms. In the Netherlands, about $75\%$ of the firms operate with ten employee's or less.

The small scale, the tendency to avoid risks and look for short-term profit, and a preference for traditional work methods explains why readiness to invest in innovative technologies is limited.

Study of attempts at standardization (of components and procedures) in the building sector lead to the following conclusions [13]:

\- It takes from 5 to 10 years to develop and implement standards.

\- Communication among parties is not primarily hampered by technical problems, but by a lack of broadly accepted definitions of building components and tasks, and because different points of view with associated codes and classifications are used in the different phases of the process (functionality, construction properties, costs, types and sequence of work, etc.).

\- Because of complexity and diversity in the trade, only broad frameworks that may be applied and tailored for each project may have a chance of acceptance.

## Use of information technology

Information technology is not used substantially in the building sector. Three different reasons were given for this: lack of knowledge of and familiarity with the technology, the small size of many firms, making the investment a significant burden, and the lack of attractive applications.

Plans to make significant investment in automation were scarce, except in the fields of finance and administration and installation advice (where the use of computers to support design calculations is rather common).

Available software primarily supported cost calculations, administrative tasks (such as order administration and account monitoring), writing of builder's specifications, and construction planning. In addition, engineers worked with design programs and construction analysis tools for advanced projects and calculations. Available software lacked user-friendliness, providing another barrier to widespread use.

Integration of software was generally limited to programs supplied by the same software developer.

Building and information technology outside the Netherlands

A search was made into useful developments and experience abroad. Particular attention was given to the U.S.A. and Japan.

In the U.S.A., construction is a very important but fragmented sector of the economy. There is little investment in R&D by the industrial sector. Developments have largely been initiated at universities. Availability and use of software for engineering and planning purposes is widespread. Use of computers is more common than in the Netherlands but integrated applications are lacking.

In Japan, a small number of large firms covers the whole area of design and construction. Computer use by these firms started in the early seventies and is now widespread. They have developed in-house software that has been more or less integrated within the firm. As a result, there is little interest in standardization at a national level. Because of the concentration, continued significant investment in integrated technology is expected.

The differences in the situation and prospects between the U.S. and Japan confirm the significance of the sector's economic and organizational structure as a factor determining the use of new technologies.

## Methodology and experience in other sectors

An investigation was made into experience with data structuring and information management in situations similar to the building trade.

The main findings were:

\- Standardization and structuring of automated information exchange is largely limited to problems of information management and automation within organizations. The methodologies developed typically consist of general frameworks or principles and procedures (such as Strategic Data Planning [6] and ISAC [4]), and specific conventions for process and data modeling.

Although a variety of different names is used, the procedures recommended are largely similar in their emphasis on a stepwise approach in which the problem is defined and then possible solutions are explored, etc. The need for top management support and user involvement and education is also stressed.

Process and data modeling techniques (such as ISAC, JSD, SADT, IDEF, NIAM) are generally useful in the phases of system design and development.

Increasingly, research and experience suggests, however, that institutional and management aspects have been underestimated as critical factors to success in developing complex information systems [11].

\- Electronic Data Interchange among organizations is receiving increasing attention in the business community. The emphasis is on electronic communication of business transactions in a standard format. Examples of systems in operation include airline reservation, banking, and order- and chargeback transactions in the retail sector (e.g., the U.S. drug industry). As reported by several authors [1,2,16], critical success factors for development and implementation of such systems include:

\- awareness of the strategic, long-range benefits;

• high-level management support;

\- support of industry leaders and/or the government;

\- strong participation and membership in industry-wide organizations (needed for standardization).

The acceptance and implementation of an international syntax for EDI For Administration, Commerce and Transport (EDIFACT) was boosted by the 1988 decision of the U.S. Customs Service to support it [12]. The threat of external competition has speeded the development of inter-organisational systems for transportation data exchange in the port of Rotterdam [8].

\- In manufacturing industries, notably the automobile and aerospace industry, standardization for exchange of engineering data is developing for product and component descriptions and for common data bases supporting the development, design, specification, and production.

Standards development is typically started and sustained by a limited number of large manufacturers that have a leading market position (such as GM and Boeing), and carried forward by active trade organizations such as the Automotive

Industry Action Group, and international organizations, such as ISO.

\- Standardization of electronic data exchange has, initially, concentrated on technical protocols rather than on the content. Attention has now shifted to higher-level layers dealing with software compatibility. Further development of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model seems to be of critical importance, here.

\- One approach to integration of information is to start building databases that are of relevance to all parties. Examples include referral data bases in the fields of water management, environmental data, etc. Such data bases provide a starting point for cooperation and learning by participating. Participants gradually get acquainted with the use of shared electronic resources and develop an awareness of the potential for further cooperation and standardization. The implementation of pilot projects in which electronic data interchange is advanced may have similar stimulating effects [15].

Our conclusion was that, while none of the approaches provides the one and only answer to the problem of EDI in the building industry, each of them offers worthwhile lessons.

## Modeling of the building process

An effort was made to describe and decompose the building process from the perspective of information generation and transfer. Major objectives of this effort were to:

\- obtain better insight into the nature of interfaces between parties: which parties communicate, about what, and for what purpose?

\- explore the usability of available methodologies for this problem;

\- develop a systematic approach to isolating the prime information problems in the process.

The process modeling and decomposition was formulated in terms of the “Systems Analysis and Design Technique SADT” [9,10]. The overall steering committee had decided that all process and data modeling efforts were to follow these conventions. In this way communication between the various groups was to be facilitated. Furthermore, the committee expected a consistent top-down description of the process to guarantee overall model integrity and to yield - at a more detailed level of description - process descriptions that might be translated into program code.

The general level of decomposition, however, was not suited to define interfaces and possible standards for the interfaces. A further decomposition at a more detailed level was necessary to come to a specification that might be recognized in the day-to-day practice of the industry. In an attempt to provide further decomposition, we encountered various problems:

\- Many of the activities are neither well defined, nor standardized. For example, functional specification of performance levels by a commissioner may be very detailed, or rather global. Therefore, it is difficult – if not impossible – to describe the current situation in detail. It also makes modeling of the future system a risky adventure: the researcher might be creative enough to design one, but acceptance of the description in the field will probably be low.

\- There is little or no consistency in the contents and definition of data and information transferred between the process parties.

\- Interfaces were sought at the points of communication among individual parties in the process. However, there is nothing like even a near-standard relation between parties and process functions. Some parties or individuals operate in a very narrow and specialized field while others, notably large building firms, extend their activities over many parts of the process.

\- There are not only problems in the transfer and interpretation of information, but also in its classification; for different purposes, different classifications are needed. The user is interested in spatial composition, light and climate of a building. These can all be derived from a description of the building's components, their relations and properties. The designer/construction engineer is interested in the mechanics of the construction, the installation adviser in physical properties and classifications, the cost calculator in a decomposition into parts with identifiable costs, and the work planner into the sequence of construction activities needed and the division and planning of work according to specialties like painting, plumbing, carpentering, masonry, etc.

\- The degree to which these problems occur depends on the specific process parts. Vagueness and lack of standardization are typical for town and country planning and the functional definitional parts of the process. But standardization of concepts and terms is beginning for the transfer and definition of technical specifications from the designer to the construction company.

## 4. Analysis

## 4.1 Introduction

Our investigation yielded a wide variety of problems, viewpoints, and ideas or strategies for solution. Our emphasis therefore became the structuring of problems and solutions that foster competitiveness and innovation by standardization.

We distinguish three levels: First, a number of problems and suggestions refer to more general approaches to innovation in the building industry. These we term the level of ‘Strategies for innovation’. Analysis at this level emphasizes the question: “Why standardization? and: What may be alternatives?” Second, at the level of standardization, we focus on the question: “What should be standardized?” This, we call the ‘Standards’ level. Third, we consider the various strategies specifically directed at promotion, development, implementation, and maintenance or adaptation of standards. The central question here is: “How do we achieve standardization?” This, we term ‘Strategies for standardization’.

## 4.2 Strategies for innovation

Only some of the problems and solutions identified in the exploration phase directly relate to standards development for EDI. Some approaches offer alternatives, or are intended to promote innovation in a more general sense. To clarify discussions and place standardization in the right perspective, the various objectives and strategies were structured in a hierarchy, using the relation “supports”. The results for the top-levels are illustrated by the structural model or objectives tree shown in Figure 3.

The overall objective is to increase competitiveness of the Netherlands' building industry. End-users (i.e. buyers and users of buildings) and the sector as a whole will benefit from this.

Two main approaches are distinguished to achieve this are to:

(1) improve building process technology and organization, for example by developing and using of new materials and production technology, by adoption of more stable and less costly organisation models, or by increasing industrialization.

(2) develop other innovations for example totally new products that will open up new markets.

Achieving the first objective may be supported by:

(3) improving coherence in Research and Development.

Achieving of both (1) and (2) is supported by:

(4) increasing the use of information technology in order to draw maximum benefits from it.

Given these objectives, we now focus on information technology. Figure 4 illustrates a further elaboration of the hierarchy in this respect.

Three main strategies were identified to improve the use of information technology:

(4.1) To facilitate electronic data interchange in the sector.

This, in turn, might be achieved along three different, but not mutually exclusive, paths: developing and implementing EDI standards for the building trade (4.1.1), developing translator software to ease communication among various parties (4.1.2), and the provision of hardware facilities for electronic communication (such as dedicated networks; 4.1.3).

Dedicated translator software might improve communication among the different parties, where each prefers its own classifications and terminology. The idea is to provide automated mappings between these “dialects”, rather than attempt to define a single universal language for the industry. This requires agreement and a certain level of standardization within the discipline groups.

The provision of a data communication network specifically tailored to the needs of the building trade will stimulate electronic data exchange and use.

(4.2) To educate the field.

There is a widespread lack of knowledge about the potential of information technology in building circles. Education will help people become aware of its possibilities, and stimulate discussion and ideas about further needs (which might include standardization).

(4.3) To increase the quality of available applications.

Better quality will lead to increased, more efficient use. Two supporting activities are: First, the development of new and innovative, possibly integrated, applications, traditionally the work of specialists at universities and research institutions (4.3.1), e.g., advanced computer aided design systems. Second, the improvement of the quality of existing applications (4.3.2): user friendliness and integration of applications are major objectives.

Conclusions

This analysis explains much of the confusion felt by the participating parties during the first phase of the research program. In reaction to the first request for proposals for developing standards for EDI (the IIT), a variety of projects was offered, including many that had to do with developing new tools and applications or network facilities instead of standards. Similarly, early discussions were dominated by proposals for other strategies than standardization. While these reactions and proposals indicate sound thinking (develop ideas to solve the underlying problem), there was confusion due to a lack of a clear distinction between the various strategies.

Our analysis shows that the standardization strategy was a lower-level result of the central issue of improving industry competitiveness. But because the choice among strategies had not been thoroughly debated, participants came up with proposals for other strategies, and did not feel committed to standardization as a solution. In effect, several strategies might support and enforce each other.

## 4.3 Standards

Given the complexity and diversity of the building process, any effort at overall standardization was doomed to fail. Therefore, it was necessary to identify the types of standards that might be of interest to the national program, and subsequently select those that seemed to be most appropriate.

Identification and selection of promising fields involved classifications of standards by:

\- objects or entities about which information is stored and exchanged;

\- the aspect of electronic data interchange which is standardized;

\- the parties involved.

Classification with respect to objects or entities

Data in the building industry might be classified into:

\- object characteristics;

\- building process aspects or elements.

Distinctions can be made about the physical scale of objects whose data are stored and exchanged. Relevant classes include data about regional objects or systems, data at the (building) project level, data at the level of the individual unit or object, data at the level of complex components or object parts (doors, walls, bathrooms), and data at the level of individual components such as beams, door handles, bath tubs, and nails.

Using the building process as a starting point, data may, among other things, relate to the parties involved (e.g., addresses, phone numbers), the agreements or commitments made, the division of tasks in the building process, and the (planned) time schedule.

Classification of standards by aspects of (electronic) communication

Electronic data interchange involves communication at different levels: hardware (the technical interface), logical (the software level), classification and logical interrelation of data, and semantic level, where the meanings or definitions of verbs and terms play the central part.

Clearly, efficient electronic communication requires standards at all levels.

## Classification by the parties involved

On a geographic scale, we may distinguish between international standards, such as the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model, standards at the national level, standards at the regional level (for example, regarding zoning regulations and utility companies) and standards with relevance at the project level only.

With respect to the number and types of disciplines or branches involved, standards may involve other sectors beside building (e.g. worldwide OSI standards), the building industry as a whole, specific disciplines or segments within the building industry, participants in a specific project only, or a specific firm only.

## Analysis and selection of promising fields for standardization

Standardization by data of global relevance, as well as standardization pertaining to technical and logical interfaces of the communication apparatus, should, in our view, not be part of the program. The Netherlands' building industry should adopt any standards developed at a higher than national level or for a broader arena than the building industry, and not attempt to define them (again). Efforts in the program might be most effective if directed at standards at the level of the national building industry, or of specific disciplinary segments thereof. This excludes project-specific standards, as well as firm-specific standards.

As previously suggested, most information exchange takes place among parties at the project level. Therefore, major benefits might ensue from standardization of object- and process-related data-exchange at this level. The standardization would primarily be concerned with semantic and classification issues.

This still leaves a wide and confusing variety of potential fields of concentration. Therefore, further selection was made by focussing on fields where:

\- maximum benefits from standardization and expanded use of information technology may be expected;

\- visible results may be achieved in the short term, and where a need is felt by the industry itself;

\- a certain degree of formalization and structuring of activities has already occurred.

As most of the crucial decisions determining functionality, cost, and quality of product are made in the initial phases of planning and design, improvement of those decisions by use of information technology would seem to offer most benefits. However, commonly accepted definitions of tasks and (interim) results in the process of planning and design hardly exist. As a result, there is ample room for innovative ideas and applications, but a basis for standardization is lacking.

Representatives from industry indicated that planning for construction, and materials management at the construction site and in the supplying industry had significant promise for application of information technology. Also, further development of classifications and descriptions for building specifications are needed.

From a point of view of computer use and standardization of procedures, the area of installation design appeared to be a good candidate for further formalization and standardization. Traditionally, installation designers have been using computers for analysis and control of air and heat circulation and other issues related to climatic conditions in the building.

## 4.4 Strategies for standardization

In addition to the fields or objects – selected for development of standards, a plan of action must include a strategy for promotion, development, implementation and maintenance of standards. Again, a wide variety of approaches was suggested during exploratory investigation. We briefly review the main alternatives.

## The 'do nothing' approach

This is based on the assumption that standardization will automatically occur in the long run, as the industry will eventually itself feel the need for it to combat incompatible applications, data structures, classifications, and definitions. Proponents of this strategy stated that attempts at speeding up the process and by-passing ‘natural’ development stages will be doomed to fail for lack of support from the users and user-based direction.

Speed up the penetration of information technology in the industry

This is based on similar considerations. By stimulation of the use of information technology, the industry will sooner realise it needs standardization, which will then develop without strong, centralized efforts.

## Stimulate leading disciplines

This assumes that leading disciplines might serve as both an example and a threat. By stimulation of cooperation and standardization in leading groups, other groups might be incited (for fear of being bypassed) to take similar steps. After the various disciplines have set things straight within their own territory, negotiations and consultations about standardization at the interfaces between disciplines may start. Opponents of this approach fear that stimulation of those who are already ahead will enlarge their advantage over the rest and alienate rather than stimulate others. Another problem might be the coordination of efforts: once a discipline has established its own standards, reaching agreement with other interfaces may be virtually impossible.

## Stimulate leading firms or consortia

If leading firms or consortia develop standards for themselves and take the lead in standardization for the sector as a whole, others will feel obliged to join. While much of the effort in this approach is provided by leading firms, potential benefits are theirs, too: they will enjoy the competitive benefits of a technological lead and have some control over the content and direction of standardization. Others will feel a strong urge to join for the sake of their long-term survival.

## Use the government's position of power

Through its responsibilities for public works and buildings, the government is by far the largest 'single' client of the building industry. In addition, the government controls regulation and might use that position by prescribing the use of certain standards for all construction-related activities or for all works commissioned by public authorities. The government, working for its own benefit (as a client of more efficient services), will therefore benefit the sector as a whole.

## Create an external threat

One of the ways to motivate people or organizations to cooperate is to identify an external threat. Increased foreign competition as a result of EC developments and the globalization of markets might be stressed to provide incentives for cooperation and joint action.

Invest in theoretical development of a basis for standards

One approach is to invest in fundamental research and development of descriptions of the building process and built objects that provide the basis for an ‘ideal’ set of standards. This would require a significant research investment.

## Stimulate cooperation and coordination of research and development

Fragmentation and incompatibility result from a tendency to develop local tools and standards. While this is, in part, the result of an open, commercial market, there are good arguments for improved coordination of government-sponsored, industry-wide research and development. The flows of money to research institutions may be used to stimulate cooperation rather than competition in the field.

## Set up a special organisation for information standardization in the building industry

As standardization involves making tradeoffs between the desires and interests of several parties, the existence of a ‘neutral’ party (mediator-facilitator) may be beneficial. In addition, once a first set of standards has been developed, modifications, improvements and expansions will be called for regularly. The responsibility for management of the updating and maintenance process may best rest with an independent organisation. But such an organisation can operate effectively only if it is supported by the major parties in the field, and if it has a sufficiently stable funding base to support its own, qualified staff.

## Analysis and discussion of strategies

Beside the do-nothing approach, the strategies can be classified into:

(1) The stimulation of user consciousness of the need for standardization. This includes all efforts aimed at speeding up the use of information technology, including more general strategies toward innovation.

(2) The introduction or use of power-related mechanisms as vehicles for speeding up the willingness for change and innovation, including standardization. This includes the stimulation of leading consortia and disciplines, the use of external threats, and the use of government power.

Practically speaking, the last two strategies are not very promising. Increase of active government control and regulation is at odds with the present emphasis on deregulation and privatisation.

(3) Coordinated theoretical development of standards, including the creation of a special organization to accomplish it.

A balanced approach, combining the advantages of different strategies, is needed. Theoretical study and standards without the development of a base of support in the field will not be of much practical use in a short to medium term. The use of power-related mechanisms might enhance the process as a whole.

## 4.5 Major recommendations

Based on the findings and analyses outlined above, our recommendations to the steering committee were, broadly speaking:

\- Re-evaluate the primary choice for standardization as strategy towards improved use of information technology in the building industry. Pay attention to education and transfer of knowledge about information technology to the industry and attempt to coordinate the ongoing development of new applications.

\- Adapt the level of ambition of the research program to reality. Instead of promising an IIT, aim at starting up a process of coordination and standards development. Set up pilot projects to develop and use definitions and standards in one or two promising areas, such as the classification and structuring of object or product descriptions.

\- Attempt to institutionalize consultations with the various parties involved in the building process, in order to start the creation of a joint platform for coordination and standardization.

\- Stimulate leading disciplines and coalitions to go ahead and develop standards, to provide an incentive for others to join the process.

\- Attempt to start development of an overall framework of standards and coordination, such that developments within disciplines will not conflict with the need for standards at the interfaces between disciplines. Focus on the most promising fields.

## 5. Continuation of the program

## 5.1 Second phase

The recommendations to the steering committee were made about 1 1/2 year after the start of the project, when it was reconsidering its plans and tactics for the second half of the program.

The steering committee drew up a revised program, and decided to concentrate on two key themes:

(1) the development of a process and data model, centered around the structure of product descriptions (the so-called Building Information Model BIM);

(2) explorative research into the possible organisation and tasks and preparations to establish a national Building Information Centre, BIC.

Other tasks, such as the promotion of education and knowledge transfer, were left to parties in the sector.

During 1989, the results were published. These will now be indicated briefly.

## 5.2 The Building Information Model BIM

The BIM report [7] is a systematic, stepwise breakdown of the building process using the SADT formalism. In addition to the process decomposition, data models describe the structure of object-related data at different levels of aggregation.

The description and decomposition is fairly detailed for the parts describing installation design, and far more global for many other elements of the building process.

## 5.3 The Building Information Centre BIC

The BIC report [5] provides a number of recommendations for a national Building Information Centre:

\- The primary task of BIC is to develop a uniform system for data-interchange among parties in the building industry by stimulating and coordinating current activities, developing rules for formalisation and adaptation of standards, and relate these to standards at the European Community level.

\- The starting point of activities is the continued development of the BIM.

\- BIC should organise educational activities to disseminate the principles of BIM to a professional audience.

\- BIC should be an independent organisation, rooted in the building industry itself, rather than a quasi-governmental organisation. Therefore, supervision and financial support of BIC should be the responsibility of the building industry's organisations.

## 5.4 Further developments

At a final meeting and presentation of the results to a broad audience in the fall of 1989, the key elements were:

\- The BIM is a major step forwards towards a broadly accepted and useful set of standards with respect to the building process but it is far from complete. An effort of about the same size will be necessary to make the standard model useful and self-supporting.

\- The government may be willing to provide further (but limited) subsidies, but only if the prime initiative and responsibility is taken by the building industry itself.

\- Exploratory talks are under way to form a platform of interested parties to continue the effort.

\- The threat of outside competition, from within the EC, but notably from Japanese companies, was stressed as one of the main reasons why interested parties should join forces.

In addition to formal final presentations, several meetings and seminars were and still are being held, focussing on extension of the BIM.

## 6. Concluding remarks

## 6.1 Methodology of analysis and research

The complexity of the problem of EDI in an industrial sector substantially exceeds the present state of the art of information system design and information management. Chances of success seem to be determined more by issues of power and conflicting interests than by soundness of technical analysis. Analytic research can be successful only if embedded in a process of discussion and negotiation among the major parties. Research should be primarily focussed on objectives, strategies, and identification of the points of leverage in the system. Process and data analysis methods may play a complementary role in the realization of a chosen approach, by providing a framework for formal description and analysis of processes and data.

## 6.2 Program results and set-up

The program's original objectives, i.e. the development of an IIT, were not achieved. The differences between the initial intentions and the situation after completion of the program are striking; illustrating the degree of complexity of EDI standardization in a rather unstructured industry such as building.

The strategy chosen (develop EDI standards) to achieve the more general objective (optimize use of information technology) was only one of many strategies.

The lack of a thorough, initial evaluation of alternative strategies generated significant confusion in the first part of the program. Instead of adopting a single strategy (“develop standards”), a balanced mix of strategies, including education, development of new applications, and increased user-friendliness of applications is needed.

Although the IIT was not realised, the partial process and data model for the building industry (BIM) provides a tangible start for improvement and a focus for discussion. Descriptions of (parts) of the building process as developed during the program increasingly appear in brochures of firms operating in the information technology market for the building industry, and in educational materials.

Another significant result of the program is a broad awareness of the importance of thinking and communicating about standardization issues. As a result, the proposals for the BIC, which emphasize the structure of the process of further development rather than the end-product, are being discussed seriously.
