# An experimental study of the efficacy of tables versus bar graphs with respect to type of task

Joan H. Coll

Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ 07079-2692, USA

A two factor experiment was performed with two levels of Display Presentation Type (table versus bar graph) and two levels of Task Type (retrieve relative information versus retrieve specific values). In the table condition specific value retrieval scored higher than relational information retrieval, while in the graph condition, the reverse was true. Despite this symmetry, the Table Group was significantly more satisfied with table use than the Graph Group was with graph use.

Keywords: Tables, Graphs, Task type, Multifactor experiment.

## Introduction

Currently ever more decision makers are enjoying easy access to hands-on computer usage. Because of this, it becomes important to ascertain what forms of data presentations are most effective under various conditions and purposes.

The question of supremacy of tables versus graphs with respect to speed and accuracy of data use has been a topic of research for some time. Most studies have been of the one variable type, which pits a table against some form of graph without considering concurrent factors such as task type, user background, etc. It is not surprising that all of the three possible outcomes have been reported in the literature with equal frequency. Benbasat and Schroeder (1977), Carter (1947), Feliciano, Powers and Bryant (1963) and Tullis (1981) have reported the superiority of graphic presentation. Ghani (1981), Grace (1966), Lucas (1981), Nawrocki (1972), Remus (1984), Vernon (1952) and Wainer and Reiser (1976) have reported the reverse. DeSanctis (1984), Dickson, DeSanctis and McBride (1986), Ives (1982), and MacDonald-Ross (1977) have found mixed results. Such disarray serves to emphasize that single variable studies are of limited usefulness in this area.

In a recent paper, Coll and Coll (1990) express this same observation. They present a classification of 16 variables which affect preference and efficiency in the use of tables and graphs and recommend that research in the area has to involve two factor (at least) experimentation. Interaction of variables becomes the focus of interest.

In the current study, working within this research framework, the author examined two levels of Task (Problem) Type with respect to Display Presentation Type. The two types of tasks were (1) retrieval of relational information and (2) retrieval of specific values. It was expected that graph use would be found superior to table use in the relational information condition while table use would be found superior to graph use in the specific value condition. Justification for these expectations follows.

First, it has been demonstrated that cues which are in the same form as a to-be-evaluated variable are given more attention and more importance than cues which are in a different form (Lichtenstein and Slovic, 1973; Slovic and Lichtenstein, 1968). Thus, requests for relational information should be more efficiently obtained from a bar graph while requests for specific values should be more efficiently obtained from a table.

Second, Form Utility (Andrus, 1971) posits that as the form of information more closely matches task requirements, users rate the information as having increased value. Specific value requests match the form of data in a table; relational value requests match the form of adjacent bar heights of a bar graph.

Third, Encoding Specificity (Tulving, 1979; Flexner and Tulving, 1978; Tulving and Osler, 1968) affirms that recall of a memory item is most likely to occur if the circumstances surrounding the memory acquisition are recreated at the time of recall. This robust construct has been extended to recognition and analysis (such as organizing information during the decision making process): for best results, material should be presented in the same form that it will be used. Again, the form of relative value requests and bar heights appear to be of the same form, while specific value requests and tables of values seem to be of the same form.

## Variables

The author examined two levels of Task Type (retrieval of relational information and retrieval of specific values) with respect to two levels of Display Presentation (table and bar graph) for joint effect on Correct Answer Score. Subjects were required to work with a two data set table or graph and then with a three data set table or graph.

One group of subjects retrieved relational information and specific values from the tables.

Another group retrieved the same data from the graphs. Thus Task Type, was a within groups variable while Display Presentation Type was a between groups variable.

## Method

Subjects

Eighty masters students in business and engineering from two adjacent urban universities (forty from each) were randomly divided into two groups. Being neighbors to each other, the universities (one liberal arts and one technical) have comparable demographics. Using data from an initially administered profile questionnaire, the two groups were compared for equality on Sex, Occupation Education (in years) and Age. A Chi-square test on Sex and Occupation, a Mann–Whitney test on Education and a t-test on Age all yielded no difference (for all variables, p > 0.1).

## Procedure

The first item of business was filling out the personal profile questionnaire. Following this, the subjects worked successively on the two tasks. Lastly, the author administered an attitude questionnaire.

One point was scored for each correct answer, so, across the 10 questions of each task, the highest score possible was 10. Subjects were tested individually and allowed as much time as needed to perform the tasks. Time for each task was recorded for check purposes.

Task 1, Two Data Set Table / Graph. Each subject was told that he/she would be given a graph (or that he/she would be given a table) and questions to answer from the data therein. Each was given a straight edge and told to use it to read across bars (Bar Graph Group) or across columns (Table Group).

The subject was then presented with two 8 1/2 by 11 sheets and asked to answer the questions sequentially. Simultaneously time was started. One sheet contained a two data set graph or table; the other contained five multiple choice questions which required specific value answers intermixed with five which required relational information answers.

The graph and table used and four typical questions are shown in Figure 1. The first two questions request specific values, while the second two request relational information.

Task 2, Three Data Set Table/Graph. The procedure here was identical to that of the previous task. This task, however, required more complex analysis in that three data sets were presented. The graph and table used and four typical questions are shown in Figure 2. Again, the first two questions request specific values, while the second two request relational information.

## Conclusions

The primary conclusion of this study is that there is a significant interaction between Task Type and Display Presentation Type. When using tables, subjects perform better on specific value work than on relative information work, but when using graphs they perform better on relative information work than on specific value work.

Despite symmetry (the fact that each group did better with one type of work compared to the other type of work) the table group was more content using tables than the graph group was using graphs.

## Discussion

As argued by the author here and in Coll and Coll (1990), this study demonstrates that in the area of Display Presentation Type, multifactor experimentation is dictated. The efficacy of graphs versus tables is relative, determined by a host of impinging factors. In this study the key factor was Task Type.

Had this been a one factor experiment of table use versus graph use, a significant difference in favor of tables would have been reported (p < 0.00001) due to the much larger difference between specific value scores than between relative information scores (Figure 3). Such a report would have been totally wide of the mark.

Considering the superior results of the Graph Group with relative information retrieval, it is surprising that this group did not rate their condition (graph use) as satisfactory as the Table Group rated their condition (table use). This presents a problem for systems designers: should they give users what they want or what produces the best results? Or should they give users both, and leave the decision to the user? The last solution is preferred by Coll et al. (1991). (This problem may also be faced by managers choosing a system for their department.)

Perhaps table use is more practiced than graph use and hence humans feel more comfortable with tables or perhaps graph use requires more cognitive effort (extract meaning from a diagram) versus table use (values give meaning directly). Studies aimed at teasing out the answer would certainly be appropriate.

In this study, specific values were directly available from the table, but had to be extrapolated from the graph. Work should be performed to determine what results would be obtained if extrapolation was required in both table and graph conditions and what results would be obtained if all values in the graph condition fell exactly on grid lines. As well, follow up studies similar to this study should be performed with tables and line graphs.
