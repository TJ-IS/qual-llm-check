# An examination of DBMS adoption and success in American organizations

Varun Grover and James T.C. Teng
University of South Carolina, Columbia SC, USA

Database technology has been available for over two and a half decades and yet widespread adoption has not occurred. This study examines a relatively mature IS technology in an attempt to understand factors that facilitate its adoption. Specifically, the maturity of the IS function and demographic characteristics are proposed as factors distinguishing adopters from non-adopters. The results of a survey of 288 American organizations are reported. The results indicate that the adoption of DBMS is consistent with several indicators of IS maturity, size, and industry. Additional results reveal that suggested database benefits are not being achieved by these organizations.

Keywords: DBMS, Database, Data resource management, Adoption, DBMS benefits, IS maturity, Demographics.

## 1. Introduction

Toward the late 1960s, the notoriety of MIS departments in corporate America was becoming commonplace. Application backlogs resulting in unacceptably slow response to user needs, was among the most prevalent complaint. In the early 1970s, however, with the advent of database technologies, a gradual reorientation began to a more data-centered approach to information systems. Along with these technologies came promises of reduced data redundancy, increased data integrity, easier access to ad hoc information and integration of applications. Corporate America responded to these contentions and adopted these technologies over the next two decades. The technologies themselves evolved from purely transaction oriented to decision oriented, from mainframe to PC based, and from relatively inflexible to more flexible, friendly, and powerful.

Despite the widespread acceptance of databases systems in both the academic and practitioner community as appropriate and good for all organizations, there has been no indication that a majority of organizations have adopted DBMS technologies. Given the fact that DBMS has been available for over twenty-five years, we are intrigued by these interesting questions:

(1) What are some of the factors that facilitate the adoption of a DBMS in business organizations?

(2) What are the reasons for its non-adoption?

(3) Do organizations that have adopted a DBMS actually realize the proclaimed benefits of such a system?

These are the central questions of this study. In other words, the study raises a cautionary flag in its attempt to evaluate a relatively mature IS technology and its impact on American corporations.

## 2. Factors influencing adoption of a DBMS

Before discussing the factors influencing adoption, the type of DBMS needs to be clarified. This study explicitly deals with a transactional database management system existing in an organization and not the multiple user oriented systems that might additionally be present.

Two sets of factors are hypothesized as distinguishing between adopters and non-adopters of DBMS. The first set involves demographic characteristics and the second reflects factors related to the maturity of organizational IS.

## 2.1. Demographic characteristics

One of the most common demographic characteristic used to predict adoption behavior in the innovation literature has been organizational size. Empirical studies involving this variable have been inconsistent. One set of studies finds a positive relationship between adoption and size, and argues the case for resource availability in larger organizations leading to more adoption (e.g. [17]). The opposing view makes the case for flexibility of smaller organizations to respond to environmental changes and thereby be more innovative (e.g. [9]). Given the nature of a DBMS as a resource that supports organizational computing, the former case seems more plausible. Further, some prior evidence in studies conducted by Kahn [13] and Gillenson [7] suggest that the likelihood of having a data management department is higher in larger firms. Hence Hypothesis 1a can be stated as follows.

Hypothesis 1a. Organizational size will be positively related to adoption of a DBMS.

A second demographic characteristic that has often been considered as a basis for classification of IS is the industry. Inherently different industry attributes, such as “information intensity”, “industry prosperity” and “need for storage flexibility” [10] would be expected to reflect different adoption patterns for DBMS across industries. This leads to the next hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1b. Adoption of DBMS will be different across industries.

## 2.2. Maturity of organizational IS

One of the first discussants of the IS maturity concept was Richard Nolan who proposed a stage model of IS evolution. While technologies have changed since the last model proposed by Nolan in 1979, it is still regarded by some as one of the few theoretical foundations of MIS. King and Kraemer [14] in their critique of the model concluded: “Nolan’s model makes a significant contribution, that the growth of computing must be due to the influence of forces within and outside the organization.”

After critiquing the empirical literature on Nolan's model, Benbasat, et al. (1) conclude that: "while the evidence to date is not very encouraging, it does not definitively reject the stage hypothesis."

The role of DBMS and data orientation has always played a role in Nolan's later stages. For instance, Gibson and Nolan [6] hypothesized that most major changes in reaching ‘maturity’ arose from advancements in software, particularly DBMS. In his 1976 model, Nolan [19] predicted that control was shifting from the management of computing to the management of data, enabled by DBMS. In his last model [20], he extended the stages to include both data administration and maturity.

This model provides a basis for capturing the elusive concept of IS maturity. While there remains some question regarding the empirical validity of the model, there is little doubt that the dimensions proposed provide a sound premise for distinguishing the IS function across organizations. other studies have used the model to develop maturity criteria (e.g., [1,10]).

Based on Nolan's benchmarks, the following dimensions have been adapted to represent IS maturity.

(a) IS expenditure. The ratio of IS Expenditure to Sales is purported to be an indicator of the importance of IS in the company. While the notion of such a ratio following a complex learning curve pattern is questionable [14], the case being made here is that more mature IS organizations would tend to spend more on IS expenses as a proportion of their sales.

(b) Technology level. The extremes of on-line vs. batch processing are used to capture this variable. The applicability of the “batch concept” in today’s environment of PCs, 4th generation languages, etc. is inappropriate at an overall organizational level. However, focusing on the transactional system (as opposed to the managerial support system) provides some indication of technological maturity.

(c) IS structure. Centralized vs. decentralized IS control provides another indicator of maturity. With the economics of hardware no longer dictating the need to keep the IS physically in one location, most organizations are now experiencing a diffusion of processing power. More mature organizations will tend to diffuse the IS organization to maintain control over distributed resources. The days of the IS specialist are now giving way to the liaison, who understands both business and technology. Therefore, the two aspects of IS structure that will be captured are: (i) processing structure as reflected by the centralization vs. decentralization of IS hardware and software; and (ii) organizational structure as reflected by centralization vs. decentralization of IS control.

(d) End-user computing. Somewhat related to the notion of IS control is end-user computing. Powerful PCs and user-friendly software is increasing the computing ability of the average user. While decentralization of IS control (above) is one aspect, more mature IS organizations should be willing to give up control to the users. End-user responsibility for data, i.e.: (i) input of data; and (b) quality of data, is used to capture this variable.

(e) Age of IS. The age of the IS organization provides a more direct indication of maturity.

(f) Size of IS. With the expansion of computing in businesses, the size of the IS organization (while related to the size of the overall organization and the IS expenditure/sales figure), is argued to be a component of maturity.

It is important to point out that the benchmark variables proposed above are applicable in today's environment. However, aggregation of these variables into a single measure of maturity is inappropriate. As discussed by Drury [5]: “While the benchmark variables change across the stages of development, they are inconsistent with each other and generally change at different rates .... However, there is evidence that the individual benchmark variables are of interest individually, in spite of their collective difficulties”.

This study will treat each “maturity variable” independently and not attempt any aggregation. Hypotheses 2 can be stated as follows.

Hypothesis 2 (a–e). The maturity variables (as listed above) will be positively related to the adoption of DBMS.

## 3. Adoption of DBMS

The dependent variable, adoption of DBMS, can be operationalized in many different ways. The most common form of measurement of adoption in the innovation literature is through a dichotomous measure (adoption vs. non-adoption); other studies use an extent of adoption (Likert scale) measure. In this study, a 4-level categorical scale was chosen to capture the richness of adoption. Not only does the scale capture the adoption of a DBMS, but it also integrates the existence of a formal data management position (generally the DBA). Kahn [13] in her survey of 56 organizations found that in some cases a formal position was established before the adoption of a DBMS. Christoff [4] emphasizes that organizations might have an advantage in doing this, as by evaluating the logical structure of files beforehand, they can move into a relational DBMS in the future that better meets their needs. Therefore, the 4-level scale used, incorporates these possibilities and is enumerated below:

(A) our organization has a formal data management position and is currently using a DBMS;

(B) our organization has a formal data management position and is currently planning to implement a DBMS;

(C) our organization has a formal data management position but no plans to implement a DBMS;

(D) our organization has no plans to have a formal data management position or to implement a DBMS.

Organizations falling in Category A represent adopters and those in category D represent the non-adopters, with categories B and C containing intermediate organizations.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Operationalization

From the two demographic variables, size was measured by the number of employees in the organization. Another proxy often used for size, i.e., sales or revenue, has problems when dealing with financial institutions. Industry was measured on a categorical scale.

The variables comprising maturity were mainly measured using 5-point Likert scales, with the exception of IS budget/sales (which was left open-ended) and age of IS (which was measured on a categorical scale). These items are listed as follows (with labels for the 2 Likert scale poles indicated in parentheses):

Technology level.

\- How would you categorize your company's overall processing? (1: 100% batch processing–5: 100% on-line processing)

IS structure.

\- How would you categorize your company's overall processing? (1: completely decentralized–5: completely centralized)

\- Is your EDP/MIS personnel/organization centralized or decentralized? (1: decentralized control–5: centralized control)

End user computing.

\- To what extent is the end user personally responsible for inputting data for MIS (not PC oriented) applications? (1: not personally responsible [i.e., done by data entry clerk]–5: personally responsible)

## 4.2. The sample

The questionnaire was pilot tested through face-to-face interviews with executives from Fortune 500 companies responsible for data management. Items were carefully reworded as per their suggestions before the final administration of the instrument.

A total of 1200 questionnaires were sent out to the “person responsible for the data management function in the organization”. The target group was medium to large corporations (over \$10 million in revenues) in the states of Pennsylvania, Ohio, New York and West Virginia. A total of 288 responses were received; out of these 171 (59.9%) had DBMSs in place and operational. This reflected a response rate of 24%. Non-response bias was not assessed.

## 5. Results

Of the 288 responding organizations, 59.4% had a data management position and a DBMS, 2.4% had a position and were planning to implement a DBMS, 6.6% only had the Position and no plans to implement and 31.6% did not have a position nor planned to implement a DBMS. These results support our original contention that despite dealing with a relatively mature technology, DBMS is not highly pervasive, with almost 40% of the organizations having no plans to implement one.

## 5.1. Use of DBMS and demographics

Table 3 illustrates the results of a chi-square analysis to test the relationship between industry and the use of a DBMS (Hypothesis 1b). Yates correction was used as some of the cell sizes were less than 5. The results indicate a significant $p = 0.00$ relationship. On collapsing the four categories of DBMS use into two (adopters and non-adopters) the chi-square statistic was still significant, indicating a relationship between industry and use of a DBMS. Of note in the table is the fact that financial institutions tend to have a lower adoption of DBMS. Given the ‘information intensive’ nature of the industry, this is somewhat surprising. While communication networks that integrate ATMs and provide up-to-date information on financial indicators are probably commonplace, internal processing in financial firms seems to still be, for the most part, in the individual file processing era of yesteryear. Informal discussion with IS professionals in banks indicate that most financial institutions have been using prepackaged software for many years and grown dependent upon it. Many banks rely on service bureaus such as EDS, Systematics, M&I, etc. under a facility management (FM) contract arrangement. Because of the huge processing volume and response time requirements in financial transactions, most service bureaus adhere to the performance efficiency of traditional file processing rather than risking the additional overhead involved in establishing common data bases.

Hypothesis 1a is also supported (see Table 3), in that the ANOVA indicates a significant $p < 0.05$ relationship between size of the organization and the levels of DBMS use. In other words, adopters tend to be larger organizations.

## 5.3. Reasons for non-adoption

Respondents from the non-adopter organizations were provided with a list of reasons for non-adoption of DBMS and asked to rank the top three. These reasons were adapted from a previous survey by Ho [12] who looked at Hong Kong firms. The responses were aggregated using a simple weighting scheme and ranked in descending order. The results are shown in Table 5.

The top three reasons are: Satisfaction with the existing system, low perceived importance of the DBMS concept, and the nature of the business. ## 5.4. Use of tools

In addition, organizations that adopted DBMS were asked to respond to a question on the use of tools to complement the DBMS. The list of tools and the proportion of the sample that acknowledged their use is illustrated in Table 6. Query languages, transaction logging, and the data dictionary were being used by about three quarters of the adopters. Automated database design tools, which are usually part of CASE (Computer Aided Software Engineering) software, are still not widely prevalent. Also, the much discussed database machine was used by less than 5%.

## 5.5. Expected vs. realized benefits

Eight aspects of a DBMS that have repeatedly been extolled as the benefits of effectively adopting such an approach were provided to the respondents. They were asked to respond on 5-point scales, to what extent these benefits had originally been expected of the DBMS and the extent to which they had been realized to-date. Table 7 shows the results. T-tests were used to test the significance of the differences between expected and actual benefits. As would be expected, the three most expected benefits of a DBMS were reduced redundancy, improved integrity, and integration. None of these expectations were realized. The two benefits that were realized according to expectations were the provision of more powerful file management and the enhanced security and privacy of data. These results suggest that the potential of DBMS has not been fully realized by organizations, at least not as per a priori expectations. The reasons for this could be manifold, i.e., inadequate technical expertise within the firm, oversell of the DBMS concept prior to adoption, excessive conflicts among users, the pressures of the operating environment or the lack of proper planning. While the reasons have not been explicitly investigated here, it is important to do so in future.

If we disregard a priori expectations as our frame of reference, and examine the actual extent of realization of benefits, some interesting observations can be made. The actual levels can be divided into three natural categories that have greater separation across groups, than within groups. These categories are indicated in the last column of Table 7, as low-level benefits (L) with scores ranging from 3.85–3.97, medium-level benefits (M) with scores ranging from 3.35–3.49 and high-level benefits (H) with scores ranging from 3.07–3.14. Low-level benefits like powerful file-management and improved integrity are being achieved to a greater extent by the sample organizations. These benefits are primarily operational in nature and can often be achieved by invoking DBMS software features. For example, data definition software enables the enhancement of integrity and file management utilities associated with DBMS are more powerful than conventional COBOL based methods. The medium-level benefits, like integration of applications, improved security, privacy, and redundancy, require deliberate action by the IS group in terms of data planning, architecture, and carefully formulated rules and procedures. The expertise of the data base administrator or the data administrator could play a significant role in achieving these benefits. Finally, the high-level benefits, although widely cited in the normative literature, have not been accomplished in practice. These include satisfying ad hoc information needs and reducing application development and maintenance time and cost. The effective integration of the structural approaches (i.e., improving data structures) advocated by database designers and the advances in development processes (e.g., CASE) could eventually lead to an improvement in these “advanced benefits”.

## 6. Conclusion

Data base technology has been available for over two decades and there has been relentless calls from leading consultants and researchers to adopt the technology $[18,16]$ in pursuit of reduced redundancy, data sharing, more efficient application development, and other promised benefits. One wonders why some organizations have still not adopted this important technology and attempt to reach a new plateau in IS sophistication.

The question is interesting, and relevant to the broader question of organizational learning of information technologies. This study uses a simple approach and a large sample to provide some credible answers, albeit preliminary. Specifically, we found that the DBMS adoption decision is contingent upon the size of the organization, confirming the prediction generally made by innovation theorists. Also, the adoption of DBMS is found to be related to several indicators of IS maturity including the extent of on-line (instead of batch) processing, degree of centralization in processing and in IS organization, the extent that end users are held responsible for inputting data, age as well as the size of the IS department.

Additional results of the study sheds further light on the question of why some organizations inclined not to adopt the technology. Firstly, the comparisons between expected versus actually realized DBMS benefits indicate that most of the promised benefits fall short of expectations. Not only is the technology still evolving and far from perfect, but there are also numerous implementation difficulties $[11,15]$ . It appears that the “data base solution” has delivered less than was promised and many IS managers may have grown cautious about it. Secondly, the pattern of benefits suggests that operational benefits are being attained rather than those involving managerial analysis and planning. Further, benefits that require integration of development processes and data structures are not being attained at the same level. The reasons given for not adopting data bases may also be interpreted in light of the technology’s credibility gap. Non-adopters may have based their decisions on the purported deficiencies and difficulties of DBMS and take a defensive stand in citing “satisfaction with existing systems” as a reason for not adopting. Interestingly, the reason “nature of business does not require a database” may, in fact, have some degree of legitimacy. It is possible that some businesses operate in a stable environment and the need for cross-function coordination and ad hoc inquiries are limited.

Over 40% of the respondents to our survey are currently operating without a DBMS. This is quite surprising given the long history and continuous availability of the technology. While our results provide some insight into this, future studies should broaden the scope of inquiry, for instance, by including other innovation-theoretic factors such as championship, formalization, and perceived need. Prescriptions for effective data resource management are one outcome of this research which can only grow in importance as we move through the 1990s.
