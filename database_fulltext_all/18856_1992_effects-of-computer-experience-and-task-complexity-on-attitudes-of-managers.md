# Effects of computer experience and task complexity on attitudes of managers

Peter P. Mykytyn, Jr.

University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington TX, USA

Gary I. Green

Boise State University, Boise ID, USA

Attitudes of managers toward computers are important determinants of behavior that may influence both utilization of and satisfaction with information systems. A laboratory experiment was conducted to study the relationship between task complexity and attitudes of 64 managers who were classified according to their computer experience. Participants completed both pretest attitude and computer familiarity instruments. The experimental session required participants to complete either a simple or complex task in a controlled laboratory environment using an interactive application on a terminal device connected to a mainframe computer. Following the experimental session, participants immediately completed a posttest attitude instrument. Attitude shift as a consequence of usage did not occur. Results indicate that managers have a positive attitude toward using computers regardless of computer familiarity and these attitudes do not change as a function of manipulating the task complexity dimension. Results also suggest that organizations should provide managers with increased opportunities for further usage and training. The complexity of computer tasks should be of concern to systems designers but may not have immediate effect on user attitudes.

Keywords: Computer experience, Task complexity, Attitudes toward computers, Attitude shift, Attitude change, Managerial training.

Do attitudes of managers change as a function of usage? Managerial attitudes about computer systems also may be dependent, to a large degree, on the type of tasks that managers face. ## 2. Background

## 2.1. Attitudes

Attitudes have received a great deal of research attention and this, according to Alport, is “the most distinctive and indispensable concept in contemporary American social psychology” [2]. The traditional school of thought purports that attitudes influence behavior (e.g., [71]). Much of the empirical MIS research, such as the work by Lucas [39,40,41,42,43,44], has assumed that attitude affects behavior. However, another school of thought suggests that an individual’s attitudes are determined by experience (e.g., [6]). People utilize experience to construct basic attitudes.

Contemporary theories of attitude have evolved from: the learning theories of Hull [27], Spence [66], and Tolman [70]; expectancy-value theories based on the subjective expected utility (SEU) model by Edwards [19]; balance theory [24]; Osgood and Tannenbaum's congruity principle [53]; and Festinger's [20] theory of cognitive dissonance. Although these theories have many similarities, they also differ in many respects; this may contribute to ambiguity and confusion in attitude research [21].

A framework has been developed incorporating the traditional components of affect (feelings, evaluations), cognition (opinions, beliefs), conation (behavioral intentions), and behavior (observed overt acts) in an effort to integrate the diversity in attitude theory and research. In the Fishbein and Ajzen framework, affect refers to a person's feelings toward and evaluation of something, whether it be some object, person, issue, or event. Cognition represents a person's knowledge, opinions, beliefs, and thoughts about an object. Conation refers to an individual's behavioral intentions and actions with regard to, or in the presence of, an object. Finally, they distinguish behavioral intentions from behavior, the actual overt act.

There is widespread agreement that affect is the most important part of the attitude concept; attitude toward some object specifically deals with the affect component. However, different labels, such as opinion, satisfaction, and others, have been applied to the affect component, confounding the distinction between attitude and other concepts.

Beliefs are the building blocks of the framework. Beliefs are formed as a result of observation or experience with an object, information received from outside sources, or by way of various inference processes. Similarly, new or modified beliefs may be formed as a result of new observations or experiences, new information received about the object, or new inferences. The beliefs formed by an individual lead to the formation of an attitude toward the object, subsequent behavioral intentions with respect to it and finally to actual behavior.

Ajzen and Fishbein [1] further refined their framework by decomposing beliefs into two subcomponents: beliefs that behavior leads to certain outcomes and an evaluation of those outcomes;

and normative beliefs that specific individuals or groups expect or do not expect the behavior. While the former leads to the development of an attitude toward the behavior, the latter leads to the development of subjective norms. Beliefs about outcomes and subjective norms influence behavioral intentions and, ultimately, behavior toward an object, called the “Theory of reasoned action” by Ajzen and Fishbein.

There is a strong theoretical basis for the relationship between changes in beliefs and attitude change. Since changes in beliefs may occur as a result of some task experience, it follows that subsequent attitudinal change also may occur. What is not clear, however, is the effect of task complexity, an attribute of task experience, in this overall relationship of behavior, beliefs, and attitudes. Does task complexity cause attitude change? Does the generalization of task experience affect attitudes?

## 2.2. Prior research on attitudes toward computers and MIS

In a longitudinal study involving 107 MBA students, Davis et al. [15] found that perceived ease of use and usefulness, two components of their “Technology acceptance model” based on the “Theory of reasoned action,” significantly influenced peoples’ behavioral intentions toward use of a computer system. However, peoples’ attitudes only partially mediated the effects of these beliefs on their intentions.

Morrison [51] conducted a study replicating earlier research by Lee [35] about attitudes toward computers. Although Morrison found differences between 1970 and 1983, it was noted that positive acceptance of computers by the general populace was not apparent. Morrison found, for example, that negative attitudes prevailed; these were associated with the possible disemploying and dehumanizing effects of computers, fears about computer reliability, and the power that computers seemed to have over the lives of people. According to Hill et al. [25], some people may be described as “techno-phobics” who simply are afraid of using computers. For many, the phobia may be caused by an initial bad experience with computers–attitude formation from a traumatic experience. For some, computer anxiety may exist even with prior computer experience and familiarity [48]. Others may choose to avoid using computers. One explanation for this 'computer aversion' may be based on social learning [49]; for example, managers should not have to type, even if it is at a keyboard.

A user's attitude, and possibly performance, can be affected by interaction with a computer. Quintanar et al. [55] administered a quiz to students, either a standard computer program or one that used friendly conversational language, e.g., using the subject's name and personal pronouns. The friendly conversational program was perceived as more human, less honest, and less courteous; more importantly, these users perceived the computer to be more responsible for poor performance. Sutter and Reid [67] found that favorable attitudes exist for learning with computer-assisted instruction (CAI) but that personality traits were important mitigating factors. Kneer [34] measured attitudes of individuals toward CAI before and after receiving instruction presented by CAI. The attitude pretest did not correlate significantly with any of the performance measures used during the CAI session; however, a significant correlation was obtained between attitude post-test and the percentage of errors made during the experimental session.

Organizational members have preconceived attitudes about management information systems (MIS) within an organization. These attitudes include beliefs, values, and expectations and would relate to how individuals perceive the capabilities of and the need for an MIS, the MIS staff, and organizational change in general $[22,60,63]$ . Lucas conducted extensive research $[38]$ and was able to support his proposition that high levels of use of an information system can result from favorable user attitudes about information systems as well as the information services staff. Also, his results showed that systems usage could be predicted from attitudes about the computer's potential and attitudes toward the information systems staff. Kaiser and Srinivasan $[31]$ concluded that user attitudes toward an MIS can be an important indicator of the success of a computer system implementation. Other related research has examined user satisfaction $[4,5,28,29]$ which has emphasized satisfaction with the information system function and its products but has not dealt directly with attitudes and computer experience.

In addition, other recent empirical and conceptual research in MIS has examined the attitude/satisfaction construct from different perspectives. Conrath and Mignen [14] noted that many organizations do not even attempt to measure user satisfaction. However, those that do are inclined to measure it directly, such as with the case of attitude measurement in our study, rather than indirectly through surrogates.

Given the current interest in the satisfaction/attitude constructs, Melone [50] has provided MIS researchers with a comprehensive assessment of satisfaction; she has also drawn attention to the similarities between satisfaction and the psychological construct of attitude.

## 2.3. Computer experience

Prior experience with computers or knowledge of computers has been demonstrated to be an important variable in predicting performance [30]. Furthermore, attitudes toward computers may be related to the amount of computer experience [36]. Lucas [37] surveyed data processing and non-data processing employees to examine the user/data processing interface. He found that some factors such as age, involvement, and contact with the system have a positive or negative influence on people's attitudes toward computers and the EDP staff. On the other hand, Guthrie's observations were unable to support the hypothesis that middle managers who have familiarity with MIS will have more favorable perceptions regarding the need for and effects of MIS than those who possess minimal or no familiarity. Additional research by Lucas [45] examined performance differences between those working with hard copy computer output versus graphics and CRTs. He found that the lack of experience associated with those working with graphics/CRTs affected overall performance, supporting Scott Morton's [64] position that extensive training was required to prepare managers to use a system. Kasper and Cerveny [32] conducted a laboratory study to simulate a business setting examining relationships among user characteristics, decision support systems (DSS) usage patterns, and decision making performance. They found that familiarity with computers significantly affected the development of decision models in a positive manner concluding that computer familiarity is an important contributor to end-user success, especially in the early stages of use.

Thus, there is a well established correlation between attitudes and behavior. The relationship of task complexity to them is not clear however. Task complexity imposed by the system theoretically should have an effect on attitudes, either strengthening them or shifting them.

## 2.4. Task complexity

MacCrimmon [46], Schroder and Suedfeld [61], Shepard [65], Taylor [69], and Wood [72] have examined task complexity in terms of information processing and decision making. Campbell [10] has developed a classification scheme in an attempt to organize the various approaches and states that task complexity can be treated as: a psychological experience; an interaction existing between task and person characteristics; and a function of objective task characteristics. For purposes of this research, task complexity will be defined as a function of the objective task characteristics.

March and Simon [47] identified several objective task characteristics which can contribute to task complexity: unknown or uncertain alternatives, inexact or unknown means-ends connections, and the existence of a number of subtasks. Campbell [9] defined a complex task as having several interrelated and conflicting elements to satisfy, similar to March and Simon's dimension based on the number of subtasks to be accomplished.

Campbell and Gingrich [11] studied the relationship between task type and participation by having computer programmers compose either complex or simple programs. Complexity was based on the interrelated and conflicting elements required of the task. Although they noted that the complex task placed high cognitive demands on the programmers, they made it clear that the demands resulted from the task itself and not from the personal characteristics of the individual. Finally, Schroder et al. [62] found that complexity increases as the number of dimensions of information requiring attention (information load) and the number of alternatives associated with each dimension (information diversity) increased.

Building on the properties of task complexity identified by Schroder et al., Campbell proposed a framework which implies that an increase in information load, information diversity, or rate of information change can be considered contributors to task complexity. This framework provides a theoretical basis for defining task complexity, independently of any person performing the task.

## 3. Hypotheses

The premise that managers who are experienced and familiar with using computers would have a more favorable attitude toward computer usage than managers classified as inexperienced and less familiar is supported by related research (e.g., [57]). Negative attitude toward computer usage by managers may be caused by a number of factors including fear of the unknown or technology. Familiarity with computers should reduce or eliminate such negative attitudes.

Hypothesis 1. Managers who are more familiar with computers will have a more positive attitude toward computer usage than managers who are not so familiar with them.

Based on the theory that attitudes influence behavior, one would not expect task manipulation to result in attitudinal change. However, a manager utilizing a computer for task resolution may experience attitude shift if the task of using the computer system is too complex and the level of frustration too great. The attitude could affect usage, productivity, continued learning, and acceptance of systems (both current and future). Managers who already are familiar with computers should not exhibit any attitudinal shift as a result of task complexity since they have well established such attitudes in a variety of circumstances. The following hypotheses are related to attitudinal shift.

Hypothesis 2. Managers who are more familiar with computers will not have an attitude shift as a consequence of working on either a simple or complex task.

Hypothesis 3. Managers who are less familiar with computers will have a positive attitude shift as a consequence of working on a simple task.

Hypothesis 4. Managers who are less familiar with computers will have a negative attitude shift as a consequence of working on a complex task.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Subjects

All subjects who participated in the study held managerial and administrative positions in a large nonprofit organization in a major southwestern city. Participants were employed an average of over six years with the organization, with more than three years in their current positions. The mean age of subjects was 38; 36% of the subjects had a bachelor's degree, 33% had a master's degree, and 14% had completed doctoral studies. Areas of managerial responsibility for the participants included business systems, accounting, finance, inventory and property management, purchasing, personnel, and others. Seventy subjects participated and 64 completed the entire experiment. Meetings, illness, and unexpected travel accounted for the difference.

Since the participants were selected from only one organization it would be difficult to generalize the results. However, they possessed varied educational and job-related backgrounds and were practicing managers.

## 4.2. Instrumentation

A questionnaire was developed to collect demographic data and to ascertain a subject's level of experience with computers, peripherals, etc. Questionnaires were delivered to the participants who returned them by mail. Each potential subject was assigned a number to guarantee anonymity as well as for control purposes. Instructions requested that only potential subjects were to complete the questionnaire. Those who had previous experience as data processing professionals were excluded from the study to avoid potential bias in attitudes due to a different value system [3].

The importance of reliable and valid instrumentation in empirical MIS research cannot be overemphasized. Here we operationally defined the level of experience with computers as an individual's composite score based on a 3-item questionnaire (see Appendix A) dealing with background knowledge of terminals and other peripherals, use of computer systems, and word processing. Point values from 1–4 were assigned: 1 indicating that a subject has ‘never’ used computer systems, etc. A final item was a self-assessment of degree of comfort in using computers based on a 7-point bipolar adjective scale; this was not included in the assessment of experience but was used as a validation check of the instrument. Previous pilot testing among different managers indicated that the three items adequately differentiated their degree of experience with computing.

A measure of instrument reliability, Cronbach's alpha, was used to determine the extent of internal consistency of the questionnaire. Interitem reliability was 0.72 which is acceptable for this type of research [8]. The validation of the questionnaire was conducted by calculating correlations between the three items. The Pearson correlation coefficients ranged from 0.30–0.69 and were significant at p < 0.01. In addition, a further validity check was conducted to determine the correlation between each of the three items with the subject's overall feeling about using computers. The correlations were significant at p < 0.0004. The correlations, though significant, represent only partial evidence for instrument validity. However, when these measures are combined with those during the construction of the questionnaire and the pilot study, we conclude that the instrument is valid.

Each subject's overall level of experience with computers was determined by summing the values for each of the statements on the questionnaire. One standard deviation (2.11) from the mean (7.05) of the overall level of experience was utilized to differentiate familiar versus less familiar. The rationale for utilizing this method is to avoid the problems inherent in classifying individuals based on a median value score (where one group is individuals who score above the median and the other below it). One standard deviation (rounded to the nearest integer value) from the mean should differentiate participants by their computer experience. All participants who scored 5 or less (1 standard deviation below the mean) were classified as less familiar. There were 17 subjects in the less familiar grouping with an average computer experience score of 4.2. Twenty-eight participants scored between 6 and 8 and were classified as somewhat familiar with an average score of 7.1. The familiar classification was approximately one standard deviation above the mean and included all subjects who scored 9 or more. There were 19 subjects classified as familiar with an average group score of 9.5. This method insured that only those persons who possessed little or no overall experience with computers were classified as less familiar, and those that had more familiarity clearly were differentiated from those who had little familiarity. Only the two groups of familiar or less familiar were utilized for testing our hypotheses.

A critical aspect of this research concerned the development of a questionnaire dealing with a person's attitude toward using computers. Recent instrumentation efforts concentrated on the establishment of measures to assess user satisfaction with information and information systems, but none of these measures deal with general attitudes toward using computers. Howard and Smith [26] did develop a new instrument that was more closely aligned to current research, but it was deemed inappropriate, since it dealt specifically with microcomputers. Therefore, an attitude questionnaire (see Appendix B) using semantic differential scales was developed by the procedures suggested in Bailey and Pearson and Osgood et al. [54].

Ten questions were developed to assess attitude. Factor analysis based on principal components using varimax rotation with an eigenvalue of 1 was then utilized to refine the instrument. This ensured that it was indeed unidimensional, i.e., measuring attitudes toward using computers. Factor loadings with a minimum value of 0.50 are shown in Table 1. The first factor is concerned with seven questions related to feelings toward computers and computer usage. The second contains two questions dealing with feelings toward people who are experienced with computers. The third factor includes only one item, feelings toward people who are not experienced with computers. The factor analysis can be interpreted to mean that questions specifically dealing with computers and computer usage only are included within the first factor providing the unidimensionality desired. These seven questions were utilized in the final instrument.

Each of the first six questions was followed by five distinct adjective pairs representing a seven interval scale. The bipolar adjectives were selected in part from those suggested by Brinton [7], Deese [17], and Dunn–Rankin [18]. The ordering of the questions was random and the adjectives were placed randomly on the scale as suggested by Dawes [16]. A sixth adjective pair, per the Bailey and Pearson procedure, negative/positive, was included for each question and this helped to assess instrument validity. The seventh question, also used in instrumentation validation, contained 15 distinct adjective pairs and the negative/positive pair. This question assessed how a person felt about computers in general, and, based on the factor analysis, it is clearly related to computer usage. Instructions for the instrument were developed based on the work of Haried [23]. The attitude instrument was administered in both the pre-test and post-test; however, the order of questions was randomized for the post-test and all bipolar adjectives were reversed with respect to their scale position to avoid response bias.

All procedures with regard to scaling, weighting, and normalizing are consistent with the method developed by Bailey and Pearson. The scaling of each of the seven intervals was quantified by assigning values of -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, and 3 to the intervals. The positive/negative adjective pair was assigned values of 1.00–0.10 in increments of 0.15, with the value of 1.00 indicating extreme importance. A raw score for each question was then computed by summing the individual responses for each adjective pair and weighting this raw score by multiplying the value of the positive/negative pair with the raw score. A total raw score for the complete instrument was determined by summing the seven weighted raw scores. A final normalized score ranging from +1.00--1.00 for all seven questions was then calculated. Cronbach's alpha for the questionnaire equaled 0.85 suggesting a high level of internal consistency.

Several correlation analysis procedures were employed to determine instrument validity. First, correlation coefficients were computed for each of the first six questions' total weighted score with the weighted score for question seven. Explained variation ranged from 0.35–0.72. All correlations were significant at p < 0.005 with four of the six significant at p = 0.0001. Second, a procedure recommended by Kerlinger [33] and utilized by Ives et al. was applied to examine the correlations between the total weighted score and each question's weighted score. This approach assumes that the total weighted score is valid. Therefore, the degree to which the question correlates with the total weighted score suggests construct validity for the question. Each question's score was subtracted from the total weighted score in order to avoid a spurious part-whole correlation [13]; the resulting 'new' score was then correlated with each of the questions' scores. These correlations were significant at p < 0.0001. It therefore appears that the instrument possesses sufficient validity.

## 4.3. Research design and procedures

All subjects were informed that they would participate in a laboratory study. Participants, classified as computer familiar or less familiar, were assigned randomly to a treatment group to complete the $2 \times 2$ factorial design. Table 2 shows the actual number of subjects who completed all phases of the study, including those classified as somewhat familiar. A determination of effect size of the familiar and less familiar groups based upon the procedures defined in Cohen [12] was undertaken, and cell sizes were found to be sufficient.

The pre-test was conducted in a controlled setting (a conference room) where instructions were read to ensure the integrity of a test-like environment. Subjects were requested not to discuss any aspect of the experiment with their colleagues. The length of time between the administration of the pretest and the experimental session/post-test was five days. After the instructions had been read, participants opened an envelope containing instructions for their tasks. Subjects had no knowledge of other tasks. All work was performed at a terminal equipped with hard copy output. After subjects logged off, they were escorted by a research assistant to a conference room where the post-test was administered.

## 4.4. Treatment

Task complexity was operationalized by the number, types, and interconnected nature of corresponding instructions imposed on the participant by the task and task/problem requirements. This operationalization of task complexity is consistent with previously discussed research by March and Simon, Campbell and Gingrich, and Schroder et al., and the comprehensive framework developed by Campbell. Task selection required that subjects would not have to learn the assigned task. The task would be a common task and subjects would have knowledge about the problem and how to resolve the problem consistent with Sabherval and Grover's [58] classification of problem homogeneity and problem knowledge.

Two checkbook reconciliation tasks, simple and complex, were chosen for this research, since all subjects have knowledge and experience with this task. Checkbook reconciliation tasks could be differentiated based on the number of operations, their types, and interaction between them. For example, the adjustment of a number of different types of transactions could represent a dimension of complexity. Task instructions, as commonly prompted by menus, could be another dimension of complexity. Problem requirements in reconciliation could further contribute to complexity, e.g., to produce a report of activity on a group of transaction items.

Two checkbook reconciliation exercises were developed for a fictitious bank and pilot tested using students enrolled in an introductory undergraduate finance class. Debriefing of the pilot subjects and analysis of pilot results led to modifications of the instructions, procedures, and exercises.

Each of the two tasks and the requirements associated with them were developed within an interactive computer-based system. Each task provided a menu from which selections appropriate to each task were made. In addition, for the simple task, participants were provided with a list with one customer name, account number, beginning customer balance, and the checks written and deposits made by that customer. Those assigned the complex task were provided with customer lists that contained all customer names, account numbers, beginning balances, transaction codes and transaction amounts.

Participants assigned to the simple task, "TASK1", were prompted by the system to enter the name, account number, and beginning balance for the single customer. They were then prompted to enter appropriate check and deposit amounts. The last requirement for this task prompted the participants to print a bank statement for that customer. This was accomplished by simply selecting a menu item; the statement itself reflecting appropriate checks and deposits was automatically calculated and printed as a result of that menu selection.

Those subjects assigned to the complex task, "TASK2", were prompted by the system to enter the names, account numbers, beginning balances, and, where appropriate, transaction codes, and transaction amounts for 20 customers. Appropriate transactions were checks, deposits, miscellaneous credits, and miscellaneous charges. Not all customers experienced period activity, while some experienced multiple transactions. After the data for the customers had been entered, the system prompted each participant to prepare bank statements for those customers who experienced some type of transaction activity during the period. If a participant requested a statement for a customer who had not experienced activity, the system responded that a statement was not required for that customer; the participant then selected another. Finally, each participant was required to prepare a management report that included five out of ten possible options. These included total number of customers in the bank, with period activity, with a negative balance, average period negative balance, total period deposits, average period deposits, total period checks, average period checks, total period credits and charges. Selection of correct options to include in the report was based on the participant determining the relationship between type of customer activity and total bank customers.

During debriefing of pilot participants, care was taken to ensure that the complex task was itself considered intrinsically complex, not complex because of the computer system. Pilot participants agreed that system menus, prompts, and instructions were very clear and did not affect their view of task complexity.

## 4.5. Dependent variables

Validity of the task treatment was assessed with two variables. Time to complete the task was operationally defined as the elapsed time between computer logon and logoff; it was automatically captured by the system. If all requirements were completed correctly by an individual, then the task was considered complete. The simple task should take less time and most subjects should complete the task.

Research has shown that attitudes toward computers and MIS are related to systems success and performance of users. Pretest measurement of attitudes served as a basis for evaluating attitudes prior to the experimental session. Another dependent variable was shift in attitude toward computers. Attitude shift was determined by a difference score reflecting any change from pre-test to post-test response.

## 5. Results

Homogeneity of cell variances for attitude, attitude shift, and time to complete were tested using the Bartlett test [52]. There were no differences for the attitude measures, however cell variances were significantly different for time to complete scores. Time to complete data was transformed with the square-root procedure to stabilize variances and the Bartlett test indicated that the transformed variances were equal.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the general linear models (GLM) formulation in the statistical analysis system (SAS) [59] was used for the analysis of the first hypothesis dealing with pre-test attitude scores of managers familiar with computers. ANOVA also was used to assess differences for time to complete the tasks. Participant performance for the binomial variable, task completion, was tested using a chi-square procedure in SAS. Finally, the hypotheses dealing with attitude shift were tested using paired t-tests based on attitude shift scores.

## 5.2. Attitudes

Pretest attitude scores indicate that the participants had significant positive attitudes toward computer usage as measured by a T-test at 0.05 level of significance with the exception of attitudes toward “User Guides” (question 1, Appendix B). The only significant difference in attitudes by grouping was the attitude toward “Terminals” (question 2, Appendix B). Participants classified as familiar had a significantly more positive attitude toward “Terminals” than participants classified as somewhat or less familiar. Perhaps experience in using terminals and/or PCs may account for this difference. Figure 1 also shows that attitudes toward “Training” (question 3, Appendix B), “At Work” (question 5, Appendix B), and “Applications” (question 6, Appendix B) were relatively more positive for all groups indicating a general acceptance of the need for computer usage.

## 5.3. Hypotheses tests

Hypothesis 1 stated that managers who are more familiar with computers will have a more positive attitude toward computer usage. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to test this hypothesis using each manager's normalized total score obtained from the pretest attitude instrument. The hypothesis was supported (p = 0.0158) indicating a significant difference in pre-test attitudes between more familiar and less familiar managers (see Table 5). The normalized total scores were positive, indicating that all of the managers, regardless of experience, had at least a positive attitude toward using computers. The scores ranged from 0.008–0.84. Managers who were familiar with computers, however, did express a more positive attitude with scores ranging from 0.24–0.84. Those managers less familiar had scores ranging from 0.008–0.70.

## 5.4. Additional analysis

In order to examine the nature of the changes in attitude more fully, paired t-test analysis was conducted on each of the individual weighted questions encompassing the complete questionnaire. The less familiar managers, regardless of task, and the less familiar managers assigned to the simple task experienced a positive, significant change in attitude toward user instructional materials (p < 0.05 in each case). Second, the less familiar, regardless of task, and the familiar managers assigned to the simple task experienced a negative, significant attitude change toward using computer terminals (p < 0.01 in each case). Finally, and perhaps most prominent, was the attitude change toward enrolling in training on how to use computers. Each task group, each familiarity group, and every combination of task/familiarity group experienced a negative significant attitude change toward enrolling in training to use computers. An examination of this particular analysis showed that of the eleven combinations (three levels of familiarity, two levels of task, and six levels of task/familiarity), significant differences of p < 0.0001 were noted in seven of them. In the other four instances, the least significant difference was p < 0.0024.

## 6. Discussion

Results of this study indicate that managers both familiar and less familiar with computers expressed positive pre-test attitudes toward using computers. It was expected that managers experienced and familiar with computers would possess such attitudes; however, positive attitudes among those managers less familiar and experienced is quite encouraging. Although this study did not address the reasons for these attitudes among less familiar managers, several suggestions are offered and should be further investigated. Perhaps less familiar managers have formulated positive attitudes through media exposure, advertisements, social learning through peers, and exposure to computer-based applications existing in their organizational environments. It also is possible that managers less familiar with computers have accepted the strategic importance of computers and information systems, particularly as organizations continue to incorporate information technology.

The lack of a significant shift in attitudes among managers less familiar with computers after completing either the simple or complex task lends further support to the generally accepted theory that attitudes affect behavior. In this study, the task did not have an effect on attitudes. Nonetheless, organizations should endeavor to foster an environment for managers to maintain positive attitudes toward using computers. Attitude change is a long-term behavioral effect that may not be observable in a laboratory environment with the manipulation of one set of tasks. However, the results of this study suggest that exposure to complex tasks will not shift attitudes.

Designers concerned with user friendly features should not abandon their goal of reducing complexity. Systems that are poorly designed with undue complexity could generate negative attitudes toward computer systems, notwithstanding poor user satisfaction. Complex tasks also may affect productivity. This study did not directly address productivity issues; yet, computer-experienced managers finished the complex task significantly faster than managers less experienced with computers. The move toward integrated systems and employment of hypertext and other emerging technologies presents a challenge in controlling complexity. The issue of user friendliness transcends far more than satisfaction and attitudes, and gives rise to a deeper concern over the difficulty of using more complex systems to support decision making. Procedures, user manuals, commands, menus, and other user interface features will require careful analysis of task complexity and a thorough understanding of the impact on user attitudes. The positive, significant attitude changes toward user guides and other instructional aids by many less familiar managers indicates the importance of factors like these in the implementation and use of computer-based systems. This study shows that there is a reasonable attitudinal basis for managers with little computer experience to engage in utilizing computers for more complex tasks.

Organizations that have developed training programs for IS applications based on the premise that “one size fits all” may be wise to consider alternative training methods and frequency of training and/or retraining based on levels of managerial computer familiarity. The highly significant, negative attitude changes among all participants, regardless of experience or task assignment, reflected in the question concerned with enrolling in training, lends support for this. Such effects as experience and attitudes even may be more significant in cases where emerging technologies are being introduced. Continuing general computer education and specific systems training for managers appear to promise increased productivity especially for complex computer usage. Further research should be undertaken to determine the types of experience or training that may be necessary for managers to learn the general process of translating work tasks for computer support. Important productivity measures should be investigated in field settings, perhaps involving longitudinal studies. Variables such as frequency of system use, features used, and types of decision-support resources used should be correlated with experience as well as education. At issue is the boost in productivity that could be achieved by managers who are well-informed and trained computer users.

In this study, task complexity did not have an immediate and direct effect on attitudes regardless of the computer experience of the participants. The assumption that attitudes influence behavior should not be ignored by organizations that are introducing emerging technologies. The concept that managers having little experience with computers will be receptive to using computer technology is very promising. It is essential that organizations capitalize on the potential for managers with little or no computer familiarity to become productive by reinforcing positive attitudes through training and appropriate user interfaces.
