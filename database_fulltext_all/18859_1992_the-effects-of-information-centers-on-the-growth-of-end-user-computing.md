# The effects of information centers on the growth of end user computing \*

Emdad H. Khan \*\*

The University of Bahrain, Isa Town, Bahrain

The role of IC in the growth of EUC in several organizations in Bahrain is evaluated. Some of them established ICs to guide and support EUC, while others adopted a sink or swim EUC strategy. The ICs standardized on the hardware and software environment; provided training and technical support to end users. The organizations are yet to introduce control procedures to monitor EUC activities. The study revealed that the EUC activities did not appear to have substantially affected the strategic orientation of the business processes. However, the EUCs helped to improve user productivity, operational efficiency, local decision making capabilities, etc. We discuss some critical EUC management issues – such as resources procurement, application development by users, decentralized support services, and control through line management. These will enable the management to face the challenge of EUC, to fulfill, through EUC a portion of organizational computing needs and finally redesign the business process.

Keywords: Information center; End user computing, Information services organization; Management information systems

## 1. Introduction

The establishment and management of a properly organized information center (IC) to encourage and enhance end user computing (EUC) is important in meeting the computing needs of an organization. EUC is changing the role of information systems (IS) and business data processes in many organizations $[1,2,4]$ . The management of the IS departments must lead and guide this new environment of EUC technology, software development environments, and user department awareness of, and attitudes to, IS functions $[6,12,13]$ .

## 2. Framework and goals

This paper deals with the strategy for the organization and management of IC and PC-based EUC in selected organizations in Bahrain. The original goal was to gather information regarding corporate strategy for support, control, and coordination of EUC activities. We wished to assess the growth patterns, end-user training, user environment and applications. We also wished to determine the problems faced in developing support services and control procedures to implement the corporate EUC strategy.

Hammond [5] elaborates the structure and functions of an IC to support EUC within an evolving IS department. Leitheiser and Wetherbe [8] discuss four possible EUC strategies: (a) sink or swim: Do nothing – let the end user do it; (b) stick: Establish policies and procedures to control EUC so that corporate risks are minimized; (c) carrot: Create incentives to encourage practices that reduce organizational risks; (d) support: Develop services to aid end users in their computing activities. Rockart and Flannery [10] suggest a strategy for support of EUC in an organization: (a) establish a distributed organizational support structure; (b) provide a range of software tools; (c) develop a broad educational program; and (d) establish efficient procedures for locating and transferring data. To minimize the risks from EUC, they also recommend control procedures: (a) identify critical applications; (b) control through user line management and not the IS department; (c) use IS experts to assist end users in making computing decisions; and (d) develop standards for hardware and software and create incentives for following them.

Using the above framework for organizing EUC strategies, we investigated the effects of the following key issues:

(a) history of IC and EUC;

(b) IC strategy and organizational structure;

(c) EUC resource acquisition;

(d) standards for user hardware and software;

(e) user education and training;

(f) technical support structure;

(g) user applications and their impact on business;

(h) effect of environmental factors;

(i) evaluation and implications of IC and EUC activities.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Characteristics of the organizations

The author conducted some preliminary field work and identified nine, from a list of fifteen, firms which agreed to participate. After some investigation, we observed that only three public sector organizations met the requirements and were studied in-depth.

(a) PETRMA. An energy company responsible for petroleum extraction, refining, and marketing. Its computer services department (CSD) maintains an organized IC to support EUC.

(b) AIRGFB. A service organization that provides air transportation of passengers and cargo. The management services division (MSD) of the company has a subunit, the information services department (ISD) that supports the EUC activities.

(c) TELCOC. A utility company engaged in telecommunications. The management services division (MSD) of this company has recently created an IC unit to organize EUC activities.

The remaining six firms had only a handful of end users. Some of them adopted a sink or swim EUC strategy, where the end users would take the initiative and do everything. Some others followed a stick EUC strategy, where the users would undergo stringent control procedures before being allowed to engage in EUC activities.

Although the number of organizations studied was small, they represent about 20% of organizations using computer-based IS in the country. A sample of three is statistically insufficient to be used to prove any facts or propositions but is still indicative of the trends in our small country. We believe that the study can provide insight into the growth, organization, management and usage of EUC in a developing country.

## 3.2. Data gathering

The data were collected through personal interviews, based on a set of twelve structured questions. They reviewed the structure of IS and IC, corporate PC strategy and policy, procurement of hardware and software resources, user training, support, documentation, standardization, etc. Furthermore, open-ended discussion, which was confidential, was carried out with IC managers, analysts and programmers of IC who are assigned to support the end users. A cross-section of end users, contacted through IS management and distributed in functional departments in each organization, were also interviewed.

Because the survey covered only a small number of end users (a non-representative sample), contacted through IS management, the results obviously do not provide an accurate measure of the nature of EUC activities. No such interpretation is intended here, as the focus of the study is the view of the central service providers. Nevertheless, it is believed that the experience of IC implementation and management of EUC may be a useful lesson for other organizations who, like them, are making corporate decisions to introduce EUC through central services.

## 4. Observed facts

## 4.1. History IC and EUC

(a) PETRMA. In 1983, the process and technical department with the approval of management procured 3 PCs and software tools for on-line analysis of refinery process activities in place of the existing batch processing software of an IBM mainframe. With its success, the management started a six month pilot project to study the feasibility of introducing EUC; formed a PC committee, acquired 10 more PCs and offered PC awareness courses for managers and potential end users. As a corporate strategy, the IC was established in July 1984.

## 4.2. IC strategy and organizational structure

The structures of ICs and the full range of mandatory and optional user support services are shown in Table 1 and Tables 2 and 3, respectively.

(a) PETRMA. The IC is headed by a superintendent who is assisted by a set of analysts and programmers. The product evaluation and selection unit helps users to procure hardware and software tools. The user education and training unit provides training courses and documentation on software.

(b) AIRGFB. The ISD manager is assisted by a set of IC support staff. The product evaluation unit assists departments in analyzing user PC requirements and also surveys to find the company's total PC requirements. This unit also helps users to procure standardized PCs and supported software. It also co-ordinates the PC installation and maintenance by vendors.

The user services unit assists users to become familiar with PC operation and use of software packages. This unit is also responsible for providing training on software packages. The ISD is presently evaluating implementation of an office automation system.

(c) TELCOC. The IC structure is headed by a manager and assisted by one analyst and three analyst-programmers. The manager is responsible for coordinating the use of PCs in various departments. The analyst-programmers evaluate hardware and software, and provide training and support services for users. The IC is still developing end user support services.

## 4.3. EUC resources acquisition

(a) PETRMA. At the beginning of the year, each user department submits its PC and software requirements with justification of usages and potential benefits to the company. The PCs for all user departments are acquired and installed by IC in phases according to an agreed priority list. (b) AIRGFB. Until 1988, at the beginning of each year, the ISD co-ordinated the PC requirements, and centrally procured and distributed the PCs in phases via an allocation committee. There were long delays in procurement and installation of PCs which resulted in frustration and resentment of some departments for the centralized policy. After investigation and analysis, the policy was revised. From 1989, each department would directly procure and install its PCs but would need approval from ISD to ensure compatibility to standards.

## 4.4. Standards for user hardware and software

(a) PETRMA. Until 1986, the departments would acquire PCs of any make and configuration; this resulted in problems of data and software transfer between incompatible machines. After field trial and cost/performance analysis in 1986, the IC standardized on the ACER PC (IBM PC compatible Taiwanese clone). A few standard Ethernet-based LANs (Novell's NetWare) to allow line managers to monitor user jobs are being installed in selected departments. The IC is also planning to standardize and provide end users with organization-wide electronic mail and office automation systems. The IC is investigating the future use of IBM PS/2 machines.

After investigation of user requirements, and evaluation of available packages, the IC selected six software as standard products. As shown in Table 4, each user is provided with the first six in the list. User request for any other software is carefully evaluated; if considered essential, IC procures and installs the package.

(b) AIRGFB. Before standardization, user departments would buy PCs of any make and configuration. To ensure a high degree of reliability, the ISD standardized on IBM PCs. With increased user awareness and availability of support services, demand for PCs grew faster than the available budget. In 1987, the ISD made a comparative study of PC models and on a cost/performance basis standardized on low cost, ACER PC. But from 1990, with a decentralized PC procurement policy, the company changed the standard to the new IBM PS/2 machines. A token ring IBM PC network was in operation in one user department before the standard was set. The ISD standardized on an Ethernet-based LAN (Novell's NetWare) and a few of them are now being installed.

## 4.6. Technical support structure

(a) PETRMA. The technical support unit maintains a set of decentralized specialist programmers and analysts located in user departments. The specialists assist users in the analysis of problems and provide technical guidance in using software tools. They also provide assistance to users in data analysis, data transfer, file maintenance, back up, documentation, etc.

(b) AIRGFB. Two analysts and two programmers, located in the ISD, offer technical support to end users. On-line telephone-help is available to users for small problems. The technical support staff visits user departments on an appointment basis.

The support unit assists users in analyzing their problems and steers them to the appropriate packages. They also provide support for data analysis, report generation, back up, etc. Small-scale applications are developed by the users aided by the ISD. Applications requiring formal development procedures and several weeks of effort are dealt with by the ISD staff. ## 4.7. User applications and their impact on business

The types of applications performed by end users are estimated in Table 6.

(a) PETRMA. Most of the end users (about 90%) utilize a word-processing package in their day-to-day tasks. The second important application (over 50%) is based on using DBMS software. A number of small function-oriented local databases were created by IC specialists for some user departments. The data were provided by users from previously prepared reports, mainframe print-outs, etc. These end users belong to engineering, finance, accounting, refinery operation and maintenance, and general service departments. The applications include cost reporting, inventory monitoring, tracking library documents, hospital medical supplies, etc. In some cases, the users bypass the mainframe systems – they cite an unfriendly user interface, restricted access, difficult procedures for extracting data.

The third major application (about 50% of the users) is the use of spreadsheet software. The Lotus-1-2-3 package is utilized for simple engineering analysis, financial analysis, marketing and sales activities. The users are unwilling to indicate their frustration with the centrally provided analytical applications.

The managers, superintendents, and engineers use presentation graphics (about 20% of users) to present progress reports, periodic review reports, etc. A limited number of users also have access to mainframe corporate databases. Managers also employ project management software for planning and job scheduling. A few departments have recently added an electronic mail facility.

(b) AIRGFB. As in the first firm, a word-processing package is also used by almost all users (about 90%). The second important application (about 45%) is in analyzing data, using spreadsheet packages. These users are from the finance, marketing, sales, technical services, and planning departments. Sales forecasts, budget preparation, and engineering calculations are some of these applications. Although related mainframe application packages are available, some users feel more comfortable in using PC-based software.

The managers, to a limited extent, use the presentation graphics. The organization also allows some line users to access and download corporate data for manipulation.

(c) TELCOC. Like the other two firms, most of the end users (about 90%) utilize the word-processing software. The second major application (about 50%) is the data analysis function using a spreadsheet program, such as Lotus-1-2-3.

These users are from the commercial, operations, finance and engineering departments and perform simple engineering analysis, and financial calculations. The users claim that similar application packages are not available in mainframe systems and their local analytical use of data is complementary to the central applications.

The use of a DBMS is another important application (about 35%) for end users. The function-oriented local databases are used in place of the corporate databases to suit the user department requirements. Some applications are network route planning, tariff assessment, local inventory reports, on-going project status reports, etc. With the reorganization of IC, some IC staff will be provided for developing these local databases.

Some PC users (about 15%) also use graphics software for presenting financial, traffic, tariff and engineering data. Selected end users (about 7%) are also provided with access to the mainframe electronic mail facility and limited access to the corporate database.

## 5. Discussions

## 5.1. Effect of environmental factors

Procurement cost of software is negligible compared with that of hardware. In the absence of a copyright law, some software are obtained free (or only one copy is bought): some overseas vendors are therefore reluctant to market their products in the Middle East. Certain hardware peripherals, special equipment and spares are not easily available in the local market. It takes at least two months to get them from overseas suppliers.

## 5.2. Evaluation of ICs and implications of EUC activities

The three organizations established formal ICs to implement corporate EUC strategy, and provide training and support services, thereby allowing end users to gain and apply computing skills in their day-to-day jobs.

During the initial phase, the ICs encountered problems in establishing control of procurement and standardization of hardware and software. It took much effort and persuasion to motivate user departments to accept these new policies.

The growth rates of PC-based EUC (cf. Tables 7 and 8) were very high in the initial years, as expected (approximately 150–250%) and gradually settled down to around 30% and continued to decline. This measure was obtained from the growth rate of PCs and the number of employees trained. Rates of 50–90% per year were observed in similar studies [11]. Such initial high growth was due to carrot strategy and corporate policies of providing required fund to procure EUC resources.

Training is primarily conducted in-house by IC staff. One company utilizes the services of an external consultant while a second occasionally sends selected employees to outside institutes for specialized training.

For the studied firms we observed that the EUC activities did not appear to have substantially affected the strategic orientation of business data processing. The reported firms, however, achieved some intangible benefits from EUC activities such as increased user awareness of computing, improvement in user work methods, reduction in paper work, and improvement in operational efficiency, department level management control, and local decision making capabilities. In terms of tangible gain, which is difficult to measure, the IS departments claimed some reduction in application development backlogs. User departments with the help of IC take of care small-scale applications and some user departments reported success in cutting operating costs. The IC also had a positive effect on the relationship between user departments and the IS department.

In some cases, IS managements were concerned that EUC costs were rising too fast and were somewhat out of control as predicted by Nolan's growth stage model [9]. They used a carrot and stick EUC strategy to minimize excesses and failures while ensuring effective utilization of EUC resources. However, a section of the IS management also felt that though EUC control was important, the EUC activities had not yet reached sufficient maturity. They were yet to formulate and implement control policies. They were contemplating monitoring EUC activities through line management to maximize utilization and minimize risks.

## 6. Critical issues in end-user computing

In the light of this case study, we feel that the following important management issues must be addressed properly for the development and growth of EUC:

(a) EUC structure and environment. We found that out of nine firms only three had formal ICs to guide and support EUC activities. Some of them adopted a sink or swim EUC strategy and ignored the importance of EUC. But the trend towards end user computing is irreversible. We believe that an organization must establish an IC to provide a formal EUC structure within the IS department. The IC must provide leadership and guidance to user departments for the growth of EUC. The IC should be responsible for developing standards for hardware and software.

(b) EUC resource procurement. We found that certain firms maintained a successful centralized procurement policy, while others could not. In our opinion the organizations must set standards and procedures for procurement of EUC resources. The centralized procurement strategy could be implemented successfully through the IC with representation from user departments. Alternatively, the EUC resources could also be procured smoothly through user line management in co-ordination with the IC. In any case, it is important to avoid unnecessary delay through a complex set of procurement protocols.

(c) End user training. We think that a centralized training policy for software packages would work for most users. The selection of potential trainees should be performed by the user department. Training could be conducted either by the IC or the central training center as suitable for the organization. Each trainee should be provided with easy access to computing facilities and special courses be offered as required by user functional departments.

(d) Application development and technical support. Probably the IC will not know the complete requirements of different user departments. We propose that each user line manager, with the support of potential users, should prepare a list of useful applications that will help to reorient day-to-day business activities. A group of specialized analyst-programmers under the supervision of IC should be available to each department according to a predetermined schedule. This arrangement will enable maximal use of decentralized technical support within reasonable overhead charges.

(e) The role of IC and EUC control strategy. In each organization, the IC was facing a dilemma given its dual role of supporter and controller of EUC activities. As a supporter, it is charged with stimulating use of computing and as a controller to ensure proper utilization of EUC resources. But only the functional managers are in a position to evaluate the activities of end users. Hence we propose implementing EUC control procedures and monitoring resource utilization through line management. The IC should provide management education to line managers concerning their role and responsibilities for managing EUC.

## 7. Conclusions

The important consideration for establishing an EUC environment is that (if provided adequate access to PC and proper training, technical support, and software tools) end users would perform a part of the business data processing. Firms must assure continued organizational learning through an on-going, in-service training program for end users, thereby stimulating use of software tools in their day-to-day work. At the same time, they need to establish adequate control and measures to monitor EUC activities through line management; this will ensure effective utilization of EUC resources. We believe that, in the future, organizational IS activities and responsibilities will be radically restructured and reorganized so that EUC, through user line management, will play an important role in meeting a major part of business information requirements. IS management must prepare to face the challenge of managing the growth of EUC and finally redesign the business process.
