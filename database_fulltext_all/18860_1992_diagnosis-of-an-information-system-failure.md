# Diagnosis of an information system failure A framework and interpretive process

Gordon B. Davis, Allen S. Lee,
Kathryn R. Nickles, Sanjay Chatterjee,
Robert Hartung and Youlan Wu

University of Minnesota, Minneapolis MN, USA

In diagnosing an episode of information system application failure, the IS professional and others doing the analysis face two challenges: (1) relevant data must be identified, collected, and organized; and (2) the data must be analyzed and interpreted to form a coherent picture of the perspectives, actions, and events which resulted in the troubled or failed system. This article provides a diagnostic framework and interpretive process for performing a diagnosis. The premise underlying the diagnostic framework is that an information system is a social system has uses information technology. The social and technical dimensions in the diagnosis of an information system are represented in a two-dimensional framework. Once the data and comments about the failure have been organized in the framework, the process of interpretation follows procedures based on interpretive methods (hermeneutics). The framework and associated interpretive process assist those doing a diagnosis in applying two powerful bodies of knowledge to failure diagnosis – socio-technical systems and interpretive methods. The article describes the framework and interpretive process, explains the rationale for them, and demonstrates their use for a case situation.

Keywords: System failure; System evaluation; Socio-technical analysis; Hermeneutics

In an episode of IS application failure, the circumstances are typically marked by complexity and confusion. An IS professional or other analyst with an assignment to diagnose what happened faces a morass of data ranging from highly technical facts describing the objective features of the technical system to conflicting perceptions, opinions, and spontaneous remarks voiced by the human participants in the social system. The article provides support for the diagnosis: First, a framework for identifying and classifying data and, second, an interpretive process for analyzing the symptoms and piecing together a coherent picture of the events leading up to and producing the failure.

The basic premise underlying failure diagnosis is that an information system is a social system that uses information technology. This terminology was first used by Land and Hirschheim [16]. Diagnosis of an IS failure therefore may not be properly explained in terms of the information technology alone. There are always social-system elements and information-technology elements to be considered in IS design, development, and implementation. In the diagnosis of an IS failure, the social and technical dimensions are represented in a two-dimensional framework. One dimension represents four components of the social system, which are related to the processes of design, development, and implementation; the other represents four characteristics of the technical system. The two-dimensional framework (for identifying and classifying data) and the interpretive process (for rendering a coherent picture from the data) use ideas from a body of IS research on socio-technical systems and from organizational research in interpretive methods. The article provides an illustration of how the framework and process operate by using them to diagnose an actual episode of an IS failure.

The first section of the article provides a short description of the IS application being diagnosed, a chronology of events culminating in the system failure, and an enumeration of issues which motivated the diagnosis. The second section presents a detailed description of the framework for diagnosing IS failure. This description includes the rationale for basing the design of the framework on socio-technical systems concepts. The third section explains the interpretive process and its relationship to organizational research on interpretive methods. The fourth section demonstrates how we applied the framework and interpretive process in diagnosing the failure.

This article provides support for IS practice in diagnosis, analysis, and postmortems on failed systems. The proposed diagnostic framework and interpretive process have ramifications beyond these objectives for IS theory. They have ramifications for IS theory explaining why systems succeed, for prescriptions of remedial actions, and for procedures to be followed in IS development and implementation projects. To examine all of the ramifications would exceed the bounds of a single article. In this article, the goal is limited to introducing and describing the framework and interpretive process for diagnosing IS failure, giving the rationale for their design, and providing a demonstration of how they work.

Furthermore, whereas any investigation into a failure will have lessons for success, we must caution the reader not to regard a diagnosis to be the same thing as a prescription. The relationship between IS success and IS failure is not a straightforward one. Consider that the removal of a sufficient condition for failure (for instance, the presence of significant logic errors in a COBOL program) would itself be a necessary condition, but hardly a sufficient condition, for success (for instance, the effective use of the information system, which includes the debugged COBOL program, in supporting managerial decision-making). Lifting a constraint or a barrier is not the same thing as attaining a goal. A prescription for success would involve identifying not only ‘negatives' and the reactive strategies for remedying them, but also 'positives' and the additional proactive strategies for implementing them. In short, a diagnosis does not, by itself, constitute a prescription. This article addresses the first step (diagnosing the failure). The concluding section of the article will offer some speculations about how the proposed framework and interpretive process could make a contribution to the subsequent step (effecting success).

Limiting this presentation of the framework and interpretive process to failure diagnosis also offers several other advantages. First, “failure” is easier to define, identify, and agree upon than “success.” $^{1}$ A sufficient condition for indicating the occurrence of failure is that the organizational participants who have a stake in the information system (something to gain from the success of the information system or something to lose as a result of its failure) agree that the system is a failure. Second, a diagnosis of “what is,” even though a complicated matter in itself, is still more straightforward than a prescription of “what ought to be”. Finally, a single case study is more easily managed to provide a clear illustration of the salient concepts where the scope of the proposed framework and interpretive process is focused on failure, rather than expanded to illustrate aspects of both failure and success.

## 2. An episode of IS failure: The faculty activities system

## 2.1. A description of the system

The specific episode of an IS failure that is the case study for this article is the failure of the faculty activities system (FAS) which was proposed and championed by the office of the dean in the management school at a major university. Essentially a system for managing personnel data describing faculty employees, FAS was to replace an older, more limited manual system of intermittently collecting resumes and other data. As a transaction-level processing system, it was intended to collect, store, update and provide information on faculty members and their activities at the management school. The faculty activities database was to be available to the dean's office and to the respective departments for faculty promotion decisions, for tenure and salary review decisions, and for general reports and inquiries.

The groups affected by or initially interested in the FAS (the stakeholders in the information system) were the following (with number in each group):

\- the dean (1);

• the faculty (107);

\- the faculty appointments and promotions committee (7);

• the department chairs (7);

• the department administrators (7);

\- the administrative staff in the dean's office (approximately 4);

\- the developer on staff in the dean's office (1).

The person initiating the system and providing continuing support (the system champion) was the dean. The only stakeholders with continuous involvement were the champion and the developer. An administrator on the dean's administrative staff apparently had some significant input in the early stages. The department chairs (executive committee) had a nominal involvement when the system proposal was reported to them; however, there was no real participation from this group.

The information requirements and input/output forms were defined solely by the dean, the application developer and the dean's office administrator. The application developer, a member of the dean's office staff, completed the system design and did the programming.

FAS was a networked microcomputer application. It used a database application development system which allowed it to be run on top of dBase III on the PC network in the dean's office. Other software tools were employed to improve the efficiency of development and documentation including a database dictionary management program, utility programs, and a relational report writer for multifile, custom or ad hoc reporting.

Twelve database files were created, each pertaining to a specific domain of faculty activity. The twelve domains were: Articles published; books/chapters published; consulting, external; doctoral student activity; faculty demographics; grants activity; honors and awards; other publications; research publications; administrative service activities; professional service activities; and working papers.

## 2.2. Chronology of events culminating in the system failure

FAS was proposed by the dean in the spring of 1987. The in-house development of the system took place during the next year. The design of the input forms was completed by late April, 1988. Faculty members submitted data on the input forms the following month. These forms (thirteen or more unnumbered pages) required data on virtually all items associated with each of the twelve database files mentioned above. The layout of the forms was cumbersome and caused dissatisfaction as evidenced by faculty comments.

Various support functions for the system were developed during the following summer. The complete system was to be available in October, 1988, for data entry. At this stage, the only report that the system was able to generate on a routine basis was the “activities summary report” listing activities for all faculty. Individual detailed reports were available on an ad hoc basis but with some difficulty.

The information from the first round of data collection in April, 1988, was unused until March, 1989, when faculty members were sent request forms for updates. These request forms were turnaround forms in which many of the fields were already filled out by the system with data stored in the database. Faculty were to make corrections and add data. It was during this second phase that the impact of earlier data entry form problems became apparent. Many faculty users expressed dissatisfaction with numerous errors in the turnaround data. The obviously high error rate in the database was cited by faculty and staff as a reason for their lack of enthusiasm for the system. Some faculty members turned in their forms late (or not at all).

Six months later, in the fall of 1989, turnaround outputs were again returned to faculty members for verification and updating. Once again, there were severe problems with data accuracy and there was evidence of increased user resistance such as refusal to fill out the forms or angry comments to staff who collected them. There were virtually no useful outputs. The faculty perception was that they were doing a lot of work with no benefit. It was at this point that the dean labeled FAS a “failure” and the project was put on hold. Both the system champion and the system developer focused on technical problems such as program logic errors or lack of input error detection capabilities as the reasons for the failure.

## 2.3. Issues motivating a diagnosis

The FAS failure raises a number of puzzling issues for which there are no immediate or obvious answers. In fact, on the surface, there are decisions, actions, and outcomes that do not make sense.

(1) FAS harbored numerous, serious deficiencies in its technical design. As at many business schools, the management school has IS faculty who teach courses and conduct research in the area of technical design (including design for error control). Some of the faculty are world-renowned for their expertise in matters of technical design. What is the explanation for technical failures when the required expertise was available and abundant?

(2) Behavioral and motivational factors and not just technical factors played a major role in the FAS failure. The school has a worldwide reputation for its research focus on behavioral factors in IS. How could behavioral problems have contributed so strongly to the FAS failure in an environment so knowledgeable relative to ways of avoiding them?

(3) The FAS failure occurred in a school of management. The process was managed ineffectively. Also contributing to the failure of the system were financial and human resource factors. All of these are specialized areas of study within management. How could these factors have been ignored in an environment rich with expertise?

(4) FAS was a technologically undemanding, transaction-level processing system. Its functions were restricted to input, storage, and retrieval of data. The most complicated operation called for data to be aggregated and reported in certain categories. FAS contained no statistical or management science model and the information technology needed to build FAS had been available for decades. Given the undemanding and elementary nature of its technological requirements (relative to general levels of expertise in system design), how could the system have failed in its technical performance?

(5) All of the participants involved in the FAS failure were properly characterized as intelligent, motivated, and rational. There were no villains. They all seemed to know what they were doing. How could they have permitted the process of IS development to proceed as problematically as it did and for as long as it did?

These five issues raised by the failure of FAS present themselves as puzzles, anomalies, or apparent absurdities in our reading of the situation. They motivated our effort to diagnose the conditions and circumstances that transformed the apparent earnest intentions of bright and capable individuals into actions culminating in failure. To explain why and how this happened was the purpose of the failure diagnosis.

These issues and the underlying problems they suggest are not unique to this case. They have their counterparts in IS failures that occur in all applied, organizational settings. Using the proposed diagnostic framework and interpretive process, our examination of the FAS failure exemplifies how an IS professional or other analyst might perform a similar diagnosis in his or her own organization.

## 3. The framework for diagnosing a system failure

A person with the assignment to perform a diagnosis of a failing or failed IS application typically encounters a large quantity of data and perceptions about the situation and experiences significant problems in making sense of those data. The data may range from highly technical facts describing the objective features of the technical system to conflicting perceptions, opinions, and spontaneous remarks voiced by the system stakeholders and other participants in the social system. In this section, a description of a diagnostic framework for assembling and classifying data about the failed system is presented.

## 3.1. The two dimensions of the framework

The two dimensions of the framework are based on the fundamental nature of an information system. As already noted, an information system is a social system that uses information technology. There are always social system and information technology elements to be considered in IS design and implementation. The same information technology can be a success in some organizations but a failure in others. The resulting IS success or failure, therefore, cannot be explained in terms of the information technology alone. The same organization can experience success in using some information technologies but failure in using others, so IS success or failure cannot be explained in terms of the social system alone. The same information technology will exhibit different properties within different organizations just as the same organization will exhibit different properties using different information technologies. Therefore, a proper understanding of the events leading to an IS success or an IS failure requires examination of the social system and the technical system together, not separately.

The social and technical dimensions in the framework are supported by a body of theory, research, and practice referred to by the term “socio-technical” [7,33,34,5]. Socio-technical theory predicts that optimizing only the technical system or optimizing only the social system will lead to infeasibility for the overall system. For optimization or even just feasibility of the overall system, the social-system component and technical-system component need to be designed and managed jointly in a coordinated effort. This concept has been studied in settings such as coal mine mechanization; it has also been examined for information system design. (For readable overviews of socio-technical theory relative to information systems, [4,25,24]).

In understanding the difference between the social and technical dimensions individually versus their interaction, it may be useful to use an analogy to the difference between mixtures and compounds in chemistry. To understand the properties of a mixture, a person only needs to examine the respective properties of the individual elements that constitute the mixture because elements in a mixture do not react to one another. Their respective properties remain intact. However, the properties of a chemical compound are different from the respective properties of the constituent elements because the elements react to one another. In this analogy, an information system is more like a compound than a mixture. Its constituent social system and technical system react to one another so that their properties in combination are different from their properties in isolation. A proper understanding requires that the two systems be studied together. $^{2}$

Fig. 1. Two dimensional matrix.

The framework for diagnosing a systems failure gives explicit recognition not only to the social system and the technical system but also to their interaction. The framework takes the form of a two-dimensional matrix (Figure 1). One dimension refers to four characteristics of the social system and the other refers to four characteristics of the IS application (the technical system). The resulting matrix suggests sixteen areas of potentially useful data for a failure diagnosis. It also presents a classification scheme for organizing not only the data that the analyst deliberately seeks out through interviews and questionnaires, but also the data, such as opinions and remarks about the system, that the analyst happens to come across in the natural course of daily events.

The logic underlying the diagnostic framework is that the characteristics of the IS application (the technical system) are the result of social-system processes in which key persons make and implement decisions pertaining to IS design, development, and implementation. Their decisions and actions reflect their ideas and beliefs about systems, people, and organizations (their theories-in-use). These decisions and actions eventually lead to performance indicators and human reactions which reflect how the organization evaluates the resulting IS application. The social-system dimension therefore supports a diagnosis which traces evidence of failure, seen through the performance indicators and human reactions, back to the deficiencies in the design and development processes and then to the theories-in-use responsible for leading to these deficiencies. The social-system dimension intersects with the four IS application characteristics of the technical-system dimension – two operational characteristics and two functional characteristics. An outcome of the diagnosis is the identification of deficiencies in the social system's design and development processes associated with each of the key application characteristics. The diagnosis may include reasons why each of these deficiencies occurred for each characteristic. These diagnostic results can eventually be used as the basis for prescriptions of organizational interventions in the redesign of the failed application (if desired), for improved design methodologies, and for changes in the requirements determination processes in which stakeholders (users and others) and designers develop the application's specifications.

## 3.2. The social system dimension

The social system refers not only to the organizational participants, but also to the features of the organizational setting which endure even as different generations of people enter, pass through, and leave the organization. The social system provides the structure through which the people are organized and through which they operate. The social-system dimension of the framework also recognizes the subjective roots of the phenomenon of IS failure. Failure is a notion arising from the social construction of reality – reality as it is continually created, sustained, modified, and experienced by the human subjects who are the participants in the social system. The four components of the social system in the framework were selected because of their relevance to the diagnosis. They categorize data describing the deficiencies as perceived, measured, or otherwise sensed by the social system; data describing the development processes selected and implemented by the social system; and data describing the theories-in-use by key participants that produced the process choices. These components are described and then illustrated by reference to the FAS application.

\- Reactions to the technical system. This component refers to the reactions of people to the technical system. The reactions may be verbal ("I'm not going to read this stuff", "FAS input forms are just like the tax forms") or they may be behavioral, such as the refusal by certain stakeholders to use the technical system. They tend to be emotional and may be quite global.

With respect to FAS, an example of a significant reaction was the conclusion reached by the system champion that the system (as an application of technology) was a failure.

\- Indicators of performance. There are specific instances or measurements of system inadequacy or failure. A longer and more descriptive label for this component might be “indicators of technical system performance meaningful to the social system”. The social system itself is a mechanism through which assessments of the technical system’s performance naturally occur. In fact, technical performance is only meaningful in connection with the needs and requirements of the participants in the social system who use the technical system. For example, the same technical system in two different organizations might give identical performance in terms of computational time but perform differently in terms of meeting the two organizations’ diverse needs. In diagnosing an IS failure, the IS professional should obtain facts and perceptions on the extent to which the technical system contributes or fails to contribute to the goals of the social system and the extent to which the technical system satisfies or respects the constraints faced by the social system.

With respect to FAS, examples of specific performance facts are the generation of reports that the users deemed inadequate or useless, the existence of response times that exceeded the patience of the users, and the occurrence of output errors that the users considered intolerable.

\- Development processes. This component refers to the processes and activities through which the technical system is designed, developed and implemented. Facts and perceptions in this category may describe shortcomings in the IS development processes or the absence of desirable development process activities.

Examples of facts that fall in this category in the case of FAS are the noninvolvement or non-participation of key stakeholders in the development process and the lack of an appropriate requirements determination process. Examples of perceptions or comments that the development processes were inadequate include statements by an end user: “What are its purposes? Who’s it for?”

\- Theories-in-use. This component refers to ideas, objectives, and beliefs held by those involved in building the technical system. Examples are beliefs about the nature and role of technology, the nature and role of management, the motivation of humans, perquisites of authority, and use of power. These are underlying reasons why the development processes and the social system and technical system design alternatives are selected. For the system builders, these beliefs initiate, set into motion, or otherwise structure the events and dynamics of IS development. They may need to be inferred or derived from observed actions. These beliefs, ideas, and agendas are described as “theories-in-use”. This terminology helps to differentiate between what people believe (theories-in-use), which may be inferred from how they act, and what they say they believe (espoused theories). $^{3}$ Theories-in-use are often unspoken, obscured, or even camouflaged and may be incorporated in hidden agendas. If failure diagnosis is to be followed by revision or redoing of the application, the first actions should be directed at changing theories-in-use that lead to the failure.

To further explain the concept of theories-in-use, a theory-in-use held by many system builders is that a technical system and a social system may be understood and treated separately. As a second example, workers in an organization sometimes hold the belief that the introduction of a new technology will deskill their jobs or otherwise encroach upon their discretion and responsibility. These theories-in-use motivate their actions.

With respect to FAS, an example of a perception from which a theory-in-use can be inferred would be the statement that “the [sponsor for the system] did not understand the need for organizational commitment”. This suggests a leadership theory-in-use by the sponsor that gives the leader the responsibility to specify the characteristics of an organizational system that other people in the organization then have the obligation to accept.

The four components of the social system dimension guide and assist the IS professional in tracing through the chain of events that result in the system's being labeled a failure. The social system dimension's first component, consisting of stakeholders' reaction to the information system (the attribution of failure), is shaped by the second component: The system's poor performance in contributing to the goals or satisfying the constraints of the social system. This second component is given form by the third component: The processes and activities through which the system builders designed, developed and implemented the technical system. The third component follows from the fourth component: The theories-in-use held by the system sponsors and builders about the technical system, the social system, and the relationship between them.

The columns of the matrix may also be used to explain why a system failed by starting from the fourth component. The theories-in-use held by sponsors and builders (stakeholder objectives; ideas about systems, people, and organizations; and hidden agendas) led the development processes to be structured and managed in a certain way. Next, the development and implementation processes led to a technical system with certain performance attributes. The performance elicited reactions of support or rejection for the application.

The design of the social-system dimension deliberately emphasizes the role played by the activities and beliefs of the system sponsors and builders (in the dimension's third and fourth components). The need for the emphasis is strengthened by the fact that, in the special case where the socio-technical system is an information system, the technical system is typically an intervention introduced into an already existing social system, i.e., an organization [4, p. 18]. The success or failure of the intervention depends on the actions and theories-in-use of the individuals who carry out the intervention.

## 3.3. The technical system dimension

The technical system dimension of the two-dimensional matrix also consists of four components or characteristics of the failed application. The facts and perceptions, already classified according to the different components in the social-system dimension, may be subclassified according to the different components of the technical system. The four IS application characteristics classify both operational and functional characteristics. The operational characteristics pertain to the technology (efficiency of the hardware/software, friendliness of the technology's user interfaces) and the functional characteristics relate to the expected functionality of the application relative to task-specific requirements as well as to general culture and behaviors (termed organizational fit). Serious deficiencies on any of these four system characteristics will result in difficulties or outright failures for the application.
• Technology. This component refers to hardware, software, and other related technology. Reactions to the technology often reflect whether or not the computer-based system ‘works’ and, if it does, whether it is ‘up’ or ‘down’ when a person needs to use it. Performance facts and perceptions relevant to this component pertain to the physical or machine aspects of the technical system (operation, response time, and capacity). Development processes pertaining to this component relate to technical design portions of the development cycle (database design, programming, etc.). Theories-in-use relevant to this component may reflect beliefs about information technology (for instance, that an information system is the computer itself).

\- User interfaces. This component refers to the points of contact between the human user and the software/hardware technology (input screens, output screens, paper input forms, and paper output reports). Facts describing human reactions at this level may refer to unfriendliness in user interfaces. Perceptions may describe the ease or difficulty with which different people (the operators of the technical system, the providers of input, and the recipients of outputs) use the technical system. Indicators of performance pertaining to user interfaces may refer to error rates in transferring data from one medium (paper forms) to another (secondary storage) and to the learning time that users require to become familiar with the system. Development processes pertaining to user interfaces may focus on activities, or lack of activities, to design and build the interfaces. Theories-in-use about user interfaces may reflect ideas about humans as information processors.

\- Information requirements. This component is task specific. It refers to the capacity of the new computerized system to support users not only in obtaining answers to the same questions and performing the same tasks as they did with the system being replaced, but also in giving new capabilities that were promised for it. Reactions to the information requirements component would refer to how the users themselves respond to the system capabilities. Perceptions and facts related to this component refer to the information made available by the system, failure in terms of data availability, or shortcomings in the process by which the information requirements were determined. Overall, this component reflects the extent to which the system meets the information requirements.

\- Organizational fit. This component refers to general functionality and the extent to which the technical system fits the requirements imposed by the organization's politics and culture. In an instructive case study, Markus [21] reports how in one corporation the users reacted by forcefully resisting an information system that disrupted the organizational balance of power. The failure to involve key stakeholders in the development process can lead to lack of organizational fit in the sense of lack of commitment and sometimes to active resistance to the system.

## 4. The process for interpreting data in the framework $^{4}$

Once organized into the sixteen cells of the two-dimensional matrix, the data need to be woven into a coherent account of what happened.

The support for an IS professional in diagnosing an IS application failure consists of not only the two-dimensional matrix for identifying and classifying data, but also the procedures for interpreting the data. These procedures and guidelines are based on “interpretive methods” as described in organizational research.

## 4.1. Background on interpretive methods

Many interpretive methods described in organizational research come from the disciplines of sociology and anthropology. The interpretive approach described here is, however, based on hermeneutics, which frames interpretation in a particularly useful way for failure diagnosis. It addresses the problem of anomalies or absurdities perceived in a situation.

The origins of hermeneutics are in textual analysis of ancient documents, but the principles derived from this activity have broad applicability. The hermeneutic discipline takes the position that the activity of interpretation is not necessarily mysterious or esoteric but is already practiced by people in everyday settings $[3]$ . Examples of how people in everyday situations practice interpretation on a regular and routine basis include how people read a text and how people come to understand one another in their everyday interactions.

When we read a text, we interpret the meanings intended and expressed by the author. In the same way, when we ‘read’ another person’s behavior (either physical or verbal), we interpret meanings intended and expressed by that person. The novelty of hermeneutics resides in its framing of people, the behaviors they render, and the organizations they create, as ‘text’ to be ‘read’. Performing the ‘reading’ are people who themselves may be involved as organizational participants.

The useful application of hermeneutics to IS problems has already been noted by researchers in the IS discipline. Boland, for instance, has conducted his own research with the help of concepts from Hans Gadamer, a contemporary authority on hermeneutics: “The world must be interpreted by us in order for our intentional action to be possible. This is a universal hermeneutic problem, fundamental to our everyday life” [3, p. 195]. Winograd and Flores echo this theme in their book, Understanding Computers and Cognition: A New Foundation for Design: "Just as we can ask how interpretation plays a part in a person's interaction with a text, we can examine its role in our understanding of the world as a whole" [35, p. 30].

How does a hermeneutical interpretation proceed? Kuhn, perhaps best known for his book, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, has articulated the important role of “apparent absurdities” in motivating a hermeneutic interpretation. $^{5}$ Whenever we encounter an anomaly or an “apparent absurdity” in a text that we are reading, we have a choice. We may draw the conclusion that the author thinks in a way that he or she would consider absurd, or we may draw the conclusion that we ourselves have not yet succeeded in reading the meaning that the author expresses in the text. When doing a hermeneutical interpretation, we reach the latter conclusion. In a sense the basic principle in a hermeneutical interpretation of a text is that the author knows his or her subject. Likewise, the basic principle in a hermeneutical interpretation of human behavior in a situation such as an IS failure is that people think they know what they are doing. That is, people behave in ways they think are rational responses to the circumstances of their situation. $^{6}$

4.2. Application of interpretive methods in diagnosis of IS failure

The basic interpretive principle has significant ramifications for the diagnosis of an IS failure. It tells the IS professional or other analyst who engages in failure diagnosis to avoid jumping to a conclusion that any observed behaviors are absurd, irrational incompetent, self-defeating, or deceitful. For example, the behavior which IS researchers label sometimes pejoratively as “user resistance” may actually be a rational response by rational employees to an otherwise dysfunctional situation. In fact, interpretation is constructively motivated and initiated by the observer’s taking notice of apparent anomalies, absurdities, and irrationalities. In the case of the FAS failure, some motivating anomalies and irrationalities were found in the five issues presented earlier.

One difficulty in performing interpretation is that the same behavior can have different meanings and play different roles in different IS failures in different organizations. How then may the IS professional or other analyst interpret the meaning of a particular behavior in a particular IS failure in a particular organization? The complexity of organizations and the variety of motives that can be ascribed to the people involved in a situation make interpretation a difficult task.

A fundamental question is how to understand the underlying causes of failure rather than surface causes. Anomalies and seemingly irrational behavior may obscure deeper, more important causes of failure. The proposed interpretive process for discovering underlying motivations and causes makes use of a method or methodological device called the “hermeneutical circle”.

Since the concept of the hermeneutical circle began with the discipline of interpreting ancient texts, it will be explained first in that historical context before applying it to modern-day diagnostic interpretation. $^{7}$ Note that a characteristic of ancient texts is that a straightforward or literal translation would result in a text with passages that are difficult to understand or absurd. The same may be said of a straightforward description of a system failure.

In applying the concept of a hermeneutical circle when reading an ancient text, the reader seeks an alternative meaning for an apparently absurd passage in the text by relating or cross-referencing it to other passages that the reader has previously accepted as making sense. The clarification or other change in the interpretation of the problematic passage typically produces changes in the reader's interpretations of other passages, some of which may have previously seemed unrelated to the particular passage under scrutiny. The hermeneutical circle refers to the network of interrelationships existing among all the passages through which their respective meanings are bound together. In making successive passes through the circle, the reader reaches a new meaning for the passage in question. Ultimately, the reader reaches a new meaning with which to interpret all the passages in the given text.

The same logic applies to ‘text’ that is presented in the form of people, the behaviors they render, and the organizations they create. Some hermeneutic authorities, such as Ricoeur [28], take the position that the entire social world is literally (not metaphorically) a text to be read. In this article, we need not take a position on whether people, behaviors, and organizations literally constitute a text. The reason is that, strictly speaking, we are not interpreting people, behaviors, and organizations, but we are interpreting a representation of them – a two-dimensional matrix containing written words. We may safely regard this representation as literally constituting a text to be read, interpreted, and understood. (Indeed, we feel that all data analysis, whether quantitative or qualitative, necessitates reading and interpretation.) We regard the two-dimensional matrix as not necessarily the observed behaviors themselves, but instead a textual ‘snapshot’ of the behaviors. It is a text that offers a description, covering a specified duration, which requires interpretation.

The concept of a hermeneutical circle would apply to the interpretation of behavior, as represented in the matrix, in the following way. An observer (a reader of the text in the matrix) would seek an alternative meaning for any apparently irrational behavior by relating or cross-referencing it to other behaviors (in other cells of the matrix) that the observer previously accepted as making sense. A clarification or change in the interpretation of the apparently irrational behavior would typically produce changes in the observer's interpretations of yet other behaviors, some of which may have seemed previously unrelated to this particular behavior. In this situation, the hermeneutical circle would be the network of interrelationships existing among all the behaviors through which their respective meanings are bound together. In making successive passes through the circle, the observer would reach a new meaning for the behavior in question. Ultimately, the observer would reach a new meaning with which to interpret all the behaviors appearing in the organization, as represented in the two-dimensional matrix. At least in principle, the process of revision and improvement could continue forever [32, p. 28].

In an IS failure diagnosis, the hermeneutical circle may turn the observer's attention to certain aspects of the IS failure that earlier were ignored. These aspects may be ‘missing pieces’ in the puzzle which the IS failure presents. Facts and perceptions already observed and recorded as data in the matrix may emerge to hold new significance. A new round of observations might even be needed to collect additional data. In this sense, interpretation guides observation, just as observation guides interpretation. In an IS failure diagnosis, the hermeneutical circle ties together the data – the facts and perceptions – that have been recorded throughout the sixteen different cells of the two-dimensional matrix.

An important, additional point is that the observer may choose to extend the hermeneutical circle to the context outside of the immediate “text”. For instance, a person who is trying to interpret a Biblical passage could take advantage of archaeological knowledge about the ancient middle east. Likewise, a manager who is trying to understand the behaviors of systems analysts in a corporate headquarters in Boston could take advantage of knowledge about the systems profession in the United States and knowledge about the surrounding economic conditions in New England. The result of extending the hermeneutical circle to the context outside of the immediate ‘text’ would be access to additional ‘passages’ on which to draw for clues when deciphering the meaning of the existing passages which appear puzzling.

In the FAS case, an example involving the hermeneutical circle follows from noting the apparent anomaly in which the system builders did not involve key stakeholders, such as the faculty experts in technical design, in the development process. This fact is recorded in cell C1, $^{8}$ which lies at the intersection of the development processes component of the social system dimension and the technology component of the technical system dimension. Initially, this behavior may appear absurd or irrational to us. However, the hermeneutical circle requires that we relate this behavior to other behaviors, the facts and perceptions of which are classified in the remaining fifteen cells of the matrix. Viewed in this light, the system builders' noninvolvement of key stakeholders emerges as sensible for them, considering their theories-in-use (the fourth component of the social system dimension). These include a theory-in-use which views the role of management as responsible for design decision (an "only-top-management-as-user" theory-in-use), as recorded in cell D3. Such a view leads naturally to the exclusion of other stakeholders. The interpretation, therefore, focuses attention on the consequences of the theory-in-use.

One issue is how to assess the quality of an interpretation of human behavior. A ‘good’ interpretation resolves any apparent anomaly or irrationality. A ‘good’ interpretation, however, need not be (and, in fact, cannot be) final and conclusive because, at least in principle, improvements in the interpretation will always be pursuable.

## 5. A demonstration of the framework and interpretive process

Applying the diagnostic framework and interpretive process may be performed through six steps: (1) collecting the data; (2) classifying the data; (3) noting any apparent anomalies or absurdities in our reading of the classified data; (4) surfacing the interpretation; (5) revising the interpretation; and (6) repeating steps (3), (4), and (5) as needed. The following is a detailed account of how we applied the six steps in diagnosing the IS application failure in the case of FAS.

Step 1. Collecting the data on the FAS failure. Research on the faculty activities system failure originated as a field-work project in a doctoral seminar during fall quarter, 1989, in which the

FAS failure was examined. Members of the research group included the professor who led the seminar, four Ph.D. students who were joined in the interviewing phase by a visiting scholar in public affairs, and a visiting faculty member who joined the research team during the data analysis phase.

Seminar students conducted open-ended interviews and collected written and verbal comments about FAS from a variety of organizational participants during the period from September through December, 1989. The participants who were interviewed included the dean, associate dean, system developer, data entry person, dean's office personnel, department administrators, and faculty members.

Researchers obtained memoranda regarding the implementation of FAS. Examples of request input forms (original and updated) were examined. Written comments about the system were collected from faculty members and department administrators. In one instance, researchers gained access to written comments regarding FAS which had been solicited and collected by an administrator for her own use. One of the researchers attended an administrators' meeting in which FAS was a main topic of discussion.

Step 2. Classifying the FAS data. Facts and perceptions were classified into the sixteen cells of the two-dimensional diagnostic matrix. All cells were rich in data. The completed matrix appears in Appendix A. The first two columns in the matrix (“Reactions to the technical system” and “Indicators of performance”) contain facts and perceptions which clearly indicate that the FAS stakeholders themselves considered FAS to be a failure.

Step 3. Noting any apparent anomalies in our reading of the classified data. The FAS failure raises five issues – issues that present themselves as puzzles, anomalies, or apparent absurdities in our reading of the data in the completed matrix. These anomalies motivate the subsequent steps. We direct the reader to Section 2.3 for a review of them.

Step 4. Surfacing the interpretation. In a diagnosis of the FAS failure, the five apparent anomalies motivated us to surface the assumptions implicit in our first reading of the data in the matrix. The anomalies were considered as such because of our assumptions about normal and rational behavior. This step in the interpretive process involved examining the relationship between these assumptions and our labeling of behavior as not sensible. After some reflection, we realized that we had unwittingly, unquestioningly, and uncritically taken a technological-imperative view. This was unsurprising, since this view is widespread. To examine the satisfactoriness of the technological imperative view, we made our assumptions underlying it explicit.

(1) The first assumption in the technological-imperative view is that technology is an exogenous force that has the power to drive or determine the behaviors of individuals and organizations so that the given technology is accepted, implemented, and utilized [22, p. 585]. Here, technology refers to all forms of “know how”, expertise, or other forms of support available to workers for making decisions and solving problems in their organizational life. In addition to a computer-based information system, technology may also take the form of “know how” or formal knowledge in fields such as organizational behavior, management, finance, personnel administration, and IS. Expectations which follow from this general assumption are that managers will put into practice what they learned in their educational programs, managers will accept and use new technologies that are introduced into the workplace, and, when lacking required expertise, managers will procure the help of someone who has it.

(2) The second assumption of the technological imperative is that organizations and people operate and behave like machines. Under this assumption, organizations are structured and governed hierarchically while people compliantly carry out the orders and commands they receive, making efficient and effective use of all information and all technologies available to them. Indeed, organizational subunits and even organizational participants are imagined to function like modules in a structured computer program. As long as they are properly programmed and controlled, they are expected to perform in predictable, machine-like ways.

In the technological-imperative view as the basis for an interpretation of rational behavior, people and organizations adapt themselves to the requirements of technology. In this view there is little or no need to adapt technology to the requirements of people or organizations. Normal and rational behavior, at least according to a theory-in-use based on the technological imperative, would have included efforts by the school of management's faculty to apply their own areas of expertise to the problems that FAS presented, efforts by the dean's office to solicit the faculty's expertise, and efforts by everyone to join in designing, developing, implementing, accepting, and using the faculty activities system. These actions did not occur. In fact, observed behaviors were to the contrary. The technological-imperative view as a basis for rational behavior resulted in a number of anomalies: It did not reduce the anomalies. Therefore, we rejected the technological imperative for our use in diagnosing the reasons the participants behaved as they did in bringing about the failure and we sought a better interpretation.

Step 5. Revising the interpretation. In the FAS 'text', we decided to give a different reading to the 'passage' describing how the stakeholders – in particular, the system builders – themselves had made sense of the events in the FAS case. The system builders were the dean (who was also the system champion) and the developer (who was responsible for the IS design, development, and implementation).

In reviewing our interpretation, we focused on inferring the participants' actual theories-in-use. There is a good reason for focusing on the theories-in-use held by the system builders. As stated earlier, in the case of a socio-technical system that happens to be an information system, the technical system is typically an intervention introduced into an already existing social system. The success or failure of the intervention turns on the actions and theories-in-use of the individuals who carry out the intervention. Therefore, it is critical to infer the system builders' theories-in-use, that is, their beliefs about the behavior or properties of the technical system, the social system, and the relationship between them.

Although we rejected the technological imperative for use by ourselves, we entertained the possibility that the technological imperative was in use by the people whom we were observing. Indeed, if we were to view the system builders themselves as operating under the assumptions of the technological imperative, their behaviors would appear rational. Three examples or scenarios explain the effect of the assumptions of the technological imperative and how they are consistent with the observed behaviors.

(1) The scenario in which the system builders' theory-in-use maintains (following the technological imperative) that there is no need to adapt the technology to the requirements of the people or the organization. According to this theory-in-use, everyone will adapt to the requirements of the technology. In this scenario, the following behaviors by the system builders would therefore appear sensible, at least to themselves:

\- Seeking no advice, involvement, or participation from any other people (cells C2, C3, C4, D1–D4); and

\- Allocating little or no resources (time, effort, and money) to processes for developing FAS to be responsive to people-factors or to specific information needs (cells C1 through C4).

(2) The scenario in which the system builders' theory-in-use presumes that organizational subunits and organizational participants will carry out orders and commands compliantly and unquestioningly in a machine-like manner. According to this theory-in-use, there are no interactive effects between the technical system and the social system. In this scenario, the following behaviors by the system builders would make sense:

\- Planning for no behavioral or political consequences from introducing the information technology and not responding to these consequences even as they materialized during the attempted implementation of the system (cells A1–A4, B4, C4 and D4); and

\- Soliciting no collaboration from the faculty regarding the new, centralized procedures that FAS necessitated (cell D4).

(3) The scenario in which normal and rational behavior, according to the technological imperative, calls for the system builders to devote their pro-active efforts primarily to the information technology. This scenario well describes the system builders' behaviors (columns C and D). This scenario explains why the system builders placed an emphasis on exhaustive data, a technological consideration, while making no particular effort to determine the specific information requirements of the organization, a social consideration (cell C3). It also explains why the system builders, upon feedback in the form of poor system performance (cells B1 and B2), responded by devoting even more effort to the information technology. Given their theory-in-use, it made sense for them to attempt a technological fix and not to respond to the social dimensions of the poor system performance. The scenario is one in which information technology has the power to shape people's thinking and behavior so that the people will eventually and inevitably come to appreciate and utilize a computer system with exhaustive data capabilities.

Given the above analysis, it was possible to use the logical structure of the two-dimensional framework for tying the data together in a revised interpretation.

First, we traced through the logic relating the components of the social-system dimension. The analysis relating these components considers one row at a time in order to avoid interaction with more than one technical system perspective (controlling for the technical-system dimension). Analysis of the second row in the matrix (user interfaces) illustrates the logic of the analysis.

\- The acerbic and cynical comments in people's reactions to the interfaces of the technical system (in cell A2: “Why not use a readable [input] form?”; “I’m not going to read this stuff”; “Mine is totally screwed up and I threw it away”) were rational and proper responses to the poor indicators of performance.

\- The poor indicators of performance for the interfaces of the technical system (in cell B2: Initial output coming back to the faculty months after initial collection; screens and input forms not matching; printing time for a single report being 45 minutes) resulted from inadequate development processes.

\- Development processes for the interfaces were inadequate because the system builders looked solely at the information technology while seeking no advice, participation, or involvement from anyone else (in cell C2: No users were involved in the design of forms and screens; no pilot testing of interfaces; insufficient time for input and output design), which in turn was a rational strategy for system builders whose theories-in-use were based on the technological imperative.

\- Theories-in-use about interfaces derived from the technological imperative were evidenced by comments from organizational participants. These comments are consistent with the belief, inferred from our observations of the system builders, that the behaviors of people should conform to the interfaces whose design is driven by technological considerations (in cell D2: Both theories-in-use identified in this cell view user interfaces as technical design issues to which people subsequently adapt themselves).

However, even though the major problematic behaviors (the five apparent anomalies) were becoming understandable to us, there were other behaviors which were problematic before and still remain so. Facts and perceptions in the matrix indicated to us that the system developer's performance lacked competence. A staff member remarked (cell B2), for example, that a full-time computer person was needed, even though the system developer was full-time. Also, observations indicated (cell D1) that the system developer saw FAS as a single-user PC application, only bigger.

## Step 6. Repeating Steps 3, 4, and 5.

Repeating Step 3. Noting any new apparent anomalies. We realized that other behaviors, which previously appeared normal or unproblematic, no longer appeared so. Reading the system builders' theory-in-use as deriving from the technological imperative, we noted the appearance of a new anomaly: The system builders did not apply the technological imperative to their own behavior. They did not adapt themselves to the requirements of the new technology in failing to seek out the needed expertise from the in-house experts, especially in the later stages when resistance to the technology was becoming apparent. Therefore, we faced another choice. We could draw the conclusion that the system builders behaved in ways inconsistent with their own beliefs or we could draw the conclusion that we had not yet succeeded in properly reading the meanings behind their behaviors. We chose the latter conclusion, which necessitated another pass through the hermeneutical circle.

Repeating Step 4. Surfacing the Latest Interpretation. In repeating Step 4, we used the remaining apparent anomaly (the system developer's seeming lack of competence) and the new anomaly (the system builders' seeming inconsistency in not applying the technological imperative to themselves) to surface the assumptions implicit in our most recent interpretation. We had assumed that competence for the system developer in this situation was the same as general system development competence as understood in the industry and that the people building the system were indeed subscribing to the technological imperative as their theory-in-use. These assumptions were responsible for producing the anomalies in our interpretation.

Repeating Step 5. Revising the Latest Interpretation. In attempting to see how the system developer might understand himself as competent, we realized that the appropriate standards were those employed by his immediate superior, the dean, who was also the system champion. Because the dean's own experience in computing was based on single-user spreadsheet applications from an end-user perspective (see cell D1), his expectations of the system developer were based on a very simple theory (concept) about the nature of an IS application. The theory was inadequate to a multiple user system. Yet, with the dean's expectations as the relevant measure, we were able to reach an interpretation that (in this context) the system developer's performance was competent (for his superior).

In reflecting on the lack of consistency resulting from inferring the technological imperative as being the system builders' theory-in-use, we entertained the possibility of another theory-in-use, generally termed Theory X, to replace (rather than just patch) the technological-imperative theory-in-use.

Schein [29, pp. 99–100]: “The Theory X person assumes that people are lazy and must, therefore, be motivated and controlled.” In general, a manager whose theory-in-use is Theory X would believe that subordinates require order, highly specific tasks, and measures for tight control over their behavior [4, p. 20; 29, pp. 99–100]. If ascribed to the system builders as their theory-in-use, Theory X would allow us to consider more of their behaviors to be sensible than would the technological imperative.

First, when we infer the theory-in-use by the systems builders as being Theory X, we may still explain and understand (though, of course, in a different way ${}^{9}$ ) the same behaviors that we were able to explain and understand when we inferred the system builders' theory-in-use as being the technological imperative. For instance, the behavior in which the system builders sought no user involvement in requirements determination or interface design would still be consistent with a Theory-X theory-in-use. If operating under this theory-in-use, the system builders would regard such user involvement, even if desirable, as not required or essential. The reason is that the system builders, operating under Theory X, would believe and expect that directives, threats, or sanctions (even if imposed unilaterally by them) could subsequently bring about the compliance by the targeted users in actually utilizing the completed system. Also, the system builders' failure to alter the overwhelmingly technical focus of their development process, even in the face of the emerging political fallout and resistant behavior, would be consistent with a Theory-X theory-in-use. The reason is that a Theory-X theory-in-use by the system builders would not preclude, but would allow, procedures for implementing and operating a technical system that are entirely separate from the procedures for dealing with the targeted users and any other human participants in the social system. After all, the reactions of these participants could always, if necessary, be subsequently controlled and altered in order to fit the operational requirements of the technical system.

Second, when we infer the theory-in-use by the system builders as being Theory X, we may additionally explain and understand a particular behavior that the technological-imperative theory-in-use allowed us to see only as an anomaly. The technological imperative, as the theory-in-use by the system builders, would have required them to seek out and take advantage of the expertise of the faculty members in the school of management – expertise which was abundantly available – for the purpose of implementing the new technology successfully. The failure of the system champion (the dean) and the system developer to do this was therefore, in the technological-imperative view, an anomaly. However, according to Theory X, the role of subordinates (in this case, the faculty) is to comply (in this case, to comply by accepting the new information system), regardless of whatever expertise they may or may not have to offer. Such expertise from subordinates, even if desirable, would be considered optional. Therefore, in an interpretation that ascribes Theory X to the system builders as their theory-in-use, the nonsolicitation of expertise or other ‘input’ from subordinates is not anomalous, but can be regarded as normal.

## 6. Additional illustrative points brought out by the case of the faculty activities system

The investigation into the faculty activities system, conducted up to this point, is sufficient for illustrating how to apply the proposed framework and interpretive process in diagnosing an IS failure. In principle, of course, iterations around the hermeneutic circle may be pursued endlessly. (An analogous situation is that, in the traditional hypothesis-testing procedures of the natural sciences, no theory may ever be proven conclusively to be true, but always remains open to further questioning and testing.) Some potential, additional iterations will be briefly mentioned for their instructive value in elucidating a few additional points about how to apply the proposed framework and interpretive process.

One potential, additional iteration would involve further scrutiny (perhaps leading to replacement) of Theory X as our inference of the system builders' theory-in-use. Consider that Theory X, while consistent with the system builders' behavior of not soliciting the expertise of the faculty, does not a priori preclude it either. To the contrary, we might even consider the possibility that, under Theory X, the system builders would have felt it natural and perhaps even imperative to order their subordinates to supply the needed expertise. To explore briefly the ramifications of this plausible rival interpretation, consider the corresponding situation in a private corporation.

Typically, a chief executive would be in a position to solicit, if not outright compel, expertise from any subordinate possessing it. In contrast, in a school of business in an American university, the chief executive (the dean) does not enjoy the same perquisites of authority. Faculty, especially those who are tenured, experience considerable autonomy and often do not behave as subordinates. Therefore, in the FAS situation, it would be reasonable for the system champion (the dean) – even if he subscribed to Theory X – to expect the faculty not to yield their expertise freely, especially when the expertise would have been used for instituting an information system to impose order and control over their own behavior. However, there are no data currently in the matrix to support or test this new explanation. In order to incorporate this new explanation as part of our interpretation, we would need to proceed with a new round of observations. We would need to return to the research site and collect additional data pertaining to the degree of autonomy that the faculty actually experience. This scenario would be an illustration of how (as previously mentioned) our interpretations guide what we observe, just as our observations guide what we interpret. Observations that the faculty experience autonomy from the dean would allow us to retain an interpretation that the system builders' theory-in-use was Theory X. Conversely, observations that the faculty experience little autonomy from the dean, but proceed nonetheless in actively resisting the dean's mandates and controls, would cast doubt on the adequacy or relevance of an interpretation based on Theory X.

Another potential, additional iteration would also involve further scrutiny of Theory X as the system builders' theory-in-use. For example, why did the dean terminate the system and admit failure without exercising more vigorous administrative actions? This could be investigated further. As previously mentioned, we may extend the hermeneutical circle to the context outside of the immediate 'text'. The results would be access to additional 'passages' on which to draw for clues when deciphering and validating the meaning of the existing passages which we are scrutinizing. Specifically, are the behaviors of the system champion and system developer consistent with Theory X in additional situations outside of the FAS case? We could seek out observations of the system champion and system developers in the following additional situations: Other IS development projects taking place concurrently with FAS; IS development projects preceding FAS; IS development projects to take place in the future; and other administrative projects not pertaining to IS. Observations that they behaved in ways consistent with a Theory-X theory-in-use in these other settings would lend support to our Theory-X based interpretation of the FAS failure. Observations allowing us to infer other theories-in-use by the system builders in these other settings would present, and require us to entertain, plausible alternatives to Theory X as the theory-in-use by the system builders in the FAS setting.

The interpretive strategy, as just suggested by the proposed iteration in which the hermeneutical circle is extended beyond the boundaries of the immediate subject matter, could benefit from future efforts to formalize it. One promising approach would be to follow Pettigrew's framework for contextualist research $[27]$ . The contextualist framework's “vertical level of analysis” appears to correspond not only to our hermeneutic interpretation of the textual ‘snapshot’ or cross-sectional observations recorded for the FAS case itself, but also to such an interpretation extending to other IS development projects, other administrative projects, other phenomena in the organization, and even the socio-economic environment outside the organization; both the inner and outer environments would be examined. The contextualist framework’s “horizontal level of analysis” appears to refer to the interpretation of observations recorded historically not only for all the different vertical levels unfolding over time inside the organization, but also for those unfolding over time in the environment outside the organization. One important result of such an interpretive research strategy would be the availability of additional historical and environmental data for inferring the theories-in-use by the individuals who are using information technology to intervene in an already existing social system.

At the point where we actually stopped in our iterations through the hermeneutical circle, our interpretation of the FAS failure may be summarized in the following way:

(1) The failure was set into motion by the distinctive ways in which the system builders themselves made sense of the technical system and the social system. They understood FAS to be a technical system which they could successfully manage and develop, even if these efforts were performed independently of the organizational context into which the technical system would subsequently be placed. They did not conceive of the combined technical and social properties as reactive and interactive. Furthermore, with a Theory-X theory-in-use, the system builders understood the other organizational participants to be in the role of subordinates who would comply with the formers' expectations for cooperation and compliance in finally accepting and using the system.

(2) With their Theory-X theory-in-use, it was rational for the system builders in their development efforts not to involve other organizational participants. It was rational for the system builders not to solicit the expertise of the other organizational participants. Under Theory X, such actions would be seen as optional and unnecessary. Also, it was rational not to recognize or attend to the negative political reactions to FAS because, under Theory X, the acceptance of the system by these people (subordinates) would be a result that eventually occurs. Furthermore, the system builders believed themselves to be fully capable of developing a technically adequate information system; the system developer's technical expertise was considered 'competent' based on the system champion's expectations, which in turn were based on the system champion's own single end-user concept of IS application requirements.

(3) Poor system performance resulted because of the characteristics of the development processes. For instance, the single-user spreadsheet perspective led to technical design problems in the hardware/software technology. The non-involvement of the other organizational participants in the process of developing FAS resulted in unsatisfactory user interfaces (not only the input screens but the paper forms and the output reports as well). The non-solicitation of input from these people also resulted in the FAS not satisfying the specific information requirements of individuals in different parts of the organization. The persistence of the problems with FAS led its intended users to resist it and even to reject it.
(4) The problems that FAS exhibited in its hardware/software technology, its user interfaces, its non-satisfaction of information requirements, and its lack of organization fit were the stimuli for the acerbic and cynical responses of the organizational participants to FAS.

## 7. Prescriptive use of the methods

A diagnosis does not, by itself, constitute a prescription. Diagnosing a failure is the first step; prescribing a remedy is the second step. The scope of this article is restricted to diagnosis, but it is easy and tempting to speculate about how a diagnosis has ramifications for ways to remedy a failed system. For instance, once the theories-in-use are identified and their probable consequences understood, ways to change the theories-in-use or reduce their impact may be entertained. In the FAS case, a project to redo the system could start with an intervention focusing on the theories-in-use of the system champion and the system builder. By collecting this data at the beginning, it would be possible to establish the difference between espoused theories and the actual theories in use. It was not possible to do this interesting analysis for the present study.

As a brief insight into how an alternative theory-in-use could lead to a different system, one observer of the failure provided a view of how the system could have been built based on a socio-technical and participative view. The basis for the system would have been software provided to faculty for use on personal computers in maintaining their vita data and for printing various short and long vitas. The vita files from faculty use of the programs would be given on diskettes (say twice a year) to the dean's office for building a school-wide system. The input, data, and output requirements would be developed through a joint effort of faculty, department administrators, and the dean's office. The participative design of the system would be consistent with the organizational dynamics of the school and the design would utilize the existing motivation of faculty to maintain an error-free vita file and produce correct, timely vita outputs.

The diagnostic framework and interpretive process, as proposed in this article, may have value for use not only in the postmortem of a failed IS, but also in the prognosis for success or failure of a planned IS and the monitoring of an

IS undergoing implementation. The IS professional or other analyst, when conducting such a prognosis or monitoring, could use the framework and interpretive process to help determine if the socio-technical conditions needed for success are present and those sufficient for failure are absent. Corrective interventions could then be made before events culminate in a failure.

In order to use the two-dimensional framework and interpretive process for prognosis or monitoring, the IS manager or analyst should also have knowledge of the set of socio-technical conditions required for success. There is no agreement on the set of such conditions, but Land [15] highlights six factors that may determine the successful adoption of an IS. The factors include motivation for introducing the new system; commitment to the system; organizational culture; the management of the implementation process; the “distance” between the existing system and the replacement system; and the technology itself. In an analysis of IS failure, it is the effect of the lack of these factors that is conspicuous. Identification of factors that have greatest value for prognosis or monitoring must await the efforts of future research.

Appendix B. Guide to the literature on information system failure

For the IS professional or IS researcher who is applying the diagnostic framework, the literature on system failure is a valuable resource for suggesting possible causes of failures. This appendix provides a general guide to selected portions of the IS research literature. There is no attempt to summarize or review of all of the literature, but valuable summaries and reviews that IS scholars have already prepared will be mentioned. Four somewhat overlapping categories of research offer insight in a failure diagnosis: (1) the topic of IS failure; (2) user involvement; (3) user resistance; (4) information system implementation.

## B.1. Research on IS failure

There are few IS studies which specifically label their topic as “failure”. Three such studies are by Lucas [19], Markus [21], and Lyytinen and Hirschheim [20].

In his frequently cited monograph, Why Information System Fail, Lucas suggests that, even though systems “exist to support decision making,” two questions commonly overlooked in systems development pertain to what types of information are needed and how the decision makers will be using the information. His analysis focuses on the organizational context within which any system must operate. He categorizes system failure primarily as an organizational behavior problem and emphasizes that user involvement is essential for avoiding a failed system.

In her article, “Power, Politics, and MIS Implementation,” Markus also emphasizes and organizational or social context. Using observations she collected in a case study of a failed information system, she tested three competing theories of user resistance. The surviving theory was an ‘interaction’ theory, which maintains that resistance to systems implementation does not follows from the separate and independent effects of ‘people factors’ on the one hand and ‘system factors’ on the other, but instead is the result of the interactive effects of the two sets of factors. Her analysis of the case showed that a system can change an organization’s balance of power and that those who perceive a loss in power because of system implementation resist system use.

Lyytinen and Hirschheim conducted an exhaustive review of the IS research literature pertaining to the topic of IS failure. Their article, "Information Systems Failures: A Survey and Classification of the Empirical Literature", provides not only a review of the literature, but also commentary on the literature, including an original framework for explicitly defining IS failure.

Lyytinen and Hirschheim provide a classification of reasons for IS failure that can be useful in identifying the causes of IS failure in a particular case. The classification scheme organizes the reasons under four headings: Features of the IS, features of the IS environment, features of the ISD (IS development) process, and features of the ISD environment. The last three headings are further specified into subheadings (for instance, falling under the third heading, features of the ISD process, are six subheadings that Lyytinen and Hirschheim call method-based reasons, decision-making-based reasons, work-based reasons, contingency reasons, implementation reasons, and system assumption-based reasons). Under each heading and subheading is a discussion that includes citations to the IS research literature.

## B.2. Research on user involvement

There is a large body of literature on the topic of user involvement. In their article, “User Involvement and MIS Success: A Review of Research”, Ives and Olson [13] classify twenty-two studies according to the different features through which the studies recognized system success: System quality, system usage, user behavior/attitudes, and information satisfaction. This suggests that a cited study may have potential value for a failure diagnosis. For example, Ives and Olson mention that one study [12], which recognizes system success through user behavior or attitudes, investigates the link between user involvement and job satisfaction, job skill, job originality, job status, and job salary. This study might be relevant to diagnoses of failure in which the observed symptoms pertain to these factors.

Before attempting to apply the user-involvement literature in a failure diagnosis, the quality of the research must also be considered. Ives and Olson conclude (p. 601): "Not only has empirical research been unable to foresee when and what types of user involvement are appropriate, it has not convincingly demonstrated that user involvement contributes to system success. Until higher quality, theory-based research strategies are employed, intuition, experience, and unsubstantiated prescriptions remain the practitioner's best guide to determine appropriate levels of user involvement."

Newman and Noble [26] illustrate how the user-involvement literature might be utilized in a critical and insightful way. In “User Involvement as an Interaction Process: A Case Study,” they classify the literature under four headings: Learning models, conflict models, political models, and garbage can models. They present a table which lists not only the theoretical strengths and weaknesses of the four types of models, but also the conditions under which each model is applicable. For example, the following are some necessary conditions for conflict models to apply:

\- users should be influential;

\- no constraints on time and budget, systems quality emphasized;

\- later stages of design.

By matching the conditions of the case being diagnosed to the conditions listed in Newman and Noble's table, the reader may locate research studies relevant to his or her failure diagnosis. They also examine the quality of the studies that they cite.

## B.3. Research on user resistance

Causes of user resistance are not necessarily the same as causes of IS failure, but the former phenomenon often contributes to or coincides with the latter phenomenon. Where user resistance plays a role, the literature on user resistance may therefore be utilized to suggest some possible causes for IS failure.

Hirschheim and Newman [11] provide a review and critique of the literature in their article, "Information Systems and User Resistance: Theory and Practice." They classify studies from the literature according to the array of causes which the different studies have theorized to lead to user resistance: Innate conservatism, lack of felt need, uncertainty, lack of involvement in change, redistribution of resources, organizational invalidity, lack of management support, poor technical quality, and personal characteristics of the designer (pp. 399–400). They present a case study to illustrate how different causes may be simultaneously present in the same IS setting (pp. 401–405).

Where the symptoms or conditions describing the IS failure being diagnosed match the conditions associated with a cause of user resistance, it should be considered as a cause of IS failure. For example, the conditions “disruption of the status quo” and “a potential attack on the groups’ interests” are associated with the cause of user resistance that Hirschheim and Newman call “redistribution of resources” (p. 399). If the same conditions describe the IS failure being diagnosed, then “redistribution of resources” should be considered as a cause of the IS failure.

## B.4. Research on MIS implementation

In the introduction to a special issue of Interfaces on the topic of implementation, Ginzberg and Schultz [9, p. 1] state: “The implementation of management science models and information systems has been a topic of considerable interest for at least two decades, and research on these issues has been going on for most of this period. (...) [T] here have been three conferences and at least three published volumes on implementation research. (...) The result of all this research attention is that we now know quite a lot about the implementation process and the factors that lead to its being more or less successful.”

The three published volumes are Implementing Operations Research / Management Science [31], The Implementation of Management Science [6], and Management Science Implementation (Supplement 1 to Applications of Management Science [30]). The different studies included in the three volumes and the special issue of Interfaces represent the full range of empirical findings and approaches to be found in the literature on MIS implementation. For the most part, this literature is relevant to situations in which the IS has already been developed and is being put into operation.
