# Task uncertainty and the use of computer technology

Jawaid A. Ghani

Department of Business Information Systems, Haworth College of Business, Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, MI 49008, USA

The relationship between task uncertainty and the utilization of computer technology was studied for a sample of 216 middle managers and first line supervisors in a large mid-Western bank. Greater task variability in Perrow's technology grid was associated with the use of a greater variety of computing tools. Task analyzability was related to the type of computer application used. Where tasks were less analyzable, users opted for personal computers, mostly for analysis and inquiry applications. For more analyzable tasks, mainframe computers were used more extensively, mostly for exception and monitoring applications. Implications are drawn for future research on the use of computer technology.

Keywords: End-User Computing, Task Uncertainty, Organizational Information Processing

## Introduction

The use of computer technology has been an area of concern for senior IS executives since the early 1980's [3], and the topic of several doctoral dissertations and major research projects (see, for example, [2]). Studies of factors influencing the adoption of computer tools have focused on user attitudes [35], perceived usefulness and ease of use [10], user training [31], and peer pressure [36]. Based on an extensive literature review, Cheney and his colleagues identified several organizational variables affecting end user computing (EUC) success. One of the key variables was task technology, defined to include task level, structuredness, repetitiveness, and interdependence. However, few empirical studies have focused on task characteristics even though task characteristics are often mentioned as an important determinant of EU behavior [14,27]. Where task characteristics are considered, they are often limited to descriptive measures, such as organizational job level (e.g., [30,19,25]).

Task structure and repetitiveness have been identified as the two main dimensions of task uncertainty $[33]$ . Environmental uncertainty and its effects on organizational structure and tasks has long been a topic of interest in Organization Theory. Uncertainty has been described as the fundamental problem with which top management must cope $[40]$ . Huber suggests that, as postindustrial organizations face increasing knowledge, turbulence, and complexity, information processing for supporting decision making and innovation will take on added importance.

How information technology can best be utilized to increase the information processing capacity of an organization is the concern of MIS researchers. Early work on Decision Support Systems (DSS) emphasized the role of task uncertainty in the design of systems. Keen and Scott-Morton suggested that semi-structured tasks were best supported by DSSs, while more structured tasks could be automated by traditional transaction processing systems. More recently, Ahn found that user satisfaction and work performance increased when the computing configuration matched the level of task uncertainty. Yet much remains to be understood regarding the relationship between task uncertainty and the use of information systems.

The purpose of this paper is to explore whether and how task variability and analyzability affect the diversity and type of computer tools used by end users. Results are based on a study of middle managers and first line supervisors in a large mid-Western bank. Conclusions are drawn for the management of end user computing and for future research in this area.

## Theoretical Background

## End User Computing

EUC has been defined as the direct, hands-on use and development of Information Systems by the principal users of the systems output or by their immediate support staffs $[45]$ . The dependent variable in many EUC studies is EUC success. Surrogate measures for it include information satisfaction, information system utilization, decision effectiveness, organizational performance, and the application of computer-based systems to the major problems of the organization. Cheney and his colleagues $[5]$ observe, “unless use is mandatory, information systems are used extensively only when they are perceived to be of value to the end user”, and hence system utilization or extent of use is considered to be highly correlated with other surrogate measures of system success. Researchers have differentiated between the extent and diversity of use. While extent of use refers to the proportion of work time spent directly working with computers, diversity refers to the number of different applications used. The latter is considered a more revealing measure of user advancement $[25]$ . Depending on the nature of the task being supported, one user may make extensive use of a single application, while another may make sporadic use of a variety of different systems.

The dimensions along which EUC applications differ, has been another concern of researchers. Ahn grouped EU applications into two generic categories: (i) analysis and inquiry, and (ii) exception reporting and monitoring. This is similar to the system types suggested by Sipior and Sanders. Analysis applications rely upon data handling and modification (including modeling) and provide support for problem finding and selection of optimal alternatives. Inquiry enables EUs to respond to ad hoc requests. Together, analysis and inquiry form a system similar to a “managerial support system”. Exceptions and monitoring applications, on the other hand, are more similar to traditional transaction processing systems. They suggest that the type of application tends to differ for personal computers compared to mainframes. The majority of data retrieval applications have remained on the mainframe, while analysis oriented applications tend to be developed by users on microcomputers.

## Task Uncertainty

Task uncertainty has been defined as “the difference between the amount of information required to perform the task and the amount of information already possessed by the organization” [12]. Researchers in both Management Information Systems (MIS) and Organization Theory recognize the close relationship between task uncertainty and organizational information processing. Daft and Lengel state that one of the basic reasons organizations process information is to reduce uncertainty. The information processing view of organizations is that organizational effectiveness is a function of the fit between task uncertainty and the information processing capacity of the organization [41]. Galbraith suggests that the critical factor limiting an organization’s performance in the face of uncertainty is “its ability to make decisions and communicate about unique, nonroutine, consequential events which could not be foreseen”. Similarly, Simon suggests that the scarce resource is not information, but the processing capacity to handle information. One mechanism for increasing the information processing capacity is to invest in information technology.

Several studies support the theory that task uncertainty is a major determinant of information processing requirements. Culnan (1983) found that, in cases of high task uncertainty, individuals made greater direct use of a commercial database system. Daft and Macintosh (1981) found task uncertainty to be positively related to the amount of information processed by individuals. Ahn, in a study of 250 end users, found that a good “fit” between task characteristics, particularly task uncertainty, and the EUC configuration resulted in high EU satisfaction and work performance.

Sources of task uncertainty may be identified in the task requirements of individuals and work units. Perrow described two dimensions of tasks: variability ${}^{1}$ and analyzability ${}^{2}$ . Task variability is defined as the frequency of unexpected and novel events that occur in the work process. High variability means that individuals typically cannot predict problems in advance, and that many tasks are unique.

Highly variable tasks cover a wide range of problems and decisions, and as a result the information acquired and processed has a wider scope and is less uniform $[34]$ . Individuals faced with unpredictable problems need to develop a variety of skills and be allowed the flexibility and discretion for handling these situations $[4]$ . It is thus expected that the opportunity to experiment with a variety of tools will be greater in such a task than in one with few unexpected events (low task variability), leading to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Task variability is positively related to the diversity of computer applications used.

Note that previous studies have found diversity of computer use to be independent of organizational level. However, given the large variations in task variability found within particular levels of an organization [16,43] results related to organizational level cannot be generalized to task variability.

Task analyzability concerns the nature of the search process undertaken by the individual when an exception occurs. An analyzable task is one wherein the individual can follow a formal and well defined search procedure to solve problems. In tasks which are not analyzable, there is uncertainty about cause-effect processes. While task variability affects the amount of information required to handle unexpected events, task analyzability affects the form of the information necessary to resolve ambiguities. Less analyzable tasks require richer information utilizing a medium capable of providing multiple information cues, immediate feedback, and a high variety language, in order that users may clarify ambiguities and define problems. Users need greater discretion in carrying out their tasks. They must “continuously restructure the data, a process that includes altering the specifications of informational inputs, alternatives, and desired goal states” [42].

In many organizations, the mainframe environment is quite different from the personal computer environment. Personal computers typically provide a more user-friendly interface which results in more playful behavior $[44]$ and experimentation $[21]$ , and also give the user a greater sense of control resulting in higher user satisfaction $[37,13]$ . In terms of the application categories described in the previous section, the mainframe is used more for exception and monitoring applications, while the PC is for analysis and inquiry applications. The PC in such cases represents a richer medium than does the mainframe, where the user is often restricted to the use of a few corporate databases using specific application packages. On the PC, however, the user typically has more control over the data and can choose from a variety of different software tools. PC packages are usually better suited to providing immediate feedback (e.g., what-if analyses using a spreadsheet) and multiple cues (e.g., ability to present data in graphical and tabular form) compared to the packages usually available on a mainframe. This leads to two more hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2: Task analyzability is positively related to the use of mainframe computers.

Hypothesis 3: Task analyzability is negatively related to the use of personal computers.

## Method

## Sample

A field study was undertaken to test the hypothesized relationships between task uncertainty and EUC behavior. The primary criterion for sample selection was to control for as many organizational variables as possible, and yet cover diverse work activities. A large Midwestern bank with over 8,000 employees and 350 branches agreed to participate in this research effort. This research site fitted well with our requirements for sample selection. Each branch is in close contact with its customers, with the economic activities of its local community, and is affected by changes in the local environment. Given the widely varying environments faced by different branches, one expects officers in different branches to face varying levels of uncertainty [24]. Yet, being part of one organization, the different branches share a common corporate culture and similar computing resources. The unit of analysis was the individual.

Most studies on task uncertainty have taken the work unit as the unit of analysis. In most cases, the work unit employees have been clerical workers with fairly homogeneous task responsibilities. The nature of professional and supervisory work, however, results in varying task responsibilities even among members of the same work unit. This, together with the discretionary nature of end user computing, makes us believe that the individual should be the unit of analysis.

Questionnaires were sent to a random sample of 400 first line supervisors, professionals, and middle managers. Top management (Vice President and above) were not included in the sample. A total of 216 usable responses were received giving a response rate of 54%. Seventy percent of the respondents were female; 75% made hands-on use of the computer in their work. Two-thirds were below 40 years old. On the average, respondents had 2.8 years of college education (std. dev. = 2.1 years). The sample consisted primarily of individuals who worked in the back offices of the various branches. Typical job titles included personal banking officer, commercial loan officer (real estate), corporate accounting supervisor, audit manager, and credit analyst.

EUC was highly encouraged. The bank had established an Information Center about 18 months prior to the study. Training was available for LOTUS, DBASE III and WordPerfect on the PCs, and for EASYTRIEVE, a fourth generation language, on the mainframe. First line supervisors and managers in most branches had access to PCs and mainframe terminals. The PCs were used in a stand alone mode, mostly for spreadsheet applications and for managing small localized databases. Examples would be an application to track billings for safety deposit boxes, or to identify customers likely to be interested in special savings programs. The mainframe was used primarily for producing reports from the corporate database, using EASYTRIEVE; e.g., a report of current balances to be used by the branch manager in making customer calls. No form of electronic mail was being supported. However, the bank was in the process of integrating the PCs and the mainframe to support data transfer between databases. In terms of the EUC growth stages suggested by Huff and his colleagues [18], the bank could be considered between stage 3 (manual integration) and stage 4 (automated integration).

## Measurement

## Task uncertainty

Considerable measurement research has been conducted in the area of task uncertainty. Withey and his colleagues (1983) evaluated six of the most important studies and designed an improved measure based on them. This instrument was used here to measure the two dimensions of task uncertainty. Each measure consisted of five questions. The task analyzability measure included questions such as “to what extent is there a clearly known way to do the major types of work you normally encounter?” and “to do your work, to what extent can you actually rely on established procedures and practices?” The task variability measure included such questions as “to what extent are your major tasks the same from day to day?” and “to what extent would you say your work is routine?”. Each question was ranked on a 7 point scale from “to a small extent” to “to a great extent”.

## EUC extent and diversity

End users were defined as individuals making hands-on use of the computer and also using the output as part of their job. The extent of computer use was determined by asking respondents to specify the number of hours a week they typically spend directly using all computers. Diversity of computer use was based on the number of applications (different computer packages) used in a typical week. Respondents were asked to name the application software(s) they used. In order to validate internal consistency, respondents were asked to specify the number of hours each application was used. This was checked against the number of hours of claimed PC and mainframe use. They were also asked in a different part of the questionnaire to specify with which application software they were familiar. This was checked against the names of packages that respondents claimed to be using.

## Findings

Table 1 shows the means and standard deviations for the key variables considered in this study. Table 2 shows the zero-order correlation coefficients for the same variables. In order to identify the relationship of task variability and analyzability on computing behavior, partial correlations were computed. The partial correlation between task variability and computing behavior, controlling for task analyzability is shown in the first column of Table 3. The partial correlation between task analyzability and computing behavior, controlling for task variability, is shown in the second column of Table 3.

The data indicates a positive relationship between perceived task variability and the diversity of EUC (r = 0.116, p < 0.05). This supports hypothesis 1. When the frequency of exceptions increases, we expect end users to have a variety of application software for a wide range of situations. Some of the loan officers, for instance, who frequently faced unexpected situations, indicated that they utilized different software tools (EASYTRIEVE, DBASE, and LOTUS) in order to access and process data from different sources (corporate and local databases). Data analysis revealed a positive correlation between task analyzability and extent of mainframe use (r = 0.109, p < 0.1) and a negative correlation between task analyzability and extent of personal computer use (r = -0.101, p < 0.1). This provides support for hypotheses 2 and 3. Supervisors, who frequently had to prepare standard reports from the corporate database indicated that they used EASYTRIEVE for this purpose. Branch managers, on the other hand, often maintained their own local databases on PCs to support special needs.

## Discussion

Models of organizational information processing suggest that, as the frequency of unexpected events increase, the need for more varied information processing during task execution increases. Also, the less analyzable and unstructured a task, the greater the need for richer information and media in order to help resolve the task. The results of our study provide modest support for these basic relationships.

Perrow combined the variability and analyzability dimensions to form the basis of four categories of task technology as shown in Figure 1.

Several researchers have suggested that information tools should “fit” the task technology in order to be effective $[15,23,26]$ . When we consider the characteristics of the mainframe and the personal computer environment in the organization, it is possible to suggest the nature of computer technology that is more appropriate for different categories of task technologies. These are described in each of the four cells. Thus engineering technology, typified by the well understood but highly variable work of professional accountants and engineers, would best be supported by a variety of less rich tools, such as the exception and monitoring applications generally available on mainframes. Much of managerial work, on the other hand, falls under craft technology, and would be best supported by the use of a few rich tools, such as the analysis and inquiry applications generally available on personal computers.

Computer tools provided to individuals in an organization should be based on individual and work unit task characteristics, rather than on a uniform company-wide policy. IS managers should be cautious about transferring their experience when they switch departments or companies. What may have worked very well in a stable environment may not be appropriate in a more turbulent one.

The results of this study are limited due to several sampling and measurement issues. The sample consisted of first line supervisors and middle managers. A study of senior management may reveal a very different set of relationships between task uncertainty and computing behavior. It is conceivable that at very high levels of task uncertainty, as faced by senior executives, both the extent and diversity of end user computing will start to decrease. This would follow from research conducted by Driver and Streufert which suggests a curvilinear relationship between information utilization and environmental complexity, research on DSS which suggests that they are more applicable to semistructured tasks than to unstructured tasks, and studies which indicate that senior management utilize very rich media, such as face to face communication and observational tours $[20]$ . Our study was conducted in a single organization in the early stages of EUC growth. The results may be different in a more mature organization, where information technology is also used for communication. Also, the instruments in our study are not sufficiently validated. Other dimensions of EUC behavior need to be identified and valid and reliable scales need to be developed to measure them.

Within these limitations, the study clearly shows that task characteristics should be an important consideration in designing an end user support strategy. Different organizations and different work units within the same organization face different levels of uncertainty. Their information processing requirements are different and the nature of support provided for end user computing should match these requirements.

March and Simon (1958) observed that the full implications of task uncertainty on work unit activities had not been recognized. Even though two decades have passed since then, the observation is still very valid. Organizations are a central concern in the field of MIS [29]. Yet reviews of research in MIS bemoan the fact that MIS research is poorly grounded in organization theory [6]. Organizational research on Information Technology has also often failed to cite MIS research. This paper has attempted to bridge the gap between the two fields.
