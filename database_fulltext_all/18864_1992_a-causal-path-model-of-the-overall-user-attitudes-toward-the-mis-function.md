# A causal path model of the overall user attitudes toward the MIS function

The case of user information satisfaction

Kailash Joshi

School of Business Administration, University of Missouri-St. Louis, St. Louis, MI 63121, USA

MIS literature has identified the importance of user attitude toward MIS, which is measured by user information satisfaction (UIS). However, poor user evaluation of the function remains a problem in many organizations. In this paper we examine factors that may influence UIS and their relative influence or importance. The paper evaluates some alternate causal path models of UIS involving traditional factors such as quality of information products, knowledge and involvement, and attitude towards EDP staff and service, along with three new factors: equity in the allocation of MIS resources, role conflict, and role ambiguity. Data from a cross-sectional survey involving 324 users is employed to evaluate the causal path models through path analysis. Equity was found to be the most important variable influencing UIS. The importance of equity identified here supports the existence of struggle for obtaining scarce MIS resources reported by other researchers. The results also highlight a need for the establishment of mechanisms and structural arrangements for fair allocation of resources.

Keywords: User Information Satisfaction, Management of MIS, Political Perspective of MIS, Role Conflict, Role Ambiguity, Equity, Quality of Information Products, User Involvement, User Attitudes, and User Behavior.

## Introduction

The study of user attitudes is important to MIS professionals and researchers. In recognition of this importance, much of MIS research has been devoted to the study of user attitudes in the form of user information satisfaction (UIS). This involves measurement of attitudes toward the MIS function and the processes, products, and services associated with it.

The desirability of obtaining positive user attitudes or higher user information satisfaction has been recognized in the MIS literature for a number of interrelated reasons. First, MIS research has recognized that user attitudes are likely to influence user behavior (Ginzberg et al., 1984; Joshi, 1990; Lucas, 1978; Lucas, 1981; Zmud, 1983). Therefore, positive user information satisfaction may be helpful in obtaining acceptance and usage of MIS.

Second, poor user attitudes towards the MIS function may restrict its ability in undertaking a leadership role in the work place of introducing computer technology for enhanced productivity. Such interventions in the work place, can be made only in collaboration with users. However, poor user attitudes may lower the credibility of the MIS function, and this may reduce the influence and status of MIS and make it difficult to obtain user cooperation. Doll and Ahmed (1983) note that, “This loss of influence may create further difficulties in gaining management cooperation, responsiveness, and involvement and in securing funding for the necessary hardware.” Thus, the effectiveness and the role of the MIS function may be restricted.

Third, UIS has been viewed as a criterion for evaluating MIS success (Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Ginzberg, 1979; and Srinivasan, 1985). If the systems are technically good but not satisfactory for users and therefore not used effectively, the mission of systems implementation cannot be considered successful.

Fourth, the MIS function is service oriented and the importance of ‘customer satisfaction’ cannot therefore be over stressed. An evaluation of the MIS function by the Chief Executive is likely to depend heavily on the feedback from user managers in view of the difficulty in assessing the MIS function on other criteria. In one informal survey of the heads of IS functions of 91 of the largest corporations in the US, Halbrecht (1978) reported that the major concern expressed by these executives was: how could they enhance the perception and reception of their activity by their superiors and users? He further states that, “These information executives indicated that they are probably evaluated most on customer satisfaction.” Moad (1989) also identified IS executives’ concern for positive user attitudes and their efforts to measure UIS. Apparently, IS executives are still looking for answers to questions about factors that help determine the overall user evaluation.

A review of MIS literature (e.g., Bostrom and Heinen, 1977; Swanson, 1974; Welke, 1979) suggests widespread dissatisfaction with the MIS function. Therefore, some important questions are: What factors influence or help determine overall user information satisfaction? and What is their relative importance? i.e., What is the relative magnitude of influence of each factor on the overall UIS; and are there any interrelationships between these factors?

This paper addresses the above research questions. It presents some alternate causal path models of UIS and reports on an empirical study to evaluate the models through path analysis. The main model considered in this paper is presented in Figure 1; the model is discussed after reviewing relevant literature in the next two sections.

## Relationship Between the Facet Approach and the Overall Approach

A user's evaluation of the overall satisfaction with the MIS function (UIS) is likely to depend upon the user's evaluation of different facets of interaction with MIS. Several theories, such as the attribution (Folkes, 1988), congruity, and balance theory (Robertson et al, 1984), support the relationship between attitudes toward individual facets (e.g., products, service, involvement, equity, etc.) and the overall assessment. The above theories have been widely used as a basis for research in consumer satisfaction, psychology, and sociology literature.

Scarpello and Campbell (1983) identified global and facet approaches as two distinct ways to measure satisfaction. In research on job satisfaction, they found that defining overall satisfaction as the sum of its discrete elements may neglect some major determinants. In their view, the “whole” appears to be more than the sum of its measured parts.

In MIS literature, user information satisfaction has been measured through global measures (e.g., Olson and Ives, 1981) as well as through the summation of measures of different facets. These instruments refer to the overall MIS environment, therefore, rather than focusing on a specific system, they attempt to obtain users' perception of all systems at once. Instruments that measure users' satisfaction with a specific system have also been developed. Identification of additional facets that influence satisfaction should suggest directions for improving satisfaction instruments that employ the facets.

## Identification of Likely Determinants of Overall UIS

Research by Ives et al. (1983) identified three major factors in UIS: quality of information products (QIP), i.e., reports and screens; users' attitude towards the EDP staff and services (ESS); and the level of users' knowledge and involvement (KIL). Therefore, users' perceptions of these factors should influence their overall satisfaction with the MIS function.

It seems likely that all relevant factors may not have been identified in earlier research due to limitations in users' ability to conceptualize and communicate all their measures (concerns). The socio-technical systems perspective suggests that factors from the social subsystem of the work environment are also relevant in determining UIS (Bostrom, 1980b; Mumford and Weir, 1979; Pava, 1985). MIS literature has also identified the primacy of behavioral and organizational factors in implementation problems (Lucas, 1976; Markus, 1983; Nichols, 1981).

Three additional behavioral and organizational factors from the social subsystem are considered in this paper. These are: equity in the allocation of MIS resources, role conflict, and role ambiguity.

## Role Conflict and Role Ambiguity

Bostrom (1978, 1980a) and Joshi (1989a) identified role conflict (RC) and role ambiguity (RA) as factors that are likely to influence UIS. Kahn et al. (1964), identified RC and RA as the major stressors emerging from the social environment of organizations. RC is a perception of incompatible expectations sent to a focal person (the person enacting the role). RA is a focal person's lack of clarity about sent roles. RC and RA have been found to be related to a wide variety of dysfunctional outcomes such as job dissatisfaction, tension/anxiety, and lowered organizational commitment (Beeher and Newman, 1978; Fisher and Gitelson, 1983; Van Sell et al., 1981).

Researchers argue that information systems design not only restructures tasks and technology (the technical system), but also influences the social system (i.e., roles and structure). IS design is viewed as one of the interrelated design mechanisms that integrate the variables in the technical and social subsystems. Choices made during the design phase result in a set of related roles to carry out the tasks of the technical system. The concept of role is viewed as the summation of the requirements with which the system confronts the individual. Poor design choices or lack of attention to the social subsystem can lead to conflict and ambiguity in the enacted roles.

The RC and RA variables have been associated with the nature of tasks (Adams, 1976; Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967; and Miles, 1976) organizational structure (House and Rizzo, 1972); communication in organizations, and incongruent matches of task-structure-technology (Schuler, 1977). These characteristics of the work system are likely to be influenced by IS design.

Users who experience RC and RA on account of poor systems design are likely to be less satisfied with information systems. Therefore the arguments in favor of influence of RC/RA on UIS are shown in Table 1.

This model is supported by Turner's (1984) finding that employees having similar jobs but using different types of MIS experience different levels of stress in their work environment. Mumford and Pettigrew's (1975) research also supports this model. They argue that “new roles are likely to emerge during periods of innovation when new technologies and new forms of organization throw up a demand for a new set of skills." They propose that people occupying these new roles are likely to face conflict and RA. They stress the importance of processes and procedures to reduce uncertainty and to clarify the content and boundaries of the new roles.

## Equity

The concepts of fairness, equity, and social justice have been recognized and discussed since the time of Aristotle. Adams (1963, 1965) formalized some of these concepts in the form of equity theory. The main proposition is that individuals will try to maximize their outcomes in social exchange. When individuals find themselves in inequitable relationships or exchanges, they become distressed. The more inequitable the relationship, the more distress the individual will feel (Walster et al., 1978).

Equity (EQT) is a relevant issue in the allocation of MIS resources by the centralized MIS function (Joshi, 1989b, 1990). Users who perceive inequity in the allocation of MIS resources are likely to be dissatisfied with the agents viewed as responsible for allocation.

The use of power and influence in the allocation of MIS resources is well documented (Danziger et al., 1982; Keen, 1981; Kling, 1980; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Markus, 1981; and Robey and Markus, 1984). Power and influence can result in a distribution of resources and tasks that may not be considered fair or equitable by all users. They are likely to feel dissatisfaction with the MIS function as shown in Table 2.

## The Proposed Causal Path Model

The relative importance of different determinants of UIS and the magnitude of their direct and indirect influences can be estimated by constructing possible causal models and evaluating them through path analysis. Our emphasis is on building causal models which can parsimoniously depict relationships, estimate their strength, and provide some indication of the relative importance of the independent variables.

In the social sciences, where cross-sectional survey data is often collected on an ex post facto basis, independent variables are often correlated. In such cases it is not possible to identify the importance of a variable by its regression coefficient, for this coefficient does not represent the square root of variance accounted for by that variable. This problem does not exist in experimental settings where the researcher has greater control over the variables and can reduce the correlation among independent variables through random assignment. Therefore, causal modeling is of greater significance in analyzing cross-sectional survey data.

Our first model, as presented in Figure 1, proposes direct relationships between all independent variables and the dependent variable (UIS). The model proposes a relationship between the quality of information product (QIP) and RC, and between QIP and RA. A user who experiences poor QIP in the form of untimely reports, or unreliable, missing, or erroneous data is likely to be subjected to greater feelings of ambiguity and conflict. The user may have to do extra work to find the required information and perform un-assigned tasks. The user may not know what to do or how to proceed in such situations and may be forced to make decisions without enough information.

The level of a user's knowledge about systems and involvement in the development of systems (KIL) can influence a number of variables in the model. KIL can influence RC and RA. A user who has received better training, who is more knowledgeable, or who was involved in the development of MIS should know how to deal with problems of poor QIP.

KIL can also influence a user's attitudes towards EDP staff and services (ESS). As training and involvement activities are generally managed by the EDP staff, the satisfaction of a user with these processes can influence the evaluation of EDP services. Similarly, a user who is not involved or consulted, in the sense of participative design of work may form poor attitudes toward the EDP staff.

Perceptions of equity can influence attitudes toward EDP staff and service. Liechty and Churchill (1979) have noted its importance in consumers' satisfaction. A user who experiences lower equity is likely to attribute it to the EDP staff and view the service as poor.

In addition to these causal relationships, the model also acknowledges the possibility of non-causal correlations between QIP and KIL, between KIL and EQT, and between QIP and EQT. It was felt that there were no strong arguments to support causal, one directional relationships between these variable pairs, therefore, we decided to evaluate this model before examining other alternatives.

## Research Methodology

Most studies examining RC and RA have employed questionnaire measures developed by Rizzo et al. (1970), consisting of eight RC and six RA items. Dougherthy and Pritchard (1985) conclude that these measures capture somewhat global perceptions and can be used for employees in any job. Their psychometric properties have been found to be adequate (Schuler et al., 1977).

Three existing factors that influence UIS were measured based on the instrument reported by Ives et al. (1983). The “progressive…regressive” pair for the item “time taken for development of systems” was replaced by an “improving…deteriorating” pair. Equity was measured through a scale developed by Joshi (1989b). This measures users’ perceptions of fairness in the allocation of information systems resources by the centralized MIS function.

To assess the overall UIS, a four item scale developed by Olson and Ives (1981) was employed. This was considered appropriate because it attempts to measure global satisfaction rather than evaluate the factors that are likely to influence the overall satisfaction. The four items were slightly modified to refer directly to the satisfaction feelings or emotional reaction of users. See Appendix A for the modified scales.

The next step involved a cross-sectional survey of users of centralized information systems to obtain data to test our model. It was felt that only centralized information systems could provide the relevant organizational context for evaluating the relationships. Therefore, individuals who use only self designed systems or personal computers were excluded from the study.

As some of the instruments employed (i.e., KIL, QIP, ESS, and EQT) have been developed primarily in the context of non-clerical users, users such as data entry clerks were excluded from the study. It was also felt that they may represent a user population with different concerns and perspective on the relative importance of factors included in this study.

Qualified users were contacted to participate in the survey through participating organizations, executive development programs, and evening business courses. In organizations, an attempt was made to obtain users from many different departments. Respondents were requested to focus on the centralized information systems function and the systems developed by it.

Besides organizations represented through executive development and evening programs, nine others from the Midwest region participated. These included five medium-sized engineering and chemical manufacturing organizations, two government agencies, a major community college system, and a major university. A total of 324 responses to the questionnaire were received (including 61 from users in executive development and evening programs) out of about 650 distributed (including 163 to users in executive development and evening programs), yielding a response rate of 49 percent. All these users held full time jobs and used centralized information systems.

## Results

An examination of demographic data from the survey responses suggested that the questionnaire was directed to experienced users who interact regularly with their organizational MIS. Responses indicate that users at all levels were well represented. Out of the 324 users, 40 were in senior management positions, 123 were in middle management, 121 were in supervisory positions, and the rest were in staff or unclassified positions.

The users were heavily dependent on their MIS (an average score of 5.8 out of a Likert Scale of 1 to 7). The responses of were based on actual experience of MIS (an average of 8.5 years). Therefore, these users were not novices with little experience in MIS. This group appeared to be a reasonable sample for testing the models.

The reliability of scales included in this study ranged from 0.79 to 0.93. The correlation matrix of all variables is presented in Table 3. All factors are significantly correlated with UIS in the expected direction with a significance of 0.001 or less. Equity (EQT) has the highest correlation with UIS. Attitude towards EDP staff and services (ESS) has the next highest correlation with UIS, followed by quality of information products (QIP) and users' knowledge and involvement (KIL). Role conflict (RC) and Role Ambiguity (RA) are negatively correlated with UIS.

## Path Analysis Results

The proposed causal path model was tested, using structural modeling procedures (Griffin, 1977) with multiple regression analysis. Path analysis treats the regression coefficients as path coefficients or measures of causal influence of independent variables on the dependent variable.

The first step is Heise's (1969) method of "theory trimming". In this, the path coefficients are calculated for each proposed path. The relationships that are not significant are deleted from the model. The trivial paths are defined as those where the regression coefficient is less than twice its standard error. The significance of each path was tested using this criterion. This is also consistent with Kerlinger and Pedhazur (1973). In the following analysis, the results of significance analysis were the same as those based on the significance of regression coefficients determined through t statistics (at a significance level of 0.05).

In order to calculate the path coefficients for Model I, regression analysis was carried out by treating UIS as a dependent variable and all others as independent variables. The regression coefficients were examined for significance based on the standard error term for each coefficient. Paths whose regression coefficient was less than twice its standard error were eliminated from the model. This resulted in the elimination of paths from RA and RC to UIS.

The trimmed model after deleting the non-significant paths is presented in Figure 2; this trimmed model is termed Model II. Path coefficients were calculated for all its paths, which were all found to be significant. The results are presented in Table 4.

The second step involved reconstructing the zero order correlation matrix using the path coefficients and comparing it with the observed correlations (Duncan, 1966). The bivariate correlations implied by the model were reconstructed by adding the direct, indirect, previous, and spurious effects. If the model can reproduce the observed correlations, we may conclude that no significant coefficients are excluded from the model and that the model fits the observed data. The reconstructed correlation matrix for Model II is presented in Table 5.

Model II has approximately reproduced some observed correlations, however in the case of correlation between UIS and ESS, there is a large discrepancy. The sum of discrepancies is also high. This suggests that Model II does not provide a good fit to the observed data. Possibly users who find poor QIP also view the service provided by the MIS department (ESS) to be poor. Therefore, Model III (see Figure 3) was produced by modifying Model II to include a causal path from QIP to ESS.

## Summary and Conclusions

This study presented the results of path analysis conducted to evaluate some alternate causal path models of the overall UIS. Data used in the analysis was obtained from a cross-sectional survey involving 324 users of centralized information systems. The models considered RC, RA, and equity along with traditional factors identified in the MIS literature; quality of information products, attitude toward EDP staff and services, and level of user's knowledge and involvement.

The results of provide significant new insights into the UIS phenomenon. All factors were found to be significantly correlated with UIS. A causal path model was identified as providing a reasonable fit to the survey data. The model suggests that equity is the most important factor influencing UIS. Attitude toward EDP staff and services is the next important factor. RC and RA drop out from the model even though they are significantly negatively correlated with UIS. The causal path model of UIS and the relative ranking of various factors identified in this study improve our understanding of the relationships among various factors and their influence on overall UIS.

Identification of the relative importance of the various facets in determining the overall UIS may help in refining UIS instruments. The validity of such instruments may improve if different factors are given a weight corresponding to their causal influence, using a weighted average of different factors rather than a simple summation of all items. In the absence of proper representation of different factors, a dissatisfied user may obtain a higher score on satisfaction instruments employing the facet approach, compared to a somewhat satisfied user. Equity also appears to be an important facet in determining overall UIS. The results support the primacy of behavioral and organizational factors identified in the MIS research. The importance of equity issues also provides support for political perspectives.

The identification of the importance of equity should also be of interest to IS executives. Establishment of fair structural arrangements and mechanisms for MIS resource allocation can motivate users to behave fairly and minimize the political struggle for resources. Decentralization of MIS resources, user control over computing budgets, greater freedom for spending the budget internally or externally, steering committees, and fair procedures for ranking projects and resolving user conflicts may be steps to minimize conflict. Effective communication can also play a significant role. If the IS group is viewed as being partial or favoring some user groups, it can lead to perceptions of inequity.

IS executives should pay attention not only to improving the quality of information products, but also to improve customer service aspects of their role. This may be neglected or overlooked by highly skilled, technically oriented MIS personnel, who view their job as a technical challenge and derive satisfaction mainly from technical achievements rather than customer service. Suitable training programs can play an important role in educating MIS personnel to be more customer oriented and pay greater attention to customer service.

## Possible Limitations of the Study

Some limitations associated with cross-sectional surveys are likely to be of concern in this study. First, it can be risky to base causal inferences on correlational evidence from cross-sectional survey data. The only interpretation of the test of a causal model can be that the model was given a chance to be rejected. Therefore, the nature of theoretical and logical support should be kept in mind in interpreting the results.

Second, this study excluded the clerical users. Users in very small organizations with few employees or without a central MIS function may face a different set of circumstances, therefore, the results may not be applicable to such users as well.

Third, common method variance may be systematically influencing all variables, thereby inflating the correlations. The influence of this problem is likely to be uniform across all relationships considered here. Therefore, it should not influence the relative ranking. Moreover, an examination of correlations between various individual items indicated that several correlations were very low, which suggests that common method variance is not a significant problem.
