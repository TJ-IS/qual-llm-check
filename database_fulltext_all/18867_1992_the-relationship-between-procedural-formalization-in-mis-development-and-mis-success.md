# The relationship between procedural formalization in MIS development and MIS success

# A contingent analysis

Jinjoo Lee

Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), Seoul 130-650, Korea

Sang-hoon Kim

Hannam University, Taejon 300-210, Korea

The relationship between the degree of procedural formalization (adherence to prespecified procedures) in MIS development and MIS success has not previously been analysed. Here it was examined under various organizational contingencies, such as top management support, size of organization, size of MIS department, age of MIS department, and level of structuring of the task to be replaced or supported by an information system (IS). For the analysis, idealized standard phases of MIS development procedure based on an information system (IS) development life cycle (ISDLC) were devised through a review of relevant literature, practitioners' reflections and logical reasoning. The empirical results indicate that the degree of procedural formalization of MIS development and MIS success are strongly correlated, and the relationship between these two variables is significantly influenced by various contingency variables. Overall, the results of the study suggest that IS developers should adapt their level of procedural formalization to the situation and that it is of great importance, particularly for firms with lower levels of management effectiveness.

Keywords: MIS development, Procedural formalization, MIS success, Contingency variables, Information system development life cycle (ISDLC)

The IS development life cycle (ISDLC) has been used as the dominant framework for IS development. It has been a very important tool for the analysis of the process $[21,31,49]$ . The ISDLC approach is also important in end user computing and fourth generation languages use. Necco et al. $[54]$ showed empirically that the approach was being widely used with the traditional/classical approach, the structured approach, the automated approach, the prototyping approach, and the information center approach. That is, the principal components of the ISDLC phases themselves are considered almost the same in most cases of system development and can be decomposed into six subphases - feasibility study, system analysis, system design, implement, conversion and installation, and evaluation and maintenance - which are the same for each of the models.

Previous research on the management of the system development process has mostly focused on cost estimating and cost control with the context of the six development phases of the ISDLC $[3,8,11,56,65,71]$ and has not empirically demonstrated the utility of the ISDLC itself in successful MIS implementation. In addition to the effective cost control, its management may have a significant influence on the performance of the system development process. In this study, the variable “formalization,” which means the extent to which the task in the process of ISDLC is systematically documented and actively conforms to the documents, is considered to represent the type of ISDLC management. Organization theory generally agrees that the formalization dimension has an important influence on organizational performance. Formalized procedures are thought to be helpful in achieving successful development. For example, Neal and Radnor $[53]$ have shown empirically the positive relationship between the formalization and proceduralization of operations research/management science (OR/MS) activities and OR/MS group success.

In this respect, the extent to which the procedures of the ISDLC are formalized may have a profound influence on the performance of MIS development. This study attempts to understand the relationship between these. In addition, the effects of some contingency variables (top management concern, organization size, age and size of MIS department, and structure of the task to be replaced or supported) on this relationship are examined. Here, MIS success was measured as the degree of contribution that the systems make to users' execution of their tasks, basically corresponding to the concept of "information satisfactoriness" described by Goodhue [26].

The empirical investigation of the relationships proposed in this study was taken into the business organizations in South Korea, which might introduce a special bias in the results. According to one survey $[74]$ , the supply rate of computers above micro-level in South Korea was 219 per million persons as of the year 1989, which was very low compared to the advanced countries. The business computing has relatively short history (about fifteen years) and is thought to remain at the early internalization stage, aiming at replacing or supporting mainly the tasks at operational level. As of the year 1989, the proportion of computing staff having experience less than five years was found to be about 74.2%. In spite of low computing capability, the in-house development proportion turned out to amount to 60.2%, which was due to the lack of competent software houses and effective application packages.

## 2. The hypotheses

Formalization is the organizational design concept (the degree to which the work processes are standardized). Organizations tend to formalize their behavior to reduce variability, and ultimately to manage, predict, and control it effectively. One prime motive for doing so is to coordinate activities $[7]$ . It is asserted in many organizational studies that excessive formalization might result in dysfunctions in organizational performance, especially in unstable task environments $[10,13,50]$ . However, as MIS development remains unsystemized and unstructured in comparison with other organizational tasks, it is unlikely to be formalized excessively.

In MIS development, formalization enhances coordination and communication between system developers and users, or among developers of specific systems. Effective communication is critical in the whole system development process [12,15,30,33]. Since software development is still inclined to depend upon personal skills, software development procedures must be standardized and explicit in order to enhance productivity and assure the system's quality. Moreover, in large-scale IS projects, turnover of participants frequently occurs during system development. In order to make this as smooth as possible, all the tasks should be well documented and systemized.

From this argument, it is desirable that the MIS development process be formalized in order to manage it effectively. As a related study, Thayer et al. [64] showed, in a survey of 294 individuals, that computer professionals believe that “using or enforcing (existing) standards, procedures, and documentation” will solve problems associated with software engineering. This leads to the global hypothesis.

HG (Global Hypothesis): The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is positively correlated with MIS success.

The measure of formalization can be studied at both the individual system and organizational level, since the level of conformance to overall ISDLC organizational procedures will vary in each individual system development project. While the individual process seems likely to be formalized in organizations that have well defined procedures, such is not always the case. Formalization in an individual system development effort will depend on the characteristics of the task.

It is generally thought that the success of an MIS depends on the extent to which the characteristics of the MIS development process fits the particular situation, specifically, the organizational and external environments and other contingency factors $[1,19,29,48,63]$ . Therefore, the relationship between methods used in a development and its success is likely to differ depending on organizational situations and project characteristics. The contingency variables in this study are top management concern, size of organization, size of MIS department, age of MIS department and structuredness level of the focal task. The contingent relationship can be reasoned as follows.

Top management concern — The main areas of top management responsibility towards MIS should be similar to those of other functional areas: policy formulation, planning, evaluation, organizing, institution building, implementation and control. Top management with concern for MIS development and related tasks is likely to feel responsible for them. Thus, the procedure of ISDLC is apt to be more formalized if there is greater top management concern.

There is general agreement in the literature that greater top management concern and involvement in MIS development will lead to greater success $[17,24,58,70]$ . Top management can motivate users to participate in the process and use MIS $[36,57,67]$ , institute formal control procedures for MIS development, promote good communications among top management, implementors and users $[18]$ , and assure that the necessary resources are provided. When top management concern is high, the need for procedural formalization may be reduced, because the concern may make the intraorganizational environment more favorable to MIS development, or the level of formalization might already be high. However, when concern is low, favorable factors concomitant with top management concern are less influential. Therefore, procedural formalization may be more important to MIS success when top management concern is lacking, in order to make up for this handicap.

H1-1: The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is positively correlated with the level of top management concern.

H1-2: The positive influence of procedural formalization in MIS development on MIS success tends to be greater when top management concern is lower.

Size of the organization — In a large organization, formal mechanisms of control generally need to be introduced [37]. Also, larger organizations are more likely to manage IS development due to greater resources and computer experience [16], and tend to depend upon in-house development, while smaller organizations are more dependent on external software support in system development [66]. Thus the larger proportion of in-house development requires a greater degree of management and control of system development, with more formalized procedures for IS development. So procedural formalization should have a significant influence on MIS success in smaller organizations.

H2-1: The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is positively correlated with the size of the organization.

H2-2: The positive influence of procedural formalization in MIS development on MIS success tends to be greater when the size of the organization is smaller.

Size of the MIS department — In organization theory literature, many studies empirically demonstrate that in larger organizations, tasks tend to be more specialized, units more specialized, and the administrative component more developed: the larger the organization, the more elaborate its structure and processes. A larger MIS department is expected to be more formalized in its structure and processes. However, the larger the MIS department, the less significant the influence of the procedural formalization of ISDLC on MIS success.

H3-1: The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is positively correlated with the size of the MIS department.

H3-2: The positive influence of procedural formalization in MIS development on MIS success tends to be greater when the size of the MIS department is smaller.

Age of the MIS department — MIS departments may be more apt to set up standard operating procedures as they grow older: it gains experience in system development methodology and learns about coping with its environment, etc. Moreover, in older MIS departments, experienced users in developing or operating computerized information systems should have been hired. Therefore, it is likely that the level of formalization of the system development process in older MIS departments is higher, while the influence of procedural formalization on MIS success will be greater in younger departments.

H4-1: The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is positively correlated with the age of the MIS department.

H4-2: The influence of procedural formalization in MIS development on MIS success tends to be positively greater when the MIS department is younger.

Degree of structure of the task — The structure of the task is concerned with the ISDLC formalization for a particular system. Although normal overall MIS development procedures exist, there will be some variation due to the characteristics of the specific task replaced or supported by the information system. The “structuredness” level is considered to be an important task characteristic which influences the management of the IS development process $[14,27]$ . Here, the structuredness level of the focal task can be defined as the extent to which the procedure for dealing with management decision-making relevant to the task is routine and unambiguous. The less structured the task to be developed into an IS, the more inclined it is to be unanalyzable, to include exceptions, and to be changeable. In such cases, it is very difficult not only to grasp the requirements correctly, but also to substantiate and coordinate the tasks concerned with system development. To cope with these difficulties, formal and systematic managing and controlling tools for the system development process are probably required. It can also be inferred that procedural formalization of ISDLC is more essential in developing an IS successfully for unstructured tasks.

H5-1: The degree of procedural formalization in MIS development is negatively correlated with the structuredness level of the focal task.

H5-2: The positive influence of procedural formalization in MIS development on MIS success tends to be greater when the focal task is less structured.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data collection

The research method employed in this study to test the hypotheses was a field investigation of a sample of companies in Korea having MIS departments. Seventy five companies were initially selected, based on stratified random sampling, which took into account industrial sector and firm size. Of this sample, only 53 companies in 14 industries were available for data collection; the others lacked cooperation or an MIS department. For each firm, data were collected on both overall information systems (overall MIS) and a typical system that had been developed within the last 2 years and was currently in use.

The respondents included two managers from each firm: one belonging to the MIS department and the other to the department mainly using the specific system. Both were selected because they were known to be the most experienced in MIS development and implementation among the managers of their department. We asked them if they were willing to be interviewed and made appointments by telephone in advance.

Two interview protocols were used for the study. One was used in an interview with the manager of the MIS department, involving the level of procedural formalization, perceived top management concern, the name of a typical system and its task structuredness level, the size of the organization, and the size and age of the MIS department. The other was used in an interview with the manager of the user department of the typical system; it provided data on MIS success and perceived top management concern.

In testing the 9 hypotheses HG, H1-1 to H4-2, the unit of analysis was overall MIS (the portfolio of MISs) of the organization, but for the hypotheses H5-1 and H5-2 it was the system nominated in the first questionnaire. Thus perceived top management concern was measured with respect to the portfolio of MISs and task structuredness level was measured with respect to the sample system. System success and procedural formalization were measured by separate questionnaire items for both the portfolio of MISs and the specific sample system.

This data collection method was thought to have some weaknesses that could admittedly bias our measurements. First, the selection of typical system by MIS manager would tend to let successful systems selected. This problem, however, doesn't seem to be so serious because the evaluation of system performance generally is apt to be different between user and MIS department and, moreover, even though mostly successful, the degree of success might be different. The second problem was that the difference of methodologies used in system development was not taken into account in the research design. Some methodologies stress the importance of defining data relations, others focus on functions or business processes. Some methodologies define a “waterfall” process, others permit or welcome iteration. Actually, as argued in earlier research, the selection of the methodology is a important factor on system success. In this study, however, this problem was also thought to be not so serous since most of sample organizations had still been using function-oriented waterfall model. The third one was likely to result from asking one person, whether MIS or user department manager, to provide an assessment of overall systems in the organization; this was more likely to happen when he or she had not first hand experience with systems. However, in our opinion the selection of competent managers as respondents could reduce this possibility somewhat. They are generally more experienced and have a better overall view of MIS development and its related areas compared to the personnel at operational levels: they often belong to an MIS steering committee, and thus enables them to get wide and detailed information about overall MIS-related tasks and problems. To further lessen the bias, each structured interview was preceded by an open-ended discussion. Whenever we sensed a discrepancy between the content of the general discussion and the specific answer to a question, we verified the respondent's answer. Our interviews with other managers also gave us a chance to verify the reliability of our data. Complete lists of the questionnaire items are provided in Appendix 2.

## 3.2. Operationalization and measurement

Procedural formalization in MIS development — The formalization of the system development process was operationalized as “the degree to which various procedures contained in a normative ISDLC are formalized and actively conformed to.” Here the normative ISDLC was a model consisting of 35 subphases constructed by reviewing and integrating 28 studies or textbooks 4,5,6,9,14,18,20,22,28,32,34,38,39,40,41,42,44,46,47,51,55,59,60,61,68,69,72,73] relating to the ISDLC (The relation between this normative ISDLC and 28 ones is presented in Appendix 1). To test the content validity of this model, 19 MIS professionals who had First or Second Degree Korean Information Processing Engineer Qualifications were interviewed. These were all actually engaged in MIS development projects. They were asked to point out which subphases were redundant and whether new subphases should be incorporated into the normative ISDLC. For each subphase, normative ISDLC subphases were included by the majority vote of those experts. Through this process, the original 35 subphases were reduced to 27 subphases grouped into six stages in terms of task sequence and linkage, as shown in Table 1. In this model, the substage “generating the alternatives” involves seeking the ways to recruit the necessary resources, and investigating the alternatives for system development methods and scheduling the project. Thus, this substage was treated as a part of the feasibility study stage, rather than a part of system design stage.

To measure the degree of procedural formalization of the MIS development process, two items using 5-point scales were applied to all procedures of the normative ISDLC model. One, which sought indications of the degree of formalization of each procedure, asked for evaluation of “the degree to which the job descriptions were documented systematically and actively conformed to in developing an IS.” The other item, which sought indications of the importance of each procedure, asked for evaluation of “how important it is thought to be as one of the ISDLC subphases.” The simple average of all responses to the latter item was used as the index of weight of each component. The rationale for weighting the subphases and for calculating an average weight across all respondents was that the importance of the subphases might be different and be differently evaluated according to the respondent. Also, the nominal scale item asking “whether each subphase was considered in performing system development” was used. If any subphase was not considered, the degree of formalization of that subphase was rated to be zero. Consequently, the way to compute the degree of procedural formalization of each stage ( $IS_{j}$ ) and overall ISDLC (TS) may best be expressed by the following mathematical formula:

$$
I S _ {j} = \frac {1}{m _ {j}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m _ {j}} \left(w _ {i j} * F _ {i j}\right),
$$

$$
T S = \frac {1}{p} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} I S _ {j},
$$

where $m_{j}=$ number of ISDLC subphases in stage j, $w_{ij}=$ the index of weight of subphase i as one of ISDLC subphases in stage j, which was calculated by $(1/n)\Sigma_{k=1}^{n}W_{ijk}$ (n = number of respondents, $W_{ijk} = \text{the importance score of subphase } i$ in stage j), $F_{ij} = \text{the formalization score of subphase } i$ as one of ISDLC subphases in stage j, p = number of stages composing the ISDLC.

This computation formula of ISDLC procedural formalization was used both for overall MIS and for the specific nominated sample system.

MIS success — The ideal indicator of MIS success would be to aggregate its organizational benefits and compare it with those from alternative investments. In reality, a strictly economic assessment of system performance is very difficult and frequently arbitrary. In this context, system acceptance or changes in user behavior and attitude [45], system quality [23] and system usage [43] have been more frequently measured as surrogate indicators. User information system satisfaction has been used as the most common variable of MIS success [35]. Goodhue [26] has argued that user information system satisfaction must be divided into two constructs: one is IS “satisfaction” brought about by the correspondence between the information system’s intrinsic benefits and the needs of the user, and the other is IS “satisfactoriness” resulting from the correspondence between the job requirements and system functionality. This study utilized a measure of user information system satisfactoriness as the indicator of MIS success. This was based on a set of ten questionnaire items that were constructed through reviewing and integrating the existing related studies. The items were scored on a 5-point Likert-type scale. Separate questionnaire items were used to measure the success of both the portfolio of MISs and the nominated system. To confirm the construct validity of these items, the principal component factor analysis using VARIMAX rotation was executed in each case, and as a result, two of the items that were the same in both cases were discarded in making up the MIS success factor. Also, to measure internal consistency with respect to these remaining eight items, a reliability test using Cronbach alpha was performed. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for overall MIS success and specific system success were 0.859 and 0.894 respectively. These values could not be improved by the deletion of any item. The result of the factor analysis and fairly high coefficients of reliability encouraged the utilization of the arithmetic mean of the remaining eight items' scores to measure overall MIS success and the success of the specific nominated information system.

Top management concern — It has been asserted in many studies that there can be considerable difference between the MIS and user departments in their views and perceptions about the degree of top management concern for MIS development and operation. To cope with this potential hazard here, the top management concern was measured by issuing questionnaires to both MIS and the user department managers. The items originated partly from Vanlommel and De-Brabander [67] and were modified to suit the Korean situation. The measurement of the indicators of perceived top management concern was based on seven items in the questionnaire for the MIS department managers and on 4 items in that for the user department managers. The scoring of each item was on a 5-point Likert-type scale. The construct validity of these two kinds of items was tested by the principal component analysis using VARIMAX rotation. This resulted in excluding three items of the former questionnaire in terms of factor loadings. The Cronbach alpha coefficients were asked as a reliability test for the remaining four items for both MIS and user department managers. By an iterative technique for item analysis, it was shown that none of the former four items needed to be changed but one of the latter items had to be dropped in order to increase the alpha values. The final Cronbach alpha coefficients after item analysis were 0.813 and 0.802 for the remaining four and three items. Hence, in order to reflect the opinions of both MIS and user department managers, the index of perceived top management concern in a firm was the average of two measurement values: the arithmetic mean of the remaining four items in the questionnaire for the MIS departments managers, and that of the remaining three items for user departments managers.

Size of the organization — Previous research has used total assets, total revenue, total sales volume, number of employees, etc., as measures of organizational size. In order to reduce the effect of the nature of the industry to which the organization belonged, this study measured organizational size by the number of employees, because the sampling was done on different industries. The number of employees of the sampled organizations ranged from 86 persons to 17,000 and was skewed to the large side. To secure normal distribution, the logarithm of the number of employees was used in the statistical analysis.

Age of the MIS department — The measure of this variable was elapsed months from the establishment date of the formal MIS department to the time the questionnaire was completed. This ranged from 16 to 216, and was skewed to the high side. The logarithm of the number of months was used as the index of this variable in the statistical analysis.

Size of the MIS department — The measure of this variable was the number of MIS personnel in the MIS department; it ranged from 6 to 280 and was skewed to the large side. Once again, the logarithm of the number of personnel was used.

Level of task structuredness — The more standardized, defined, specified, routine, simple, concrete and easily documented a task is, the more structured it is generally said to be [62]. The measurement of task structuredness was based on a set of 5 questionnaire items which described these task characteristics. Each item was scored using a 5-point Likert-type scale. To confirm whether these items measured the construct of the degree of structuredness, the principal component factor analysis using VARIMAX rotation was performed; this resulted in 2 factors containing 3 items and 2 items respectively. But one factor consisting of 2 items, which seemed to represent task repetitiveness, had very low factor loadings and its eigenvalue was small (0.8). The other factor with the remaining 3 items representing the construct of task standardization had high factor loadings and a large eigenvalue (3.2), and thus was selected as the measurement instrument for the structuredness level in this study. The internal consistency with respect to these 3 items was tested and the Cronbach alpha coefficient was 0.787 without eliminating any item by item analysis. Hence, the index of task structuredness level was defined as the arithmetic mean of these 3 item scores.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. General findings

Some descriptive statistics of the sample in this study are presented in Table 2. It shows that the standard deviation of the variables substantiates enough variation for statistical analysis.

To explain the relationships between variables, a matrix of Pearson correlation coefficients is shown in Table 3. The contingency variables are strongly intercorrelated except for the relationship between top management support and size of organization. As “structuredness level” was different in its unit of analysis from the other contingency variables, the relationship between the structuredness level and the other contingency variables was not applicable. The significant and positive correlation between the degree of procedural formalization of ISDLC and MIS success in cases of both overall MIS and specific sample systems supports the global hypothesis in this study. Also, MIS success is significantly and positively correlated with top management concern and size of MIS department. In particular, the positive correlation between MIS success and top management concern is consistent with many previous studies. The degree of procedural formalization of ISDLC is significantly positively correlated with top management support, organization size and MIS department size, but negatively correlated with structuredness level of the task to be developed into IS.

However, where the intercorrelation between the variables is significant, the simple Pearson correlation coefficients between specific two variables may be spurious. In order to clarify and test the hypothesized relationships between the variables more effectively, it is necessary to use partial correlation coefficients. That is, the correlation between any two variables should be investigated while controlling for the effect of the other variables.

## 4.2. Relationship between procedural formalization in MIS development and MIS success

The simple (Pearson) correlation coefficients between procedural formalization of ISDLC and MIS success are shown in parentheses on Table 4. Also, in order to examine the relationship between them, while removing the effect of the other variables included in the model, the fourth-order partial correlation coefficients controlling simultaneously for top management support, size of organization, size of MIS department, and age of the MIS department were computed with respect to overall MIS. The computation results show that the procedural formalization for overall ISDLC is significantly positively correlated with overall MIS success.

The procedural formalization for all the stages of ISDLC, except for the stage of conversion and installation, are significantly positively correlated with MIS success. The correlation is particularly strong in the implementation stage: the partial correlation coefficient is 0.44 with respect to overall MIS. The lack of a significant relationship between procedural formalization and MIS success in the stage of conversion and installation might be due to the fact that the tasks at this stage tend to be unique to the situation and are thus hard to formalize; therefore, managerial efforts like formalization are hardly influential on successful system development. Overall, the global hypothesis HG is supported and we may conclude that procedural formalization of the MIS development process is significantly associated with MIS success, although its association varies depending on the specific stage of ISDLC.

## 4.4. Moderating effects of contingency variables

To find the moderating effect of contingency factors, a structural analysis using regressions where the independent variables are entered in one step and moderators in another was desirable. However, there were some potential problems with using regression in this study. First, with a limited sample size (a maximum of 53 cases) and nearly a dozen explanatory variables after interaction terms are included, power was thought to be obviously reduced. Secondly, there was a serious multicollinearity between independent variables. Lastly, moderating effects are known to be notoriously difficult to examine via path analysis; this problem would be compounded by the sample size. Taking these problems into account, the use of partial correlation was thought to be appropriate. That is, the total sample of organizations was divided into two groups using the median value for each contingency variable as a basis for the split, and third-order partial correlation coefficients between the degree of procedural formalization and MIS success controlling for the other contingency variables for each group regarding four contingency variables (top management concern, organization size, age and size of MIS department) were computed as Table 6. However, zero-order (Pearson) correlation coefficients were figured for groups on structuredness level, which was due to the difference in unit of analysis between the structuredness level and the other four contingency variables.

The correlations between overall procedural formalization and system success were positive at beyond the 0.10 significance level for both sub-samples of high and low top management concern, and large and small MIS departments. While the procedural formalization was significantly correlated with system success for small organizations, young MIS departments and high levels of task structuredness, it was not in the opposite cases.

The Fisher Z statistics were used to determine if the correlation coefficients of both sub-samples represented populations having the different true correlations with respect to all contingent variables. The results showed that the difference in the correlations between the procedural formalization of overall ISDLC and system success was statistically significant at the 0.09 level for only one variable, organization size. That is, hypothesis H2-2 predicting that the association of procedural formalization with system success were positively greater in the smaller organizations was supported, but the remaining hypotheses were not. This may be because the MIS development procedure of large organizations is to a considerable extent formalized, and so the procedural formalization appears to be less influential on MIS success. Also, it seems to be likely that great resource capability of large organizations may reduce the effect of procedural formalization on MIS success.

Looking at the correlations between procedural formalization and system success in the individual stage of ISDLC, a different picture emerges. The procedural formalization of system analysis was found to be more important to system success in young MIS departments than in old ones. The relationship between the procedural formalization of the system design stage and system success was more strongly associated in small MIS department and high task structuredness sub-samples. The procedural formalization of the conversion and installation stage was associated with system success where the organization was small and the task was more structured. The procedural formalization of the evaluation and maintenance stage was associated with system success in small organizations and MIS departments than in large ones.

Other than these results, in spite of substantial differences in correlation coefficients for subsamples regarding almost all the contingency variables, we could not obtain statistically significant differences using Fisher Z test. In our opinion, however, the failure to achieve significance at the pre-arranged 0.1 level should not lead to immediate dismissal of the hypothesized relationship due to the possibility of Type II error and the small sample size. What is more, this study was exploratory research and it focused on generating hypotheses rather than testing them rigorously, so the application of a low significance level is not necessarily desirable. Since Type I and Type II errors are inversely related to each other, it is impossible to minimize both of them at the same time without increasing the degree of freedom (or, sample size). Whether to emphasize minimizing Type I error or Type II error depends on the problem being investigated. It was thought that the procedural formalization of ISDLC does not require a great deal of an organization's resources, while having a relatively strong impact on system success; moreover, procedural formalization turned out to be at least positively associated with system success for the most part. Therefore, it is desirable to minimize the possibility of neglecting the formalization of the system development process (that is, to minimize Type II error in this case). Also, it was thought that the sample size was not enough large for the differences of the partial correlation coefficients to be statistically significant. From this point of view, the results in this study could give an opportunity for more rigorous testing and, on the other hand, provide potentially significant but cautious implications as a guideline to managing system development.

In this sense, we suggest that though the results were not significant by the Fisher Z test, they were worthy of attention. For example, the procedural formalization of ISDLC had a significant association with system success in young MIS departments (correlation coefficient: 0.53), but had a insignificant and weak association with system success in old MIS departments (correlation coefficient: 0.25). This finding might suggest that procedural formalization may be less important in old MIS departments due to the accumulation of system development know-how and user experience.

In task structuredness level, contradictory results among the ISDLC stages were found. In the less structured task sub-sample, procedural formalization was significantly positively correlated with system success in the feasibility study and evaluation and maintenance stages (correlation coefficients: 0.49 and 0.44 respectively) but not in the other stages, the correlations were just the opposite in the more structured sub-sample. As a whole, Fisher Z test validated the differences between both sub-samples concerning task structuredness at only two stages: system design and conversion and installation. These results are suggestive, though they are statistically weak. In developing systems for less structured tasks, the procedural formalization of the four stages – system analysis, system design, implement, and conversion and installation – had no significant relation to system success. These findings are consistent with the argument of the literature about prototyping methodology (the inadequacy and rigidity of the ISDLC procedure for dynamic and complex task environments). However, procedural formalization at the feasibility study and evaluation and maintenance stages seems to have significant positive influence on system success in developing less structured areas.

## 5. Conclusions and implications

In managing the MIS development process, the formalization of the development process itself was found to have a significant positive relationship with MIS success for overall ISDLC and all the ISDLC stages except for conversion and installation. This relationship turned out to exist above and beyond the effects of top management concern, size of the organization, size and age of the MIS department, and structuredness level of the focal task. From this, we could conclude that in the case of MIS development and operations, formalization is likely to ease coordination more than it leads to dysfunctional outcomes, probably because MIS development tasks are relatively unstructured compared to other tasks. The lack of a significant relationship in the conversion and installation stage might be due to the fact that tasks in this stage, such as preparing a users' manual, training users, and executing database conversion, are very technical, specialized and somewhat automatically guaranteed activities; thus, managerial efforts like formalization of procedures are not influential in achieving system development success. Also, some of the variables considered as a set of contingency factors in this study turned out to be associated with the degree of procedural formalization: top management concern had a significant positive association with the procedural formalization, while age of MIS department and task structuredness level had significant negative associations with procedural formalization. However, size of organization and MIS department had no significant association with it.

The study also gave weak but partial evidence for the moderating effects of these contingency variables on the relationship between the procedural formalization of ISDLC and MIS success. With respect to overall ISDLC, the moderating effects were found to be significant only for size of organization and not for the other variables. This implies that procedural formalization could make more contributions to MIS success in smaller organizations. Considering the positive relationship between organization size and procedural formalization, this moderating effect of organization size suggests that procedural formalization may be necessary but not sufficient for MIS success. Depending on the stage of ISDLC, the relationship between the procedural formalization and system success was moderated in different ways by the contingency variables. That is, at the system analysis stage procedural formalization had more influence on system success in younger MIS departments, and at the system design stage it had more influence when the MIS department was smaller and the IS-object task was more structured. Formalization of conversion and installation was more positively related to system success in cases of smaller organization and greater task structuredness. When the size of the organization and of the MIS department were smaller, formalization of the evaluation and maintenance stage was more contributive to system success.

Obviously, there were some limitations in this study. First, the differences in partial correlation coefficients between both sub-samples split using median value for each contingency variable were statistically significant using Fisher Z statistic for only a few variables and stages. However, this might be partly due to the small sample size. The hypotheses need to be validated with larger samples in different situations. Second, MIS success was measured only by a subjective indicator of user information satisfaction. This measure may be inconsistent with objective indicators, such as cost and speed of system development, use rate of MIS, benefits from the MIS, etc. For example, procedural formalization tends to increase administration cost and so it is unclear whether procedural formalization, even though an aid in increasing user information satisfaction, can make a contribution to the ultimate goal of organizational and economical benefits. Third, the contingency variables were not exhaustive and should be extended. Finally, since the effect of procedural formalization on system success is very likely to depend upon cultural and environmental characteristics, the findings in this study carried out in a developing country, South Korea, must be validated in other situations, particularly in advanced countries. The procedural formalization of IS-DLC may have less influence on MIS success in advanced countries, because most organizations have already formalized the development process to a considerable extent and have accumulated various techniques and know-how relating to system development. However, in advanced countries the moderating effects of contingency variables may also occur in the same manner as here. Thus our findings should be interpreted very cautiously and the generalization of our findings should be restricted.

Nevertheless, the findings of this study offer several theoretical implications. First, the procedural formalization of ISDLC can be conceptualized and operationalized originally through an integration of literature reviews and practitioners' refinement. And the relationship between the procedural formalization of MIS development and MIS success was demonstrated empirically. Second, a contingent approach was taken in analyzing the relationship between procedural formalization of MIS development and MIS success and how the relationships varied depending on organizational situations and the characteristic of the task to be developed into an IS was tested statistically by means of the Fisher Z statistic. Finally, starting from the findings of this study, further research areas and questions relating to the effective management of MIS development could be developed. It would be interesting to validate the causal relationship between the procedural formalization and MIS success. We might hypothesize that procedural formalization is an indicator of successful groups rather than a cause of success. And when we accept that procedural formalization is a necessary condition for MIS success, then the next task is to identify the sufficient conditions for MIS success, or the major successful factors for the group of greater degree of procedural formalization, other than the variable, procedural formalization.

The practical implications of the findings are as follows: First, the idealized and formalized process of MIS development in Table 1, which might be a benchmark of ISDLC (Information System Development Life Cycle), could be utilized as a mandatory IS development management tool for MIS success. Second, the findings propose criteria for selecting effective management strategies for successful MIS development according to different organizational situations and project characteristics.
