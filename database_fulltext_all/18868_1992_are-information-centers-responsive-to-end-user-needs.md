# Are information centers responsive to end user needs?

Rex Kelly Rainer, Jr. and Houston H. Carr

Department of Management, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA

End user computing (EUC) has developed into a critical part of the information resource of the organization. The information center (IC), an essential support and control mechanism for EUC, has also become a critical component of the organization's information resource. ICs support the end users by providing services for them. Our study surveyed 126 ICs to determine which services they presently offer, which ones they do not offer, and which ones they previously offered but discontinued. In addition, the study collected data on services requested by the end users. This paper furnishes a definitive catalog of services offered by ICs and compares them with the services requested by end users. The findings illustrate the responsiveness of ICs to their end user communities. Even though ICs typically offer a broad variety of services, there are differences in the services most offered by ICs and services most requested by end users.

Keywords: End User, End User Computing, Information Center.

## Introduction

Since the late 1960's, the end-user computing phenomenon has emerged to become a vital part of the organization's information resource. [3,4] Many firms, noting the need to support but control this phenomenon, have created information centers (ICs).

IBM Canada formalized this concept in 1974, and ICs have proliferated rapidly since that time. [14] The 1986 American Management Association Report on ICs stated that one third of them were less than 18 months old and that the annual rate of increase in user requests for IC services exceeded $15\%$ . [2] This indicates that the IC has become an important organizational information resource that impacts the manner in which end users receive information services.

## Review of Literature

Hammond first described the character of IC services with his premise:

“If provided proper education, technical support, usable tools, data availability, and convenient access to the system, users may directly and rapidly satisfy a part of their business area requirements that depend on an IS environment.” [14]

Chian et al. [9] derived a model of categories of services that an IC should nominally offer:

“(1) education in the use of hardware and software applications;

(2) timely assistance to user questions;

(3) consultation to find solutions to work problems through information technology;

(4) administrative support (e.g., database access, authorization, and passwords);

(5) rapid access to reference manuals;

(6) assistance in developing and debugging programs;

(7) product evaluation and planning (e.g., standardization issues, etc.).”

Other researchers have investigated IC services. Wetherbe and Leitheiser [18] generated a general set of IC services. Vijayaraman [17] developed a list of 49 discrete IC services as an expansion of Chian's categories of IC services. In our research, we combined the IC services from previous studies, eliminated duplicates, and compiled a comprehensive list of 35 IC services, grouped into six service categories.

The explosive growth of EUC and management's consequent need to support and control the phenomenon have been extensively chronicled by previous authors [1,3,4,5,6,8,10,11,13,16]. They recognize that end users have needs and that organizations must provide services to meet those needs. Additionally, these studies see the IC as an excellent method for providing many, if not all, of these services. However, previous research has not empirically examined IC responsiveness to end user needs.

## Methodology

We utilized a multi-part questionnaire to investigate IC services. After pretesting, the questionnaire was mailed to IC managers in a large sample of geographically dispersed firms. The first part of the questionnaire gathered demographic data on each organization; this included industry type, age and size of the IC, size of the user community served by the IC, size of the IS department, and to whom the IC manager reported. The second part of the questionnaire asked each respondent whether each of the 35 services were now provided, not provided, or previously provided but now discontinued. The third part provided space for respondents to indicate services that were not included in the questionnaire. In the fourth part, the IC managers indicated if end users requested the service.

The survey was sent to 211 organizations known to have ICs, resulting in a convenience sample. One hundred responses were received from the initial mailing. Three weeks after the initial mailing, a follow-up was sent to non-respondents. An additional 34 responses were received. T-tests were performed on the demographic variables, comparing the first and second groups of responses. No significant statistical differences were noted, so we concluded that there was no non-response bias, and the two groups of responses could be considered together in the study. The overall response rate is therefore, 134/211, or 63.5%.

However, eight organizations indicated that their ICs had been disbanded and did not complete the questionnaire. Consequently, the percentages and statistical tests in this study are based on a sample size of 126.

## Findings

Demographics of Organizations and ICs

Industry Type. Respondent organizations represent a variety of industries from diverse geographic areas solely within the United States. Manufacturing, utility, government/military, and educational organizations account for approximately one half of the firms in the survey.

Age of the IC. As previously stated, the 1986 American Management Report indicated that one third of the IC's responding to their survey were less than 18 months old. In contrast, only two of our IC's were less than 18 months old. The median age is 50 months as of December, 1988. This suggests that the respondent ICs in our study are older and more established, and therefore may tend to have a more fully developed and stable group of services.

IC Staff Size. The ICs range in size from one to 48 people. The mean size is 6.8 and the median size is five. Ninety-three percent of the ICs have a manager. An interesting finding is that 49% of these managers also have other duties.

IS Department Size. IS departments in the survey employ, on average, 275 people. However, the median size is 85 people. This occurs because several IS departments are very large, with six firms reporting staff sizes of 1000 or more.

Number of Users Supported by the IC. Survey results indicate that the ICs support an average of 1625 users, but the median size is 500. Again, this occurs because nine firms report that their ICs support user communities of over 5000 people. Noting that the median number of IC personnel is five, we see that the typical IC staff supports approximately 100 users each.

Centralized IC Facilities. The survey gathered data on whether the IC had formal, centralized facilities in which to serve users. Eighty-one percent of the IC's report that they have such facilities.

## Information Center Services

Overview of All IC Services. The first objective of this study was to develop a comprehensive list of IC services. The authors reviewed previous research to develop an initial list. This list was pretested with five IC managers. The additions, deletions, and modifications they suggested were incorporated into the survey instrument.

To allow for addition of IC services, the questionnaire provided space for respondents to list services they felt should have been included. Thirty-two respondents added 24 services (some were mentioned more than once). After careful examination, the authors concluded that all 24 had already been included in the questionnaire, but in different terms (e.g., local area networks from respondents was included in the questionnaire services of telecommunications hardware and telecommunications software). Therefore, we feel that the list of IC services is comprehensive.

Our research groups the 35 IC services into six categories: hardware, software, data, functional, training and education, and miscellaneous. Table A in the Appendix shows all 35 grouped under their respective categories, as well as how often each service is offered, not offered, or previously offered. Table B ranks all services, from highest to lowest, by the percentage of ICs that offer each service. Previous research noted that IC services did not vary among different industry types, but did vary with IC age, IC staff size, and the number of users supported [7].

Tables A and B support conventional wisdom in many areas. Services that the ICs offer most often are expected (e.g., consult on user problems, assist users in choosing software, etc.). Some of the services that ICs offer least (e.g., providing application development and generating prototypes) do not typically fall within the purview of an IC.

Tables A and B do point out several surprises. ICs offer security seminars the least often of any service, even though security is a global MIS/DP concern. We would expect it to be a more important concern of ICs, especially with the continued proliferation of end user computing in the firm. Another unexpected finding was that the data category is not frequently offered. Data would be expected to rank high among IC concerns as providing users with access to data is considered a major advantage of providing an IC. We note that Hammond dealt with the mainframe aspects of EUC and the IC (circa 1981). However, the issue of end user access to corporate data remains critically important even in today's PC/EUC environment.

Table A also shows what IC services have been discontinued. The newsletter service has been the major casualty, perhaps as a cost-cutting measure. We note that ICs seem to be discontinuing all three types of training (basic, advanced, and seminars). This may indicate that the user community is becoming more knowledgeable and therefore needs less training. Alternatively, users may be receiving training through other channels, perhaps informally from others.

## IC Services Requested by End Users

Tables 1–6 show each service category and the individual services in that category. Each service has the percentage of ICs that offer it and its rank among all 35 services. In addition, each service has the percentage of ICs in which end users requested it and its rank among all 35 services. Finally, each service has the difference between its two rankings. A negative difference means that a service ranks higher among all 35 services in percentage of ICs offering it than in percentage of ICs in which end users requested it. We use these differences as a construct for the relative value of a service. That is, a negative difference implies that ICs appear to place relatively more importance on a service than end users do.

The implication is that a difference of zero means that ICs and end users appear to place equal importance on that service. For an IC to be completely responsive to end user needs, all differences would equal zero.

Five of the six services in the Hardware Support category were offered by at least two-thirds of the responding ICs. The large number of ICs offering these services is not surprising as they provide support for technical aspects of EUC.

Three of the services in this category rank much higher in IC offerings than in end user requests (i.e., have large negative differences). The end users seem to place less importance on the services of evaluating hardware, selection of hardware, and dealing with hardware problems than the ICs do. This suggests that end users are typically neither concerned nor knowledgeable about the hardware infrastructure of their computer based information systems. Further, they may not request these services from the IC because the MIS/DP department in their firms is responsible for providing such services.

Three services rank higher in end user requests than IC offerings. Particularly, end users place more importance on demonstrating hardware than ICs do. They may not have an organizational entity to perform this service, while the MIS/DP department may handle other hardware services but not have the time nor inclination to demonstrate hardware for end users.

The percentage of ICs that will install and support hardware is unusual; such services would typically be provided by the MIS/DP department. Perhaps ICs offer this service because the MIS/DP department has delegated all PC operations to the IC.

ICs offer services in the Software Support category with high frequency. The large negative differences in evaluating software packages and assisting in selection of s/w packages indicate that ICs assume more interest than end users exhibit. Possibly, as they become more familiar with software packages over time, end users need these services less.

Demonstrating software receives a high ranking from ICs and end users, indicating its overall importance. Maintaining a software library, by contrast, has a low ranking with both ICs and end users.

The large positive difference in assist with application maintenance signifies that end users request the service with higher relative frequency than ICs offer it. This indicates that end users have trouble changing applications as business conditions change.

The Data Support category consists of services which attempt to meet corporate data needs. Most ICs offer to assist user in locating data and assist user in data transfer while far fewer ICs offer to maintain catalog of available data and provide backup, recovery, and archiving. These variations suggest that the MIS/DP departments usually perform the latter two services.

In all four services, there are positive differences, signifying the importance that end users place on corporate data. In particular, end users request assist user in locating data and assist with data transfer more, even though a very high percentage of ICs provide them. End users also emphasize providing backup, recovery, and archiving; however, we note that this service is not requested with particularly high frequency. The service, maintain catalog of available data is considered unimportant by ICs and end users.

The Functional Support category consists of services pertaining to the development of end user applications. These all have differences with relatively large absolute values, indicating divergences between the views of ICs and end users.

The three services with large negative differences, determine if application suitable for EUC, consult on user problems, and assist user in choosing specific s/w, are almost universally offered by ICs. End users appear unconcerned with, or lacking knowledge about, the suitability of their applications. This finding is not surprising, as end user are usually not trained in the differences between formal development versus end user development. The other two services are considered fundamental to ICs, but perhaps end users take them for granted or see them implicitly included in other services.

Five services have large positive differences. Conventional wisdom holds that ICs should help users do their own work, not do their work for them. In contrast, nearly two-thirds of ICs offer develop applications for or with users and one-third help to generate prototypes. However, end users rank both services very highly, signifying that they need assistance in application development.

The Training and Education category is integral to the basic charter of an information center. The three services with negative differences suggest that the user community is becoming more knowledgeable and does not need training services as much as they did in the past. End users may also be seeking training through informal channels, such as other users. Also, easier-to-use software packages may be lessening the need for training.

Less than one-half of ICs offer providing training in data transfer, but end users often request the service. End users are consistently showing that they are very interested in access to corporate data. The increased importance of information systems' security throughout the organization makes the lack of interest in providing security seminars surprising. Maybe the MIS/DP department handles security issues, although the IC would be a logical group to deal with PC security issues. Perhaps end users are not overly cognizant of security issues.

Four services in the Miscellaneous Support category are ranked similarly (and not highly) by ICs and end users. One service, conduct user-group meetings, ranks relatively higher in end user requests than in ICs offering it, but neither rank it particularly high. Sixty percent of ICs publish a newsletter, but end users rank the service last.

Surprisingly, end user and IC rankings differ in four of the six service groups. The end users rank the Data and Functional Support categories first and second, followed by the Software Support and Hardware Support groups. Clearly, end users want access to corporate data and assistance in all phases of application development. Table 8 provides support for this. The 15 services with negative differences have traditionally been included in the charter of an IC (e.g., hardware support, software support, consulting, and training). Not surprisingly, all of these services are offered by at least 60 percent of the ICs. However, the negative differences suggest that end users find them less important.

## Conclusion

The finding that 33 of 35 services have significant differences suggests that ICs are not responsive to end user needs. ICs still appear to be offering services first delineated in the middle 1970's. By continuing only to offer them, ICs are not responsive to the changing nature of end users and EUC. Two methods are possible for ICs to become more responsive: they may regularly survey end user requirements and/or they may distribute IC staff members to end user departments.

However, the picture is not all bleak. This study shows some indications that ICs are changing their service offerings to meet end user needs. For example, conventional wisdom recognizes that many end users are now trained in computer and software use throughout their education and therefore may need less training when they enter the workplace. In response, ICs appear to be phasing out training and some hardware support services. The focus of end users appears to be changing from how to use automation to how to apply computers and software to their business problems. ICs should continue to adapt their service mix to best serve their users and their changing needs.
