# Rethinking the effectiveness measures of decision support systems

Godwin Udo

College of Business Administration, Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN 38505, USA

Although many laboratory studies conclude otherwise, field study researchers still measure DSS effectiveness by system usage, based on the assumption that frequent use of a system implies its potential benefits. This study used data from a field study to test the accuracy of the assumption. Based on literature review, eight potential benefits of DSS were identified and used as measures of its effectiveness. The respondents included 201 experienced users from manufacturing and financial services industries who used DSSs on a regular basis. The results suggest that system usage has a significant relationship with only one of the eight effectiveness measures, namely: competitive edge.

Keywords: System Usage, DSS Evaluation, Performance Measures of DSS, Tangible and Intangible Benefits of DSS, Strategic Role/Benefits of DSS

## 1. Introduction

The two commonly used measures of DSS effectiveness for most field studies are user satisfaction and system usage. Many observers have concluded that these two are used because other, more realistic measures of DSS are difficult to determine. The underlying assumption of studies that measure the performance of DSS by system usage is that the more a system is used, the greater the benefits. Many laboratory experiments have shown that this assumption is not always true, which leads us to rethink the realistic measures of DSS effectiveness. More than ever, they are critical now that DSS has assumed such strategic importance in the organizations, and since the DSS budget has reached a significant level that must be justified. DSSs are now considered an investment from which specific, tangible returns are to be expected.

As DSS budgets grow, resource allocation becomes more challenging to managers. As a result, reliable methods of assessing DSS's effectiveness, both in absolute and relative terms, and of identifying causes of poor performance assume greater importance [32]. Research studies must evolve and change as the role and expectations of DSS evolve and change. Measuring effectiveness of DSS with system usage might have been sufficient in earlier stages when DSS served a less important role, but certainly this is not enough now.

## 2. Previous studies

Many researchers agree that more studies are needed in the area of DSS evaluation. In response to this need, some $[27,42]$ have proposed different DSS evaluation frameworks. Adelman and Donnell [1] propose a framework that addresses issues regarding the interface between the DSS and its users, their organization, and the larger competitive environment. According to them, the quality of the DSS evaluation depends on two factors: (1) the types of measures of effectiveness of the evaluation factors and (2) the “realism” of the experimental simulation of the interface.

There are three types of data collection procedures to consider with this evaluation framework: subjective judgment, expert observation, and objective measurement. Subjective judgment requires users to score their experiences with the DSS. Expert observation also involves subjective judgment, but DSS experts do the scoring. Objective measurement is usually associated with empirical experimentation and comparison.

Almost all the field studies reviewed fall under subject judgment (users scoring their experiences with DSS). However, it appears that none of the field studies will be considered as having a quality evaluation by this framework. It states that the quality of the evaluation depends on the type of effectiveness measure used. But field studies measure effectiveness either by (1) system usage, (b) user satisfaction, or (c) system usage and user satisfaction. A summary of these studies is given in Table 1.

Table 2 is a summary of the studies that cast a shadow of doubt on system usage and user satisfaction as measures of DSS. Some of the studies were compiled by Shara, Barr and McDonnell [37]. Other authors have begun to question the use of system usage as a measure of DSS effectiveness. Trice and Treacy [40] have proposed that it be used as an intervening variable to other variables affecting the performance of DSS. In a previous study, I used system usage as an independent variable on a regression analysis study. My findings indicated that, of the eight measures of DSS performance, system usage was significant in explaining the variability in decision quality, competitive edge, improved communication, and production cost reduction, but no relationship was established between system usage and increased productivity, time-savings, overall user satisfaction, or overall cost effectiveness. Janulaitis [21] and Nolan [34] use return-on-investment (ROI) to measure the impact of information systems (IS) as a strategic weapon. Some writers criticize ROI as a benchmark for DSS effectiveness on the grounds that an information system is only partially responsible for it. A compromise approach would be for all DSS users to collectively give their perceived impact of the system on ROI: e.g. they could be asked to give the percentage of ROI due to DSS utilization using delphi method. Vincent [43] attempts to assess information as an “economic commodity” which could appear on a balance sheet as an asset.

The benefits, as listed in the literature, are both qualitative and quantitative. Keen [22] offers the following list:

\- increase the number of alternatives examined,

\- better understanding of the business,

\- fast response to expected situations,

● ability to carry out ad hoc analyses,

\- new insights and learning,

\- improved communication,

\- better control,

\- cost savings,

\- better decisions,

\- more effective teamwork,

\- time savings, and

\- making better use information.

These potential benefits lead many researchers and practitioners to believe that DSS can be the most powerful strategic weapons ever known to organizations. The organizational and individual impacts of DSSs have been projected by many. Turban [41] categorizes the impacts and benefits into organizational and individual impacts. The first reflects on structure, centralization of authority, and power and status; while the latter reflects on: productivity, job satisfaction, changes in supervision, and shortening employee career ladders.

The use of information for competitive advantage has received renewed attention in the field of management strategy. Organizations generally accept the fact that information systems can serve as the leading edge of a firm's strategy. Raghunathan and King [35] maintain that if strategic system planning is to continue to grow and prosper, its worth must be demonstrated at two levels: (1) in the firm, where specific planning approaches can be evaluated and where diagnoses can be made of their benefits, costs, strengths, and weaknesses; (2) across firms, so that the overall worth of IS planning can be assessed. Information systems have become a key part of distinctive competitiveness in the marketplace. Through literature review, Mason [28] shows that information plays three roles in a firm's strategy, namely: stewardship, guidance and vanguard roles. Stewardship keeps track of “what things are where” and “who owes what to whom.” Guiding, the role of business decision making, is an integral part of the product or service the business offers its customers. The third role is that of creating the opportunities for organizations to make IS the vanguard of their strategy by getting closer to the customer. This entails focusing on revenue generation (instead of cost reduction) and providing service and outstanding quality products.

McFarlan [29] suggests that the competitive environment influences whether an information system can make a strategic contribution. When the generic strategy is “price leadership,” the information system should be assessed in terms of cuts on production buildup and clerical staff, reduced unscheduled machine downtime, increased provisions for new services or products, decreased inventory level, etc. When product differentiation is the basis, IS should be assessed in terms of reduced product development lead time, enhanced ability to confirm order or shipment status; ability to open new, more consumer-oriented electronic distribution channels to customers; and improved product customization or service levels.

## 3. Hypotheses

Several hypotheses were formulated to test the relationship between system usage and specific measures of DSS benefits, grouped as: decision quality, competitive edge, improved communications, production cost reduction, increased productivity, time savings, overall user satisfaction, and overall cost effectiveness $[22,29]$ . The main hypothesis of this study is: frequency of use of DSS (System Usage) is related to its potential tangible and intangible benefits.

Ha: Frequency of use is related to decision quality.

Hb: Frequency of use is related to increased competitive edge.

Hc: Frequency of use is related to production reduction.

Hd: Frequency of use is related to increased productivity.

He: Frequency of use is related to time savings.

Hf: Frequency of use is related to overall user satisfaction.

Hg: Frequency of use is related to user overall cost effectiveness.

These hypotheses can jointly be tested with a single regression model. The significant variables in the model can be said to relate either positively or negatively to frequency of use. The null hypotheses will be rejected for those variables whose contributions for explaining the variability in system usage are not significant.

## 4. Methodology

A survey instrument was used to collect test data. Participants were made up of top, middle, and low-level managers from large companies in manufacturing and financial services industries. Although the companies were then selected at random, the participants had to meet the following criteria:

1. at least six months of DSS use by the organization,

2. at least six months of DSS experience by the user, and

3. at least either 1,000 employees or \$500,000 per year sales volume.

Since very many types of DSSs are in use in organizations, no specific systems were mentioned by name. Instead, we gave a general description of a DSS and asked the respondents to take part in the study only if their systems meet our description. Several directories were used to identify the participants including Standard & Poors, Industrial Index, Moody's Industrial Manual, and Polk's World Bank Directory.

## 5. Data analysis

Of the 214 (30.6%) returned questionnaires, 201 (28.7%) usable responses were left for this analysis. Based on our knowledge of the organizations sampled, the characteristics of those who took part in the study were not different from those who abstained. Pearson coefficient correlation was obtained for system usage and the eight measures of DSS effectiveness investigated in this study. As shown on Table 5, system usage was not highly correlated with any of the effectiveness measures. However, the correlation was significant (at Prob > |R| = 0.01) for all the effectiveness measures except increased productivity and overall cost effectiveness.

To test the hypotheses of this study, system usage was defined as a dependent variable for regression on the eight measures. In Table 6, the regression analysis indicates that competitive edge is the only significant variable (P < 0.0021) in explaining variability in system usage. The regression model has R-squared of 21.56% and an adjusted R-squared of 18.26%. This could be interpreted as: system usage implies about 21.56% of DSS effectiveness, at least for the group of users surveyed here. That is to say: when system usage is used to evaluate the effectiveness of DSS, only 21% of the total effectiveness is, in essence, evaluated. And the 21% is likely to be the potential benefit of enhancing competitive edge of the organization. The model is significant at $\alpha > 0.0001$ , which implies that the main null hypothesis cannot be rejected. Since at least one of the eight effectiveness measures is significant in explaining the variability in system usage, the hypothesis that frequency of use of DSS relates to its potential benefits is concluded. However, all the sub-hypotheses except Hb, are rejected.

## Study Limitations

One of the weaknesses of this study is common to all surveys. The inconsistency in the personality, perceptions, and values of the participants can lead to inaccuracy. Since the instrument measured the users' perception of DSS effectiveness, it is likely that several users will give differing performance scores to the same DSS. Another limitation of the study is the low response rate. Out of 750 questionnaires mailed out, only 214 (30.6%) were returned. A higher response rate would be desired, although we found no difference between those who returned the questionnaires and those who did not.

## 6. Conclusion

This study indicates that system usage does not imply seven out of eight possible measures of DSS effectiveness. A weak regression model (R-square = 21.56%) indicates that system usage implies only the benefit of competitive edge and not the other benefits assumed by researchers. Quite contrary to the popular assumption that system usage implies system effectiveness, this study indicates that those users who used their system more frequently reported less benefits in terms of increased productivity and overall cost effectively.

The implication of this study to the researchers is: system effectiveness (or benefits) should be measured with more practical and realistic performance indicators. It is difficult to come up with such realistic measures but the increased importance of the role of DSS demands it. For the practitioners, the implication of this study is: when evaluating a now system or an existing one, usage should not be the main or the only criteria. The most frequently used system may not be the best in terms of operation cost reduction or user satisfaction, and so forth. Attempt should be made to evaluate DSS based on the goals and objectives of the activities it supports. Measures such as time savings, quality of decision, communication improvement, etc., are more realistic than usage.

This study may be considered exploratory. However, it serves to point to the fact that the present and the expected roles of DSS in the organization render system usage insufficient as an effective measure of DSS. More realistic measures must be employed in evaluating DSS performance. Further studies may also consider developing and testing the instrument for the DSS evaluation. Evaluating DSS effectiveness with the right measures, as suggested in this study, may give field studies the same credibility and value enjoyed by laboratory experiments.
