# Factors that affect user-friendliness in interactive computer programs

Houston H. Carr

Department of Management, Auburn University, Alabama, USA

The increased use of interactive programs on mainframe and personal computers has made designers (creators) of programs acutely aware of the man-machine (or user-program) interface. Such awareness is a result of users becoming more informed that computers and programs can be made friendly. This research discusses factors that affect the acceptance of interactive programs and presents the results of a survey of 124 computer program designers and users. Respondents rated the importance of 20 factors and the frequency with which they are used, and placed them in order of importance. The results should guide designers in improving acceptance of their product.

Keywords: User-friendliness, Man-Machine interface, User-Program interface.

## Introduction

People at all levels of business and home life depend on computer programs to gather data, to produce reports, to support decisions, and to entertain. The point of contact between the user and the computer is the user–program interface, usually the video display terminal. Users want the interface to be friendly and support their activities.

“Many people avoid the computer or use it with great anxiety; they suffer from ‘computer shock’, ‘terminal terror’, or ‘network neurosis’. … These fears are generated in part by poor designs that have complex commands, hostile and vague error messages, tortuous and unfamiliar sequences of actions, or a deceptive anthropomorphic style.” These statements by Shneiderman [26], so contrary to our view of the computer-based society, suggest that acceptance of computer-based capabilities depends on the program user’s attitude.

Computer program designers must consider usability and the ultimate user satisfaction of their product. Gould [8] believes the importance of usability of computer programs to cover the total development project and has user-friendliness imbedded in the process: usable designs involve (a) early and continual focus on users, (b) integrated design, (c) early and continual user testing, and (d) iterative design.

DeSanctis [5] notes that “MIS implementation research suggests that at least five factors other than user friendliness and system flexibility are important in determining the success of MIS projects: (1) top management involvement; (2) a desire for the system by the intended users; (3) an immediate problem to address; (4) an established MIS group; and (5) involvement of users in the design process". But, the total interface involves aspects ranging from the hardware used to the background of the intended audience.

The Rushineks [24] say, “Past studies have found that human-factors design, interfaces, user-friendliness, . . . are important factors in establishing user satisfaction with a computer system”. Additionally, “redesign of the human-computer interface can make a substantial difference in learning time, performance speed, error rates, and user satisfaction” [26].

To the user, the program interface is the computer. Although the individual must work with the hardware (keyboard, mouse, printer, etc.), it is the format of the display, the program's guidance and treatment of the user as a human, and the way the designer expects the user to react to the program that is most important to the program user.

As the number of users grows, designers and users alike see a need for systems that are more friendly to the casual, infrequent, or computer illiterate user. Raduchel [22] summarized the task of the designer as primarily a problem of defining the user. Barb [1] echoed these views: “User friendliness primarily relates to the intended user and the nature of user-system interaction that is desired.” Dolotta et al. [6] believe that users associate user-friendliness with their comfort level while interacting with a system.

The objective of this research is to (1) create a definitive list of characteristics or factors, that affect user acceptance (user-friendliness) of the user-program interface, and (2) survey a group of program designers and users to determine the importance and frequency of use of the factors.

## Methodology

The first part of this research was to review the relevant literature to create a list of factors that have been considered to affect the computer user-program interface. The review began with Hiltz and Kerr's [13] categories of factors appropriate for computer conferencing systems. Other research from the MIS and man-machine literature listed components, supporting and augmenting this list [10,16,18,19,23,26,27]. We then conducted a survey of designers and users of such programs to find their opinions of each factor's importance and frequency of use to determine which features make a program most appealing to users.

We assumed that specific factors are important for affecting user-friendliness at the user-program interface, that this list of 20 is a comprehensive list of such factors, and that the individual factors will vary in importance. Furthermore, the use of systems with good ratings will reflect the importance. And finally, the importance of any one will vary by demographics; gender, work position held (staff or supervision), task (creator or user), age of the respondent, and years of service in the field.

A questionnaire for this survey was created that first requested demographic data of the respondents. Our reasoning for controlling by demographics was as follows: as for variation by gender, research results suggest that boys and girls through college age consider computers more appropriate for boys, that males are more comfortable with computers, and, in general, there is a significant gender difference in attitude toward computers $[4,31]$ . As to age, time in service, position held, and experience (as evidenced by these variables) affect our views of what systems can provide. The questionnaire then presented the list of factors, one-by-one, to allow the respondents to rate the importance and frequency of use on a seven-point scale.

The following sections discuss 20 factors that influence the quality of the interface in interactive computer-based systems, their affect on the user, and the attitude of systems designers toward them. This is followed by a discussion of the survey and an analysis of the results.

## Interface factors

The Computerized Conferencing and Communications Center (CCCC) conducted a study in which systems designers rated several interface factors and their respective characteristics, based on the extent to which they are important for computer-mediated communication systems [13]. These and other factors from the literature are included in Figure 1, the list of factors identified during this research. Because these CCCC-study characteristics are not system-specific, they comprised a preliminary list of interface factors important to a user-friendly, interactive computer system interface. The factors are grouped into four categories of interface factors: learning, adaptability, behavior, and error control.

## Factors affecting user learning

Learning is the process through which the user comes to understand the system. It begins when the user acquires the knowledge and skills necessary to access the system and culminates when the user sufficiently comprehends the available capabilities. Several important program factors affect how quickly learning takes place: accessibility, comprehension, segmentation, guidance, and informativeness. The designer must decide how much effort to devote to refining the program in relation to each factor so that the resulting program best meets the user's needs.

Accessibility is the knowledge and effort required for users to gain access to a system. In general, the simpler the access to a particular task or system, the better. Commands or sequences of commands should be well documented and consistent throughout the system. Accessibility is also a function of the availability of (mainframe) terminals. With the personal computer, accessibility becomes significantly easier due to the absence of logon procedures and job control language. Designers often sacrifice ease of accessibility for considerations of security.

Comprehension is “the ability of users to understand as a whole what the system is capable of accomplishing, before having to learn how to do it” [13]. Users fail to benefit from potentially profitable uses of a system if they are not aware of the full range of capabilities available. They do not have to understand exactly what each capability entails but merely be aware of its existence, in case they need it.

Segmentation is the “ability of the user to learn only the minimum in order to carry out a specific task”. Achieving a balance between comprehension and segmentation is one of the more controversial issues in system design $[13]$ . With comprehension, the user understands what the system can do without necessarily knowing how to make the system do it. However, designers seem to favor segmentation (minimum learning). This may mean that the infrequent user never realizes that the system can do more than what he or she learned. One solution is the segmentation of the system into different levels of complexity for different groups of users $[15,25,26]$ .

Guidance is “the degree to which a system allows users to learn as they use it” [13]. Research indicates that users prefer trial and error learning, that is, “most people learn to do something, not by reading about how to do it in manuals, but by watching others do it or by actually doing it themselves” [9]. Help messages that may be triggered for display at any point during an inquiry or task are the most effective form of guidance [13]. Designers often consider guidance (and self-documentation) of crucial importance. Most personal computer programs have in-context help available at the touch of a ‘help’ key.

An informative system provides users with clear information about what they are being asked to do in terms of operations or error messages. (A system may inform users about the type of error made, rather than just stating that an error occurred.) Since clear information means an additional 30% to 200% programming effort for a somewhat complex system, informativeness is sometimes neglected [13,26]. There is a fine line between being informative and being intrusive with too much information. Even here, a balance can be achieved by allowing the user to specify the level of prompting and response desired.

## Factors affecting program adaptability

An adaptable system is one that conforms easily to changing circumstances. Different users make different demands of a system and a given user will change his or her demands over time. Thus, the system must be able to meet these changing needs. The factors considered under adaptability are control, flexibility, variety, leverage, and simplicity.

Control is “the ability of users to feel in control of the computer, while making sure they understand what they are doing and where they are in the interaction” [13]. Problems potentially result when users do not understand how to master the machine and feel bullied by poor interactive dialogue. This results from a lack of many of the factors introduced under learning, i.e., comprehension of the system and guidance. Designers surveyed by Hiltz and Kerr almost unanimously felt that control is a crucial characteristic for computer-based systems [13].

Flexibility and variety involve the ability of users to “tailor the system to their own style of interaction in carrying out tasks” [13]. One method designers can use is an interface that has both commands and menus. Menus can “range from simple lists of item names to sets of graphic images (icons)” [18]. With proper hardware support, this idea can be carried on to the use of window-icon-mouse-pulldown menu (WIMP) techniques [3,32].

Another option available to the designer is to permit the use of user-defined commands that express the way the user operates, rather than requiring the use of new functions for the machine to execute. For example, abbreviations and replacement words (e.g., aliases) could be stored in a user-defined reference table.

Leverage and simplicity allow the user to execute significant operations without supplying many lengthy entries. A system that has these characteristics allows users to define their commands or provides general high-level commands for all users. This may involve permitting the use of abbreviations, as well as stringing commands together for multiple sequential actions to make the best use of computer and user time [26].

Factors affecting program actions and user behavior

As users expect more from interactive systems, they place greater demands on designers concerned with the behavioral aspects of systems. Behavior deals with the environment surrounding the man-machine interface and with the users' comfort with the system. This characteristic has six factors: humanization, relationship, regularity, predictability, responsiveness, and information overload.

Humanization and relationship involve “treating the user as an intelligent human being rather than as a slave of the computer” [13]. This implies that the system should be polite and respectful to users. Yet, what constitutes humanization is interpreted in various ways. What seems friendly and natural to the beginner can seem repetitive and burdensome to some experienced users. An important way the user can be made to feel that he or she is being treated like a human being is in the way the program indicates an incorrect action, an ERROR. The wording of an error message is critical to creating a comfortable environment for the user [26].

Regularity and predictability deal with “the ability of a user to anticipate the actions of the computer and to expect consistent responses to operations and functions” [13]. In other words, once the user is familiar with the system, it does not do anything that the user does not expect [26].

Responsiveness is “the ability of the system to respond quickly and meaningfully to user requests to carry out various operations and functions” [13]. Competent users can easily become impatient with a system, while inexperienced users spend much of their time trying to understand what the computer is saying. Users are willing to wait longer when they believe that operations they requested take more effort. From a technical standpoint, responsiveness relates to what is being done, the processing power available via the computer, and the efficiency of the program. Designers generally make an effort to keep response rapid, but this is not always possible [13,26].

A potential problem with data-intensive programs is information overload. This means that the data or information is coming to the user too rapidly or is crowded on the screen. An example of the former is to have lines of data appear on the screen faster than the user can read, or in the extreme, to have the data scroll off the screen before the user has a chance to view them.

The more important problem is that of crowding elements on a single terminal or computer screen. When a display is crowded by the presence of too many items, the user may become irritated, confused or overwhelmed. Heckel [12], in his discussion of the way Walt Disney studios created its classic movies, indicated that screens must be created to present single ideas. Like Disney Studios, the programmer should use simple means of presentation, even when using such technology as windows. Otherwise, the impact may be lost, and the user may feel that the system did not meet his or her needs.

A less obvious method of overloading users is with color, music, and sound [26]. While each may be included to enhance the information content and transfer or focus the user's attention on a specific block of data, these may nonetheless distract the user from the central theme, interrupt a train of thought, or simply be a nuisance. The points to be emphasized are that humans can be easily overloaded with information, and the designer must take this trait into consideration.

## Factors affecting program error control

This aspect is probably the most important to the inexperienced user. The very word ERROR strikes fear in the hearts of many users. Besides the previously mentioned wording of messages, error control includes four factors: forgiveness, recovery, security, and reliability.

Forgiveness and recovery deal with how the system responds to mistakes and provides ways to correct user errors. The ideal system is one in which the user does not have to exert any effort to correct an error. The system that shows forgiveness and the ability to “undo” steps that created the error should reduce the user’s fears and help a user accept computer-based information systems.

In the context of program design, security means protecting the users' data from either intentional or unintentional errors. If the system has provisions for recovery, it is less likely to be susceptible to loss of data due to an unintentional or incorrect action by the user. Measures to prevent intentional intrusion, however, usually make it more difficult for the inexperienced user to access the system. Lengthy logon procedures, passwords, and tight rules are appropriate for good security, but they diminish ease of use.

Reliability is “the ability of the system to function without error or loss of data… (and) the frequency and length of instances of the system being unavailable during scheduled operation” [13]. In other words, a system is reliable if it is available to the user and maintains data without loss. The importance of reliability is generally accepted by designers [13].

## The task facing the systems designer

Man must become the prime focus of systems design. The computer is there to serve him to obtain information for him, and to help him do his job. The ease with which he communicates with it will determine the extent to which he uses it. Whether or not he uses it powerfully will depend upon the man-machine language available to him and how well he is able to understand it. [17]

The discussion of the characteristics of interface factors associated with user-friendliness suggests the considerable task facing the systems designer. It is difficult to incorporate all desirable features in the design of a single program. Unfortunately, designers are often confronted with conflicting objectives, forcing them to make compromises that reflect the nature of the system's use and the user population. A system for general use that is advanced enough for the experienced user should not overwhelm a beginner. Too much guidance may leave users feeling they are not in control, while full comprehension often reduces the chances for segmentation. Further, the conflict between easy-to-learn, easy-to-use, and application-power adds to the problem.

Designers must realize that this is an environment of conflict, of compromise, of choice. Without guidelines, designers have used their experience and intuition, with mixed results [7]. The research that follows ranks an extensive list of factors that the designer must consider. The use of this guidance has the potential for greater product acceptance.

## A survey of program creators and users

The characteristics described previously constitute a list of factors that were believed important to user friendliness of interactive computer programs. We developed a computer-based questionnaire to determine the importance of these factors to designers and users of computer programs. The survey instrument was pretested by ten faculty members and graduate students in the Department of Management at the University of Georgia. We asked the initial participants (all familiar with design or use of computer programs) to respond to and critique the questionnaire, noting any problems encountered with the instrument. These responses were incorporated into the subsequent versions of the questionnaire program.

The first sample population for the test consisted of members of the Athens Georgia IBM Users Club, a diverse group of programmers, private business owners, students, and faculty members interested in personal and mainframe computers. Other populations surveyed included: Atlanta-based employees of the IBM Corporation, Bank South, and Lockheed-Georgia; employees of several companies located in St. Louis; and faculty colleagues located throughout the United States and Canada. We designed the computer-based questionnaire to be friendly to the intended population. Its use simplified all data input, especially ranking the 20 factors. Diskettes for IBM-compatible computers were mailed to selected groups.

The electronic questionnaire first asked for demographic information. Next, respondents were asked to rate the importance of each factor associated with user-friendliness in computer programs on a 7-point Likert-type scale. Following this, respondents were asked to indicate on a similar scale how often they as program designers incorporated each factor into programs they created, or how often they as users saw the factor incorporated in programs. For the rating exercise of the questionnaire, the factor title was presented on-screen, with a full definition of the factor. Figure 3 shows a representative survey screen. After rating the importance and frequency of use of individual factors, respondents were asked to list factors they believed were of value but were not included in the list to capture factors missing from our list. If a respondent included new factors, he/she was asked to rate their importance and frequency of use. The entire list of factors was then presented to the participant, with abbreviated definitions (see Figure 4). Participants were asked to place the factors in importance rank order. The initial order that appeared on-screen was based on the importance ratings provided by each participant. By indicating the factor number and a new position in the list, respondents could reorder the factors.

## Data analysis and results

## Factor importance and use rating

The intent of the survey was to determine how designers and users of interactive computer programs rated the importance and frequency of use of the factors. Figure 5 show the mean values and standard deviations for the ratings of importance and frequency of use and overall ranking of importance by all respondents. The factors are listed in order of importance rating. Reliability, the factor with the highest importance rating, had a score of 6.7 out of a possible 7.0. One-half of the factors had a mean score of 6.0 or higher out of 7 points and 19 of 20 scored above 5.0. This suggests that all factors are important and that all respondents value a reliable, informative, and recoverable program. (See factor ranking below.)

The factors are shown in Figure 5 with mean values for usage rating. The two factors with the highest rating (5.6) are used between 65% and 75% of the time. The respondents used 18 of the 20 factors over one-half of the time. Conversely, 13 of 20 are absent one-third of the time. This seems inconsistent with the importance ratings.

Rating scores for usage were well below those for importance, due in part to differences in rating meanings. The difference in means seems to suggest that factors that are considered important (all 20 had a mean rating above the indifference level of 4.0) are not used as often as their importance warrants. (The median rank for the five most important factors was 6.5 of a possible 7, while the median rank for the five factors most frequently used was 5.3, indicating use 65–75% of the time). Reasons given by Hiltz and Kerr (1982) for why factors are not incorporated into more conferencing systems were extra coding costs and complexity. The same reasons likely apply to the design of interactive programs.

We believed that the use of a factor would follow its importance. To determine the relationship between the importance of factors and their usage, a correlation analysis of the two variables, importance and use, was performed for each factor. The result was that the correlations varied from 0.043 to 0.491 with 15 of the 20 factors having significant correlations (p < 0.05). The mean correlation of the factors was 0.301, suggesting a weak association between importance and use. Therefore, we treated the importance and use of factors as separate constructs and rejected our belief that the use of factors is according to their importance.

## Factor (importance) ranking

Figure 5 also shows the rank order (the mean and standard deviation of the number of votes per position) for the factors. For rank order, low numbers indicate a high ranking, i.e., a ranking of 1 would be the highest possible rank. Particularly noteworthy is the high standard deviation for rank order, which suggests that participants held widely divergent views (low consensus). Two of the factors in the top five in importance remain in that position in rank order, and all the top five importance factors are included in the top ten in rank order. While respondents agreed on the relative importance of factors, they did not agree on those that were the most important.

We next performed an analysis to determine the variance by gender of the respondents on the importance, use, and ranking of each factor. The few factors listed in Figure 6 were significant, at the 0.05 level or less, with the mean for female and male given as (F:, M:) following the factor name. Designers of corporate computer-based systems must realize that women are becoming an increasing and important part of their audience. Given a change in audience and the indication that women are more sensitive to the user-friendly factors given herein, designers will become under pressure to adapt.

Male and female respondents differed significantly (at the 0.01 level) in terms of the level of position held, age group, and years in the computer field. Men held higher positions and were older. The mean number of years in the field was 4.3 for women (N = 30) and 7.4 for men (N = 94). These characteristics are simply representative of a field into which women are just entering.

Differences between educators / students and others

Because one-third of the respondents were educators and students, a t-test was performed to see if the responses of educators and students differed from the responses given by the other respondents. The ratings of the overall importance of factors did differ significantly for the two groups, at the 0.01 level (the mean rating was 6.01 for educators and students, and 5.79 for others). Ratings of the frequency of use also differed at the 0.01 level (the mean was 4.56 for educators and students and 4.87 for others). Thus, educators and students considered the factors studied to be of greater importance on the average than did other respondents. Yet, educators and students thought they incorporated the factors or saw them incorporated less often than did the other respondents. The same two groups were tested for differences attributable to gender. The educator/student group's ratings of the importance and frequency of use of the factors differed at the 0.01 level. In each case, the mean for women was higher. It was the influence of the educator/student group that caused the overall factor importance to be higher for females than males.

## Differences by position held by the respondent

We made two comparisons to test our belief that the position held by the respondents would affect their responses. First, responses of those holding staff versus supervisor/manager positions were compared. These differences were significant (at the 0.01 level) only for ratings of frequency of use (the mean for staff members was 4.8, while the mean for supervisors/managers was 5.6). Supervisors were significantly older and had been working in the field longer, as expected. Secondly, responses of users and designers were compared. Again, only the ratings of frequency of use were significantly different. Designers thought they used the factors more often then users saw them used (the mean for designers was 5.4, while it was 4.4 for users).

Thus we see that designers and users alike perceive these factors to be important for the friendliness of the user-program interface. However, managers and designers thought the factors were used more often then staff personnel and users. We would expect users to want the factors incorporated and believe that they were not. Additionally, managers would tend to have less exposure to computer-based programs and would likely make assumptions about the use of these factors. One interpretation of this is that users want the factors applied and may not receive support from management because of the later's lack of exposure.

## Additional factors

Nineteen responses produced the 35 additional factors. The new factors were different for each respondent and were variations of the factors given in the questionnaire. Because there was no consensus regarding the new factors, none of these were added to the list. No participant recommended deleting any factor in the comments received.

## Limitations and directions of future research

Limitations of this study

The primary limitation of this research is in the use of a convenience sample, which reduces the generalizability of the findings. This does not invalidate the findings, however, especially when noting that this is the first research to address a specific list of factors that affect the user-program interface.

## Directions of future research

This interface research builds on other work in the area of the man-machine interface. Future research should continue the search for factors that affect the user-program and man-machine interfaces, especially in the light of new technology, such as object-oriented interfaces and programming, input devices such as remote controllers and voice response units and the extended potential for voice output. Further, the survey population should be widened to seek further difference by type of user.

## Summary and conclusions

The analysis of 124 responses from designers and users of computer programs indicates that 20 factors contribute to user-friendliness, but that they are not used as often as their importance would suggest. Individuals who have experience with interactive computer programs indicated that products must emphasize reliability and security, provide recovery from error, be informative as to intent, and be clearly written. In addition, they must be predictable and responsive, forgiving of user errors, provide guidance to their users, and be accessible to those with limited knowledge of how they function. The high importance ratings given to this list of factors and the rankings assigned can help resolve required tradeoffs in the design and marketing of new programs.

Significant differences were found in the responses of male and female respondents. These differences must be addressed as women become a larger portion of the work force. Designers of corporate computer-based systems must not assume, as have designers of video games, that it is a male-dominated world or that women view the user-program interface as do men. To continue to do so courts danger of rejection.

Responses by people in different job categories (correlated to age and years of service) also differed significantly. Some of these can be attributed to the participant's level in the organization, and thus their exposure to user-program interfaces. As more computer-based applications are used in higher levels of the organization, these differences will probably disappear as users learn their systems and make greater demands for friendliness.

The degree of agreement among respondents was high when they rated the importance of individual factors, but there was no consensus about which factors were most important (rank order). Program designers should take this as a warning not to ignore any factor that is considered important for achieving user-friendliness

During the time of the research, the microcomputer has made great inroads into organizations, and software developers have responded with zeal to user demands for friendly interfaces. The mouse-driven icon interface is one such example. Simultaneously, users are becoming aware of possible alternatives at the user-program interface. A general problem with this type of research is that respondents say one thing and do another, as noted in the lack of correlation between importance and use. Thus a significant outcome of longitudinal research will be in finding what users genuinely do, not just what they say. Until such discovery is made, this list of factors should aid software developers in providing user-friendly interfaces.
