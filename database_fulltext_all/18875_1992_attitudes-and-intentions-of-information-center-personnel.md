# Attitudes and intentions of information center personnel

Yash P. Gupta \*

Department of Management, University of Louisville, Louisville, KY 40292, USA

Tor Guimaraes

Tennessee Technology University, Cookeville, TN 38505, USA

T.S. Raghunathan

College of Business Administration, The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

A growing number of organizations are adopting the concept of an information center (IC) to alleviate the problems of backlog of requests for MIS services and to support end user computing. The characteristics of IC personnel required to operate a successful IC are significantly different from information systems(IS) personnel. A number of studies have been undertaken to examine the turnover process of IS personnel, but no attempt has been made to study the turnover process for IC personnel. This paper examines the antecedents of job satisfaction, commitment, and turnover intentions of IC personnel. It was found that role ambiguity and conflict are dysfunctional variables for IC personnel.

Keywords: IC personnel, Job satisfaction, Commitment, Role ambiguity, Role conflict, Boundary spanning, Turnover intentions.

## Introduction

Information Systems departments are experiencing radical changes in resources, demands, and responsibilities. Rising user expectations, pressure to integrate applications, and pressure to accomplish more with fewer resources are examples of these changes. They have exacerbated the problems associated with the information systems development backlog. It has been estimated that the invisible backlog of user needs, that is, those which have not yet formally entered the queue of pending requests, are over 100% of the declared backlog. The total backlog has also been suggested to be over 100% of the entire base of installed applications [e.g., 3]. This problem has been heightened by the fact that an increasing proportion of data processing (DP) budgets are being allocated to maintaining existing software systems developed by DP departments. For example, it has been suggested that up to 80 percent of DP budgets and 75 percent of DP personnel time are allocated to maintenance [14].

In 1974, IBM-Canada introduced the concept of the “Information Center” (IC) to alleviate the above problems and improve individual productivity by delivering computational resources to the actual user [10]. The objective of an IC is to provide users access to data on their own terms so that they can solve their own business problems [18]. Hammond [25] described the IC concept as follows:

“... a portion of the information systems (IS) development resource organized and dedicated to support the users of IS services in activities such as report generation and modification, data manipulation and analysis, spontaneous inquiries, etc. The fundamental premise underlying an IC is that if provided proper education, technical support, usable tools, data availability, and convenient access to the system, users may directly and rapidly ... and willingly ... satisfy a part of their business area requirements that depend on an IS environment.”

Since its inception, the concept of IC has been influenced and altered by two significant changes in the industry, namely (i) a growth in the popularity of third party software and (ii) the appearance of personal computers. Strassman [52], for instance, estimated that by the year 2000 there will be more than 200 million work stations in organizations worldwide.

Concomitantly, there has been a phenomenal growth in the number of organizations which have established ICs $[44,48,54]$ . For example, Rifkin $[42]$ estimated that more than 400 organizations established ICs during the time period 1981–1983. CRWTH $[19]$ stated that 2100 ICs have been installed since 1984. The AMA surveyed 295 organizations in 1988; 58 percent of the respondents had a discrete unit that supported end user computing $[4]$ . In addition, 70 percent of ICs received budget increases with a vast majority of ICs budgets rising by 6 to 10 percent $[22]$ . The primary benefits that have been claimed from the installation of ICs include enhanced (more competent or literate) users, improved productivity, better information, and an enhanced view of DP $[15]$ .

Carr [16] studied the way 20 organizations operationalized the IC concept, comparing their information centers with the IBM/Hammond model. In this study he-addressed issues such as the type of work performed and the nature of the management of ICs. Garrity and Rockart [20], Oplinger [39] and Schultz and Redding [46] suggested that, in general, information centers do not compare favorably with the ideal. Sprague and McNurlin [50] discussed the various other types of IC concepts developed so far. White and Christy [56] presented a normative model of an IC in terms of the end user computing environment and discussed the results of extensive field interviews with IC personnel and endusers. Bergeron et al. [11] examined the relationship among the key features of IC in its support role and user satisfaction. Based on the data collected from 172 users and 19 IC managers, they found that the proximity of IC, the diversity of the services offered and the proportion of the IS budget directed to the IC were positively related with various dimensions of user satisfaction.

## Purpose of the paper

The purpose of this paper is to report on a study of the relationship between turnover intentions among IC personnel and organizational commitment, job satisfaction, and their antecedents. The importance of gaining an understanding of these relationships is heightened by the fact that there is a tremendous shortage in the supply of IC personnel [41]. In addition, rising personnel costs relative to equipment costs is exacerbating the problem of turnover [2]. Magal et al. [33] identified the most important critical success factors for IC managers. These include competent staff, communications with end users, and top management support. This implies that IC professionals with appropriate skills are crucial for successful IC performance.

The turnover intentions among IS personnel have been addressed by many authors $[6–8,24,32]$ . These findings however cannot be extended to IC personnel, as they differ significantly from IS employees on a number of demographic, personality, and motivational factors. IC personnel must have interpersonal skill; their business skills are generally more important than technical skills. They tend to be more flexible and creative, have greater need for social interaction and are significantly more end user oriented $[23]$ . Carr $[16]$ and Rainer et al. $[41]$ noted that nearly 50 percent of IC staff members are female, approximately 50 percent are 30 years or younger, and a large majority are better educated than their IS colleagues. The IC personnel generally have limited advancement opportunities. This problem is compounded by the fact that IC personnel tend to be faced with negative salary differentials with respect to IS personnel.

The above findings have major implications for the management of ICs and reinforce the need to examine the importance of relationships between turnover intentions, job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and their antecedents. An examination of the above literature suggests that researchers have mainly concentrated on either describing the general concept of IC or validating the ideal model in the field, explaining the critical success factors of IC managers $[53]$ or explaining their relationships with end user computing problems. However, no effort has been undertaken, so far, to determine the turnover intentions of IC personnel.

## Research model

Our study examines the process by which IC personnel develop their intentions to quit. Since there is no comprehensive study which has examined the above mentioned relationships, the research model will be developed using literature drawn from several areas, including IS, IC, and general management.

Mobley [36] suggested that the presence of intentions is the best predictor of actual turnover, because, in periods of economic slowdown, alternative jobs may not be available and thus actual turnover may be severely restricted.

Baroudi [6] posited “... even though personnel may be generally unhappy and intend to leave, this may not be possible until the economic climate improves and alternative jobs are more plentiful.”

It has been observed that the IC personnel duties include (i) user support, (ii) training and education, (iii) consultation, (iv) product evaluation and planning, (v) troubleshooting and problem resolution, (vi) technical interface with DP, and (vii) assistance on PCs. In performing these duties, these personnel are required to interact with the IS department and with the users. They must disseminate information and coordinate activities among several functional groups within the organization. Sometimes this requires IC personnel to cross many boundaries. As for IS personnel, [12,21,57] the role conflict and role ambiguity for IC personnel is expected to be positively related to boundary spanning, i.e.

H1: Higher levels of boundary spanning activities that IC personnel perform are associated with higher levels of role conflict.

H2: Higher levels of boundary spanning activities that IC personnel perform are associated with higher levels of role ambiguity.

Hammond assumed that the user area would act as a potential source for recruiting IC personnel. These personnel would probably come from varied backgrounds and thus would be unlikely to develop many strong bonds with other members of the IC. Moreover, as their work would span many boundaries they would be exposed to a great number of alternative job opportunities. This heightened awareness about opportunities could increase their intention to quit.

H3: Higher levels of boundary spanning activities that IC personnel perform are associated with higher levels of intentions to quit.

Many researchers including Keller and Holland [30], Keller et al. [31], and Miles [34] suggested that a positive relationship exists between boundary spanning activities and job satisfaction. This is due to the fact that the IC personnel have an opportunity to engage in inherently interesting and varied type of work which would provide a higher level of job satisfaction.

H4: Higher levels of boundary spanning activities that IC personnel perform are associated with higher levels of job satisfaction.

Adams [1] argued that boundary spanning activities weaken organizational linkages such as commitment.

H5: Higher levels of boundary spanning activities that IC personnel perform are associated with lower levels of organizational commitment.

House and Rizzo [27] studied the relationship of role conflict to role ambiguity. They suggested a strong positive correlation between these independent constructs. Ostensibly, increased levels of role conflict create more role ambiguity, since a large number of conflicting expectations communicated to a person create more uncertainty about how to perform or how to prioritize and fulfill them.

H6: Higher levels of experienced role conflict of IC personnel are associated with higher levels of role ambiguity.

Rizzo et al. [43], and Schuler [45] have shown that both role conflict and role ambiguity are generally associated with dysfunctional job outcomes, i.e. less job satisfaction and lower job performance.

H7: Higher levels of experienced role conflict of IC personnel are associated with lower levels of job satisfaction.

H8: Higher levels of experienced role ambiguity of IC personnel are associated with lower levels of job satisfaction.

In some organizations, ICs either do not have a specific IC manager or the IC managers have alternate duties, and this dilutes the amount of attention paid to the users or the IC staff. It has been shown that the presence of a dedicated and enthusiastic IC manager was the most important factor in determining the success of an IC [16]. The empirical research in the organization behavior literature [26] supports the theory that a supervisor's behavior may also be a direct stress factor for a subordinate, resulting in role conflict and role ambiguity and causing anxiety which, in this study, have been shown to be major factors contributing to an employee's intentions to quit [9].

H9: Higher levels of experienced role ambiguity are associated with higher levels of intentions to quit.

H10: Higher levels of experienced role conflict of IC personnel are associated with higher levels of intentions to quit.

Literature reviews on organizational commitment suggest that role conflict and role ambiguity deter organizational commitment [40].

H11: Higher levels of experienced role conflict of IC personnel are associated with lower levels of organizational commitment.

H12: Higher levels of experienced role ambiguity of IC personnel are associated with lower levels of organizational commitment.

This hypothesis has been supported by the findings of a number of authors including Arnold and Feldman [5] and Jamal [28]. Further support for this hypothesized relationship was provided by Bartol [7] who found that job satisfaction significantly reduces actual turnover.

H13: Higher levels of job satisfaction of IC personnel are associated with lower intentions to quit.

This hypothesized relationship has been supported by an empirical study conducted by Brief and Aldag [13].

H14: Higher levels of job satisfaction of IC personnel are associated with higher levels of commitment.

This hypothesis is supported by empirical research $[38,51]$ . It views organizational commitment as an organizational linkage. The more strongly linked an individual is to an organization, the less likely it is that he or she will quit.

## H15: Higher levels of commitment of IC personnel are associated with lower levels of intentions to quit.

## Implications for managers

The primary finding of this study is that role conflict and role ambiguity are the most important factors that managers must try to reduce. This will eventually improve job satisfaction, commitment, and may reduce intentions to quit.

In some organizations, senior management has strongly advocated the use of end user computing without setting up policies or procedures to review end user applications or committing additional resources. As a result, the IC has been under tremendous pressure to keep up with the demand for end user computing services which may lead to role overload. Senior management in conjunction with IC management should allocate appropriate resources for IC and develop policies which would reflect the level of support their organization will provide for end user computing. Higher level of resources for ICs will allow IC management to provide the services users require and improve the overall quality of IC services. Resources can also be spent in other ways such as hiring more competent IC personnel, increasing the variety of services offered, and buying and installing more equipment dedicated to end user computing, thus, alleviating the some pressures IC employees may be facing.

In a number of organizations IC supports the use of a large number of software tools. In these organizations end users are generally less satisfied with the quality of technical assistance and the quality of applications developed, and feel more dependent on the IC than in organizations supporting the use of a smaller number of tools. In the former case the IC personnel may experience difficulty in becoming knowledgeable about and adequately supporting a large number of tools. As a result, the IC personnel may feel added stress which in turn could lead to a decline in job satisfaction. It may be appropriate for IC management to limit the number of tools available to and supported by IC.

In some organizations an IC staff job has been viewed as being highly qualified, however it has not been recognized as such by the formal MIS personnel system. Thus the job lacks a clear career path or appropriate rewards for meeting the challenges encountered. IC staff may not always have specific job descriptions or their jobs are described as being similar to the jobs in the IS department in spite of the previously described differences inherent in the IC staff jobs. These issues may lead to a reduction in job satisfaction and an increase in role ambiguity. IC managers can significantly increase job satisfaction, organizational commitment and reduce intentions of an IC employee to leave by assuring the jobs are clearly defined, the expectations with respect to performance are conveyed and that a job does not require incompatible behavior.

The IC managers can reduce attrition by properly managing the job satisfaction component via establishing clear promotion and supervision criteria and providing regular feedback. The results indicate that both job satisfaction and organizational commitment have an inverse relationship with intentions to leave. This indicates that an IC employee's decision to leave is based on both the job and organization as a whole. IC management may be able to improve their employees' perception of their organization by effective communications.

The IC managers should pay special attention to recruiting employees for IC. Ideally these employees should have a relatively higher tolerance for role conflict and role ambiguity. They should have better communication skills than IS employees. IC managers should make every effort to ensure that the levels of role ambiguity and conflict are not unnecessarily raised by lack of appropriate policies within the IC department.

## Future research

Magal et al. [33] suggested that IC organizations go through several stages in their organizational life cycle. It may be interesting to examine these relationships in various stages of the life cycle and compare each stage.

Here we have outlined the differences between IC and IS employees. In some organizations the IC is a separate, independent department; in others it is located within the IS department or the user departments. Since there are significant differences between IC and IS employees it may be worthwhile to examine the relationships, paying particular attention to the exact location of the IC department.

Future studies may also examine the turnover problem among IC personnel using multiple data point in a longitudinal context. Such studies will capture the turnover process more completely, since individuals in an organization may be at different points in the turnover process and a single study may not be able to collect the individuals perceptions, preferences, and evaluations of self, organization, and alternatives.

In a number of studies in the information systems area, it has been found that job characteristics and leadership characteristics have a significant correlation with job satisfaction $[17,21]$ . Future studies should explicitly include these variables so that these models can explain the attitudes, intentions and behavior of IC personnel more elaborately.

## Conclusions

With increasing competition and evolving information systems(IS) technology organizations are being faced with enormous demands for IS services. This is causing backlogs of new systems development and maintenance of existing systems. In order to alleviate this problem, the concept of information centers (ICs) was introduced. This concept has been increasingly adopted by organizations. Here we have examined the turnover process of IC personnel: the effect of boundary spanning, role ambiguity, role conflict, job satisfaction, commitment, and turnover intentions. Apparently, boundary spanning does not significantly affect any variable contributing to the intentions to quit. Further, role conflict and role ambiguity are the major dysfunctional variables for IC personnel turnover.

The results of this study differ significantly from the results on turnover intentions of IS personnel.
