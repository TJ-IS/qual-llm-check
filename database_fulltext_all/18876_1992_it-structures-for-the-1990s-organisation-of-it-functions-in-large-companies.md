# IT structures for the 1990s: organisation of IT functions in large companies

A survey

Stephen L. Hodgkinson \*

Templeton College, The University of Oxford, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

How is the information technology (IT) function organised in large multi-business companies? A two stage postal survey investigated the distribution of responsibility for IT activities and the role of the corporate IT function in 50 Times 100 companies. Results indicate that the vast majority of large companies have federal IT management structures – distributed responsibility for IT combined with some central functional leadership or guidance. Four approaches to the organisation and management of IT activities were identified: Centralised, Strategic leadership, Strategic guidance, and Decentralised. In companies with a Strategic Leadership IT management style, the corporate centre has a dominant influence over IT activities and is predicted to retain its dominance in the future. In companies with a Strategic Guidance IT management style, divisional and business unit managements hold most responsibility for IT activities and the role of the centre is predicted to decline in importance. Using responses from general management executives, results reveal that the degree of centralisation of corporate management functions and the importance placed on the central managerial planning and control roles are correlated with IT management style.

Keywords: IS organisation, IS strategy, Corporate IS, IS management, Federal IS management, Decentralisation and IS.

## 1. The decentralised organisation

Most large companies are today managed in the decentralised manner originally identified by authors such as Cordiner [7] and Chandler [5] in the early 1960s. In 1964, Drucker [9] proposed the term federal decentralisation: emphasising devolution of responsibility for operational decisions to lower organisational levels, while senior management retain responsibility for decisions of more strategic importance. Decentralisation brings decision makers closer to the customer and the information needed for effective decisions [24]. Empirical studies [6,14,28,37] and the use of terms such as unbundling [19], disaggregation [15], demassification [4] and devolution [10,36] indicate the dominance of decentralised management philosophy in today's organisations.

Federal decentralisation, however, is not total abdication of responsibility by senior management. While the majority of large companies are decentralised, a variety of corporate leadership styles may exist. The head office may have a

## 2. The federal IT organisation

The management and organisation of information technology (IT) functions is also becoming increasingly decentralised. A combination of technological and business factors has led to increased dispersion of both IT resources and responsibility for IT activities. Synott & Gruber asserted that “... the distribution of data processing activities will spread dramatically in the 1980s”. [33] The empirical studies of Martino [21], Benjamin et al [2] and Feeny, Edwards & Earl [10,11] indicate the accuracy of this prediction.

Not all IT activities, however, are devolved to the same extent. Separating the 'IT function' into its component activities provides a useful perspective. Three main categories are commonly recognised [22,23,26,27,38]:

(a) Systems management; including strategy and policy formulation, planning, coordinating, resourcing and controlling.

(b) Systems operations; including data centre & network management, procurement, systems programming, operations management and technical support.

(c) Systems Development; including identification of business system needs, selection and specification of systems, project management, system programming and testing, implementation, user training and support.

Feeny, Edwards & Earl [10] concluded that a federal IT management structure is now the dominant structural form in multi-business companies. This was defined as follows:

“IS is a distributed function, with each business unit containing and largely controlling its own capability. However, there is in addition a central IS unit reporting to corporate management which has responsibility for defined aspects of policy and architecture across the organisation, and which may deliver some common or shared services. It may or may not be coincident with the IS unit for corporate HQ.”

A key characteristic is the distribution of IT responsibility between the head office and business units. It is neither entirely centralised nor decentralised, but a hybrid. While many authors have provided general guidelines for organising and managing IT in decentralised organisations $[18,20,29,32,33,35,36,39]$ , no empirical study has specifically addressed the management of IT activities in organisations with a federal structure.

Exploratory research by the author found that the role of the corporate IT function is of great interest to IT managers in large companies. What should the centre do in a federally decentralised IT organisation? Few studies shed light on this area. IT leadership is, however, asserted to be a major issue of corporate management in the 1990s. Dixon & John [8] state:

"The dispersion of the management of information technology as a by-product of its deep penetration throughout the corporation is today a major topic of discussion.... Dispersion and the potential of costly chaos detrimental to the profitability of the company are symptoms of a lack of understanding by senior managers of their role in leading and managing the business use and impact of technology."

## 3. The survey

The survey set out to answer the following questions:

\- How prevalent is the federal IT management structure? What varieties of federal structure exist?

\- How is responsibility for the three main IT activities (IT management, systems operations and systems development) distributed in large companies?

\- How is the distribution of responsibility for IT activities expected to change?

\- What roles does the central IT unit play and how are they predicted to change?

\- What is the relationship between centralisation of a company generally and centralisation of its IT organisation?

The population for the survey was the 1989–90 Times 200 – the largest companies, by turnover, in the UK. Matched pairs of postal questionnaires were used: (1) a five page IT Organisation questionnaire was sent to the IT Director or Senior IT Manager, and (2) a two page General Management questionnaire to a senior general management executive. The population for the IT Organisation questionnaire was derived by telephoning the head offices of each of the companies. The General Management questionnaire was sent to the respondents of the IT Organisation questionnaire, with a request that it be passed on to a general managerial colleague. This was felt to be a necessary to enhance the response rate. Both questionnaires were designed in accordance with the commonly accepted principles [16,17] and were pretested prior to being sent out.

50 companies responded to the IT Organisation questionnaire, a response rate of 25%. 28 of the sample of 50 responded to the General Management questionnaire.

## 4. IT organisation survey results

## (a) Who responded?

Figure 1 depicts the business sector distribution of the sample - showing that it covers a representative range of industries. Figure 2 depicts the job titles of the respondents. The majority (64%) were responsible for group-wide IT issues, and the rest were typically IT planners or central IT unit managers. Out of the 32 managers with group-wide IT responsibility, only 9 were without responsibility for a central IT unit – 7 full time IT Directors and 2 IT Planning Executives. The majority of Group IT Managers appear to combine group wide responsibilities with those of managing a central, corporate, IT unit.

## (b) How prevalent is the federal structure and what variety of federal structures exist?

A list of 17 IT activities, as shown in appendix one, was presented in the IT management questionnaire. Respondents were asked to estimate the degree of responsibility for each activity at the head office, divisional and business unit levels. The IT management structure category of each company was derived by taking the average of the central IT unit's responsibilities for IT management activities only. (All, Most, Some or None coded as 3, 2, 1, 0). The data thus fell into four categories. The terms used by Main & Short [20] are adopted as preliminary labels as follows:

In the rest the centre has a less dominant role, with responsibility for IT management being distributed throughout the group. The low proportion of companies with decentralised IT management is due to sampling bias. The focus of this research, however, is on companies with a federal structure. Little emphasis is placed on the centralised or decentralised structures except to include them for completeness and comparison where appropriate.

(c) How is IT responsibility distributed?

Responses were grouped and averaged to create a single figure for each of the 3 main IT activities. Figure 4 reveals the relative % responsibility for each IT activity at each organisational level. There are distinct differences in the distribution of IT activities in the four categories.

\- Centralised IT management is characterised by devolution of only 10–15% of systems operations and systems development activity.

\- In Centralised hybrids around 50% of IT responsibility is devolved. The centre, however, retains a dominant influence and is responsible for group-wide IT management and for developing and operating many of the group's systems.

\- In Decentralised hybrids 70–80% of IT responsibility is devolved. Development and operation of systems is primarily a business unit responsibility. Significant use is made of external resources: consultants, facilities management companies and contractors.

\- In companies with decentralised IT management, all activities are fully devolved.

The old debate about centralisation vs. decentralisation of IT is thus largely irrelevant. A federal IT organisation cannot be usefully described in these terms alone. Todays companies exhibit a variety of structures simultaneously – organising IT management, operations and development activities differently. Overall, IT management activities are the most centralised, with systems operations being more devolved, and systems development the most devolved.

## (d) How is the distribution predicted to change?

Respondents were asked to predict change in the distribution of IT responsibility over the next 3 years. Figure 5 depicts the net percentage of respondents in each hybrid category predicting an increase or decrease in responsibility at each level. (This data, however, describes the predictions of IT managers only. The extent to which individual respondents are aware of wider corporate influences on the structure of their IT functions cannot really be assessed. Also, it is possible that responses are biased by a commitment to the status quo.)

## (i) IT management

In companies with a centralised hybrid IT management structure, approximately equal numbers of respondents predict an increase as a decrease in corporate IT management responsibility. IT management responsibility at the divisional and business unit levels is, however, predicted to increase. Decentralised hybrid companies, on the other hand, predict a major shift away from corporate control of IT management. Although the responsibility of business units will increase, most responsibility will be focused at the divisional level.

## (ii) Systems operations

Centralised hybrid companies predict little change. Divisions and business units will, however, assume greater responsibility for operational activities. In companies with a decentralised hybrid structure, once again, a strong decrease in central responsibility is predicted.

## (iii) Systems development

Responsibility for development activities at the centre is predicted to decrease slightly in centralised hybrid companies, primarily in favour of business units and external companies – contractors and software houses. The vast majority of decentralised hybrids predict a major decrease in the development responsibility of the centre.

(e) What roles does the corporate IT function play and how is this predicted to change?

Roles reveal more of the objectives of the central IT function – what it seeks to achieve in terms of group-wide IT management. The average of the responses to the 17 roles was then used to indicate the perceived importance of the central role overall. Figure 6 reveals a fairly consistent continuum of perceived importance of the central role across the IT management structure categories. This provides further evidence of the existence of the four discrete categories identified in section 4(b).

The lower figure for the importance of central roles now in the centralised category reflects the fact that many of the central roles in the questionnaire concern IT management in a distributed, federal, setting, and so have little meaning in a fully centralised organisation. Interestingly, respondents in the centralised and decentralised categories predict that the centre will assume greater responsibility in the future. This is perhaps indicative of a trend away from full centralisation or decentralisation to more federal IT management structures.

Figure 7 reveals the relative importance placed on each of the 17 roles by the centralised and decentralised hybrids. It shows both those roles which are perceived as most vital for the centre to perform, and the key differences between the two categories. The most universally acknowledged role of the centre was to build senior management IT awareness. Centralised hybrids regarded functional leadership, promotion of IT initiatives, detailed review of business unit IT strategies and the development of group-wide infrastructure systems as important central roles. Decentralised hybrids emphasised provision of consulting services and review of business unit IT strategies (though to a lesser extent than centralised hybrids).

Other roles of the centre suggested by respondents were:

\- Ensure consistency of management practices across the group.

\- Controlling the rate of investment in IT.

\- Providing delivery capability for innovative IT solutions.

## 5. IT organisation survey conclusions

While most large companies have relatively devolved structures for IT management, systems operations and systems development activities, a variety of management structures and central roles exists. The central IT unit may have a major or minor role in group wide IT affairs depending upon the corporate approach to IT management.

The survey has provided further evidence of the dominance of the federal IT management structure in large multi-business companies. It has added useful refinements to this concept by defining in detail the distinction between two categories of federal structure: the centralised and the decentralised hybrid.

This distinction provides a new perspective on corporate IT management, indicating that not all central IT units have the same objectives. The key differences between the centralised and decentralised hybrid categories are summarised in Figure 8.

The two categories of federal structure are different on two principal dimensions: structure and role. The centralised hybrid is relatively centralised in structure and is predicted to stay much the same. The decentralised hybrid is relatively decentralised, and is predicted to become more decentralised. The perceived importance of central IT roles is, overall, much higher in centralised than decentralised hybrids.

Figure 8 suggests that IT leadership, IT strategy and infrastructure are the key roles which differentiate the categories. A more useful label for centralised hybrids is Strategic Leadership, and for decentralised hybrids is Strategic Guidance. The word ‘strategic’ is used to describe the high level management of a portfolio of IT resources on a group-wide basis in the same way as the term ‘strategic management’ denotes the high level management of a portfolio of businesses.

The Strategic Leadership and Strategic Guidance categories characterise two styles of IT management. The word ‘style’ is used to include the two dimensions of structure and role – with role reflecting corporate management style in a manner similar to Goold & Campbell’s planning and control dimensions [12].

A major finding of the study is that divisional IT managements have a more significant role in overall group-wide IT management than has previously been recognised in the literature. Most notably, companies with a Strategic Guidance style of IT management focus most of their IT management activities at the divisional level. This conclusion puts a new perspective on the concept of the ‘Chief Information Officer’ (CIO) [2,13,33]. In Strategic guidance style IT managements, it would seem more likely that IT leadership will come from a team of divisional level IT managers, a ‘CIO team’ rather than a single CIO.

## 6. The general management survey

The objective of the second, follow-up, survey was to relate the IT management styles to more general organisational characteristics – further developing the work of Ahituv et al [1], Olsen & Chervany [23] and Tavakolian [34]. What type of organisation would adopt a Strategic Leadership IT management style in preference to a Strategic Guidance style?

28 companies responded to a follow-up questionnaire. Most respondents were senior executives, including CEOs, Finance Directors and Corporate Development managers. Two questionnaires were received from companies with a centralised IT management style and 13 from each of the Strategic Leadership and Strategic Guidance styles.

Eleven head office roles were presented, as shown in appendix three. Respondents were asked to rate them on a scale ranging from unimportant to important. These two measures, degree of centralisation of general management functions and perceived importance of the central role, provide a useful description of the ‘general management style’ of the companies studied.

The Spearman Rank-Order Correlation Coefficient [31] was used to test the association between IT management style and the degree of centralisation of general management functions. The coefficient, $r_{s}$ , was calculated at 0.66, which is significant at the 0.01 level. We can conclude that a significant positive association exists between general management style and IT management style.

Figure 9 charts the data, revealing the extent to which IT management style is more or less centralised than general management style. Woolfe asserted that “…many groups are well advised to adopt an organisational style for information systems that is offset towards the centre… (in the interests of) …enabling the central information systems function to place more emphasis on its responsibilities for integrating its plans with those of the group as a whole, for developing group resources, and for defining standards.” [36] With such a small sample the difference is not definitive, but it is apparent that 15 respondents had an IT management style which was more centralised than the general management style; only 10 had a less centralised IT management style (3 were ‘on the line’).

Companies with a Strategic Leadership IT management style also appear to be more likely to adopt an IT management style that is more centralised than the general management style, and Strategic Guidance style companies are less likely to do so. Strategic Leadership companies had an average degree of centralisation of general management functions of 1.58, compared to 1.89 for IT functions. In the Strategic Guidance companies this relationship is reversed, with average figures of 1.30 and 1.01 respectively. Companies with a Strategic Leadership IT management style, it seems, perceive some benefit in more centralised management control over the IT function than of other general management functions.

## 7. Summary and conclusion

This paper has provided further evidence of the trend towards increasing decentralisation of the IT function in large organisations and has documented the federal IT organisation: the dominant organisation structure for distributed computing.

Two styles of federal IT structure were identified, based on the distribution of responsibility for IT activities in the companies and the perceived importance of the central IT management role: A Strategic Leadership IT management style is characterised by strong leadership and coordination of extensive centralised and distributed IT resources. A Strategic Guidance IT management style is characterised by less formal central IT management of largely autonomous distributed IT resources, where divisional IT units dominate, and the central IT function has a more advisory, guidance, and consultative role.

The federal approach is based on the need to promote both responsiveness to the IT needs of individual business units and group wide coordination and/or integration of systems efforts – balancing local effectiveness and responsiveness with efficiency, control and promotion of group wide synergy. The Strategic Leadership style is one focused on IT integration and synergy, but not at the expense of business IT responsiveness. The Strategic Guidance style is one focused on business IT responsiveness, with IT integration and synergy where possible and appropriate.

The extent to which corporate management pursues business integration and/or business unit autonomy and responsiveness is a major determinant of corporate management style $[12,25]$ . This survey has revealed that IT management style is closely related to aspects of corporate management style: firms that are more highly centralised in terms of the distribution of responsibility for general management functions and the perceived importance of the corporate role are more likely to adopt a Centralised or Strategic Leadership IT management style. Conversely, firms that are less centralised are more likely to adopt a Strategic Guidance or Decentralised IT management style.

This conclusion stresses the need for IT management style to reflect the overall management style of the company. A highly decentralised IT management style will likely be inappropriate in a company whose strategy emphasises increasing integration and coordination of business operations. Conversely, an excessively centralised IT management style will be inappropriate in a company with fiercely independent, autonomous, business units.

There would seem to be two obvious directions for further research in this area. Firstly, what are the implications of IT management style in terms of the effectiveness of the IT function? Is any one of the styles demonstrably superior? (as suggested by von Simpson [35], who advocates a “centrally decentralised IS organisation” similar to the Strategic Leadership style). This study has suggested that the styles are a consequence of different corporate objectives, and so this question will be a difficult one to answer. There will likely be no optimal style because the corporate IT objectives of companies differ.

A more appropriate research direction would be to further explore the concept of fit between a company and its IT management style. An important determinant of IS effectiveness is the extent to which the corporate approach to IT is congruent with the requirements of the firm. This survey has provided further descriptive evidence of the existence of fit, but many questions remain: What is the rationale for fit between a company and its IT management style? What are the consequences of ‘good’ or ‘bad’ fit? What factors explain achievement of good or bad fit? What can be done to promote good fit?
