# Post-divestiture telecommunications Priorities, issues and concerns

Gholamreza Torkzadeh

The University of Toledo, Toledo, Ohio, USA

The rapid growth of online systems has made telecommunications technology a critical component of information systems in most organizations. Many of these online systems support crucial operational activities of the organizations. In the USA, since the divestiture of AT&T, telecommunications function has expanded in both complexity and importance. There is a great need for understanding the role and positioning of telecommunications in the enterprise and the major issues that concern management of this function. Using a survey of 137 US organizations, this article reports organizational concerns of telecommunications management. It describes telecommunications position in organizations, level of investments in telecommunications technology, issues affecting its management, and factors influencing vendor selection, network management, and telecommunications system design. Some implications for national and international organizations are discussed.

Keywords: Telecommunications, Management, Data communications, Information systems, Divestiture.

## 1. Introduction

The field of telecommunications is playing a vital role in public and private organizations. The emerging integration of computers and communications technology has expanded the scope of business opportunities to achieve the economic and competitive benefits of information systems. Large corporations may spend anywhere from 1.5% to more than 2.5% of their total revenue on communications (Hall and McCauley, 1987). Every work day, U.S. computer networks transmit close to \$1 trillion among financial institutions, an amount equal to 25% of the GNP (Haftner, 1988). Efficient computer-mediated communication to support geographically remote group work (Gallupe and McKeen, 1990) or to offer alternative communication tools to alleviate common communication problems (Brittain White and Massello, 1987) depends on efficient telecommunications. As Hammer and Mangurian (1987) suggest, it is no longer meaningful to talk about information processing and communications as independent activities.

Handling data communications is an influencing factor in effective utilization of information systems and individual performance. Human growth and development, facilitated by the rapidly emerging end-user computing phenomenon, is necessary for the creative and effective use of computer based information systems: opportunities provided by telecommunications technology are essential in realizing its full potential. In this domain, data communication assumes a new role which merits serious consideration of its efficacy, since systems usage is enhanced when users within the enterprise can jointly develop applications. Examples abound. Couger (1988) cites plans for telecommuting as one of the top 20 human resource issues for the 1990s. In their study of measurement and analysis of computer user abilities, Cheney and Nelson (1988) found that ability to handle data communication was one of the four important factors in measuring user ability to develop applications, and one of eleven factors measuring user ability in general. Thus, computer related education and training programs should assume greater integration of data communication skills.

The growth of telecommunications technology and its impact on the competitive position of organizations is presenting new challenges for information system (IS) managers. Hall and McCauley (1987) contend that the most successful corporations in the future will be those that view the network as a critical tool in seizing new business opportunities, improving productivity and the quality of work life, and gaining a competitive edge in the marketplace. In a recent survey, Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) report that 53% of the information system executives ranked telecommunications in their top ten issues in information systems management. In the USA, this perceived importance is attributed to factors such as American Telephone and Telegraph's (AT&T) breakup, rapid technological change, and use of telecommunications for competitive advantage.

The divestiture of AT&T in January 1984 has affected most aspects of telecommunications management, including organizational, personnel, acquisition, and implementation. New communication infrastructures and services have been planned and developed within the post-divestiture context (Dutton and Makinen, 1987). Since the AT&T divestiture, four of the seven Bell regional holding companies have established their own proprietary research and technology centers; moreover, all seven have begun information gateways and voice and electronic mail services, lately allowed by U.S. District Judge Harold H. Green, when he relaxed his earlier restrictions against their offering any information related services (Bell, 1989).

There is a need for better understanding of telecommunications management: its priorities and concerns. This paper presents results obtained from a survey of 137 US organizations with telecommunications functions. The survey focussed on:

a. Position of the telecommunications function within the organization;

b. Telecommunications expenditure compared with that of information systems;

c. Organizational concerns over telecommunications before and after AT&T divestiture;

d. Factors influencing telecommunication manager decisions on design, vendor selection, and network management; and

e. Factors affecting network management by type of industry and telecommunications budget.

## 2. Business telecommunications

Business value depends on the total system, rather than on the separability of the communications or processing function. There is an accelerating trend toward integrating telecommunications, data processing, and office automation into an overall IS business function. In addition to its traditional functions, IS is rapidly expanding to include decision support systems (DSS), office automation, factory automation, end-user computing, and telecommunications. The key motivation is to take advantage of the entire range of computer/communications technology to enhance the performance of individuals and the organization. Sullivan and Smart (1987) contend that telecommunications planning should view enhanced communication as the primary purpose of an investment in IS, and endeavor to show how emerging networking technologies could be used to support business objectives.

Telecommunications have become essential to the success of the enterprise, directly affecting the quality and profitability of products and services (Dickinson, 1984). The new technologies of communications have the power to change the competitive game for companies of all sizes. Many companies already use telecommunications to gain a competitive edge. The restructuring of communications has provided new opportunities and new environment for companies. Clemons and McFarlan (1986) contend that many companies have reshaped their customer/supplier relationship, in some cases changing a company's position in its market, changing its market, or creating a new product markets. The technological convergence has now made possible the coordinated management of entire corporate IS to achieve specific corporate goals (Misra and Belitsos, 1987). This technology facilitates what Hiltz and Turoff (1978) call the “collective intelligence” of an organization.

In recent years, telecommunications management has been drastically affected by a number of important phenomena. It is possible to look at these under three categories: (a) the integration of semiconductor technology and telephony, creating advanced digital electronic communication affordable by businesses of all sizes; (b) the divestiture of AT&T, deregulating the industry, and altering the market; and (c) increased demand for on-line interactive information to meet the needs of ad hoc DSS.

The integrated technology has lead to enhanced modern digitized electronic communication to meet the demands of the information age. The technology now allows integrated transmission over the same telecommunications medium of digitized voice, data, and visual information, under computer software control. Use of packet switching technology, primarily run and controlled by minicomputers, optimizes network utilization and minimizes transmission delay. Modern electronic telecommunications have increased transmission speed, capacity, efficiency, and reliability. Decreasing cost of semiconductors and computer technology have made advanced telecommunications technology accessible to more businesses of all sizes.

The second factor setting a trend in the US telecommunications industry, affecting the management of this technology within organizations, is the divestiture of AT&T. The breakup of AT&T in January 1984 is considered the world's largest corporate divestiture. This activity had significant consequences for the industry, consumers, and even telecommunications research and development. The divestiture of AT&T and its effect has been the subject of numerous discussions (see the special issue of IEEE Communications Magazine, December 1985) but the subject of few research studies. Major changes have been brought about by changes in the regulation of communications in the United States (Kriebel, 1984; Hart, 1988). As was hoped, open competition in the industry is greater now than before the divestiture. Hundreds of companies now provide just what Federal District Judge Herold H. Green and the Justice Department wanted to encourage: long distance services, customer-premises equipment, and information services (Bell, 1988). AT&T was allowed to enter the computer industry.

Another important factor, affecting the management of telecommunications, is the emergence of what is called the “information age” with its increasing demand for integrated up-to-date information in electronic form. Because of its high speed of adoption, information can be managed as never before (Stalberg, 1987). Within less than three decades, the networking concept that allows these computers to exchange data has evolved into a necessity for almost any IS. In many organizations, networks, particularly local area networks (LAN) that link desktop computers, large data bases, and even telephones, have increased the value of personal computers just as much as they have helped the growth of IS. The demand for information has also increased due to the growth of end-user computing (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988).

In addition, there is growing international competition for the telecommunications market. The blueprint for a single Europe after 1992 calls for a Western Europe-wide telecommunications infrastructure based on the integrated-services digital network (ISDN) and other digital communication links (see the issues of IEEE Spectrum, June 1990 and Communications of the ACM, April 1990). To better compete on a worldwide basis, Siemens AG, the biggest electronics company in Europe, has increased its worldwide marketing efforts seeking increased share of computer and telecommunications markets (Guterl, 1990). Important changes have been made or proposed for other providers such as British Telecom, France Telecom, and Alcatel NV of Belgium (Bell, 1991; Davignon, 1987). Goodman (1991) suggests that Europe now has the opportunity to end the century with some world-historical changes resulting in an enormous impact on information technologies. The telecommunications Council of the Ministry of Post and Telecommunication of Japan has published a report in March 1990 suggesting that Nippon Telegraph & Telephone Co. (NTT) should be split into three companies to increase competition (Saito, 1990).

New technology has made it possible for computer-based IS to structure the transmission and accumulation of information in ways never before possible. The technological convergence of telecommunications requires new management skills and the expertise to utilize the resources. Telecommunication managers face challenging tasks, including network design, vendor selection, voice/data integration, reliability, ease of use, new technology, innovative use, organizational issues, and personnel. Network management has become a necessity for increased complexity in wide area networks as well as LAN (Stix, 1989). Presumably the lower costs and opportunities require managers to choose the best reliable equipment from the many offerings. There is a great need for better understanding of priorities, issues, and concerns.

## 3. The study

In order to obtain responses for our questions, a questionnaire was developed based on literature reviews and five interviews conducted with three telecommunication managers from manufacturing, services, and wholesale industries and two academics involved in research studies on the management of telecommunications. Next, the same respondents were solicited for their critique of the questionnaire and its content validity. Their feedback resulted in revision of the questionnaire, thereby improving its focus and constructs.

This questionnaire surveyed 622 telecommunication managers selected from a directory of three thousand MIS/telecommunication managers/directors in the USA from New England, the Southern States, Mid-Atlantic, Mid-West, and Washington D.C. A sample of 137 usable responses, representing as many firms, was obtained; resulting in a return rate of 22%.

## 4. Results

Management experience in the telecommunications field is indicative of its emergence in organizations. The results indicate that almost all telecommunication managers have had experience in the IS fields: less than one percent indicate no experience in it. There is a significant relationship $r = 0.28 \text{ at } p < 0.005$ between respondents experience in IS and telecommunications, indicating possible responsibility overlap for the two functions. This is also reflected by the name of the departments responsible for managing the telecommunications function. About ninety percent of the respondents mention three departments: Information Systems (44%), Telecommunications (30%), and Computer/Data Services (16%). Though the primary source of experience for respondents is IS, 47% indicate having worked in at least one other functional area within their current and/or previous organization. They have experience in: Operations/Engineering, Computer Systems, Finance, and Marketing/Sales.

## 4.1. Issues and concerns

A set of questions addressed vendor selection criteria using a five point scale: 1 = not at all important to 5 = very important. Managers responsible for the telecommunications function consider vendor support the most important factor for vendor selection (see Table 3); cost is not among the top five.

The telecommunication managers were also asked about their organizations' level of concern over technological, organizational, behavioral, personnel, vendor, and strategic aspects both before and after the divestiture of AT&T. A five point scale was used for these questions: from 1 = no concern at all to 5 = very great concern. There is a significant (p < 0.001) increase in the level of concern over each issue since the divestiture of AT&T (see Table 4). With an increase in the mean score from 2.65 to 3.75, vendor issues have replaced strategic importance as the most important issue.

Using two sets of questions, the survey addressed issues pertinent to telecommunications design and network management. A five point scale was used for both sets of questions: from 1 = not at all important to 5 = very important. Tables 5 and 6 provide statistics on factors influencing telecommunications design and network management, respectively. With a mean of 4.7, reliability and consistency is slightly more important than the others. When network managers were asked the importance of the issues, network management and control was marginally most important.

The telecommunication managers were also asked about the relative changes they foresee in the next five years. A five point scale was again used, with 1 = no change to 5 = great deal of change. Table 7 shows their response. The managers expect most changes in networking and least in protocols.

## 4.2. Type of industry and budget size

To determine whether factors influencing vendor selection and network management are uniform, data were analyzed by type of industry and size of budget. In order to obtain an adequate sample size, responses by industry were divided into three groups of “manufacturing & processing,” “finance, banking & insurance,” and “others.” Similarly, capital budget for telecommunications was divided into three groups of “less than \$0.5 million,” “\$0.5 to \$5 million,” and “more than \$5 million.” These groupings are termed “small,” “medium,” and “large” in the following text.

Using the new groupings, Scheffe's test was used to contrast mean scores for the three possible pairs of industry types and budget sizes for each factor. Analyses by type of industry reveal significant contrasts at $p < 0.05$ for two vendor selection factors and four network management factors. Tables 8 and 9 provide statistics on significant pairs for vendor selection and network management factors respectively. No significant contrasts were found for the remaining items.

The analyses indicate that compared with financial companies, manufacturing and other industries are more concerned about cost and speed factors, while manufacturing and other industries are more concerned about two issues of network management: vendor selection and network technology. However, financial companies seem to be more concerned about shortage of telecommunications personnel and integration of voice/data than other industries. However, the results still show general concern in all areas.

## 5. Summary and discussions

Increasingly, telecommunication is recognized as a vital component of information systems in most organizations. Transmission of information has grown at a faster rate in the last five years than in all of the previous thirty, and this is only the beginning (Stalberg, 1987). Technology has seen major changes during the last decade; miniaturization of electronic circuitry accompanied by reduced power requirements, has contributed to the development of powerful telecommunications technology. This combined with the deregulated market environment has provided choices that can affect a business: its position in a market, etc. Dramatic reductions in the cost is changing the way business is done (Porter and Millar, 1985).

The study suggests that telecommunication managers are more concerned with vendor selection, vendor support, maintenance, expandability, and reliability than with cost, speed, or environmental restrictions. The breakup of AT&T seems to have increased the concern over technological, organizational, behavioral, and personnel. Obviously, it has also introduced new vendor issues. With the increase of end-user computing and its demand for more access to data bases and connectivity, ease of use has become more acute. Factors influencing vendor selection and network control seem to vary with the type of industry and size of the telecommunications budget.

The telecommunications equipment and services is strongly affected by various national regulatory regimes for telecommunications (Hart, 1988). Most nations have established regulatory systems based on the idea that there was a “natural monopoly” in telecommunications infrastructure. In November, 1990 the British Government announced that it would deregulate much of the nation’s telecommunications industry. In January, 1991 the French Government decided to remove France Telecom from the control of the Posts & Telecommunications Ministry in order to make it more “internationally competitive.” This breakup would be analogous to the way the U.S. Bell System was split and for similar reasons: to solve the problems of unequal competitive conditions (Stix, 1990).

There are practical implications of the changes in the telecommunications industry and technology for those involved with its management in the organization. Enhanced technology, growing complexity, and wider offerings necessitate continuous management training for making decisions and more carefully selecting equipments and services. International competition is adding a new dimension to the task of managing the telecommunications function. Concepts such as “global companies or global village”, reflect this wider market environment and its impact on production, availability, and applications of computing and telecommunications among and within countries. Europe would likely become the world’s largest integrated market in terms of total manufacturing and buying power, providing opportunities and risks for decision makers.

There are important and interesting research questions related to telecommunications management. For example, it is useful to know whether the focus of telecommunications planning differs from IS planning. While building on the heritage of IS planning, there is a need for more focused planning for telecommunications function. Sullivan and Smart (1987) contend that network planning should concentrate on business information exchange. Such planning, they suggest, would view enhanced communication as the primary purpose of an investment in information technology, and endeavor to show how emerging networking technologies could be used to support business objectives.

Another research question is related to the impact of telecommunications on end-user computing efficiency or productivity. As end-users develop their expertise and confidence, they would move toward developing larger and more complex applications. This often means involvement in an application being developed by another end-user, such as functional support personnel. User friendly data communication can facilitate this trend.

The emergence of information networking as an important management issue is largely due to the transformation of the telecommunications industry and distributed technology. Perhaps the most important reason for the emergence of networking as a business priority, is the search for competitive advantage. The competitive edge occurs because new communications channels are being opened to forward marketing and logistics strategies to customers and suppliers. The growth of global information systems and the search for international competitive advantage provide a rich research agenda for the field of computing and telecommunications.

## 6. Conclusions

The growth of telecommunications technology and the entry of competition with the divestiture of AT&T have influenced the management of the telecommunications function. There is a need to identify the type and level of this impact. This paper suggests that major changes have occurred because of advancements in integrated technology, competition, and the increased demand for on-line information by organizations. Since new technology has made telecommunications more affordable, medium and small companies are now acquiring it. For many businesses, the decision is not whether or not to use this technology, but how best to deal with its organizational, behavioral, strategic, and control issues.
