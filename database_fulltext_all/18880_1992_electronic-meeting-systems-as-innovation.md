# Electronic meeting systems as innovation A study of the innovation process

Joey F. George and Jay F. Nunamaker, Jr.

Department of MIS, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

Joseph S. Valacich

Decision & Information Systems Department, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN 47405, USA

Electronic Meeting Systems (EMS) are slowly moving out of university environments into work organizations. They constitute an innovative method of supporting group meetings. This paper reports on the innovation process in one organization that recently adopted and implemented an EMS. The paper traces the innovation process through four stages: conception of an idea; proposal; decision to adopt; and implementation. Important factors from the innovation literature are considered as an explanation of the innovation process involving EMS in this particular organization. $^{1}$

Keywords: Innovation, Cooperative work, Implementation, Electronic meeting systems.

## 2. Relevant innovation literature

According to Zaltman and colleagues [33], the term innovation is usually employed in three different contexts. In the first, innovation “blends the idea of invention with that of adoption.” Two or more existing concepts or entities are combined into something that has not existed before. In the second context, innovation is used to describe the process of bringing an existing innovation into some context and making it part of that context. This is a “process of adoption and internalization.” In the third context, innovation refers to the specific idea, practice or thing that has been invented, independent of the adoption process. In this paper, we use innovation in the second context, specifically, as the process through which an existing technological innovation is brought into an organization and eventually implemented.

The innovation process is commonly thought of as having four steps: conception of an idea; proposal; decision to adopt; and implementation [3] $^{2}$ . If the idea is new and must be developed from scratch in the conception stage, that development is called process “creation.” If the idea is already well developed when the innovation process begins, it is called process “diffusion” [35]. Thus, the amount of time and effort devoted to the first step of the innovation process depends on whether the organization is developing an idea on its own or adopting one that is already established.

The innovation process tends to begin when someone recognizes that there is an organizational need that should be addressed, or that a new technology is available. Successful innovation is thought to occur when the recognition of the need and the recognition of the technology to address that need occur simultaneously $[8]$ . However, several factors affect the likelihood that the proposal to innovate will be approved and that the implementation will be successful.

One of the most important factors is management support for the innovation. Some studies have found that management's attitude toward the innovation was important to its assimilation and perceived success [e.g., 23]. Other studies, however, have found management-related variables to be less important than such organizational level variables as size and centralization [18]. Leonard-Barton and Deschamps [21] found no general support for their hypothesized relationship between the perceptions of management support for an innovation and its use, but they did find support for this relationship for particular types of employees. For example, if the employee was low in innovativeness for this type of innovation, or if the task was of low importance to the employee, then there was a positive relationship between perceived management support and innovation use.

Daft found other factors which may be just as important as management support for the successful initiation and adoption of innovations. One of these is the origination of the innovation proposal; whether the innovation is proposed by those workers who are closest to the technical core of the organization or by administrators. He proposed a dual-core model of the organization, with an administrative core and a technical core. Technical innovation is more likely to be adopted if it is proposed within the technical core, while administrative innovation is more likely to be adopted if it is proposed within the administrative core.

Contextual factors play an important role. In some organizations that Daft studied, all of the innovations were proposed in the technical core. In others, the administrative core was relatively more important. The dominance of one core over the other depended on such elements as the professionalism of the workers and the degree of coupling between the cores. Where technical core workers were more professionally-oriented, the technical core was more innovative. Where the cores were tightly coupled, and hence more hierarchically centralized, the administrative core was more innovative. Zmud found that group receptivity to change had a greater impact on technical innovations than on administrative ones. Kimberly and Evanisko [18] found that different sets of variables better explained the decision to adopt a technological innovation than the decision to adopt an administrative innovation. Organizational variables were important predictors for both types of innovation, but certain managerial and organizational context variables were relatively more important for technological innovations than for administrative ones.

## 3. EMS and meeting support

EMS have been designed to provide computer support to groups engaged in organizational meetings. Recent studies show that meetings take up 60–70% of the time of information system managers and 30–80% of the time of general managers [15,24]. In the future, as organizational environments become more turbulent, the time spent in meetings is expected to increase [14].

EMS are designed to improve the productivity of meetings and reduce the opportunity to turn the meetings into political battlegrounds [4]. Much of what we know about how successfully EMS accomplish these goals comes from laboratory studies. In five studies that compared EMS decision room support to no computer support, group performance was better with EMS support in four studies [10,16,27,34], with no differences found in the fifth study [11]. Most of these studies also reported on the satisfaction of group members, with either the group process, the group outcome, or both. The findings vary considerably. For satisfaction with group process, EMS groups were more satisfied [27], less satisfied [10], or no different from non-EMS groups [11,16]. For outcome satisfaction, EMS groups were more satisfied [27] or less satisfied [10]. The findings from field studies are more consistent. In four published studies of the use of Arizona's GroupSystems in the field, EMS decision room use led to increased effectiveness and highly satisfied users [5,25,26,31].

Most published studies of EMS, then, whether conducted in the laboratory or the field, indicate EMS decision room use can lead to improved group effectiveness. The prognosis for satisfaction is not so clear, but real groups using EMS for their own problems are satisfied with system use. The benefits such a system can offer to an organization do not necessarily ensure their instant adoption and smooth implementation, however.

## 4. Methodology

The current study can be generically described as a case study. It is in the form of what Downs and Mohr [7] called the “Innovation-Decision Design,” but it has an N of 1, as only one innovation decision in one organization is being studied. The rationale for using the case methodology over other research methodologies has been discussed in detail by many authors (e.g., [1,17,32]). The primary motivation stems from the opportunity to study a phenomenon in a natural setting. The case methodology allows “how” and “why” questions to be asked, e.g., how does the adoption of an EMS affect organizational decision making; why should an organization adopt an EMS? Case research also allows the integration of both quantitative and qualitative information [30]. Lee [20] argues that case studies conducted according to a scientific method satisfy the standards of the natural science model of scientific research. We now describe our research design, data collection and data analysis techniques.

## 4.1. Research design

The research design can be described, using Yin's terminology, as a single-case, embedded design. Yin suggests that a single-case design be used in any of the following conditions: the situation represents a critical case for testing a well-formulated theory; the situation represents a revelatory case (i.e., one previously inaccessible); or the situation represents a unique case (i.e., the situation is rare, so it is worth documenting). The motivation for selecting the single-case design in this study is that the situation represents a unique case: the organizational adoption of a technology virtually unknown inside many organizations.

There are two types of single-case designs: embedded or holistic. A holistic design focuses on only the global nature of the case outcomes (i.e., will IHS adopt the technology). A limitation of the holistic approach is that the findings are often too abstract to infer causality. Also, due to the global nature of the measures, researchers often lose touch with the nature of the study. Embedded designs examine both global and local outcomes. The outcomes of specific groups meetings (i.e., effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction) can be used to develop a trail of evidence that can help to better explain global outcomes. Here, the embedded levels of analysis are (1) the global decision to adopt or to not adopt the technology; (2) the administrative and technical cores, and (3) the outcomes of individual meetings.

## 4.2. Data collection methods

Case data were collected by two researchers, gathered from multiple sources, and using multiple methods. By employing more than one researcher in the data collection process, higher validity might be attained as different interpretations or “gaps” in the data may be rectified $[2]$ .

“Two researchers can capture greater richness of data and rely more confidently on the accuracy of the data” [1]. Data were collected using multiple means, including: internal documents, archival records, interviews (both structured and unstructured), and direct observations. Data sources included IHS managers, meeting participants, group leaders and so on.

## 4.3. Data analysis

Data analysis was based on the integration of multiple data sources over the case's duration. A case database of meeting outcomes, interview transcripts and internal IHS documents was developed, allowing meeting outcomes and the perceptions of key actors to be analyzed over time. This strategy was chosen to help facilitate the development of a “chain of evidence” to support the more global nature of the case, as well as improve its internal validity.

## 5. The IHS project

## 5.1. Background

The Indian Health Service is part of the federal Department of Health and Human Services. It is one of seven agencies in the Public Health Service, under the control of the Undersecretary for Health, who in turn reports directly to the Secretary of Health and Human Services (Figure 1). The Director of IHS, based in Washington, D.C., controls 12 geographically defined areas, each under an Area Director, and a small number of offices that are unrelated to the geographic areas and that are controlled by Office Directors. These offices have specific charges and act as staff support to the IHS Director.

The Tucson Office is one of the 12 geographic area offices and is run by a Director. Unlike the other 11 area offices, however, the Tucson Office is simultaneously a staff support office, being the site of the Office of Health Program Research and Development (OHPRD). The Area Director of the Tucson office is also the Office Director of OHPRD. Tucson is the smallest of the 12 area offices, having responsibility for the health of only the reservation-based Tohono O'odham tribe and Tucson's urban Native American population. To this end, the office runs a hospital at Sells, Arizona, a health station, and a clinic. OHPRD has responsibility for several different programs: epidemiology; outreach field activities related to epidemiology; research review for the entire IHS medical program; the granting of research funding, and the IHS management development program. Both offices are based on the San Xavier Reservation, where 130 employees are stationed.

The organization chart for the Tucson Office is shown in Figure 2. The Division of Administrative Support oversees several smaller offices, such as property, procurement, financial management, and personnel. The Division of Health Information Systems Research and Development is in charge of medical records and billing, as well as systems development and ADP operations. The Division of Health Services Delivery has responsibility for the Sells hospital and the two out-patient clinics, and also houses the mental health and environmental health staffs. The remaining three divisions, Health Services Systems, Human Resources Systems, and Medical Systems Research are all part of OHPRD. All divisions report to the Director of the Tucson Office, who is listed in the organization chart as an Associate Director of the IHS.

GroupSystems was located in a small room at one end of the ground floor of the main building at San Xavier. A dozen networked NCR PC-IV microcomputers were located on tables configured in a U-shape. It was difficult to see the faces of the people across the U, as the machines were not recessed into the tables. The PC-IV's were supported by a BARCO projection display directed toward the open-end of the U.

The facility had problems with cooling and lighting. The room that was used has two windows with a southern exposure and limited central air conditioning. The room does have a window-mounted room air conditioner, but it is very noisy and has to be turned off for any verbal group interaction. This can be very uncomfortable for people and computers in the Tucson summer. The room has an abundance of fluorescent lighting, but one switch controls all of it. When the lights were on, group members could not see the output of the BARCO projector because information was “washed-out.” If the lights were off, it was difficult for people to see each other. A makeshift solution was to keep the lights on during verbal exchanges, and to turn the lights off and open the blinds of one window during nonverbal EMS activity.

## 5.2. Conception of the idea

GroupSystems was developed at the University of Arizona and can thus be regarded as a process “diffusion” innovation. Credit for the idea of bringing GroupSystems to IHS goes to the key actor in this entire process, a Ph.D. candidate in the MIS Department at the University, who also works for the IHS. We will give him the pseudonym: Jim Walker.

Walker has worked at IHS at the Tucson Office as a doctor since 1980, although he spent two years there as a systems engineer before he earned his M.D. Walker works in the DMSRD, but he also works informally for ISD because of his systems background. His desire to bring Group-Systems to IHS grew out of his longstanding concern with the limited productivity of IHS meetings. It was a situation he had wanted to address for some time, and it also provided an appropriate link between the University and IHS for his dissertation work.

Walker began a one-year long sabbatical in October 1988 to work on his dissertation. At about the same time, a new grant to the MIS Department allowed the hardware in one of its two GroupSystems facilities to be upgraded. This freed up old hardware that was used for more than three years. Suddenly, hardware needed for GroupSystems at IHS was available at little or no cost. With the consent of the MIS Department Head, Walker was able to propose to the Tucson Office of IHS that GroupSystems be installed there.

## 5.3. Proposal

Even with the availability of computer hardware at little or no cost, the management of the Tucson IHS office was not ready to accept the proposal to install GroupSystems. Negotiations between the MIS Department and the IHS over the installation of GroupSystems began in October. The primary area of disagreement was liability. The IHS did not want to be liable for the hardware but neither did the MIS Department, since it would no longer be on-campus. The negotiations lasted through December. IHS finally agreed to take reasonable precautions with regard to the hardware, and MIS agreed to limitation of liability.

Walker also had to request that the Tucson Office provide a dedicated room for the EMS; this was difficult, since office space is a rare commodity at San Xavier. Walker had to show that IHS would have to make no investment in the system. At the same time, he had to ask them to commit to provide chairs, tables, and a projection device. In the middle of the negotiations, some of the top managers in the Tucson Office left for temporary assignments elsewhere, making the decision process longer than it might have otherwise been.

## 5.4. Decision to adopt

The decision to adopt came very late in 1988. Almost all of the conditions stipulated by Tucson Office management had been met, so it was decided to let the installation of GroupSystems proceed. The lease of the equipment and software from the University to the IHS began on January 1, 1989, with a planned end-date of December 31, 1989. Walker began installing the personal computer and network hardware in January. After several systems tests, the system was declared operational in February.

## 5.5. Implementation

Walker did not learn until late-February that only seven of the 12 chairs he had asked for would be provided. During the same month, he entered negotiations over the use of a projector. The University still had possession of the one previously installed and was trying to use it for the new hardware. This proved to be impossible, so the projector was offered to Walker, provided that IHS would take over its maintenance contract. Management at the Tucson Office were reluctant to do this. At the same time, they refused to appropriate funding so that Walker could purchase a projector. Although the system can be used without a projector, Walker felt the system was not complete without it. Using the projector, the facilitator running the meeting could create displays for the public screen that would differ from those that meeting participants saw on their individual screen.

While Walker waited for the projector issue to be decided, he took several different small groups into the decision room to demonstrate the system. He used one of the personal computers as a surrogate for the public screen. These demonstrations could provide only a hint of what GroupSystems was capable of doing; Walker would have liked to have done more, but he felt constrained by the problems.

In the meantime, Walker had scheduled a real IHS group to use the facility. They were to use the room in April, but the projector issue had still not been decided when they arrived in Tucson. Walker decided to go ahead with use of the facility anyway, even though he felt the session was primarily a demonstration. The group that used the facility consisted of the dental program directors for the 12 IHS areas, who were in town for a quarterly meeting. The director of this group liked the system so much he wrote and circulated a memo advocating its use. He was especially impressed by the ability of the system to get participation from all of the group.

Finally, in late-April, the IHS agreed to accept the maintenance liability for the University's projector, which Walker installed soon thereafter. Implementation would be complete after a test session in which the system would be used by a local IHS group, the Contract Health Services/MIS work group.

This group met for about three hours and seemed to enjoy using the system. One observer noticed that the Native American participants were very quiet during verbal parts of the meeting, but participated as much as anyone else during those times when most of the interaction was through the system. In a follow-up interview, the Chief Medical Officer said he was impressed with the system because of increased participation levels, because it allowed the group to work better together, and because they went away from the meeting with a better product than usual. He indicated that he could see this group using the system as often as twice per month.

## 6. Analysis

One of the predictors of a successful innovation is whether the recognition of the need for innovation and the recognition of a technology's ability to provide that innovation occur at the same time. In this situation, it appears that the need was felt for some time before the EMS technology was discovered. In fact, according to Walker, other methods to improve the productivity of meetings had been considered locally before Walker was introduced to GroupSystems. However, once IHS was given the opportunity to implement GroupSystems (at essentially no charge), other alternatives were forgotten.

There are several factors pertinent to the innovation process, discussed in the literature review, that are relevant in this case. The first is management attitude. From the unwillingness of Tucson Office management to commit resources to GroupSystems and from their delays in considering Walker's offers in the proposal stage, it is clear that the attitude of management toward GroupSystems was ambivalent at best. Even though management knew in October 1988 that Walker felt the projector was essential to system success, management did not accept maintenance liability for it until April 1989. It is also clear there were no management champions of GroupSystems. According to Walker, management agreed that bringing GroupSystems to the Tucson Office seemed a reasonable thing to do, but they left all recruiting of groups to use the system to him. No one was openly antagonistic toward the room, although one member of the management team was reportedly not convinced it should be supported. The Tucson Director was said to be enthusiastic about the system as long as it did not cost much. Yet many members of the top management team formed opinions without using the technology.

Another important factor was the origin of the innovation. If the management team in the Tucson Office is viewed as the administrative core, and the doctors and other professionals in the office are viewed as the technical core, then this innovation began in the technical core. Note, however, that it started on the OHPRD side of the house, not on the health side. The doctors in the OHPRD side are not primarily involved in health care delivery. They and the other professionals are involved in research, a type of knowledge work that lends itself to enhancement through increases in meeting efficiency and effectiveness. GroupSystems, then, was a technical innovation. Without considering any of the contextual features of IHS, a technical innovation proposed from inside the technical core should stand a good chance of being adopted. Two contextual features (professionalism and the degree of coupling between the two cores) reinforce the adoption prediction. The workers we were concerned with, primarily M.D.'s, are members of one of the most professionalized groups. Highly professionalized workers in the technical core are more likely to be innovative than those without such workers. Similarly, in an organization where the administrative and technical cores are as loosely coupled as they are at the Tucson Office, the administrative core is not as innovative as the technical core. It should not be surprising, then, that the proposal to introduce GroupSystems at the Tucson Office of IHS was approved and that GroupSystems was indeed implemented, despite the ambivalence of top management.

The promise of EMS to improve meeting effectiveness, and to do so with high levels of satisfaction on the part of the participants, no doubt helped get the EMS adopted. The low cost of acquiring the system also helped. EMS technology, which involves not only the physical equipment and facilities of the decision room but also software licensing and maintenance costs, can be quite expensive. IHS was able to acquire Arizona's GroupSystems for only a small fraction of what actual costs would have been. The primary factor that led to the adoption decision, however, was Walker himself. He was able to find a technological solution to the problems he saw at IHS and was able to negotiate terms that were favorable to both the Tucson Office of IHS and the University of Arizona. In this particular case, it is clear there would have been no GroupSystems at IHS without Walker.

## 6.1. Postscript

The process of introducing GroupSystems as an innovation at the Tucson Office of the IHS could be considered successful. The innovation was adopted and implemented. However, the system itself could not be considered a success. GroupSystems was dismantled and returned to the University in February 1990. An analysis of the nine months that GroupSystems was in use at IHS is beyond the scope of this paper, but some factors that could explain what happened are worth mentioning.

One of the key factors was lack of use. Fewer than one dozen meetings were held in the decision room in the time it was available at IHS. The system may not have enjoyed widespread use because of a lack of a coherent, active marketing plan on Walker's part. He produced and distributed a brochure that described the system, but apparently that was not enough. In general, the Tucson Office of IHS may not generate enough "business" on its own; i.e., have enough meetings, to have justified keeping the EMS past the end of its lease. A second factor was the lack of an EMS champion in management. Since no one in management pushed for system use, all recruitment efforts fell to Walker. A third factor is a planned reorganization of IHS. This has not yet been officially announced, but the rumors hold that the Tucson Office will be closed, with health care provision responsibility transferred to Phoenix, and with staff support functions transferred to a new technical services center in Albuquerque, N.M. The reorganization will not occur for some time, but knowing that it will happen undoubtedly influenced management's decision in December 1989 to dismantle the facility.

## 7. Conclusions

In this paper, we have reported on the workings of the innovation process in a local office of a U.S. federal agency. In this instance, a key factor determining the success of the innovation was its origin in the technical core. Recognition of the need for the innovation came before it was proposed, and there was no management support for adoption, but these conditions, which typically point to an unsuccessful innovation, appear to have been overshadowed by other factors. Another key factor, and perhaps the most important one, was the activism and persistence of Walker himself. The role of key, motivated individuals in the innovation process should not be overlooked.

To our knowledge, this is the first paper to consider an EMS as an organizational innovation, and to describe the decision to adopt and implement an EMS in a “real” organizational setting. EMS differ from more traditional information systems in that they are designed to intermediate between people rather than between people and machines $^{3}$ . As such, it could be expected that the innovation process for systems such as EMS would differ from that for other types of systems. Looking at the innovation process for GroupSystems in the Tucson office of IHS has allowed insights into that process, and how it differs from other innovation processes, that we might not otherwise have seen. We believe that studying this situation will be useful to both academics studying technological innovation and post-implementation effects, especially with regards to EMS, and to practitioners who are considering adopting EMS in their organizations.
