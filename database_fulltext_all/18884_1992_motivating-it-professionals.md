# Motivating IT professionals The Hong Kong challenge \*

Janice M. Burn, J. Daniel Couger and Louis Ma

Hong Kong Polytechnic, Hong Kong

The brain drain problem in Hong Kong is causing chaos in its IT profession. The reasons for this arc mainly assumed to be political. Whilst a decrease of confidence is undoubtedly a major factor, there are also factors related to the satisfaction that can be obtained by professionals from the job itself. This paper describes the results of an international research study investigating the motivation potential of jobs in the computing profession. The high mismatch between the motivation potential in computing jobs in Hong Kong and the growth needs of practicing professionals would seem to be a major contributor to the exceptionally high turnover in staff; it must also contribute to reduced productivity within the computing community. The challenge for MIS managers is to develop a long term strategic IT plan involving re-organisation of services, training, investment and, especially ‘people strategy’, including job design, future staff development programmes, and compensation packages in the industry. Furthermore, Hong Kong is not a singular phenomenon: results from Korea and Thailand suggest the presence of a unique Asian factor related to the need for social growth.

Keywords: Motivation, Personnel, IT training, Asian organisations.

## 2. Cross-cultural comparison of computing staff motivation – the background

Over the last 10 years or so, Couger and Zawacki [4] have completed extensive research concerning the motivation of Computing personnel in the United States using the Job Diagnostic Survey/Data Processing (JDS/DP) version. They found that computing personnel have a much higher growth need than their peers (e.g. they have a need for greater responsibility for their own work and tasks which involve new technology, advanced development environments, and state-of-the-art applications). They have a noticeably lower need, however, for social interaction and growth. This particular aspect has been explored in greater depth by Tompkins measuring different social dimensions related to communication and social behaviour. The result has been to develop an extensive questionnaire which has been tested worldwide and which has been used as the basis for the current research to determine if there are significant differences between the findings in the United States and the Pacific Basin nations. To-date Hong Kong, Korea and Thailand have completed the survey and work continues in China, Japan and Taiwan.

The survey instrument was the extended Couger-Zawacki-Tompkins version of the JDS/DP. Its reliability and validity was substantiated in 1978, when used to develop a database of more than 8,000 persons in the U.S.A. Motivating norms were derived from this for 19 jobs within the computing field. The instrument obtains respondents perceptions on 21 variables related to the work environment. The research group in Hong Kong extended this survey in two directions. Firstly, a number of local issues of significance were included, and secondly a master survey was completed using the computing management in each organisation. This latter is used for comparison with employee results.

The model of motivation in this research is job core theory, as developed by Hackman et al. [7] The basis of this is now well understood throughout the international community and a definition of the survey variables is provided in Appendix 1. The research has shown that individual growth need strength (GNS) is high for computing professionals compared to other professionals and other job categories. Table 1 shows this effect.

By contrast, Figure 1 shows that the Social Need Strength (SNS) of computing professionals is exceptionally low compared to other professionals. This is generally thought to be the outcome of training programs that concentrate on programming skills initially, regardless of the eventual development of the specialist. It is thought to underpin many of the problems of poor analyst/user interaction. This would necessarily inhibit project team management.

Tompkins has used three instruments which measure different social dimensions – communication apprehension (PRCA), communication interaction involvement (CII) and social behaviour (FIRO-B).

The Fundamental Interpersonal Relations Orientation Behaviour (FIRO-B) scale developed by Schutz in 1967 [13] measures three social needs that are satisfied in the company of other people on two dimensions - expressed and wanted. This gives six scores as follows:

1. expressed inclusion - desire to include other people in my activities

2. expressed control - desire to exercise control and influence over others

3. expressed affection - desire to become close to people

4. wanted inclusion – desire to have people include me in their activities

5. wanted control - desire for others to control me

6. wanted affection – desire to have others express friendly feelings towards me.

7. CA (Communication Apprehension) refers to an anxiety syndrome associated with either real or anticipated communication with another person or persons. It is restricted to communication situations and the anxiety materialises only when some form of communication is imminent. The PRCA, first reported by McCroskey [11] consists of 25 statements dealing with one's perceptions about communicating with others. Its result is represented by the 7th variable on Figure 2.

The IIM (Interaction Involvement Measure) developed by D.J. Cegala [2] measures how a person relates in a social situation. It has three dimensions:

8. My Face – a person's ability to adapt to a situation by knowing what to say and when to say it – i.e. a verbal face saving strategy!

9. His Face – one's knowledge of the meanings that others apply to one's own behaviour and what meaning ought to be applied to other's behaviour.

10. Respect - the respect that one accords to others in a conversation.

When these tests have been applied to the US computing profession and compared with the US norm, they show a significantly lower social need in the computing profession, as shown in Figure 2.

These results have been validated in a number of European countries with surprisingly consistent results, but recent research work on Cultural Measurement [9] in China PRC has indicated that there may be a much stronger link to the “Confucius Connection” within Asian countries with relation to employee motivation factors rather than to individual job satisfaction norms. This cross-cultural research project was therefore established with a view to replicating the US research in Asian countries and establishing whether there were differences that could be identified as the “Asian Factors”.

## 3. Asian study findings

## 3.1. Approach in Hong Kong

125 companies in Hong Kong were contacted. They had previously participated in a detailed survey of Information Technology usage within their organisations. 66 companies volunteered to participate in the research and eventually 44 completed sets of data were received: they consisted of an organisational questionnaire completed by senior management and a set of confidential replies returned directly by their employees. The data was input to a database and a number of analyses completed. In addition, the data was sent to the US, where a subset (261 individual responses from 31 companies) was processed using the standard US model and compared to other findings from Asian countries. The subset was limited to three categories of employee (analyst, analyst/programmer, programmer) to achieve consistency in the cross-cultural comparison. This explains some discrepancy in the local processing figures.

Interestingly, whilst goal clarity is quite good for Hong Kong computer personnel there is much less acceptance of the goals. Programmer/Analysts, in particular, have problems participating in goal setting, and find goals insufficiently demanding. The goal setting and feedback, norms for the US also hover around the mid-point of the 7 point scale showing a universal problem among computer professionals in these areas. Overall general satisfaction is significantly lower for Analyst/programmers and Programmers whilst pay satisfaction is significantly lower for Analysts. These would all suggest that the Hong Kong computer professional has very similar needs to his US counterpart – only those needs are not being met by their jobs. The initial need in Hong Kong is therefore, to make jobs richer for all three job types. Managers also need to allow employees to participate in goal setting and in providing improved feedback.

One area where an obvious anomaly exists, however, is in the Hong Kong computer professional's expressed need for social interaction (SNS). SNS for professionals in the computer field in the US is low compared to the national norm of other occupations (5.48) but in Hong Kong the difference is significantly less. In the absence of Hong Kong norms, a comparison can only be made between the two computer populations and this has been further explored through analysis of the three tests of social interaction variables. Table 3 shows the results.

Despite their greater Social Needs Strength, the Hong Kong group scored significantly lower in ‘not wanting to make efforts to get close to other people’ (7) and a higher desire for ‘wanting others to control and influence them’ (6) when compared to the US computer population. This would seem inconsistent, but for the very high rating associated with Communications Apprec hension (7). For comparison purposes, studies conducted in China by Alter et al [1] showed no significant differences between the Chinese national norm (Mean = 61.21, SD = 10.58) and the US norm (Mean = 60.45, SD = 11.58). Anyone scoring at the mean or below is considered as having average CA. Those scoring 61–70 are considered moderately apprehensive, 71–80 highly apprehensive and over 80 severely apprehensive. In the US, scores of 71 and above would be considered to be orally handicapped. It would seem, then, that the presence of such a high level of Communications Apprehension would inhibit the development of employee/manager relationships, and social needs would relate to peer level interaction only. In addition, the Hong Kong group had a significantly higher tendency to adapt to the communication context by “saving face” (8) and this is also true of maintaining “his face”. It appears that this emphasis further detracts from communication, as the attribute Respect, which measures the attentiveness of a person in dialogue with another person, is significantly lower for the Hong Kong computer professional.

Overall and perhaps not surprisingly, the US computer professional had the highest preference for expressing, whilst the Hong Kong group had a higher preference for receiving. The cultural differences that the Hong Kong group appear to be identifying would suggest, however, that a different approach would be required to stimulate job satisfaction for the Hong Kong computer professional, and that this would have an effect on management roles, training programmes, and career development planning.

## 4. The motivation and training aspects

## 4.1. Relative importance of Herzberg factors

As part of the Hong Kong study computing professionals were asked to rank Herzberg factors, the results are shown in Table 5.

The results show that computing professionals in this Hong Kong survey ranked five hygiene factors (considered as dissatisfiers by Herzberg) as the least important (from 7th to 11th in Table 5). The five motivation factors (ranked '1st' then '3rd to 6th') together with "pay & benefits" (ranked '2nd') were considered to be more important by computing professionals. These results not only support Herzberg's Motivation–Hygiene theory but also imply that computing professionals on average are very much achievement and growth oriented. "Opportunity for growth" was ranked by all 3 grades of computing professionals as the most important factor on average.

It is interesting to note that although “pay & benefits” has been classified as a hygiene factor, it has often been argued that it could be an effective motivation factor as well. Alternatively, if certain employees have yet to exceed the ‘security level’ as defined by Maslow’s [10] hierarchy-of-needs, they may consider “pay & benefits” as both a hygiene factor and a motivation factor.

(Hence, the inclusion of ?? in the table for this entry.) This reflects the emphasis on pay and benefits in all sectors of society in Hong Kong, and a lack of stability and security.

## 4.2. Motivation factors

The average rankings of motivation factors are shown in Table 6. Computing professionals and managers on average agreed that “job satisfaction” and “promotion prospect” should be more important.

Systems Analysts on average considered their compensation package to be important, but they were also more concerned about “job security” than analyst/programmers and programmers. Their expectations for better “compensation package” may be due to the exceptional opportunities caused by the ‘brain-drain’ problem in Hong Kong. At the same time, however, it would seem that some analysts may have been promoted too soon and are now beginning to worry about competence and “job security” in the new role. On the other hand, they are relatively less concerned about “training & career development” than analyst/programmers and programmers, and may believe that they have reached the ultimate goal in career advancement.

More Analyst/Programmers ranked job satisfaction as their top motivation factor - this is consistent with their relative importance of the factors: opportunity for achievement, meaningful work, and increased responsibility.

## 4.3. Training Requirements

The average rankings of training requirements are shown in Table 7. In general, computing professionals in the survey tend to rank training requirements associated with their career higher, while systems managers prefer that their staff receive training on the areas directly associated with their current jobs. It is also noticeable that “communication skills” was ranked higher by systems managers than all three grades of computing professionals respectively, although one must still question whether managers are attempting to address the problem.

For Systems Analysts: their priority of training requirements matched overall with their managers quite well.

Analyst Programmers ranked “systems analysis” as their top training requirement and ranked “management: administration & project management” higher than their managers. Systems managers on average considered “technical design” to be most important.

For Programmers: “Programming” was ranked as the top training requirement by systems managers, but only ranked 4th on average by programmers. Obviously, the majority of programmers perceive the programming role as a stepping-stone, and want to be promoted to more senior positions. This would require training in both “systems analysis” and “design”, which programmers ranked as top training requirements.

## 5. Conclusions

The analysis shows that Hong Kong computer personnel have a similar profile to their US counterparts in having a high growth need but there is a significant mismatch in the motivation potential of their current jobs.

There is, therefore a need to examine the five key job dimensions:

\- skill variety

\- task identity

\- task significance

\- autonomy

\- feedback

and identify ways through which these areas can be developed to more fully satisfy the need for self achievement in the computing specialist.

The problems associated with the lack of goal setting, participation, and feedback are universal to the computing profession and need to be addressed at management level with a reassessment of role. Greater interaction would also help.

Hong Kong computer professionals enjoy the social aspects of their working environments, but have apprehensions about certain levels of communication, preferring to act as receptors rather than expressors. This could have implications in the development of user-friendly systems and also for the development of the discipline as an active strategic force in organisations. The presence of an “Asian Factor” would suggest that there is a need to develop specific education training and development programmes which can build on the inherent social growth needs of the Pacific Basin nations and produce the effective computing specialist with a sound balance of technical, managerial, and communications skills.

Current training programmes are inadequate to satisfy the growth need of professionals. The skills that served the programmer well may be only marginally useful to the systems analyst, less so to the project manager who must co-ordinate people and resources, and virtually useless to the DP manager who wants to project a business air. The fault, it would seem, is not solely with the individual, although there has been an undoubted tendency to see oneself first and foremost as a member of the computing profession rather than as a business employee. The fault also clearly lies in the management, where technical skills are given far greater emphasis than overall growth skills, such as communication and management training.

The computing industry in Hong Kong is going through a period of severe crisis, with a severe shortage of manpower, high turnover of existing trained professionals and a growing need for Information Technology developments in major industries. At such times it is often difficult for Computing management to justify expenditure on new application development tools and advanced training and development programmes. It is also difficult to adopt a long term view of staff advancement policies and participative management. Experience in other countries would suggest, however, that such steps are necessary if Hong Kong is going to increase productivity and meet the challenges of a technology based future in the Pacific basin century. In developing such programmes, managers or academics must also be aware of the need to tailor these to Asian requirements with full recognition of the cultural factors involved.

## Appendix 1: Definition of survey variables

1. Key Job Dimensions: Objective characteristics of the job itself.

A. Skill Variety: The degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities in carrying out the work, which involve the use of a number of different skills and talents of the employee

B. Task Identity: The degree to which a job requires the completion of a whole and identifiable piece of work.

C. Task Significance: The degree to which a job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people.

D. Autonomy: The degree to which the job provides substantial freedom, independence, and discretion to the employee in scheduling his own work and the procedures to be carried out.

E. Feedback from the Job Itself: The degree to which carrying out the work activities required by the job results in the employee obtaining information about his effectiveness.

2. Satisfaction Measures: The private affective reactions or feelings an employee gets from working on his job.

A. General Satisfaction: An overall measure of the degree to which the employee is happy and satisfied with his work.

B. Specific Satisfaction: These scales tap several specific aspects of the employee's job satisfaction.

B1. Pay satisfaction.

B2. Supervisory satisfaction.

B3. Satisfaction with co-workers.

3. Social Need Strength: This is a measure of the degree to which the employee needs to interact with other employees.

4. Goal Clarity and Accomplishment: These scales measure the degree to which employees understand and accept organisational goals. Further it taps into the employees' feelings about goal setting participation, goal difficulty and feedback on goal accomplishment.

A. Goal Clarity: How clear and specific the goals are for the job or person.

B. Goal Difficulty: The extent that goals or work objectives require a great deal of effort to complete.

C. Goal Acceptance: The extent that the person is willing to accept organisational goals.

D. Goal Setting participation: Their feeling of having involvement in the goal setting process – having an impact!

5. Individual Growth Need Strength: This scale measures the individual's need for personal accomplishment and for learning and developing beyond his present level of knowledge or skills.

6. Motivating Potential Score: A score reflecting the potential of a job for eliciting positive internal work motivation on the part of the employees.
