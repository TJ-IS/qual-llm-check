# A measure for determining the strategic relevance of IS to the organization \*

Seev Neumann and Niv Ahituv
Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv, Israel

Moshe Zviran

Naval Postgraduate School, Monterey, CA, USA

Several recent studies ranked the strategic planning and policy foundation of information systems as one of the most critical issues facing IS and general management. The IS strategic grid was suggested as a framework to investigate IS importance to the organization: a prerequisite to consideration of IS policy issues. This paper seeks to construct a measure that allows an organization to find its position on the strategic grid by analyzing a small number of measurable organizational and IS variables. The suggested measure is empirically tested by using data from 127 organizations, and its applicability is then demonstrated.

Keywords: Strategic grid, Strategic impact, IS policy, Strategic planning, IS relevance to organization,

## Introduction

Strategic planning for information systems (IS) is one of the most critical issues facing information systems executives $[5,13]$ . Effective IS strategic planning can help organizations understand their role and impact on the overall organizational strategy and use of information systems to reach business goals.

Almost all IS policy issues are affected by the role IS plays and by its perceived importance. This varies from great strategic importance to useful, being supportive in nature. Moreover, while current IS activities may not have strategic importance today, they may have great significance for the future $[23,38]$ .

In order to perform effective IS strategic planning, organizations conventionally apply one of several methodologies $[15,25,41]$ . One of the useful IS strategic planning methodologies is the IS strategic grid scheme $[6,22]$ . This is a contingency approach that can be used to determine the importance of IS to the organization. The scheme defines a continuum over a two-dimensional plane along which a firm moves as it assimilates IS technology, as illustrated in Figure 1. Four major zones are identified:

\- Strategic, where IS activities are critical to existing operations, and planned IS applications are critical for future success.

\- Turnaround organizations are not totally dependent upon current IS to function but there are new IS developments that are vital for reaching organizational objectives.

\- Factory organizations are dependent on IS for their day-to-day operations, but will not gain significant competitive advantage from further development of IS.

\- Support organizations are neither highly dependent on IS, nor will new IS applications be critical in future.

Each of the four classes has very different needs for IS and technology. Once the current and future impact of IS is determined, senior management can make a decision on the IS mission and strategy $[2]$ . Moreover, the position of an organization on the grid explains the needed level of top management involvement, the close relationship between the IS and organizational plan, the role of the executive steering committee, the organizational placement of the IS function, and the appropriate type of IS management control system $[10,22]$ .

Thus, the major advantage of the IS strategic grid framework lies in its use in identifying the strategic importance of IS when considering IS policy issues. However, there is no formal and implementable model which can be used to find an organization's position on the strategic grid.

A possible explanation is the lack of practical guidance and a formal measure for locating an organization on the grid. Thus, managers who wish to use this approach use only intuition, not necessarily considering the many factors that may impact their position on the grid.

The advantage of a standard, formal, measure to locate an organization on the grid is twofold $[40]$ . First, it will allow IS practitioners to utilize a readily available instrument and, therefore, facilitate its use in the IS planning process. Moreover, the use of a standard measure will enable a review of changes in grid positions over time. Second, since different business units within an organization can be located in different positions, a standard measure will enable a comparison among them.

The primary objective of this research was the design and test of a multivariable, valid, measure to locate an organization's position on the IS strategic grid framework.

## 1. The basic measure

A fundamental issue is how to identify a set of measurable variables that are likely to impact a firm's location on the grid. A primary source for this process were two questionnaires (applications portfolio analysis and operational dependence analysis) presented by Cash et al. [6]. The first, consisting of 14 questions, is designed to indicate whether the development work on the IS applications portfolio is critical to the future competitive posture, or whether it is useful but not at the core for competitive success. The second questionnaire has 11 questions and is designed to analyze how critical the existing systems are in achieving basic operating objectives.

Since the total number of questions is relatively high, and some were overlapping, a preliminary study, aimed at reducing the number of variables, was performed.

The Delphi process was used for selecting the reduced set of variables. Delphi is a group process that uses written media to solicit and aggregate the judgement of a number of individuals $[11,24]$ . It generally starts with an open ended questionnaire. Succeeding questionnaires provide statistics on the group's responses to the preceding version, and ask for further information until sufficient consensus has been reached. The procedure discussed here consisted of the following three steps:

1: In November 1986, institutional members of the Israeli Chapter of the Society for Information Management (SIM) (58 organizations) were asked to participate in a one-day seminar on IS planning. Participation was free-of-charge provided that at least the CEO and highest ranked IS executive attended the seminar. As a result, 42 top executives, including the CEOs and chief IS executives from 17 organizations, participated in the seminar. The first part of this seminar addressed IS planning issues and methodologies. During the second half, participants were asked to take part in defining the factors impacting IS importance to an organization.

2: Participants were provided with the two original questionnaires [6] and asked to independently select up to 8 questions that they considered most relevant to IS importance in the firm. In addition, they were asked to propose other variables that directly contribute and express their comments on the operationalization of all questions.

3: About two weeks after the seminar, all participants were mailed material summarizing the results of the first round. New issues were listed only if they were suggested by at least three respondents. All issues were presented in descending order of frequency. Respondents were asked, again, to give their top 8 issues in order of importance and provide a rationale for their selection. Usable responses were received from 36 executives, a response rate of 86%.

The construction of the final list of variables was based on the union of responses in step 3. This second round led to a high level of consensus, with 13 variables that impact the current and future relevance of IS; they are presented in Table 1.

CSF, the fit of the IS applications portfolio to the organization's critical success factors, was selected by all to be included in the final set of variables. Although not suggested in the original questionnaires, the respondents felt that this variable reflected the alignment of IS with the organization.

COM focuses on IS applications which appear to offer new ways for the firm to compete (i.e., fast delivery, higher quality or broader array of services). Computerized reservation systems (CRS) represent an example of IS support to business competitive strategies in the airline industry $[12]$ and support a possible relevance of COM to the overall IS importance.

NEW focuses on IS applications which allow a firm to develop and offer new products or services, or enable new features to be added to existing product; e.g., in the banking industry, where many new services depend heavily on information systems. A possible impact of these variables on IS importance is further supported by Parsons [29]; Porter and Millar [30]; Tom [37]; and Wiseman [38].

STR, TAC and OPR represent IS support to the three generic management levels of an organization. Sprague and Carlson [33] provide support to the inclusion of these three, suggesting that IS are important to all levels of management, but that different types of information and IS are required at each level.

PRF represents IS contribution to improving profitability (e.g., by increasing sales or revenues). FIN refers to direct contributions of IS to financial gains through improved operations (e.g., cost displacement or cost avoidance). The relevance of both these issues is supported by many (e.g., Beath and Ives [3]).

BDG pertains to the share of the IS budget as a percent of the corporate revenues. A recent survey $[36]$ found that the most effective users of IS in the USA spend a growing share of their revenues on IS, up to 13.88% in the extreme. case. Another survey $[8]$ , found major differences in IS budget share among industries and between firms within those industries, reaching only 0.2% at the low end of this comparison, with an average IS budget of 2% of the total revenue in US firms.

DIS, refers to the distribution of the software budget between development efforts and maintenance. Since one of the strategic grid's axis measures the importance of the future IS applications portfolio, the responding managers felt that DiS may impact the location of an organization on the grid.

CRT, 1HR and 3WK are variables focussing on different levels of the degree of criticality. While CRT addresses the overall perceived criticality, the other two, 1HR and 3WK, examine, respectively, the impact of a short-term and long-term shutdown of IS operations on the operations.

The basic measure is defined as two functions of these variables: $P_{level}$ is the degree of importance of the Present IS applications portfolio, while $F_{level}$ is the degree of importance of the Future IS applications portfolio.

IS literature does not show any evidence concerning if, how, and at what weight, each variable impacts the degrees of importance of current and future IS. The point of departure is that the two equations are not identical and must be individually implemented in order to place the organization on the strategic grid.

In the graphical representation of the measure, as shown in Figure 2, the current degree of IS importance ( $P_{level}$ ) is reflected by the vertical axis of the grid, while the horizontal axis reflects the degree of importance of future IS applications $(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{level}})$ . The organization's placement on the grid is determined by its location on the axes.

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. Instrumentation

The instrument used to collect data was a 4-part questionnaire. The first aimed at collecting basic information about the organization and its characteristics (i.e. size, structure, etc). The second related to measuring the dependent variables – present and future importance of IS. Interviewees were asked to evaluate the present and future IS importance on a five point, Likert-type, scale.

The third included 13 questions relating to the independent variables. The first portion of each question pertained to current IS applications, and the second to future planned applications; thus, there were 26 questions. Again, answers were marked on a five-point, Likert-type, scale (1 = lowest; 5 = highest). A sample questionnaire is presented in Appendix I.

The last part of the questionnaire was filled by the interviewer who, based on data from the interviewees, had to assess the organization's position on the grid and determine that the interviewees agreed with this classification.

## 2.2. Data collection

Requests for participation were distributed to IS managers in 1360 organizations in Israel; 131 of them agreed to take part. This response rate (less than 10%) is relatively low. A possible reason for this low rate is the requirement that two executives were to be interviewed in each organization.

Interviews with the highest ranked IS executive and the CEO of each organization were performed between January and July 1987. During each interview, one of the authors first investigated the role and importance of IS to the organization and mapped it to one of the strategic grid quadrants (Part IV of the questionnaire). The interviewees from each firm were then asked to work together to fill out parts I–III of the questionnaire.

A primary purpose of the interview was to determine the consistency between interviewer's perception of the organization's position on the strategic grid and the degree of importance that interviewees attached to present and future IS applications in part II of the questionnaire. The degrees of importance determined the organization's position on the grid. This was then matched with the interviewer's position in one of the quadrants of the grid. An inconsistency between the two was found in only 4 of 131 questionnaires (i.e., $96.9\%$ compatibility). They were discarded.

Ten organizations were randomly selected for a hold-out sample, and their questionnaires were used later to validate the measure. The remaining 117 comprised the data base for this study.

## 2.3. The sample organizations

The sample included 29.1% manufacturing firms, 18.8% commercial firms, 23.1% service firms, 24.8% not-for-profit organizations, and 4.3% holding companies. The sectoral distribution of sampled organizations is similar to that of all firms in Israel.

The size of the sampled organizations was measured by the number of employees. Three size categories were defined – small (less than 250 employees), medium (251–1000 employees), and large (more than 1000 employees). The range of employees varied from 15 to 33,000, with 32 organizations (27.4%) classified as small, 48 (41%) as medium and 37 (31.6%) as large. 49.6% of the sampled organizations had a functional structure, 30.8% were organized on a product basis, 15.4% had a matrix structure and 4.3% had a combination of structures (holding companies).

While the sample is not random in a purely statistical theory sense, it closely reflects organizations in Israel. It, thus, facilitates use of the data in building and validating the measure.

## 2.4. Instrument evaluation

To determine the validity of the questionnaire and the basic measure, evidence for content and construct validity was examined, using the sample data.

Content validity of a questionnaire refers to its representativeness. In general, it is the manner by which a questionnaire and its items are built that ensures the reasonableness of the claim of content validity $[26]$ . First evidence to content validity of this questionnaire lies in the way it was structured. The systematic process of selecting the variables to be included in the questionnaire and the use of the Delphi method lend credence to the validity of the instrument. Additional circumstantial evidence can be generated statistically, by examining the internal consistency among all variables. Appendices II and III show that all inter-item correlations are positive and significant at a 0.05 level.

Construct validity is established by showing that the measure is an appropriate operational definition of the construct it purports to be measuring $[34]$ . The method of evaluating construct validity was to compare the correlations between each variable and the two dependent variables: $P_{level}$ and $F_{level}$ $[18]$ . The first column in appendices II and III presents these correlations. All of the correlations are positive and significant at the 0.05 level. The 13 correlations for $P_{level}$ range from 0.44 to 0.82, with 12 at levels greater than 0.5. For $F_{level}$ , correlations range from 0.18 to 0.84, with 11 correlating at levels greater than 0.5. If the total score is valid, than this evidence suggests that the individual scales in the questionnaire are also valid.

Predictive validity implies that a measure is consistent and agrees with other independent measures, so it can be used to predict outside the bounds of the experiment. Since the questionnaire served as the data collection instrument for constructing a measure and determining which variables should be included in it, predictive validity was not examined until a final measure was constructed (see section 5).

The third step was intended to eliminate those variables whose coefficients were not significantly different from zero. The two reduction techniques selected were backward elimination and stepwise regression [21].

Under backward elimination, a regression is run with all independent variables; then, variables whose t values are low are successively deleted, until only variables with coefficients significantly different from zero remain.

Under stepwise regression, each time the regression is performed, the variable with the highest contribution to the variance is entered into the equation; simultaneously, the variables already included are tested and any becoming insignificant is excluded. At the end, only the variables whose coefficients are significantly different from zero remain. While this technique is based on the inclusion of variables, the previous technique eliminates variables.

The next test concerned the phenomenon of multicollinearity; i.e., the situation where a linear dependency exists among the explanatory variables. A high degree of multicollinearity may lead to an incorrect estimation of parameters and to instable models. Since some Pearson's correlation coefficients appeared to be relatively high (see Appendices II–III), testing for multicollinearity was necessary. Three different tests, based on the examination of the condition number as well as testing the equations' stability under the omission of observations and omission of variables, were performed. The first test, calculation of condition number, was proposed by Belsley et al. [4], as a diagnostic tool to detect multicollinearity. This statistic is calculated for the coefficients' matrix of the sample observations. Applications with experimental and actual data sets suggest that condition numbers higher than 20 indicate serious collinearity problems. The two other tests, omission of observations and omission of variables [17,21], examine the regression equations' stability when several observations or several variables are omitted. The relatively low condition numbers (7.23 for $P_{level}$ and 6.85 for $F_{level}$ ), and the low variance (less then 8%) in the regression coefficients when the two other tests were performed, suggest that a high degree of multicollinearity did not exist in the two equations. Hence, the final equations were judged to be stable and to include the relevant explanatory variables. Moreover, the percentage of explained variance ( $R^{2}$ ) indicates that those equations provide a good estimate of an organization's position on the strategic grid.

According to the findings, $P_{level}$ varies between the values of 0.7 and 4.7, while $F_{level}$ varies between 0.7 and 4.2. Since both $P_{level}$ and $F_{level}$ are almost symmetrically distributed, mid-points of these ranges were used to define the four quadrants of the strategic grid. ## 4. Discussion of findings

The basic measure that was reviewed at the outset included 13 operationalized variables. This section focuses on them and suggests possible reasons for their inclusion or exclusion (see Table 3).

![](/api/attachments/MJ2NC5RA/fulltext/images/c873effc69ac22a015b41caa941f5c50bd98b6af9f273bdbef23c2ff0d632520.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Mapping the model to the strategic grid.

CSF, representing the fit of applications portfolio to the organization's CSFs, was included in present and future measures, with the highest coefficient among all variable coefficients. Critical Success Factors define areas where the organization must succeed in order to survive [31]. Inclusion of this variable, thus, directly reflects the contribution of IS to the organization's strategy [32] and the fit between IS importance and the need to align IS to the organization.

Contribution of IS to an organization's ability to compete (COM), was included in f'the present equation but excluded from the future equation. It seems that organizations that recognize the competitive edge afforded by IS are taking action to implement them now. At the same time, others do not attach importance to this variable, either because of its irrelevancy to the nature of the organization, or of unawareness of the potential of strategic IS.

NEW (IS contribution to developing new products or services) was surprisingly excluded from both equations. Various researchers $[6,12,16,38]$ cite case studies involving new IS-based services that were introduced and had a strategic impact on business firms. Nevertheless, a review of the 117 questionnaires show that most of the CEOs and IS directors interviewed, ranked NEW low, independent of their perceived importance of IS and the placement on the strategic grid. This might suggest that the cases discussed in the literature represent rare opportunities.

STR (IS support of the strategic planning level) was excluded from both equations. We assume: (1) information needs of strategic planners are not satisfactorily met by contemporary IS, and (2) DSSs for strategic planners are only in partially use (see David [9] and King [19]).

OPR, dealing with IS support of operations, was included in the $F_{level}$ equation only. IS applications supporting operations are now a standard in all organizations that use computerized ISs, regardless of its importance. The more advanced version of IS applications at the operations level involves their use in critical operational processes. However, such applications have not yet been implemented in many organizations. It seems that the consideration of the CEOs and CIOs in this study focused on the future.

PRF, the variable dealing with IS contribution to improved profitability, was only included in the $P_{level}$ equation. Maybe improved profitability is easy for CEOs and IS directors to grasp at present, but hard to project into the future. Moreover, many IS applications are developed for competitive considerations and not efficiency [28]. Therefore, IS contribution to improved profitability is not necessarily linked to the importance of the future applications portfolio.

The inclusion of FIN (IS contribution to financial gains through improved operations) in the two equations was expected. Most organizations already use a large variety of applications supporting operations; these improve the organization through better management of inventory, quicker invoicing, etc.

BDG (IS budget as percent of total corporate revenues) and DIS (distribution of software budget between development and maintenance) were excluded from both equations, suggesting that the size of the IS budget was not found to be related to IS importance. This notion is also supported by Strassmann [35]. Much of the IS budget is locked in fixed costs, and only a small part is really left to the discretion of the IS director [14]. Another explanation is the recent growth of end-user computing, where a large volume of information processing activities is handled by end-user equipment, which, in many cases, is budgeted by the user and not central IS.

CRT, representing the evaluated criticality of IS, was included in the future equation only. A large part of current IS applications are based on old information processing technologies (such as batch processing). Planned applications are based on on-line/real-time technologies (e.g., CAD/CAM, robotics), without which the organization would cease operations.

1HR and 3WK, representing the impact of a one-hour and a three-week shutdown of the main computing center, were excluded from both equations. Possibly they are excluded because of the low variance in the sample and the strong correlations between them and CRT. Another explanation may be found in a study [1] of a group of organizations, indicating that when the computer was shutdown for half a day, $96\%$ of the organizations' activities were still going; however, after only 10.5 days, these organizations continued only $9\%$ of their activities. Hence, the time frames of the two variables (one hour and three-week shutdown) may have been simply irrelevant.

## 5. Testing the measure

Questionnaires of 10 organizations were randomly removed from the original set at the start. The measure was finally tested by applying the two equations to the data of these 10 organizations (see Tables 4, 5).

It is clearly seen from Table 4 that the goodness of estimation of the impact of present IS application ( $P_{level}$ ) is very high. The average value of the differences between the estimated value and the value recorded by the interviewee was 0.14 (in absolute values). Table 5 also supports the conclusion that the $F_{level}$ equation (impact of future IS applications) facilitates a good estimation of $F_{level}$ , the average value of difference being 0.18.

This shows that the measure positions an organization on the strategic grid to a relatively high degree of accuracy. In addition, it provides some support to a predictive, external, validation of the proposed measure. A second indication of predictive validity is provided by correlating the self-evaluation of the perceived importance of present and future IS applications, as measured in Part II of Part II of the questionnaire (“Placement $P_{level}$ ” and “Placement $F_{level}$ ” in Tables 4 and 5, respectively) and the results of applying the two equations to the test data (Estimated $P_{level}$ and $F_{level}$ in Tables 4 and 5). The correlation between these two sets is 0.97, which is very high, considering that the self-assessment scores could take on one of five values.

## 6. Conclusion

The primary objective of this research was the design and test of a multivariable, valid, measure to locate an organization's position on the IS strategic grid. The measure was empirically tested, then validated by comparing the measure's results to expected results.

The proposed measure facilitates the process of finding the location of an organization on the strategic grid on the basis of a small number of independent variables that are easy to estimate and quantify. Based on this, general management and IS management can more judiciously consider IS policy issues.

Four advantages of a standard, formal, measure to locate an organization on the grid are foreseen. First, it provides IS practitioners with a readily available tool that can be employed to determine an organization's location on the strategic grid, enhances its use in the IS planning process. Second, since organizations may change their position on the grid in different points of time, the use of a standard measure enables a review of changes in grid positions over time. Also, since different business unit within an organization can be located in different positions on the grid, a standard measure will enable a comparison of grid positions among them. Lastly, the measure can be incorporated in an automated tool that will be used as a part of the IS strategic planning process.

In summary, the proposed measure represents an initial effort in providing a standard measure to locating an organization on the strategic grid. The real value of the proposed measure lies in its ability to locate organizations on the grid. It, further, facilitates evaluating changes in grid location over time as well as comparing the location on the strategic grid of different business units within an organization or among different organizations.
