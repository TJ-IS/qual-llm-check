# Personal DSS success in small enterprises

Louis Raymond

Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières, Trois-Rivières, Que., Canada

François Bergeron

Université Laval, Québec, Que., Canada

A ‘personal DSS’ is here defined on the basis of the features and applications that are actually ‘in-use’ and valued by users, and not on the degree of structure of the problem it addresses. This paper presents the results of an empirical investigation of selected application, task, and user determinants of personal DSS effectiveness in the context of the small business. Managers were found to be more successful when they develop their own numeric applications using spreadsheets to provide greater analytical support for decision-making. Task autonomy and microcomputer training were also found to have a significant effect, both directly and indirectly through their relationship with the application characteristics.

Keywords: Personal decision support systems, DSS success, User satisfaction, Application characteristics, Task characteristics, Personal computing, Small business.

## Introduction

When they first appeared, decision support systems (DSS) were described on the basis of their capacity to assist managers in complex tasks requiring human judgement $[16]$ . However, the characteristics of these systems have continuously evolved; new computing tools, such as microcomputers and fourth generation languages, have also become more available. As a result, Snitkin and King $[36]$ have argued for a revision of the DSS concept, employing an “in-use” approach rather than one based on the degree of problem structure. These authors coined the term “personal DSS”, depending on the features and applications that are actually valued by users. For instance, a manager using a spreadsheet for financial modelling purposes would be defined as having a personal DSS, whatever the complexity of the task.

Thus, personal DSS can be viewed as the intersection of traditional DSS and the newer “end-user computing” domain. If we define end-user computing (EUC) as the capability of users to have direct control over their computing needs [19], we see why many of the EUC applications developed on personal computers using spreadsheet or database packages [21], are personal DSS. In particular, end-user computing seems more appropriate for small businesses [4,17,24], especially in terms of their limited human, technical, and financial resources [9]. While some researchers have studied small business IS successes [10,27], little is known on the nature and effectiveness of the systems developed and used by these managers [7].

François Bergeron is a Professor of Information Systems at the Université Laval. He has published in various journals such as Information & Management, the MIS Quarterly, the Journal of Management Information Systems, the Canadian Journal of Administrative Sciences and the Journal of Systems Management. Dr. Bergeron is currently interested in end-user computing, including executive support systems, and in the strategic uses of information technology.

In contributing to a cumulative DSS/EUC research tradition, this study aims to identify essential determinants of personal DSS effectiveness within the small business context. In particular, the characteristics of DSS applications, their nature, and their development will be examined. Greater understanding of the effects of these characteristics is required, as managers are found to use applications that can be ill-suited to their managerial tasks $[1]$ . Also, often lacking in microcomputer training and experience, end-users employ the wrong DSS development tool and produce applications of poor technical quality $[33]$ , thus generating significant risks for their organization.

## Research model and literature review

Figure 1 presents the research model, in an attempt to extend previous work on the technical, individual, and organizational factors of DSS success [2,35], of end-user computing success [3,13,34], and more specifically of personal DSS success. This model proposes that selected variables pertaining to the application, task and user environments of personal DSS be measured to determine the effectiveness of utilization of such systems by small business managers. It is also assumed that the application environment will be affected by the task and user environments. This model can be viewed within the DSS research framework proposed by Liang [23]; it can also be viewed within Amoroso's [1] EUC framework.

The research model was elaborated on the basis of parsimony and practicality. The variables were chosen in terms of their fundamental nature as descriptors of the application, task and user environments of personal DSS, on their a priori relevance to the small business context, and on their having been previously used in empirical studies of DSS or EUC success.

## Application environment of personal DSS

It is assumed that the success of a personal DSS is influenced by three characteristics of the application environment. The first one refers to the type of application; monitoring or analysis $[37]$ . The former allows reporting and querying, while the latter provides for simple or more complex data analysis involving the use of models and simulation. Snitkin and King noted the importance of the type of application in determining system effectiveness. More specifically, analysis systems should provide more assistance than monitoring systems.

A second characteristic of the application environment is whether the personal DSS has been developed by the user alone or by others [26]. The basic premise is that self-developed applications represent the ultimate in the user's control over the information requirements [15]. However, EUC also includes applications that are only operated by the user and whose development originates with other users, consultants, or IS professionals from inside or outside the organization [37]. The managers themselves should be in the best position to analyze, implement, and refine the models and heuristics that they use to make decisions.

Third, the application environment is characterized by the tool employed to develop the DSS. Such tools basically include spreadsheets and database management systems (DBMS) packaged as fourth-generation languages; this applies in large as well as in small organizations $[22,32]$ . Both tools can be used to develop transactional and administrative applications $[6]$ . However, from an organizational and individual perspective, the successful implementation of such applications will first depend on matching the right development tool to the application. DBMSs are usually more appropriate to data-driven applications oriented toward periodic or exception reporting and interrogation, whereas spreadsheets are more suited to model driven applications requiring flexible data manipulation capabilities. The use of a spreadsheet should amplify the manager's decision-making capacity through interactive modelling, a what-if capability, and other features, such as graphics.

## Task environment of personal DSS

As DSS and EUC focus on supporting a user-manager's task, its uncertainty is a potential success factor $[5,23]$ . For instance, individuals with greater task complexity are heavier users of online database systems $[8]$ . Managers with higher levels of task newness, difficulty and interdependence tend to be somewhat more satisfied with a DSS $[35]$ . Also, end-users generally have more skill variety and autonomy than other individuals $[39]$ .

In view of the small business context, two dimensions of the task environment are particularly relevant here, namely: task variety and task autonomy. A small firm manager is regarded as a “jack-of-all-trades” [12], having to resolve problems which cover a wide spectrum. Hence, the more uncertain the task due to its variety, the more decision support is needed. Small businesses are also often characterized by the owner’s unwillingness to delegate decision-making to other managers [20]. As such, a manager’s opportunity and incentive to develop and use a personal DSS would become greater as the uncertainty of the task increases, when more responsibility and authority to make important decisions are given.

## User environment of personal DSS

A large number of demographical, situational, and psychological characteristics, such as age, education, experience, and cognitive style, have been studied in relation to DSS and EUC success $[13,23]$ . Results of these studies are often mixed or contradictory, with no clear pattern having emerged. In Snitkin and King's case, neither education nor cognitive style could be related to the effectiveness of a personal DSS. In view of this, only two user attributes are included here, namely microcomputer training and experience, as a good case can be made a priori as to their effect and explanatory power.

Computer training has been proven to be a significant success factor, both in DSS research $[14,18]$ and in EUC research $[25]$ . In small firms, trained managers have better attitudes toward organizational computing and higher usage levels $[31]$ . A manager trained in the use of microcomputer tools should have more ability to develop and operate a personal DSS and a greater acceptance of microcomputing technology.

Previous research has also related computer experience to the successful implementation of DSS and EUC applications $[34,35]$ . In the small business context, managers with longer computing experience have more positive attitudes toward information systems $[28]$ . The prototyping approach of DSS and EUC implies that experiential learning will occur as the end-user becomes more familiar with personal computing applications and tools $[30]$ . This learning process should result in better decision and data models and in applications of higher quality.

## Relationships between application, task and user environment

There are, as of yet, few empirical results on the relationship between the application environment of personal DSS and the task or user environment. One study did find that spreadsheet users had a higher level of task identity than database users [39]. Furthermore, users with more computer experience tend to develop their own DSS applications earlier on [19].

Returning to the research model, other relationships seem likely. For instance, as a spreadsheet is generally easier to grasp than a DBMS by the non-IS professional, one would expect end-users who develop applications with this tool to be more trained and experienced in computer methods.

## Methodology and research hypotheses

## Sample

A preliminary one-page questionnaire was mailed to 650 small manufacturing firms (those of less than 250 employees) in the province of Quebec, Canada, randomly chosen from a government directory. A total of 120 firms (18%) responded that they had at least one user of microcomputer applications developed with a spreadsheet or a DBMS and were willing to answer further questions. Among these, 35 were randomly selected for data collection to aid in the purposes of this study, through the use of structured interviews with the help of a questionnaire (one user per firm for 29 firms, two users per firm for the other 6). The users were first or second-level managers in the accounting, finance, sales, and production functions.

The 41 end-users in the sample were asked to describe the main microcomputer-based application which supports their important decision-making tasks. These applications included budgeting, financial analysis, costing, inventory control, sales analysis and forecasting, production control, and production scheduling. Of these applications, 31 (76%) had been developed by the user alone and 10 (24%) by others, while 32 (78%) were spreadsheet-based (Lotus in all cases but two) and 9 (22%) were DBMS-based (Dbase in all cases).

## Measures

User satisfaction is the most widely used surrogate measure of effectiveness and a success measure for DSS [2] and end-user computing [11]. A two-dimensional measure of personal DSS success was employed here by adapting a previously validated instrument [35]. The first dimension consists of seven 5-point scales indicating the user's improved decision-making or satisfaction. The second dimension has six scales to determine the user's overall satisfaction with microcomputing due to successful performance, however not necessarily in a decision-making context. In this study, the first measure was found to be more reliable, with an internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) of .92 compared to .60 for the second one (the scales are presented in Appendix A).

## Hypotheses

Given the research model and preceding argumentation, the following hypotheses were tested:

H1: A personal DSS will be more successful when the application is of the “analysis” rather than the “monitoring” type.

H2: A personal DSS will be more successful when the application has been developed by the user rather than others.

H3; A personal DSS will be more successful when the application has been developed with a spreadsheet rather than a DBMS.

H4: A personal DSS will be more successful when the user has a higher level of task variety.

H5: A personal DSS will be more successful when the user has a higher level of task autonomy.

H6: A personal DSS will be more successful when the user has more microcomputer training.

H7: A personal DSS will be more successful when the user has more microcomputer experience.

## Results and discussion

The results of testing hypotheses 1 through 7 are presented in the left-hand portion of Table 2. Globally, one can see that none of the application, task, and user factors had any effect on the overall (as opposed to the decision dimension of) personal DSS success. Alternatively, the user's satisfaction with the overall aspects could be determined by the organizational environment, with its variables not present in the research model. These could include the level of top-management support for microcomputing, the available resources, the managerial and technical expertise of the organizational computing function, and the support provided by outside sources upon which small firms are often highly dependent. The relative unreliability of the overall satisfaction scales and their more general nature might also be the reason for the lack of significance in the observed relationships; the following discussion thus excludes this dimension of personal DSS success.

Hypotheses 1, 2 and 3 linking application characteristics to personal DSS success are confirmed; managers who use an analysis-type application, a self-developed application and especially a spreadsheet-based application tend to have higher levels of decision-making satisfaction. Spreadsheets should be the tool of choice in allowing small businesses to realize the added benefits of DSS and end-user computing as opposed to traditional MIS, i.e. benefits in terms of decisional effectiveness rather than operational efficiency. The spreadsheet's model-based approach is easier to integrate in the manager's own decision logic than a data-based approach, and as such, would be easier for small business managers to learn and use effectively. Spreadsheets can also support the more important managerial roles related to strategy, as opposed to operations, and the more important activities related to planning, as opposed to control. There could also be an interaction with problem type, which was not tested here, as certain types of problems (e.g. budgeting) naturally lend themselves to spreadsheets more than others.

Hypothesis 4 is not confirmed, there being no significant direct correlation between task variety and personal DSS satisfaction. However, hypothesis 5 is confirmed as task autonomy is a significant determinant of the user's decision satisfaction. The promotion of personal DSS development and use by managers in small firms should thus be aimed at two types of task environments. One would be for young firms at or near the start-up stage, or even before start-up, where the only individual who is in a position to use a personal DSS effectively is the owner or entrepreneur. Another environment would be in more established firms where individuals other than the owner have been given some managerial responsibilities and authority.

Microcomputer-trained managers were found to have a significantly higher level of decision satisfaction, in conformity with hypothesis 6. While tools such as Lotus 1-2-3 are very user-friendly, they can still be used inappropriately or ineffectively by untrained users. In this regard, present or future managers should be trained not only in using a particular tool, but in choosing the appropriate tool and in conceptualizing their vision of a managerial problem into a decision model for a spreadsheet, or a data model for a DBMS. However, hypothesis 7 is not confirmed, there being no significant direct correlation between microcomputer experience and personal DSS satisfaction.

Since the research model includes an effect of the task and user environments upon the application environment of personal DSS, the application characteristics are mediating variables in the association of task and user characteristics with success. Partial correlation coefficients were thus calculated by controlling for the three application variables simultaneously, as presented in the right-hand portion of Table 2. The lower values of the partial correlations, with the exception of microcomputer experience, indicate that part of the effect of task autonomy and microcomputer training on personal DSS effectiveness is indirect, i.e. through the more immediate application environment. In the case of experience, the relationship with success becomes significant when the application environment is controlled.

In addition, the fact that managers who develop their applications with a DBMS have more experience would indicate that this tool is less appropriate for the novice end-user than a spreadsheet.

Also of interest is the link between training and user-development of DSS. In the small business context, resources are generally insufficient and preclude the creation of an internal information center. These firms will thus need support from outside sources such as university consulting centers in order to effectively develop and use personal DSS. And as they gain more experience with personal computing, small business managers will be in a better position to realize the benefits of personal decision support systems fully.

There are however limitations to this research, related to the nature and size of the sample. These evidently preclude outright generalization of the results. The cross-sectional nature of the study means that causality cannot be assumed. There is also a potential source of bias in the fact that the sample consists mainly of successful personal DSS applications. Finally, other potential determinants of personal DSS effectiveness could have been included in the research model, including those related to the organizational environment.

## Conclusion

A coherent picture has emerged from this study of selected personal DSS success factors. Small business managers were found to be more successful when they develop their own DSS applications, using spreadsheets to provide greater analytical support for decision-making. Task autonomy and microcomputer training were also found to have a significant effect, both directly and indirectly, through their relationship with the application characteristics. Aiming to build upon and enhance previous DSS/EUC research, this study has provided new insights on the fundamental role played by the application environment, especially in a context where personal DSS have rarely been investigated, namely in small business. Evidence was also obtained as to why the task and user environments must be taken into account, as these differ markedly from equivalent environments in large organizations.

More research is needed however, particularly on the impact of more global organizational and environmental factors of personal DSS effectiveness, if we are to gain a better understanding of this phenomenon. Other measures of success should also be employed, including not only end-user satisfaction or the nature and frequency of use, but also decision performance (whenever possible). By doing so, we will be in a better position to help small enterprises and their managers make more effective use of information technology.
