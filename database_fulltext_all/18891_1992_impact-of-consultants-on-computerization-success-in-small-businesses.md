# Impact of consultants on computerization success in small businesses

Charlie P.P. Soh, Chee Sing Yap and K.S. Raman

National University of Singapore, Singapore

This paper presents the findings of a study comparing the level of computerization success between two groups of small businesses: those with consultants and those without consultants. The comparison, based on data collected from 96 small businesses in Singapore, is performed at three levels: project success, service success, and economic success. The results show that the level of computer system usage of small businesses with consultants is higher than that of small businesses without consultants. The computer systems of the former group also tend to provide more up-to-date computer reports and information than those of the latter. However, small businesses that engage a consultant are less likely to complete their computerization project on time and within budget. Further analysis shows that computerization success of small businesses with consultants is positively associated with three consultant attributes, namely, capability, experience, and effectiveness.

Keywords: Small businesses, Computerization success, Consultant.

## 2. The need for consultants

Small businesses are known to suffer from resource poverty, characterized by severe constraints on financial resources, a lack of trained personnel, and a short-range management perspective imposed by a volatile competitive environment $[16,36]$ . They face many of the same problems and decisions as larger businesses but without the benefit of specialized staff expertise and multiple managerial levels $[31]$ . In the area of computerization, most small businesses lack computer experience and do not have related in-house technical expertise $[9,10,18,19,32]$ . Montazemi $[22]$ examined the computerization environment of small businesses and found that: (1) few small businesses have access to a qualified system analyst, (2) none of these businesses has a specific policy towards the development of required application software, and (3) evaluation of the required technology is based mainly on deficient analysis. Thus, they turn to external consultants for assistance with computerization $[35]$ . Indeed, previous research has established the positive role that outside consultants can play in helping small businesses in the area of planning, accounting, finance, and marketing [25]. In the case of computerization, it is a myth that small businesses, because of their size, “know” how to go about computerizing and therefore do not need the services of computer professionals. On the contrary, a study by Raymond [29] shows that the majority of small businesses: (1) neither plan nor control formally the computerization process, (2) do not identify their problems and opportunities prior to needs analysis, (3) do not formalize their needs in terms of a request for proposal document, and (4) do not sufficiently preoccupy themselves with the system’s start-up phase. He commented that the obstacles to successful computerization in small businesses are much more organizational and individual in nature, such as the lack of management and training methods and tools; small businesses will thus need to rely on external sources of help and consultation. The use of external consultants may provide the much needed expertise to help small businesses in project management and software selection [17]. Consultants have breadth of experience in problem identification, alternatives generation, and implementation and can assist in computerization planning, information requirements analysis, and application package selection [15].

However, engaging a consultant does not guarantee successful computerization. Many instances of failures, where consultants and external experts played an integral part, have been documented $[2,24,37]$ . Lees and Lees $[19]$ observed that small businesses often overestimate the impact of consultant and vendor support, or conversely, they underestimate the importance of their own role in computerization. Thus, there is a need to study the impact of external consultants on computerization success in small businesses.

## 3. Three levels of computerization success

Measuring computerization success is complex and many approaches have been suggested: computer usage estimation, user satisfaction, incremental performance in decision-making effectiveness, cost-benefit analysis, information economics, utility analysis, analytic hierarchy approach, and the information attribute examination [34]. Lucas [20] reported that most research has employed one or more of the following measures: (1) use of the system as measured by intended or actual use, (2) user satisfaction such as self-report measures on a questionnaire or interview, (3) favourable attitudes, either as an objective alone or as a good predictor of the use of the system, (4) degree to which a system accomplishes its original objectives, and (5) payoff to the organization from a system. He commented that no single success measure is uniformly applicable across all implementation efforts. Ein-Dor and Segev [12] identified five criteria of success that are most commonly reported in the literature: profitability, applicability to major organizational problems, quality of decision or level of performance, user satisfaction, and widespread use. They defined a successful computer system as one that: (1) is profitably applied to an area of major concern to the organization, (2) is widely used by one or more satisfied managers, and (3) improves the quality of the manager's performance. Ein-Dor and Segev argued that use is the most significant criterion since a manager will use a system intensively only if it meets the other criteria. In the context of small businesses, computerization success is often measured in terms of user satisfaction and system usage [3,10,23,27,28,30].

In this study, computerization success is categorized into three levels: project success, service success, and economic success. The rationale for such a categorization is that the computer system must first be successfully developed and installed (project success) before the system can provide service to the users (service success) and/or for any potential economic benefits to be realized (economic success). Although the three levels of computerization success may not necessarily form a causal chain, we would expect that the successful achievement of one level will have a positive effect on the successful achievement of the next level. Lyytinen [21] recognized the need for such a categorization in his discussion of one form of computerization failure which he called expectation failure. Expectation failure is a gap between stakeholders' expectations expressed in some ideal or standard and the actual performance. He explained that stakeholder groups can face computerization problems in two distinct phases: in developing or using the computer system. Consequently, computerization failures can be divided into two distinct classes: development failures and use failures.

In summary, this categorization of computerization success into three levels serves three purposes. First, it distinguishes between the process and outcome of computerization. Second, it clarifies the perspective from which the computerization process and outcome are to be evaluated. Third, it facilitates the selection of variables and measures most appropriate for each level of computerization success.

The first level of computerization success, project success, evaluates the extent to which the computerization project is completed on schedule and within budget, and how well the implemented computer system satisfies user requirements [11]. In this study, project success was measured by the extent to which the implemented computer system is completed on time and within budget, and meets user requirements established during feasibility study. The second level, service success, seeks to evaluate the quality of service that is provided by the computer system after it has been implemented. Any attributes that can provide some indication of the quality of the system's service are suitable measures of service success. Most attributes of service success are found in user satisfaction instruments [3,23,28]. Two examples are system response time and accuracy of computer reports. Another complementary measure of service success is system usage. It is envisaged that a system which is highly used does provide some indication of its quality of service. System usage has also been used by DeLone [10] and Raymond [27,28] to measure computerization success. In this study, service success was measured by: (1) the frequency of using the computer system and reports and (2) the degree of user satisfaction with the computer system and reports. The service success instrument was adapted from Raymond's [28] study. The third level of computerization success, economic success, seeks to evaluate the economic benefits of computerization. In this study, economic success was measured by: (1) the change in operating costs after computerization and (2) the impact of computerization on staff productivity. Both are commonly cited economic benefits of computerization [13,19].

## 4. Research method

The criteria for defining a small business are adopted from the Association of Small and Medium Enterprises (ASME) in Singapore. A small business is one that satisfies any two of the following criteria: (1) the number of employees in the business should not exceed 100; (2) the business's fixed production assets should not exceed S\$8 million $^{1}$ ; and (3) the business's annual turnover should not exceed S\$15 million.

The research was carried out in two stages: a pilot study and a questionnaire survey. The addresses of 282 small organizations were obtained from the Association of Small and Medium Enterprises Directory [1]. Out of the 282 small businesses, only 211 were computer users. Eleven small businesses were randomly chosen from the list of computer users for the pilot study. Structured interviews were conducted with the Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) of five small businesses and the Project Questionnaire and Manager Questionnaire were pre-tested with six small businesses. The experience gained from the pilot study provided valuable inputs to the design of the final version of these questionnaires used in the survey.

In the questionnaire survey, the Project Questionnaire and Manager Questionnaire were sent to the CEOs of the 211 small businesses which were computer users. The Project Questionnaire was completed by the person in charge of the computerization project. It sought data on: (1) organizational characteristics such as industry sector, number of employees, annual sales, computer experience, and cumulative computerization expenditure; (2) computer system characteristics such as type of hardware, type of computer applications, and management of the computerization project; and (3) levels of project success and economic success achieved. The Manager Questionnaire (which is indeed a User Questionnaire) was completed by the users of the computer system and reports. It consisted of items which measured the level of service success, that is, the satisfaction of the users with the computer system and the frequency of using the computer system and reports.

## 5.2. Computerization characteristics

Table 3 shows the characteristics of the computerization projects of the sample organizations. Three-quarter of the small businesses were micro computer users, with only 9% using a local area network to link up their micro computers. ## 6. Results and discussion

Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7 present statistics of variables measuring each level of computerization success for the total sample and two sub-samples: (1) small businesses with consultants and (2) small businesses without consultants. Two non-parametric tests were used because these tests can be used with a nominal or an ordinal scale of measurement [5]. For service success which was measured using multiple items, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to detect differences in means between the two sub-samples. For project success, economic success, and overall computerization success, the Chi-square test was used to determine whether there is any difference in frequency distribution of the variables between the two sub-samples. A condition for applying the Chi-square test is that no more than 20 percent of the expected observations of each frequency table are less than 5 [5]. Because the expected observations of some cells in these analyses were less than 5, the expected observations were increased by combining adjacent categories into a single pooled category, as suggested by Siegel and Castellan [33].

## 6.1. Evaluation results

Project Success: The total sample results in Table 4 show that: (1) only six (6%) businesses had more than 90 percent of their requirements met by the implemented computer system whilst thirty-seven (39%) reported that the implemented system satisfies less than 70 percent of their requirements and (2) only thirty-six (41%) small businesses managed to complete their computerization on time and within budget. The Chi-square test results show that the level of requirements met by the implemented system was not correlated with the use of consultants. However, there was a significant relationship between the extent to which the computerization project was completed on time and within budget and the use of consultants. The data show that small businesses with consultants were less likely to complete their computerization on time and within budget.

Service Success: The total sample results in Table 5 show that most small businesses were generally satisfied with and made quite frequent use of their computer system and reports. Of the eleven items measuring user satisfaction, relevance of computer reports had the highest mean value. Table 5 also shows that the means of all service success items in the sub-sample with consultants were higher than those of the sub-sample without consultants except for two: accuracy and reliability of computer reports. However, only two items had a significant difference in means at the 0.1 level: currency of computer reports and frequency of using computer reports. Also, the mean value of system usage of small businesses with consultants was significantly higher than that of small businesses without consultants (p < 0.05).

Economic Success: The total sample results in Table 6 show that: (1) only four businesses (4%) reported a significant decrease in operating costs after computerization whilst fifty-three businesses (58%) experienced “no change” to “significant increase” in operating costs after computerization and (2) a large number of businesses (87%) experienced “slight” to “significant” increase in staff productivity after computerization. No significant relationship was found between the use of consultants and the two variables of economic success: the impact on firm’s operating costs and staff productivity after computerization.

Overall Success: The total sample results in Table 7 show that only three businesses (3%) were extremely successful in their computerization, thirty-nine (41%) were quite successful, and five (5%) were unsuccessful. The Chi-square test results show that there was no significant relationship between overall success and the use of consultants.

Years of Computer Experience: Further analysis was carried out to determine whether the use of consultants was related to the experience level of the small business in terms of previous computerization projects. The Kendall correlation coefficient between the use of consultants and the number of years of computer experience was calculated, and was found to be insignificant (r = 0.093, p = 0.151). This finding indicates that computer consultants were engaged by both experienced and inexperienced small businesses.

## 6.2. Discussion

The most interesting finding is that the computerization projects of small businesses with consultants are less likely to be completed on time and within budget as compared to those without consultants. This finding appears to be counter-intuitive, but our interviews with managers of small businesses provided some insights into the underlying reasons. First, small businesses with consultants are more likely to go through a formalized approach to computerization with a proper feasibility study and requirements analysis. Second, these small businesses tend to go through a lengthy software and hardware evaluation process and are more cautious in committing to a computer solution. Third, small businesses using consultants have more communication (e.g. meetings, presentations) amongst interested parties during computerization. Fourth, the quantity of information (concerning various alternatives) processed in decision-making is higher in small businesses with consultants. These factors tend to make it less likely for the computerization to be completed on time and within budget. This finding is consistent with Lees' [18] finding that small businesses with consultants tended to underestimate the costs of computerization.

Another interesting finding is that the level of system usage of small businesses with consultants is higher than that of small businesses without consultants, particularly, in the use of computer reports. Furthermore, the computer system of small businesses with consultants produces more current computer reports. This finding indicates that small businesses with consultants are more likely to keep the data in their computer system up-to-date and use the reports produced by the computer system. The reason may be that the use of consultants had helped in ensuring that: (1) critical information needs of the business are reflected in computer reports, (2) software applications are designed to capture and store current information, and (3) data entry procedures are established to keep business information in the computer system up-to-date. The end results are up-to-date computer reports which are frequently used by small businesses with consultants.

## 7. Attributes of consultants

Further analysis was performed on the subsample with consultants to study the relationships between the consultant attributes and computerization success. The analysis was motivated by observations from Gable's [14] case study which identified several common concerns with consultant performance: (1) lack of commitment to computerization success, (2) lack of demonstrated expertise, and (3) no methodical approach. From our interviews with managers during the pilot study, we noted that these problems were related to three attributes of the consultant: capability, experience, and effectiveness during feasibility study. Seven hypotheses were formulated to test the associations between these three consultant attributes and computerization success.

H1a: The degree to which requirements (REQUIREMENT) determined during the feasibility study are met by the implemented computer system is positively associated with the consultant's capability (CAPABILITY), experience (EXPERIENCE), and effectiveness (EFFECTIVENESS) during the feasibility study.

H1b: The degree to which the computer system is completed on time and within budget (TIME-BUDGET) is positively associated with the consultant's capability (CAPABILITY), experience (EXPERIENCE), and effectiveness (EFFECTIVENESS) during the feasibility study.

H2a: The level of user satisfaction (SATISFAC-TION) is positively associated with the consultant's capability (CAPABILITY), experience (EXPERIENCE), and effectiveness (EFFECTIVENESS) during the feasibility study.

H2b: The level of system usage (USAGE) is positively associated with the consultant's capability (CAPABILITY), experience (EXPERIENCE), and effectiveness (EFFECTIVENESS) during the feasibility study.

H3a: The decrease in the firm's operating costs (OPER-COST) is positively associated with the consultant's capability (CAPABILITY), experience (EXPERIENCE), and effectiveness (EFFECTIVENESS) during the feasibility study.

Correlation analyses were carried out for the sub-sample with consultants. The results, summarized in Table 8, show that six of the seven hypotheses are supported at the 0.05 significance level, with correlation coefficients ranging from

0.224 to 0.469. The results of Hypothesis 3a are not conclusive. Although some evidence was found to show that the decrease in the firm's operating costs is positively associated with the consultant's effectiveness during the feasibility study, no significant relationship was found for the other two consultant attributes, namely, capability and experience. In a separate study of small businesses in Singapore, Raman [26] observed that computerization in small businesses does not offer much scope for cost reduction in the traditional areas and intangible benefits become the key factors in decisions on investment in computer systems. On a similar note, Cron and Sobol [8] reported that computerization may significantly increase the total operating expenses of small businesses and the potential for significant cost savings may be limited in this situation. These observations suggest that any saving in operating costs of small businesses may not be so easily achieved through computerization. Further research is needed to determine the impact of computerization on the economic performance of small businesses and the role of consultants in helping small businesses to achieve economic success.

These overall findings suggest that the professional attributes of the consultant prior to the job engagement and his/her performance during the engagement process are important to computerization success. In particular, there is evidence to show that computerization success is positively associated with the capability and experience of the consultant, and his/her effectiveness during the feasibility study. Other attributes of the consultant important for successful computerization include facility with communication and the change process, and flexibility and creativity in project management [17]. The implication for small businesses is that they should first assess the capability and experience of the consultant through an interview before engaging him/her. To work effectively with small businesses, a consultant must not only be well-versed in computing and data processing, but also in the management and operation of small enterprises [32]. Thus, small businesses should assess not only the consultant's technical knowledge but also his/her experience in servicing small businesses. Also, to ensure more effective consultant engagement, small businesses should pursue the following three main activities identified by Gable [15]: (1) assess client and consultant compatibility, (2) identify and address specific organizational goals, and (3) accommodate evolving project objectives.

## 8. Summary

This paper has discussed the concept of computerization success in the small business context, proposed its categorization into three levels, project success, service success, and economic success, and demonstrated its applicability through a survey involving 96 small businesses in Singapore. The paper has also compared the level of computerization success between two groups of small businesses, those with consultants and those without. The results show that the level of system usage in small businesses with consultants is higher than those of small businesses without consultants. In particular, these businesses tend to make more frequent use of their computer reports and their computer systems also tend to provide more up-to-date information and reports. However, small businesses with consultants are less likely to complete their computerization projects on time and within budget. The results of further analysis indicate that computerization success is positively associated with the capability, experience, and effectiveness of the computer consultant.
