# The stage hypothesis revisited An EDP professionals' point of view

J.-F. Grégoire and F. Lustman

University of Montreal, Montreal, Que., Canada

The stage hypothesis is one of the models proposed for explaining the evolution of MIS and of its management in organizations. Several attempts to validate the concept empirically have brought mixed and sometimes disappointing results. In this article, the stage hypothesis and the empirical studies are reviewed from an EDP professional's point of view. Our approach is to consider the empirical studies as experiments for testing an hypothesis according to a specified experimental protocol. The experiments are reviewed first, then the protocol is discussed, and finally the hypothesis is re-examined. This process leads to some suggestions for revising the assumptions underlying the model and for defining the concepts of stage and of transition more accurately.

Keywords: Management of information systems; Stage hypothesis

## Introduction

For some time, the evolution and management of organizational data processing, has been investigated by many researchers. One would expect that after so many years of experience, organizational data processing would not still be a problem. Unfortunately, there is evidence that such is not the case: organizations still have MIS planning problems ([13], [7]) and are often unable to apply their experience to new technologies [12]. Software development and quality are still major problems [17].

Among the proposals for explaining the management and evolution of organizational data processing is the stage hypothesis ([5]; [14]; [15]).

In this model, an organization goes through a sequence of stages, from initiation to maturity. The authors also suggest a set of benchmark variables for assessing the stage of an organization. The model has raised much interest, and several attempts have been made to validate it in the field, with mixed, if not disappointing results. Some people have therefore dismissed the stage hypothesis as not been supported by the evidence; others, in spite of the shortcomings still believe in it. In short, no clear conclusion has resulted from the field tests of the stage hypothesis.

Strangely enough, little assessment of, or opinion on, the model has been by EDP professionals. This work is an attempt to provide their point of view. The presentation will take the form of a review of an experiment set up to test an hypothesis.

## 1. Testing an hypothesis

## 1.1. The stage hypothesis

The stage hypothesis proposes a model of the growth of organizational data processing.

First version: Four stages of growth

In Nolan's first proposal, there are four stages of growth.

Stage 1: Initiation. Data processing is located in one functional unit and works only for that unit. Stage 2: Contagion. The use of data processing spreads to other units. There is a proliferation of applications, an explosion of demand, that the information systems resource can hardly satisfy. This leads to a crisis.

Stage 3: Control. Management reacts to the crisis by imposing controls on the demand and on the management and internal workings of the EDP resource to be reorganized.

Stage 4: Integration. Data processing is integrated into the organization. The role of the resource is revised in view of its contribution to the objectives of the organization.

## Second version: Six stages of growth

This version was introduced to reflect a change of orientation: the emphasis shifts from the management of the computing resource to that of the data resource. The first stages are essentially unchanged.

Stage 1: Initiation.

Stage 2: Contagion.

Stage 3: Control. This has the two additional characteristics:

\- a transition from computer resource management to data resource management;

\- an effort to rebuild and professionalize the data processing resource to give it a better standing in the organization.

Stage 4: Integration. Where reliable, high quality services are provided to the users.

Stage 5: Data Administration. The shift in emphasis materializes: heavy investments are made in data base technology.

Stage 6: Maturity. The application portfolio is completed and its structure reflects the organization and its information flows.

## Underlying assumptions

Several assumptions underlie the model, some explicit and other implied by the model.

Theory of evolution: The organization goes through the stages in an uniform way, from first to last.

Influence of Technology: Technology is the major driver of the change.

Influence of management: Management is the major agent in the process. Although not stated explicitly, this assumption comes out clearly in the description of the mechanics of stage change.

## The benchmarks

For operationalizing the model, Nolan suggests a set of six variables: "Any of the benchmarks taken alone could be misleading, but taken together these criteria provide a reliable image". The variables are: data processing expenses, technological benchmark, applications portfolio, data processing organization, data processing planning and control, user awareness.

## 1.2. The tests

The first benchmark, the S-shaped curve of data-processing budgets, was considered as characteristic of the stages. An attempt to validate the variable ended in failure $[11]$ : the results “do not support the use of budgets as a basis for a stage theory of computer development in an organization.” and, “the data do not support the S-shaped curve as a model for computer budget growth.” However, these negative results do not invalidate the stage model: it is intuitively appealing and very useful.

DP organization was also investigated and this led to more positive results $[4]$ . There seems to be a trend towards the creation of an autonomous DP unit; one not dependent on or part of another administrative unit. Also the location of the DP unit in the organizational chart follows a general path along which the dependency of the DP unit on a functional unit decreases. At the end of the path, DP is autonomous.

In another experiment, the existence of the Data Administration function, an attribute of maturity, was investigated and brought disappointing results [6]. A linear combination of the maturity variables was a poor indicator of the existence of a formal DA function. Furthermore, “these variables cannot, in fact be regarded as aspects of a single concept, maturity or anything else”. This suggests that either the maturity concept does not exist or that the results “simply reflect the failure of our set of independent variables to capture the essence of Nolan’s maturity concept”.

An attempt was made to validate the model globally, i.e., to test the six benchmark variables [3]. The results were complex and mixed. The hypothesis that the grouped variables define a single stage for an organization was not confirmed: they did not make possible the classification of organizations in stages. Variables seem to evolve uniformly but at different, apparently uncorrelated, rates. On the other hand, there seemed to be a significant correlation between the stage and the number of planning and control techniques used. Apparently the model is difficult to operationalize, and there are problems of terminology and interpretation of the variables.

One review of the published results focused on the empirical studies [1]. The positive and not so positive results were mentioned and some comments were made on the studies, which did not appear to be flawless. The questionnaire approach is debatable, the methodologies are sometimes arguable, and the studies “did not properly capture the essence of Nolan’s model”.

In a synthesis of the model and of the empirical results, some of the underlying assumptions were reviewed [8]. In particular, the role of technology as the major driver was debated: there are many other factors, such as user demand, organizational politics, prestige, etc. However, the evolution theory aspect of the model has some value.

In summary, results are contradictory, methodologies are sometimes debatable, and no consensus has been reached on the validity of the model.

## 2. Review of the experiment

Now, the various studies are reviewed as experiments for testing the stage hypothesis, which is assumed to be correct. Two possibilities are investigated.

## 2.1. Poor experimentation?

In order to assess the quality of the experiments, we first consider the unequivocal results and then the confused ones.

## 2.1.1. Clear results

The S-shaped DP budget curve was the first victim of the experiments [11]. The experiment was focused on that single variable, the sample was homogeneous (29 Californian counties), and the data covered a long period of time (in some cases twelve years), corrected to eliminate inflation and business cycles effects. In our opinion, the methodology appears valid.

The maturity concept was also put to trial $[6]$ . The sample was large (273 Canadian corporations) and diversified. A questionnaire approach was used. Statistical tests were performed rather carefully, and led to clear results. In this case however, one methodological problem was raised by the authors: they complained of the lack of information on how to measure the variables. Left to their own devices, they adapted the results of other work and were careful to conclude that their choice of variables might be at the origin of their negative results.

Another study [4] focused on a single benchmark variable, the organizational location of the DP unit. Fifty three organizations were investigated, the sample being diversified with respect to concentration in computers and electronics industries. The methodology consisted of interviews with officers; past and present locations of the DP unit were considered. The result, positive, was clear, although not supported by statistical tests. The history of organizational locations of the DP unit showed a uniform upwards trend towards autonomy. Here, the methodology appears valid.

## 2.1.2. Blurred results

An attempt to associate a state with the set of corresponding variable values, ended in partial failure [3]. The approach involved sending a questionnaire to senior DP executives of the target organizations. One hundred and forty four answers were received. The published parts of the questionnaire do not show collection of historical data. The statistical analysis shows that there were problems of interpreting some variables: for example, user awareness, technology and management issues.

## 2.1.3. Conclusion

There seem to be two methodological problems, the use of questionnaires, and the necessity to interpret the variables of the benchmark. It is true that questionnaires do not always provide historical perspective. Questionnaires have been widely and often successfully used in empirical studies. The problem of interpretation and definition seems more serious. Assessing the values of the variables is impossible without a lengthy and in-depth study of the organization.

In summary, some studies do not deserve the criticism of poor experimentation while others do. However, even clear-cut results both support or contradict the stage hypothesis: obviously, blurred results are not all related to poor experimentation.

## 2.2. The experiment protocol

The six benchmark variables are now discussed.

## 2.2.1. The S-shaped budget curve

The results of Lucas and Sutton come as no surprise to anyone familiar with the typical budgeting process. Generally, we do not believe that any finance related variable should be used as a stage assessment tool: DP expenses are budgeted according to the traditions and rules of the organization. DP is still often viewed as a profit or service center. The same is true of other service activities, such as telephone, mail, or office cleaning. Small or middle sized organizations do not even consider chargeback (or the profit center approach), because of the administrative overhead, which seems useless.

## 2.2.2. Technological benchmark

The benchmark describes the mode of operations (batch, time sharing, remote entry) and the type of application (database processing, inquiries, personal computing, etc). This raises questions on the definition and relevance of such variables.

From a technological point of view, the benchmark is incomplete. The kind of operating systems, the programming languages used, and the methods for developing applications, which are relevant features of the technology, are not mentioned.

The benchmark is technologically “dated”, i.e., characteristic of the seventies. when mini and micro computers and work stations are widely used, the benchmark or its proportions will have to be revised. It may already be obsolete; in future, it certainly will be. Finally the benchmark suffers from what we call “%-mania”. How does one measure 65% batch? Is it the number of

CPU cycles? The number of inputs and outputs? The number of applications?

In addition to variable definition problems, we see relevance problems. The first is related to the age of the applications: they almost live forever: they out-date machines and mode of operation. Therefore, if an organization has used DP for very long, it will have many old applications, designed at other times and still running. This will obviously bias the stage assessment. The second problem is more serious. We argue that the mode of operation as defined in the benchmark variable is not a good stage indicator because it is often dependent on the nature of the application. In a payroll or an accounts receivable system, for example, there will always be an important batch component. Moreover, classifying mini or micro processing as different from the other modes of operation excludes small organizations from the stage hypothesis: a micro is often all they need, and they process all their applications on it.

In conclusion, the variable is open to interpretation problems, it is technologically dated, it restricts the application of the model to large organizations, it will provide biased results, and thus its relevance is debatable.

## 2.2.3. The applications portfolio

Three concepts underlie this variable: decentralization, the MIS framework, and the ideal portfolio.

Decentralization becomes visible in stage three and increases in importance up to stage six. Operations are physically decentralized to user sites, and control over the operations shifts to the users. This evolution seems to correspond to reality and may well be a major characteristic of the level of maturity of the users and of the IS resource. However, there is no measure for a clear stage assessment.

The classical three level pyramid of MIS, at the lowest level, consists of operational applications, level two consists of operational control applications, while level three consists of strategic planning and control applications. A careful study of the benchmark shows that as an organization advances through the stages, the applications “climb” the MIS pyramid. In other words, the higher the stage, the more high level applications. This establishes a relationship between the nature of an application (operational, control, strategic) and its justification (improve operations, control operations, serve strategic objectives). It implies that an application is justified at its own level; e.g., applications serving strategic objectives are strategic planning applications. We contend that there is no relationship between the nature of an application (operational, control, planning and control) and its objectives or justification. This is supported by some well known examples: the on-line flight reservation system is typically operational, and while the first airline that implemented it did gain competitive edge, most others had to follow to survive. The same was true of automatic bank tellers. Similarly, the settlement system of the New York Stock Exchange is a very complex system for low-level operations, but the objectives were survival, because without it the NYSE could not settle all the transactions in 24 hours as required by laws and regulation. If there is no relationship or only a loose relationship between the nature of an application and its level of managerial decision-making, the classification of the applications according to an MIS framework provide a biased stage assessment.

The “ideal portfolio” deals with possible applications in the organization, enumerated to show those with a potential financial interest. The stage assessment consists in comparing the actual portfolio with this ideal. Stage six, maturity, is achieved when “the portfolio is complete and mirrors the organization and the flows of information in the company”. This is a complex variable and its assessment is long and tedious, especially for an outsider of the company. It is therefore not surprising that questionnaires failed with this variable. But the concept itself is questionable. The actual portfolio contains applications designed in the past, under past business pressure. Moreover, the ideal portfolio is influenced by present and future business conditions. Today’s ideal portfolio may not be valid tomorrow. The approach therefore requires the elimination of the effect of business cycles. For a profit-oriented organization, business conditions are not just troublesome incidents, they are the drivers. Thus the ideal portfolio is an ever changing thing and has little reference value at a time different from the moment it was defined; it will never be completed, because the organization is part of a changing world.

In summary, the applications portfolio is impossible to measure without an extensive knowledge of the organization; the relationship between the nature of the application and the stages does not hold, except maybe in the past for stage one, and the ideal portfolio concept is static and useless in an ever-changing world. If some relationship between application and stage does exist, a clue for its nature might be found in the concept of level of maturity of an organization: "It is not feasible to implement a complex information system until the organization has reached a "mature" stage of information processing" [2]. There is a relationship between the level of maturity of an organization (level of assimilated knowledge, as revealed by the stage) and the level of complexity of the applications. This however poses a question: how can we assess the level of complexity of an application?

## 2.2.4. DP organization

This involves the position of the IS manager and the IS unit in the organizational chart, the existence or non-existence of a DP Steering Committee, and the various areas of activities of the DP unit (technical services, database research, maintenance, etc.). The value of the variable is not difficult to assess, even with a questionnaire. In fact, one characteristic, location of the IS unit in the organizational chart, has been used to confirmed the stage hypothesis [4].

## 2.2.5. DP planning and control

This is defined rather accurately and does not raise any problem as long as the emphasis is on the extent of planning and control rather than on specific tools. One experiment [3] showed that the number of planning and control tools used increases with the stage.

## 2.2.6. User awareness

This is a very difficult variable. Although it appears obvious that the users play a role in shaping or characterizing a stage, its definition is rather fuzzy and its value very difficult to assess. Several concepts are involved: extent of the user involvement plus the nature and extent of responsibilities. what is lacking however is the user's attitude towards DP and the diversity of these attitudes when one takes into account all the potential users of the organization. User awareness is a fuzzy concept, difficult to assess, somewhat incomplete, and implies that the community of users behaves like a group. This variable undoubtedly raises interpretation problems.

## 2.3. Conclusion

One variable contradicts the hypothesis, one supports it, some are well defined, others are poorly defined, and some seem irrelevant. In our judgement, the experimental protocol is partially responsible for the rather limited and confused results of experiments. Moreover, it seems that some important aspects of the stage hypothesis are lacking; e.g., the role of top management, the user profile, and the proficiency of the DP unit.

## 3. Review of the hypothesis

## 3.1. The observations

For those who have been actively involved in organizational computing, Nolan's observations are accurate. The attitude of DP's management and staff, striving for a greater role in the organization during stage two and the poor quality of level one (operational) applications are examples. Even the technological benchmark, although flawed, is nonetheless an accurate description of what was done at the time of observation (the seventies).

Therefore, we state that the problems may be related to the interpretation, of results.

## 3.2. The underlying assumptions

The translation of observed facts into the stage hypothesis is driven by three important assumptions.

## 3.2.1. Theory of evolution

“Organizations go through the stages of the model in a uniform way”. This assumption has been challenged by authors who feel ill at ease with this “providence-based” theory, and argue that the motion is not always in the same direction, and can be reversed [9]. In our view, the assumption is not related to some belief in supernatural forces, but based on observations which are accurate.

Regarding the underlying motor of this directed evolution, Nolan names it: acquisition of knowledge, where progressive acquisition and use of external and internal information influences the movement through the stages. If this process of knowledge acquisition is accepted, there is no longer any difficulty in agreeing with uniform evolution. It is hard to imagine a permanent process of learning resulting in decrease of knowledge in the absence of some “serious illness”. Moreover, the assumption is supported by economic considerations. Each higher stage should result in a more rational, more efficient use of the DP resource. There is therefore a business incentive to push for a higher stage.

## 3.2.2. Technology as a driver

Technology seems the major factor in the stages and evolution, though not all authors agree with this. Indeed, some evidence is not consistent with the major role of technology. In a 1985 survey of IS managers concerning their major concerns, planning and software development were the top, while issues dealing with new technologies (such as fourth generation languages or artificial intelligence) ranked very low in the list of preoccupations [7]. More recently, a study showed that IS managers have a tendency to be cautious towards new technologies and to adopt a wait-and-see attitude. In fact, we too argue with the major influence of technology.

Let us start with the budget. Although the S-shape of the budget curve has not been confirmed, technology might nonetheless explain it. This leads, however, to a contradiction. The components of a DP budget are technology (hardware, software, methods), human resources, and supplies. It is an easy exercise to show that, over the years, the technology part of the budget has steadily decreased, while the human resources part has increased, in spite of the fact that technological progress increases human productivity. Technology should therefore bring a decrease in budget or at the very least, a decrease in the unit cost of services (which indeed it has). The cost of DP professionals has been going up, which can be explained by an increased demand for DP services. In our opinion, demand increase is the real reason for budget increase, and the demand is driven by an increased knowledge by the users of the possibilities of IS.

If technology presides over the increases, there should be some relationship between stages and major technology pushes. Some examples of major technological events are: transistors, multiprogrammed systems, telecommunications and time sharing, integrated circuits, databases, microcomputers, workstations, etc. Each inventor or “missionary” of a new technological feature pretends that this invention or discovery will revolutionize the industry. However, except for database technology, no stage in the Nolan model corresponds to a major technology breakthrough. If technology is a major factor of evolution, then there should be some relationship between stage-to-stage transitions and major technological events. Once more, the model does not reflect the pushes of technology.

In summary, the stage hypothesis shows no link with technology change. In fact, we argue that technology has no effect on the stage hypothesis. The model has been applicable to a broad spectrum of organizations, small, medium, large, public, and private, to IS as well as to scientific and engineering-oriented computing. Such different kinds of organizations and applications make use of very different technological features. In spite of that, the model seems applicable to all. The common driver is the process of acquisition and use of the computing discipline. By this we mean not only the knowledge of the technology but mostly the art of using it efficiently.

## 3.2.3. The role of management

According to Nolan, it is management that initiates the transitions from stage to stage and which positions the organization in a given stage. For example, contagion results from a management policy of encouraging the use of DP. Other reasons have already been proposed for it, such as the rush of would-be users to DP, regardless of company policies or promotion by the DP unit. In fact, for many who have lived through stages one and two, user rush seems more convincing than “deliberate management policy”. The “relaxing of rules to promote the use of DP” looks much more like a lack of policy. Nolan has noted that applications developed in stages one and two are mostly operational, cannot support higher level systems, are poorly developed and that their maintenance requires up to 70% of the analysts’ and programmers’ time. The reason advanced for that state of affairs is of “inexperienced programmers working without the benefit of effective DP management control systems”. This draws a smile or a sigh from every DP manager who has out-lived the times. Some other reasons for the poor quality may be: user incompetence (may be the user forgot or did not want to specify a requirement) and poor hiring policies (resulting in incompetent DP staff). As early as 1971, Weinberg noticed that management favored “quick and dirty” solutions, which perpetuate the poor quality of systems [16]. Finally, some concepts, like modularity, integration, methods in systems analysis and design, the art of good testing etc., were not understood in the seventies and were not used widely enough in the eighties. In summary, many reasons could explain the poor quality of the systems developed during stages one and two, involving management, the users, the DP staff, and the state of the art.

The inception of stage three also supports the contention of non-involvement of upper management. The contagion stage ends in a crisis characterized by unhappy, frustrated users, an exhausted, frustrated DP manager, and runaway DP budgets. Often the reaction involves the hiring of external consultants to try to find out what is wrong and what should be done.

In summary, the role of management has been overestimated in the stage hypothesis; sometimes management attitudes have even had negative effects.

## 3.2.4. Conclusion

The influence of technology and the role of management have been dismissed as major factors of influence in the model. On the other hand, the learning process and the use of the acquired knowledge seem to provide a good explanation for the stages and evolution.

## 3.3. The stages model

## 3.3.1. Four stages or six stages?

The four stages model describes the spread of DP through the organization, how it is managed, and to which ends it is used. Nothing in this contradicts what has been argued. On the other hand, the six stages model contains a stage based exclusively on technology (data administration).

We dismiss it as incompatible with our view about the noninfluence of technology.

## 3.3.2. Stage definition

If a stage model is to be applied, the concept of stages should be defined independently. In other words, a stage definition should not be an enumeration of stages but a set of properties or characteristics that each stage should have. Some variables are fuzzy and difficult to measure, others are not appropriate or irrelevant, others are related to assumptions that do not seem to correspond to reality. A guideline for a formal definition of the stage concept could be the level of organizational knowledge and of its use.

## 3.3.3. Evolution through the stages

If a stage model is appropriate, how does an organization go from one stage to the next? Surely not overnight. This aspect of the model has been completely overlooked, and may explain why some authors could not recognize the stage of a specific organization. The organization goes from initiation to contagion when more than one functional unit requires or uses DP services. Although this may be considered an instantaneous event, it is not. Before the privileged user relinquishes exclusive control over DP, there will be pressures, arguments between DP and users, etc. Moreover, how and when will the organizational location of DP change? The transition from contagion to control is a clearer problem. First, the crisis may linger for some time before upper management reacts. Then, even when controls are imposed, they will not become effective overnight. Project management for example does not become a reality by decree; it requires much learning, technical tools, new procedures, etc. In other words, even if management actions were limited to the imposition of controls, it would take a long time before they become effective. They would surely be implemented progressively, and be a long, may be a very long time in transition.

In summary, transitions are part of any stage model and the model definition is not complete without their definition and description. In fact, a dynamic organization which acquires and uses knowledge quickly would be in transition longer than it would in a given stage.

## 4. Conclusion

In this article, the stage hypothesis and validation attempts of several researchers have been reviewed. The model seems to be based on accurate observation of reality but attempts to validate it have brought mixed results: some support the hypothesis, most experiments contradict it or were inconclusive. An attempt has been made to investigate the reasons for this. Sometimes, the variables are not well defined. It has been argued that some stated variables are not relevant. Finally, the model seems incomplete: a formal definition of the stage concept is lacking (only an operational one is given), and transitions have been completely overlooked. It is suggested that the process of organizational learning and using DP may be the major factor of influence.
