# Purchasing software systems Managing the risk

Susan A. Sherer

Lehigh University, Bethlehem, PA, USA

Companies no longer develop customized systems for every application. In fact, the growth in specialized software companies, coupled with diverse skill requirements, rapidly changing technology, and increased demand for automated applications provide impetus for purchasing software products. These must often be tailored in order to satisfy unique requirements, then integrated with existing facilities.

Software purchase can reduce some components of risk, such as attempting to produce a system with inadequate technology or personnel. However, it can increase others; e.g., the system fails to provide needed functionality or the buyer becomes dependent upon the supplier for future support.

We discuss the different components of risk in software purchase and then focus on the risk that the software fails to operate correctly in the user environment. A case study demonstrates how formal methodology to assess failure risk can be integrated into decisions regarding the purchase and installation of software.

Keywords: Software purchase; Failure risk; Functionality risk; Implementation; Risk management; Software selection; Packaged software

## 1. Introduction

Software purchase has become an attractive alternative to inhouse development. It provides economies of scale while altering the risk profile of the implementation project [4].

Specialized software companies meet demands for skills that are difficult to find and maintain in IS departments. Faced with the increasing demand, IS managers can reduce internal backlogs with purchased software. Tailoring this software is often more cost effective than expensive in-house development that can delay the useful operation of a system.

While purchase can reduce some components of risk, such as attempting a project with inadequate technology or personnel, it also increases other elements of risk. Perhaps the greatest is failure to meet user requirements, that can result when customers are not cognizant of all system and procedural changes needed to use the system effectively. Buyers often rely on paper exercises, customer testimonials, and vendor-supplied documentation to make purchase decisions $[5]$ . They are not involved in internal design reviews where potential operational problems can be identified. Purchasing a generic system rather than developing a customized one may entail the loss of some functionality that can limit the system usefulness. But tailoring the generic system to provide these functions can introduce new risks. The buyer often depends upon the vendor for support.

The controllable components of risk should be identified and measured [1] so that they can actively be managed during implementation [2]. In particular, failure risk – that the software fails to operate correctly in the customer's environment – can be managed when selection and installation decisions are guided by the measurement of this risk. While failure risk assessment can also guide inhouse software development and testing [15], purchase decisions are unique, because they involve tailoring systems or adapting procedures to match these systems. Moreover, the user organization generally has limited information regarding the code and its development.

## 2. Software purchase risk

Purchasing software modifies the significance of the different elements of risk in software development and use, as summarized in Table 1. Purchase often reduces risk elements that impede development but can increase risks that arise during the system operation.

Development risks that are often reduced by software purchase include: project risk – the organization cannot complete the project on time or within budget, and technical risk – project goes beyond what is possible. While there may be uncertainty regarding package modification time and cost [6], it is more difficult to plan and meet a schedule for a complete development effort rather than simply install a developed system. Technical risk is reduced because the developers tend to have more expertise with specialized technology.

Political risk – development or use hindered by organizational politics – can increase with purchased software if the internal systems development group perceives the acquisition as a threat to their livelihood. This risk can be managed by staff involvement in decision making and demonstration of purchased software's advantages.

Failure risk - failure of the system to operate correctly in the user environment - can result from either functionality risk - failure to meet user requirements or performance risk – inadequate software quality. Since purchased software may have a customer base, it is likely to have less errors, reducing performance risk. But failure risk is reduced only if users truly understand how the proposed system will operate. Inhouse prototyping has decreased the traditional advantage of purchased software: preview of system operation allows more concrete identification and validation of user requirements [11]. It is often more difficult to relate to the previewed operation of a purchased system, especially if the environment is very different. Moreover, revising a completed software package is more expensive than a prototype [10]. Functionality risk increases if software does not address all user requirements. Since generic software packages may not do so, users may have to contend with a system that does not meet all their needs – increasing functionality risk. Tailoring the application to meet specific needs may leave the company more vulnerable to incorrect operation than if the software were developed inhouse – increasing performance risk.

Software purchase affects all risks associated with the need to change software as it is used: maintainability risk – system is incapable of being properly maintained; adaptability risk – system cannot be enhanced or changed; and portability risk – system is not transferable to different configurations. Purchase establishes new cooperative relationships, introducing risks of appropriable rent and loss of resource control [3]. If the vendor maintains the software, risk from appropriable rents [7] occurs if the vendor attempts opportunistic renegotiation of the contract, increasing maintenance cost or refusing to provide maintenance. Since there is often a high switching cost to convert to another system, the relationship can result in substantial transaction risk and lock-in to the supplier. Moreover, if the buyer allows required technical skills to atrophy, he loses resource control and may. no longer be capable of internally maintaining, adapting, or transferring the system.

Financial risk – the system will not generate forecasted return on investment – can be reduced with software purchase only if the customer truly understands the customization and integration requirements and can accurately estimate the effort needed to install the system in the new environment.

Systemic risk results when the environment changes, causing loss of system value from competitive, regulatory, or internal response. Software purchase can alter the competitive nature of the industry if the software vendor obtains proprietary information which is then used to alter the basis of competition.

## 3. Software purchase decisions

While all components of risk should be actively managed when purchasing software, we focus on consideration of failure risk, often an implicit component of software purchase and implementation decisions. Both costs and benefits must be analyzed when selecting systems $[17]$ . The selected system should not only meets user requirements but provide minimal risk of failure in the new environment. Proper implementation procedures manage this risk.

Functional capabilities must be analyzed in the context of the business environment in which the system will operate. If the software does not meet a large number of the requirements, the customization and integration could require almost the same level of effort as needed to develop the system internally. Review of the system in operation at other installations can indicate compatibility with the new environment. Requirements should be reviewed, insuring that they fit the existing technology or verifying that the technology can be expanded to accommodate the software.

Formal methods, such as scoring models or weighted ratings, can be used to choose among competing software systems. These methods generally focus on what the proposed system can do, whether it meets user requirements, and how it can improve the organization's capabilities. Risk components can be weighted or risk preferences can be incorporated in utility functions [18]. But while these methods incorporate risk attitudes, they do not explicitly measure the expected loss associated with a risk component.

The evaluation of proposed systems should explicitly consider what can go wrong when using the software in order to measure its risk of failure in the proposed environment. To avoid high cost and delay associated with changing software packages, customers often modify existing procedures to reduce discrepancies between the package and user needs [9]. Failure risk associated with these modifications can serve as a basis for choosing among competing systems.

Installation planning follows software acquisition. If the software is to replace an existing system, the company has several options: it can be installed in parallel with the existing one or it can simply replace the old – a “cold turkey” or “big bang” installation of the new system. In some cases, a small pilot application of the new system may be feasible, and this may be particularly attractive for a system providing completely new functions.

Installation decisions should consider both the cost and risk of failure. The parallel system is costly and difficult to maintain; it is often difficult to discern whether the new output are more accurate. However, a “cold turkey” installation can place the company in a position of unacceptable risk if the system does not work correctly. Testing is especially critical in this mode of implementation. A small pilot application may not invoke the environmental changes necessary to test the failure risk of the complete system.

Since software can have latent errors; a key consideration is the amount of time to either run a pilot or parallel system or test at the new installation. This decision should consider the cost of continued testing and schedule delays as well as the risk. Continued testing would be warranted if failure risk exceeded cost. However, software with minimal risk may not justify the cost of either additional testing or parallel operation.

## 4. Failure risk assessment

This begins with estimation of external exposure, the magnitude of loss that could result when a system is used. These assessments can guide system selection and provide the basis for measuring module failure risk [16], the expected loss due to failure in the modules of a software system, to guide system implementation decisions.

## 4.1. External exposure assessment

This requires identification of all hazards, or conditions leading to loss, that can occur when a system is installed. Each installation has a unique environment with its own personnel and procedures. Moreover, alternative systems provide different functions requiring distinct operating methods. These can result in unique accident scenarios and failure modes leading to hazards. For example, some systemis have more extensive controls or audit functions that can alter the possibility of loss from invalid input.

Software system selection should include an external analysis of anticipated system use in the proposed environment; one that focuses on what can go wrong. This analysis should incorporate both “variance analysis” [12] which concentrates on system weaknesses that occur when integrating technical and social parts of the system and “future analysis” [8] which predicts changes that will occur and their impact on the system. The analysis should identify all actions that can precipitate hazards and assess their probability of occurrence. The risk assessor must thoroughly analyze the environment and the system. Inability to identify all hazards or failure modes can, of course, result in under-estimated exposure assessment.

The magnitude of loss that may result from inappropriate actions is a function of the environment and the context in which the system operates. Since there is virtually an unlimited number of environmental factors that can affect loss, the information is consolidated utilizing expected values for each condition defining the purchaser's environment and the particular application.

External exposure assessment provides estimates of the potential loss associated with each hazard j during operational time T, $L_{j}(T)$ . These will differ for each installation and system and can provide a basis for selecting it.

## 4.2. Module failure risk

This is an estimate of the potential loss that could result from faults in components of a system. It is estimated as the product of module exposure, the potential loss due to failures that could result from faults in a software module, and the expected number of failures resulting from those faults:

$$
R ^ {M} (T) = E ^ {M} (T) N ^ {M} (T),
$$

where $R^{M}(T)$ = failure risk during calendar time T of module M, $E^{M}(T)$ = exposure due to module M during time T, $N^{M}(T)$ = expected number of failures due to faults in module M during time T.

## 4.2.1. Module exposure

Once a new system is acquired, the internal information system team generally reviews the specifications before implementation. This review should be structured with the objective of understanding the failure risk of individual modules. Both the module's function and its intended use are related to the external exposure analysis. Exposure can be expressed as:

$$
E ^ {m} (T) = \sum_ {i} p \left(U _ {i} ^ {M}\right) \sum_ {j} p \left(H _ {j} / U _ {i} ^ {M}\right) L _ {j} (T),
$$

where $p(U_{i}^{M}) = \text{probability module } M \text{ has use } i$ , $p(H_{j}/U_{i}^{M}) = \text{probability hazard } j \text{ occurs when module has use } i$ .

The module's function at any time, and the associated exposure level, could vary with the way the system is using it; this, in turn, is related to the way the system itself is used. Estimates of the expected use of different system functions by the customer provide the basis for the system use distribution. The module use distribution, $p(U_i^M)$ , is derived by relating a module's function to system use. Since each customer may use a purchased system differently, module use and, therefore, exposure will differ for each installation.

To estimate the conditional hazard probability distribution, $p(H_{j}/U_{i}^{M})$ , it is necessary to first determine which hazard scenarios can result from faults in each module. Software failures that can result in actions leading to the hazards are identified. Since software failure results in invalid system output or lack of anticipated output, faults in a module could cause or effect a hazard if the module is involved in the production of such output. By reverse engineering the specifications, critical data elements related to this output are revealed, enabling the systems team to determine which modules could be involved in the production of the output. The conditional probability distribution of these hazards can be assigned uniformly or based upon the relative severity of the different hazards.

## 4.2.2. Expected number of failures

The expected number of failures resulting from faults in a module can be estimated with a software reliability model [13]:

$$
N ^ {M} (T) = \mu \big (1 - e ^ {- \Theta t (T)} \big),
$$

where $\mu =$ mean number of faults in the module, $\Theta =$ per fault failure rate, the probability that a fault would cause a failure per unit CPU time, $\iota(T) = \text{CPU time during operational time } T$ .

This model requires estimates of the number of faults and the probability that a fault will cause a failure: parameters that depend upon the characteristics of the module and its development. This information may not be readily available for purchased software. Customers can request software problem report records from vendors in order to derive initial estimates, but vendors may be unwilling to provide this information or may provide overly optimistic parameters. Other installations' problem report records could generate first estimates or information on similar products could be used. As test information is gained, these parameters are updated with Bayesian analysis.

Operational failure risk assessment can guide the allocation of testing and training efforts during installation, insuring that the customer's high risk system components are thoroughly tested and that users are trained in procedures involving these components. While testing and training can be costly, they will generally decrease failure risk. The optimum amount of testing, the amount of test time that maximizes the reduction in failure risk minus the cost of testing (assuming no benefit from early use of poorly tested software), is: $^{1}$

$$
\tau^ {* M} = - \Theta^ {- 1} \ln \left\{K _ {2} / \Theta [ E ^ {M} (T) N ^ {M} (T) - \mu K _ {1} ] \right\},
$$

where $K_{1}=$ cost to run and analyze test cases, $K_{2}=$ cost to find and remove fault leading to failure. This information can guide testing and implementation plans.

## 5. Case study

The use of failure risk assessment to manage the purchase of software is now illustrated with a case study involving the acquisition and implementation of a Commercial Loan System by a savings and loan association. After describing traditional selection procedures, we discuss how to augment the decisions with exposure estimates. Then we recount how the system was installed and conclude with examples to illustrate how risk estimates could guide testing and implementation.

## 5.1. Acquiring the commercial loan system

Prior to the early 80's, savings and loans were restricted to lending fixed-rate, long term home mortgages, funded by short term deposits. In 1983, deregulation provided an environment for a savings and loan organization to adjust its traditional business by broadening its product line to include shorter maturity loans.

First Savings and Loan $^{2}$ established its commercial business lending department in 1984, acquiring a basic information system to keep track of its simply structured loans. Within the next several years, it expanded quickly through an aggressive acquisition policy and entrance into new markets. Loan production offices grew from 3 to almost 20; the commercial loan business grew to include over 10% of all loans within 4 years. This rapid growth began to tax the capabilities of the loan information system. Furthermore, changes in the external environment necessitated changes in the information systems. For example, a significant change occurred in the method of accounting and reporting for both loan origination fee income and the related origination costs $^{3}$ .

First Savings and Loan recognized the need to implement an enhanced system to provide for more efficient and effective delivery of commercial customer services as well as more comprehensive management reporting and control. The system would need to maintain all information about new accounts, borrowers, lines, commitments, loans, takedowns, renewals, participations, syndications, repayment schedules, indirect liabilities, interest and fee schedules, collateral, and payment transactions. To provide improved service, borrower, loan, and collateral information would need to be kept online for immediate access by loan officers.

The initial task, determining what was required in the new system, was assigned to a group of approximately 10 people, consisting of main office and field personnel and system developers. The task force developed a list of requirements in the following six areas: loan accounting and operations, loan structures, collateral recordkeeping, audit and security controls, management reporting, and remote inquiry capabilities. For example, loan accounting and operations requirements included ease of new loan establishment, small number of screens, ability to carry-over common account information, complete edit verification on all input items, flexible billing schedules, and multiple transaction options.

The task force proceeded to develop a questionnaire addressing these requirements. First Savings and Loan identified five commercially available software products. All were from companies with at least 15 years in the business and with over 50 technical support people; four had at least 25 installations. The systems ranged in age from new (less than 1 month) to 17 years. The number of program modules in these systems ranged from 200 to 800. Using their questionnaires as the basis for telephone interviews, the corporation obtained information regarding each of the items on their requirements list. Each system was rated on these requirements. In addition, a summary of the major strengths and weaknesses of each was prepared.

Based upon the results of the ratings, the group recommended the system provided by Financial Software Inc. (FSI). This met almost all their requirements; the cost of acquiring this system and customizing it to fit needs was less than the cost of internal development.

First Savings and Loan made a very careful, thorough investigation of how the proposed system would meet the identified requirements. The formalized analysis concentrated on how the system would meet specific requirements. Although informally considered, neither explicit risk analysis nor consideration of what could go wrong when using each system was included.

## 5.2. improving software acquisition decisions with exposure assessment

External exposure assessment forces explicit consideration of the external use of the proposed system and its associated hazards, information that can supplement an analysis of a system's ability to meet functional requirements.

We identified major hazards using a commercial loan system at First Savings and Loan. These are summarized in Table 2. Different features offered by the proposed alternate systems would change the external processing and procedures used by loan personnel. Hence, the external exposure for each of these potential hazards would differ. Variations in audit reports and inquiry screens provided by the systems contribute to differences in external exposure. Differences in necessary training procedures affect exposure. Assessment of each system's external exposure for each hazard could help the customer select a system that would minimize such exposure.

To illustrate, consider the hazard “invalid interest”. Several assumptions were made to develop estimates for each of the contending systems. While these are subjective, they incorporate reasonable assumptions regarding expected operation and behavior. First, average monthly interest was estimated at \$2.4 million. It was assumed that there would be a 10% chance that customers would not alert the savings and loan institution if interest charges were incorrect. Overcharges were assumed to result in customer service problems;

undercharges in loss of interest. In the FSI system, a loan officer would enter interest, effective rates, and codes for interest accrual, while a loan input control clerk would input and maintain interest rate schedules. If these data were not entered, the system would accept a loan but would not bill interest. Personnel were responsible to recognize that interest accruals were missing from information included in massive audit reports. This led to the assumption that missing accrual schedules would lead to interest not billed 10% of the time (expected loss \$24,000). Costs to cover customer service problems resulting from interest overcharges were estimated at \$3000 (.01% of average monthly bills plus additional clerical effort). Loss of interest on undercharges was estimated at \$2000 (50% of accounts at 2%/year). The resulting exposure estimate for this hazard for the FSI system at First Savings and Loan was \$29,000/month.

Exposure from alternative systems differed because processing requirements and system functions for loan accruals differed, changing the probability that external errors could be made. Table 3 compares exposure estimates from the alternative systems for this hazard. Loan officers using Alternative 1 would be required to enter a substantial amount of additional information regarding loan structures. This information would be obtained from separate loan manuals rather than be incorporated in the system logic. There was a much greater probability that either loan officers or loan input control would enter invalid information or neglect to enter the loan schedules. The exposure estimate was \$48,000, assuming a 15% chance that accrual schedules would be missing (\$36,000); \$7000 to cover service problems resulting from corrections; and \$5000 potential interest loss on undercharges.

Another hazard that had very different exposures for the five systems was “overadvance committed dollars”. This means that customers receive loan advances exceeding their lending limit. Since loan commitments would be made at various levels of an organization (e.g., corporate, department), linkages were required so that total commitments could be monitored. One system required a great deal of manual effort by loan officers in order to identify these linkages. This resulted in very high external exposure. A system which used participations, affiliations and indirect liabilities to establish the linkages required additional officer training. External exposure was high, because more extensive training was needed and ongoing training procedures were traditionally poorly instituted and executed in this organization. A third system had pointers across organizational levels to establish loan linkages, reducing exposure because of minimal manual processing.

Each system had different exposure assessments for the hazard of “not producing bills”. Several systems required billing defaults to be set manually, increasing the exposure; others included different automatic defaults, each with differing exposures. While some systems had fixed payment frequencies, others had overlapping schedules that would affect the billing cycle and external processing. Some systems calculated the customer billed amount; one billed only the payment amount on a note record; while another billed a fixed percentage of the outstanding balance. The external exposure assessment forced consideration of how these differences would affect external processing.

External exposure assessment requires consideration of how the environment will change. First Savings and Loan planned to begin direct posting to their general ledger at some time in the future. The various procedures required by each system to accomplish this task were analyzed to determine which would minimize external exposure due to the identified hazard, “misposting of transactions to General Ledger”. One alternative had a much higher exposure for this hazard than any of the other ones because the interface was not supplied with the system.

Comparisons of total system exposure can supplement requirements ratings to select a system. If a choice is not apparent, sensitivity analysis can ascertain what change in the exposure estimates can alter decisions and then concentrate on obtaining additional information to support these estimates. In this case, the FSI system had one of the lowest total exposures; its functional capabilities made it more desirable than another system with slightly lower exposure.

## 5.3. Implementing the commercial loan system

The system was implemented at First Savings and Loan in 1987. Test procedures and training plans were developed during implementation planning. The system was tested during a three month implementation period, followed by a planned one month parallel operation with the existing system. Due to problems that arose during the first month of parallel operation, management approved a second month of parallel testing, after which the system became totally operational. During the first eight months of operation, 34 problems were uncovered, requiring corrections to 21 modules.

Training programs were developed for both loan and main office personnel. Loan office personnel were trained to enter specific types of loans and provide responses to customer inquiries. The FSI system offered many capabilities (e.g., alternative loan structures) not planned for immediate use; information regarding these features was provided in manuals. Main office personnel were instructed in data entry and verification procedures; exception handling was outlined in data entry manuals.

## 5.4. Improving system implementation decisions with risk assessment

How should the new system be installed? Where should the test effort be focused? How much testing is needed? What type of training procedures are needed? These questions are particularly difficult to answer when a system is purchased, because the organization has limited experience with the software.

The type of implementation – parallel, pilot, "cold turkey" or "big bang" replacement – is a function of the type of system, the cost of installation, and the risk of failure. In all cases, testing is required, since the system is operating in a new environment. Usually, this is functional rather than structural. Functional testing uses system inputs to develop test cases while structural testing uses the internal structure of the program. The decision to stop testing is made when the manager feels that the functions have been adequately tested or the deadline for the turnover to the new system arrives. Module risk assessment should be used to test the structure of the system so that those portions of the system that have the greatest risk of failure can be identified and receive adequate testing.

These risk assessments could have guided implementation, testing, and training plans. The analysis did support the choice of a parallel installation. The risk of failure was substantial for several modules, rendering a “cold turkey” replacement installation unreasonable. A small pilot operation would not test an adequate volume of transactions through the high risk modules, all large batch modules processing a variety of financial transaction types. A parallel implementation was most desirable, but how long should it last?

The optimum amount of test time was determined for each of the modules. Only three of approximately 135 modules required extensive additional testing, approximately 4000 CPU minutes. Some modules did not require additional testing, because their failure risk did not substantiate the cost of testing. However, since this information was based upon failure parameters derived from modules in similar systems, it was validated by preliminary testing of all its modules at First Savings and Loan. Sensitivity analysis can be incorporated by evaluating the degree of change in each of the parameters that would alter the decision to terminate further testing.

The ability to use these methods early in the implementation effort is dependent upon the availability of data. If failure data from the vendor are unavailable, more generic likelihood data would be used initially, but the results would need to be validated with test data from the new location before the information could be used to focus further testing efforts. In instances where purchased software is supplied without technical systems documentation, specific risk estimates may be difficult to obtain. Modules or system functions could still be ranked by exposure, even though failure likelihood information would be lacking.

Finally, training procedures can be developed from risk assessments. Modules that have the greatest risk of failure should be monitored most closely. For example, training should focus on procedures to review audit reports incorporating output from high failure risk modules.

## 6. Conclusions

The increasing use of purchased software offers unique risks resulting from the need to refine and integrate existing software into a new environment. Knowledge of the software's failure risk can guide the acquisition and implementation of the system.

We have used a case study to demonstrate how significant software purchase decisions can be guided by failure risk assessment. Whereas traditional approaches to software selection evaluate a proposed system's capabilities and limitations, the risk assessment emphasizes how proposed systems contribute to hazards in the customer's environment. While this information can supplement traditional selection procedures, it can also disclose future operational difficulties and sources of risk in a selected system. The explicit analysis of operational use and hazards is the most significant contribution of the risk assessment procedures to the purchase of software.

Module risk assessment can guide implementation efforts by focusing attention on those portions of a system that pose the greatest risk in a new installation. It can help determine how much test effort is needed as well as installation and training plans.
