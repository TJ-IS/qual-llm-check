# A management information system for a Chinese university \*

Ying Qirui and Zhu Mingxue

Shenzhen University, Nantou, People's Republic of China

Therold E. Bailey

University of Central Arkansas, Conway, USA

This paper describes a comprehensive Management Information System (MIS) designed and developed at Shenzhen University, People's Republic of China, using off-the-shelf hardware and software. The data base provides information on all aspects of the university, including, but not limited to, operations, maintenance, programs, enrollments, students, faculty, and financial records. Management of all aspects of the university depends heavily on the MIS. The MIS has had enormous administrative consequences and has potential for expansion.

Keywords: China; Data base management systems; Decision support systems; Developing countries; Information management; Management information systems; People's Republic of China; University administration

## MIS in China

China, as a developing socialist country, has only recently begun application of modern management principles and techniques in state enterprises. Management information systems have not reached the strategic level of management, and are not widespread at operational and tactical levels.

China shares with other developing countries some major deterrents to the application of MIS technologies: inadequate telecommunications systems, lack of sophisticated and comprehensive data gathering systems, and lack of trained users. All of these problems are exacerbated by lack of hard currency to purchase expertise and technology from world markets. Governments at national, provincial, and municipal levels are in early stages of developing a modern telecommunications system for the entire country, but only major cities have modern telephone service and only some of them are linked together. Shenzhen has a modern electronic telephone system connected to international communication systems through Hong Kong.

The central government has installed a number of regional data centers, eventually to be connected into a national data gathering and processing network so essential for a centralized command economy. Shenzhen is one of these centers, having relatively modern hardware and skilled, highly motivated system development personnel. However, the center is often idle, according to personnel, because of lack of data.

## Background of Shenzhen University

Shenzhen University established in 1983, embraces a beautiful, new campus in a majestic seaside setting in Shenzhen Special Economic Zone.

The university is a comprehensive university with approximately 6,000 students and 600 faculty. The university's three "classrooms" include the following: (1) instruction through traditional and nontraditional classroom and laboratory experience; (2) work-study programs to develop attitudes and skills in self-support, work, and societal responsibilities; and (3) extracurricular activities, including various student organizations, to develop social, recreational, and cultural skills.

One of the university's primary missions is to serve the scientific, technical, economic, and managerial needs of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone. The administration wants to bring technology, modern management and business principles, and technical and management expertise to the SEZ. $^{4}$ : important part of this mission is to demonstrate, by example, a well managed enterprise.

Shenzhen University's need for a comprehensive MIS

University officials identified three reasons for proceeding with the development of a comprehensive MIS:

\- We live in the age of the “three C’s”: Communication, Computers, and Control. The university must create a “three-C’s” environment for faculty, students, administrators, and support staff. Information is an important resource that must be managed. To be freed from routine collecting, processing, and delivering of information is important, even necessary.

\- The SEZ is a window through which China communicates with the outside world. Managing the university with efficient, scientific, modern techniques is especially important in this context to prepare students for the requirements of the SEZ. Therefore, university officials are enthusiastic about MIS.

\- Some prerequisites for an effective MIS had already been met.

Shenzhen University, being new, has fewer employees than other universities in China. The employee-student ratio is one to ten, contrasting sharply with the usual one to three at other universities: Thus consideration of the alternative of using computers instead of hiring personnel is possible.

Some computerized management systems already existed, such as comprehensive management systems for the library, management of faculty, student registration, and financial management of the university. These existing systems proved their worth in managing the university, but there were limitations. First, there were no data or message exchanges among these systems. Second, there was no sharing of data. Visiting different systems might be necessary in order to get summary or comprehensive information. Third, since data could not be shared, the same data were stored in different systems, resulting in redundancy. Fourth, there was no consistency among systems. Information from different systems was not always consistent or available in a timely manner. In other words, These were many of the same problems as experienced in most modern societies in the past few years. Creation of a comprehensive MIS seemed to offer solutions to these problems.

## System design criteria

In the design of the campus MIS, there were five basic problems to address:

1. Definition of system requirements.

2. Standardization of analysis tools and design and development procedures to ensure compatibility of all components.

3. Protection and sharing of data resources.

4. Network topology.

5. Criteria for software.

Three fundamental requirements for the system were identified: reliability, economic feasibility, and leading technology.

Choice of well established technology and proven hardware and software helps to ensure reliability. The 3COM 3 + LAN system was chosen for the network, and dBASE III Plus, supported by CC-DOS (Chinese Character DOS), for data base implementation. These products have proved to be popular and of high quality. Another requirement of reliability is security of data. The LAN provides password protection, assignment of specific disk partitions, and different levels of access rights. The LAN's SFT/DM (System Fault Tolerant/Disk Mirroring) provides backup and recovery capability by automatically copying data in one partition into another partition. When one partition fails to work properly, the system uses the other partition.

The data base management system of choice was dBASE III Plus (multi-user version). It supports network implementation, can process Chinese characters, and is efficient in programming and ease of use. It also enables the user to take part in the design and implementation of the system after some training, filling the gap between system programmer and user.

To ensure economic feasibility, hardware and software were selected, based on compatibility and quality rather than on brand names. Campus computing facilities included an IBM 3032 with 70 remote terminals, a MICRO-VAX II, and more than 100 IBM- and IBM/AT-compatible personal computers. The MIS hardware and software must share compatibility with existing hardware and software. MIS components must be easily and economically expandable to ensure long-term system feasibility.

The 3COM 3+ LAN and dBASE III Plus, being leading products in the LAN market, satisfy the criterion of leading technology. The LAN is easily connected to the IBM 3032 and MICROVAX II and is IBM/AT-compatible. The modular design of the LAN allows for easy and economical expansion of the network. The LAN hardware also includes an optical Fiber Data Distribution Interface (FDDI) to allow use of optical fiber technology in the near future. A laser printer and a PC-FAX are also installed in the system.

## Development time and cost requirements

At the beginning of 1988 the MIS Development Office was set up with a project development team of 30 faculty who came from different parts of the university (such as the University Computing Center, the Electronic Engineering Department, and the Office of the President). One of the vice-presidents was the team leader. Most of these faculty were part-time.

The guiding philosophy of the project development team included the following points:

\- The MIS must serve the needs of its users.

\- The MIS must be versatile and adaptable as users' needs change and software and hardware technologies advance.

\- The MIS must be continually developing as users conceive of new applications.

\- A perfect, finished MIS is not possible. Continued planning, refining, fine tuning, and expanding are essential.

In 1988–89, the LAN was set up with about 80 workstations. During that time, members of the team also finished the data base specifications and application software. The MIS became operational in early 1989.

The total cost was a million ren-min-bi yuan (about US\$270,000). The funds were provided by the university from a budgetary item termed Device and Maintenance Money for which the local government provides funds every year. It is usually used to buy new equipment for educational and scientific research purposes. About onefourth of a year's device and maintenance money was given to the MIS project.

The time period was short and the cost low compared with most MIS projects. The primary reasons for success were (1) a clear and concrete objective and (2) a well managed team effort.

## General description of the MIS

The MIS includes many subsystems covering almost every aspect of university management. Brief descriptions of the subsystems follow.

Electronic Mail. This is one of 3COM's software products available on the LAN. It readily accommodates Chinese, and even has registered mail and broadcasting functions.

Finance Management System. This is one of the most successful systems. The Finance Management Office uses it to keep account of the university's finances and for general accounting. What formerly required the efforts of several people for several days can now be accomplished in several minutes by the FMS. It manages the monthly payroll for university personnel, prepares financial reports for the president, and maintains the money account with the bank.

Teaching Affairs System. Student records and information about curriculum, scheduling, and enrollments are stored in this system. Departments are required to enter students' grade records into the system. (This system performs functions similar to those of enrollment systems in North America.)

Student Job Appointment Guide System. Graduating students can find information about companies in the SEZ, personnel needs, salaries, and so forth. When a student gets a job, the system continues to collect and update information about the student.

Device and Maintenance System. This maintains an inventory of all university equipment for teaching and research and equipment maintenance schedules and records.

Comprehensive Inquiry System. This system provides information for orienting newcomers to the campus.

President's Inquiry System. This allows access to all other systems in the MIS to provide the president with any information, including sensitive information, needed for management purposes.

Science and Technology Research Management System. This system includes all records related to funds for research and their management.

Sports Activities Management System. This maintains records on all intramural sports and activities. It assigns people to teams, matches teams, and keeps results.

Environment Protection and Safety System. This system maintains records on losses and damages related to university facilities, lost and found articles, and student and faculty records for law enforcement purposes.

Management System for the Communist Party. This maintains party rosters and records of hours spent in party activities, including the study of theory.

## Personnel and training requirements

Training of personnel for the MIS falls into two categories: users and programmers.

Users must learn how to operate and use the MIS. All management and related personnel are required to have this training, including the president, vice-presidents, and deans of departments. The training program takes about 20 hours, including 10 hours of hands-on practice with the system. Users must have an understanding of the basic structure and principles of the personal computer and the LAN: be able to log on and off using different applications subsystems, acquire the information they need, and obtain a hard copy of the information.

One to three persons in every department receive training as programmers. Besides their training as users, they must have the ability to do simple maintenance on and functional expansions of relevant application subsystems. Training comprises 40 hours of instruction, including 15 hours of hands-on practice with the system.

The training curriculum includes the following courses:

1. An introduction to the MIS.

2. Operation and applications of dBASE III Plus.

3. Operation and applications of WordStar 2000 (Chinese character version).

4. Introduction to the functions and operation of the ETHERNET LAN.

## Effectiveness of the MIS

The MIS has been in use about one year. It has proven to be a successful system. Shenzhen University's president once said, "If the MIS goes out, we can do no work." However, the MIS is not without operational problems: some deal with data entry, integrity, and security. In general, departments are responsible for entering and updating data in the appropriate system. Faculty and department deans do not always take this responsibly seriously. Some departments still have not entered the required data at all, and some do not enter and update their data in a timely manner. Some faculty let untrained persons enter student records into the Teaching Affairs System, including grades, and this produces inaccuracies and problems with security.

Such problems are not the fault of the hardware and software design: they are more procedural than theoretical. We must strengthen people's view of the MIS and their respective responsibilities.

## Plans for future development

Plans are in place to improve and expand the MIS. Some systems need continual or periodic functional expansion and modification to meet changes in requirements, functions, and environment.

Procedures must be improved to overcome the problems of data entry, integrity, and security; but they will not be effective if not enforced. Therefore, procedures for enforcement are also required.

Effectiveness of the MIS will be increased with the addition of a Decision Support System (DSS).

As the number of users increases, there will be a strong necessity of high-speed, wide-band data transmission. The 3COM 3 + LAN alone cannot meet this requirement. The FDDI to connect optical fiber cable is already in place to facilitate conversion. The following are specific plans for the near future:

\- Connect the university mainframe computer to the LAN so that personal computer workstations can be imaged as terminals of the mainframe system.

\- Develop a decision support system.

\- Implement a ring network architecture to improve the performance of the LAN.

\- Increase the speed and capacity of communication channels with optical fiber technology.

The eventual goal is a system that includes several LAN's. Each LAN will have its own server and a relatively small number of users. Communication among the LAN's will be by a ring network of optical fiber cable. These enhancements will provide for increased usage without degradation of the performance of the MIS.
