# A comparison of business school computer usage in Europe and North America \*

Gert Bechlund and Niels Bjørn-Andersen
Copenhagen Business School, Copenhagen, Denmark

Ephraim R. McLean

Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA

Building upon the successful series of studies of business school computing usage in North America conducted by UCLA, this paper reports on a parallel study on computer usage in European business schools conducted by the Copenhagen Business School in 1987. Although there are a number of differences between the two samples, there are also many similarities. In fact, the overwhelming conclusion is that computer usage on the two continents has now reached parity, with both facing the common problem of attempting to improve the computing resources available to the least fortunate schools while at the same time solidifying the progress made at the top schools.

Keywords: Computer usage; Business schools.

## 1. Introduction

Business schools' use of computers for research and for educating future managers plays a major role in determining the speed, quality, and direction of computer usage in industry. The interest of computer vendors and their extensive grants for new equipment bears witness to this fact. This use of business schools as display windows for future business usage of computers makes it especially interesting to follow the extent to which this equipment, software packages, local area networks, etc. are used.

To identify the characteristics of business school computer usage, an annual survey of North American business schools has been carried out by the Graduate School of Management at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) for several years $[1,2]$ . In late 1987, this survey was also conducted by the Copenhagen Business School (CBS) in Denmark, covering European business schools and other European schools offering degrees and conducting research on business subjects. Both of these latter groups will be referred to as “European business schools.”

This paper provides a comparison of computer usage in the business-research and business-teaching institutions on these two continents.

## 2. Data collection

All academic institutions of higher education in Western Europe mentioned in the publication The Book of Learning [3] that appeared to have research professors and/or education in business subjects were identified; a one-page questionnaire was mailed to each to identify a contact person. Approximately 50 replies were received. These were used for updating the name-and-address list, but no institution was deleted because of nonresponse. In mid-July of 1987, 327 full questionnaires were mailed to this expanded list. They were virtually identical to the 1987 UCLA questionnaire, with slight adjustments for the European environment.

Reminders, with an extra copy of the questionnaire, were mailed in September 1987. By the end of the 1987, a total of 43 questionnaires had been received. These form the basis for the European sample, referred to as the CBS sample, while the North American sample will be termed the UCLA sample. Table 1 lists the European business schools that participated in this study. For comparison purposes, all budgets and fees are stated in European Currency Units (ECUs). Also, schools are listed by country, and alphabetically within country. $^{1}$

The response rate (13%) for the CBS schools was disappointingly low. However, there are a number of possible reasons. Firstly, one should note that the total population of “real” business schools (including economics and other doing research in business and/or offering degrees in business) is probably substantially less than the 327 schools that originally received the questionnaire. One has to realize that there is no formal accreditation of business schools in Europe as there is in North America, and the structure of research and education differs from country to country. The CBS sample includes a diversity of institutions, from fairly new, highly specialized schools offering only graduate degrees in close cooperation with industry, to those that are part of 600-year-old universities, to full scale universities having more than 10,000 students.

Secondly, in comparing the CBS sample with the UCLA sample, it should be noted that this was the first such study ever carried out in Europe, while the UCLA study, conducted four times, had already established its credibility and usefulness.

Thirdly, in spite of the integration efforts within the European Community, there is still a lack of coherence in Europe. Every country has its own research and educational policies. Financing of higher education is different from country to country. Each institution is first and foremost a national one, and competition is almost exclusively within each country. Until recently, interest in pan-European comparisons have been rare.

Finally, it is possible that even though modifications were made in order to match the European situation, the questionnaire, with its language (English) and 145 questions on systems, packages, and networks, may have been more difficult for Europeans to complete than for North Americans.

## 3. Respondent demographics

An examination of the demographics of the surveyed schools (see Table 2) shows some interesting differences between this European sample and the earlier American study [2]. Most European schools are public, with almost all their income coming from government sources. The other three categories reflect, to a large extent, the lower degree of homogeneity in the CBS sample. Almost half of the European institutions offer only undergraduate or graduate education, while almost all the North American schools offer both types of degrees. Perhaps for this reason, the European schools tend to be smaller than the North American schools; nearly 90% have less than 2000 students.

## 4. Spending patterns

In examining the spending patterns, the differences between continents are most apparent when comparisons are made by quartiles (see Figure 1). While spending for computing in schools in the lower quartiles is virtually identical for Europe and North America, the top quartile exhibits dramatic differences. The leading schools in the North American sample spend nearly double that of their European counterparts.

In terms of the sources of financial support for business school computing, the patterns are similar (see Table 3). Both hardware-and-software acquisitions and operations and maintenance have similar funding patterns with one exception. In North America, 14% of acquisitions and 4% of operating funds come from donations from the private sector; while in Europe, no such contributions were reported. As a result, the percentage of support provided by the various European governments are correspondingly higher.

## 5. Staff support

The human support for the use of computing shows remarkable differences between schools in the top quartile and those in the bottom (see Figure 2). For both European and North American schools in the highest quartile, there are approximately 100 students per FTE computer staff member, while schools in the lowest quartile have more than 1000 students served by each FTE staff member!

In comparing the two, it is interesting to note that the North American schools in the highest and lowest quartiles provide relatively more staff support than their European counterparts, while the reverse is true in the middle quartiles. Even though the interpretation of the European data should be treated with some care, due to the relatively low number of respondents, these numbers suggest that the difference between the “support-rich” and “support-poor” schools is larger in North America. This is all the more surprising if one assumes that the North American sample is more homogeneous; e.g., all are accredited, have well established curricula, are parts of large universities, etc.; and yet the ratio of resources (including computing staff) per student differs greatly across schools, exhibiting a larger variance than in the European data.

Beyond the totals, it is illuminating to examine the composition of this staff support. Figures 3A, 3B, and 3C show the technical, user, and management support personnel for the two samples. The European schools allocate a relatively higher portion of their staffs to technical work that North American schools, with a relatively lower percentage devoted to user support. ## 6. Equipment

The penetration of the use of mainframes into North American business schools took place roughly two to three years earlier than in Europe; but today, almost all business schools, except for two North American schools, have access to mainframes (see Figure 4). On the other hand, it is interesting to note that microcomputers were introduced on a large scale in Europe somewhat earlier, probably due to the lack of adequate mainframe facilities. Today, however, almost all schools on both continents report access to microcomputers. Specific types of computers are shown in Table 4 for minicomputers, and Table 5 for microcomputers.

In terms of numbers of microcomputers installed, the North American schools are clearly ahead, with the average North American business school having 129 microcomputers and the average European schools having only 60 (see Table 6). However, this wide disparity is more due to the relative sizes of the schools in each of the samples than to their relative commitments to computing.

In addition to asking the schools to report the number and types of microcomputers they have installed, the questionnaire also asked how satisfied they are with this equipment. In other words, do they like it and would they recommend it to others? The rating scale is 1 to 5, with 5 being the highest “strength of endorsement.” The standard deviation of the distribution of the responses is shown in parentheses.

## 6.1. Minicomputers

Not surprisingly, Digital Equipment, IBM, and Hewlett-Packard dominate the North American minicomputer scene, with a combined total of 70% of the installed base (see Table 4). ## 8. Software usage

Each of the schools was asked to identify the software packages that it used in each of nine categories. The results are shown in Tables 11A and 11B. The number of schools (“N”) reporting in each category is less than the total, because some schools did not report usage in some categories. Conversely, in a given category the number of packages identified may total more than the “N” because many schools reported using more than one package (e.g., WordPerfect and WordStar).

Although the levels of usage differ, the list of software packages for all computer applications is virtually identical for Europe and North America. To the extent that there are differences, they appear largely in the “Other” category. For instance, North American schools identified 32 different brands of word processing packages and European schools named 33. Thus, although there is great commonality, both continents are far from standardizing on a few packages.

In addition, the European schools were asked about their use of office systems, graphics, and artificial intelligence (AI). The responses indicate that, with few exceptions, no software package was mentioned by more than one school. Nine schools reported having some sort of office system on their mainframe, mostly centered around electronic mail. Graphic systems were mentioned by 17 schools, predominantly running PCs, but no two schools reported using the same package. Finally, 14 schools indicated that they were using artificial intelligence or expert systems packages; however, only ESP Advisor was mentioned by two schools.

## 10. Sources of courseware

Turning from the uses of courseware to the sources of courseware, Table 13 shows sources of undergraduate course software by classes. Because the numbers of European schools reporting is fairly small (less than a third of the North

American sample), the findings should be viewed cautiously.

Of the four sources – internal development, textbooks, commercial firms, and other universities – only the commercial firms column shows any similarity. North American schools make intensive use of this “free” software (although to a lesser extent than their use of commercial packages); while, with the exception of accounting courses, virtually no use is made of this source in Europe. ## 14. Summary and conclusions

What is most striking about the comparisons between North American and European business school computer usage is the fact that the similarities far outweigh the differences. Both sets of schools buy from the same hardware vendors (though in different proportions) and use the same software packages. It would be difficult to distinguish one set of numbers from another without the column headings. The only consistent difference is the smaller size of European schools, with about half the student population of their North American counterpart. Thus the larger number of microcomputers installed per school in North America (129 compared to 60) can be largely explained by school size.

Even the number of schools in Europe is smaller. The North American sample consisted of 128 AACSB-accredited schools, while the European sample consisted of only 43 schools, despite extensive efforts to locate and elicit the cooperation of more. In fact, without a central accreditation body, it is sometimes difficult to decide what is a “business school.” With substantial differences among countries and with nearly half of the European schools either undergraduate or graduate, the European sample is not only smaller but also much more heterogeneous than the corresponding North American sample.

In terms of types of equipment, the two use mainframe, minis, and micros in about the same proportions. North American schools began using mainframes or minis two to three years earlier than European schools, but Europe preceded North America slightly in the introduction of micros. About two-fifths of the schools have their own in-house minicomputers.

Not surprisingly, telecommunications is playing an increasingly larger role. Here the European schools are slightly ahead in having communication links in place, either through local area networks or direct connections to a mainframe. On the other hand, North American schools are making much greater use of wide area networks, particularly BITNET; considerably fewer European schools are using its European equivalent, EARNET.

European schools also lag in the penetration of computers into the core courses in the business curriculum. Over 60% of North American schools have a requirement of hands-on computer usage in some sections of their core courses, while only about half as many have this requirement in Europe. The use of computer courseware also shows a comparable disparity, with 56% of North American schools using it for their core courses but only 31% for European schools.

By grouping the schools from both samples into quartiles, a potentially troubling picture emerges. In both Europe and North America, the differences between the “haves” (top quartile) and the “have-nots” (bottom quartile) are dramatic, with the North American data being somewhat higher at both ends. Bottom quartile schools in North America spend \$10 per student per year on computing, have one microcomputer for every 80 students, and one staff support person for every 1100 students. Top quartile schools, on the other hand, spend \$500 per student per year, have one microcomputer for every 12 students, and one staff support person for every 65 students. In Europe, the picture is equally divergent, with orders of magnitude differences between the top and the bottom.

It should be emphasized that the preceding are not comparisons between the richest and poorest schools, but between the average of the top quartile and the average of the bottom quartile. As a matter of educational – indeed national – policy, such extreme differences merit careful attention. Without significant efforts to improve the situation in the lower quartiles, a substantial number of graduates will be ill prepared to enter the marketplace and compete in the decade ahead.
