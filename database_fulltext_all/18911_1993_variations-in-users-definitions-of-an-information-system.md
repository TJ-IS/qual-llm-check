# Variations in users' definitions of an information system

Kieran Mathieson

Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309-4401, USA

Information systems (IS) are complex and abstract. When users are asked to evaluate “the system,” there is no guarantee that they will all be thinking about the same object. Laboratory research has shown that differences in the way an IS is defined affect users’ evaluations of the system. If such variations exist in the field (that is, if the term “the system” invokes different images in the minds of different users), they might affect user evaluations. This paper reports a study of variations in users’ definitions of a human resource information system. There were significant variations in opinions about what the IS was. Most users were certain that their definitions were correct, even though they disagreed with one another. Users with more IS-related expertise included fewer objects in the system than less expert users. The findings have implications for MIS research and management. In particular, developers should provide each user with a definition of an IS from the time of their first contact with the system. The definition should be aligned with the organizational responsibilities of the developers.

Keywords: Variation; Difference; Definition; Evaluation; Opinion; Users; Case study; Survey; HRIS; Expertise.

Kieran Mathieson is an Assistant Professor of MIS at Oakland University. He received his doctorate from Indiana University. His research focuses on the manner in which beliefs about information systems are formed, and the factors individuals consider when deciding whether to use information systems.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) are complex, abstract objects. Large IS have many physical and abstract components, including computers, peripherals, programs, data sets, processes and manual procedures. While the complexity of a modern system can be daunting for an IS professional, it can be overwhelming for a user.

People share definitions of some abstract objects. For example, the meaning of “three” is standardized to the extent that someone using a different definition would be considered, at best, eccentric. The same is true for some IS-related objects. For example, most people use the same definition of the spreadsheet Excel. However, many custom-built systems are not clearly defined. Consider a “Sales IS” that tracks customer orders for a firm. It is possible that not everyone would agree on what the Sales IS is. Does it include data entry? Does it include the programmers who built the system? Does it include the terminals used to access the system?

Given this ambiguity, it is possible that different users will define systems in different ways.

Fig. 1. Different definitions of an information system.

Figure 1 shows that users might disagree about which organizational components are part of a given IS and which are not. Such definitional variations could have several effects. First, they could hinder communication between different people. For example, if an analyst and a user are talking about “the sales information system,” they may be attaching the same label to different objects.

Second, definitional variations could lead to differences in inferences about a system. Perhaps the most important of these, from the system analyst's viewpoint, is the user's evaluation of an IS. User evaluations of systems affect not only user behavior (e.g., Goodhue [12], Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [7], and Mathieson [15]), but may also affect their evaluations of the system's developers. For example, suppose a user believes that his or her terminal is part of the Sales IS. A terminal failure could contribute to a negative evaluation of the Sales IS and its developers. In fact, the system's developers might not have been involved in the selection of the terminal and have had no control over its maintenance or use. The user incorrectly blames the developers, however, because of the way he or she defines the Sales IS.

There are many evaluation instruments measuring users' satisfaction with specific objects (such as reports), individual systems, and the general IS environment. Examples include Gallagher [11], Bailey and Pearson [1], Ives, Olson and Baroudi [13], Jenkins and Ricketts [14], and Torkzadeh and Doll [21]. A typical evaluation instrument includes items such as "Is the system accurate?", "Is the system easy to use?", and "Does the system provide up-to-date information?" ([21], p. 30). These questions require users to decide, at least implicitly, what "the system" is. However, as noted above, "the system" may be an ambiguous object, even when a specific label such as "the Sales IS" is used.

There is evidence that the beliefs and attitudes people form about an object are affected by the way they define the object. Mathieson [16] showed that an individual's definition of an IS interacts with his or her evaluation of each component to affect the individual's evaluation of the entire system. For example, suppose two users interact with the IS diagrammed in Figure 1. They both agree that the database and report programs are of high quality, but that the sales data entry group is poor. One user feels that the data entry group is part of the Sales IS, and so has mixed feelings about the system. The other user feels that the data entry group is not part of the Sales IS, and so evaluates the system positively. The users form different opinions about the system, despite the fact that they agree about each of its components.

Notice that the concern here is with the users' definitions of the IS, since it is the users who complete evaluation instruments. As far as this research is concerned, the system developers' definitions of the IS are irrelevant. Most users are not systems developers. The "correct" definition of the IS, as given by a systems theorist (e.g., [5]), is also irrelevant. Most users are not exposed to systems theory. Attempts to provide standard definitions for terms such as "IS" (e.g., [3]) are also irrelevant. Most users have not read this material.

What is important is the image that users have in mind when responding to an item such as “Is the Sales IS reliable?” They must decide which information to take into account when making a judgement. While information about some objects (such as the user’s supervisor) is obviously not applicable, the importance of other information is less clear. For example, the individual has to decide whether information about his or her terminal is relevant. Whether this decision is made when the evaluation instrument is administered, or when the events that provide information about the terminal occurred, the decision must be made. The user may not be consciously aware of making the decision, but by choosing which of his or her experiences to take into account when forming an evaluation, the decision is still made.

The problem of definitional variation is not restricted to MIS. In the political science literature, Conover and Feldman [6] argue that the terms “liberal” and “conservative” have different meanings for different people. Some of the variance captured by an item that asks voters to rate themselves on the liberal/conservative dimension may be caused by variations in definitions across the sample.

This paper reports a study of variations in users' definitions of an IS. The main objective of the research was simply to find out whether significant definitional variations exist in the field. If not, the issue is of little practical interest, despite laboratory work showing that definitional variations affect evaluations ([16]). The focus was on “membership beliefs,” defined here as a user’s belief about whether an object is part of an IS. For example, a user might believe that his or her terminal is part of the Sales IS, while the programmers who developed the system are not.

The second objective of the study was to examine the certainty with which definitional beliefs were held. Certainty is important for two reasons. First, behavior is affected more by beliefs held with certainty than by beliefs that are less certain [9]. Definitional variations might only exist among people who are very uncertain of their beliefs. If so, such variations may have little impact in practice. However, if users are certain of their definitional beliefs, variations may be of more significance. The second reason for investigating certainty is that beliefs held with certainty are more difficult to change than beliefs held with less certainty [9]. If users' beliefs about system definitions are uncertain and easily changed, it might be possible for analysts to simply tell the users what system definition they would like the users to apply. However, if users are certain about their beliefs, this will prove more difficult. This issue will be discussed in more detail later.

The third objective of the research was to examine the relationship between IS expertise and system definitions. It was noticed during the pilot study that people with more IS-related experience tended to use different definitions from novices. If there is a systematic difference in the way novices and experts define an IS, this might account for differences in experts' and novices' evaluations of the same system.

## 2. Methodology

The study focused on the users of a university's human resources IS (HRIS). First, the system is described and the pilot study discussed. The measures used in the study are presented, followed by an explanation of the survey procedure.

## A. System description

The HRIS is a typical mainframe-based information system, with transaction processing and management reporting functions. It queries a database containing information on employees, positions, assignments and appointments. It runs on a large IBM mainframe and is accessed through terminals and PCs. Users navigate from one data screen to another. For example, given an employee identification number, users can enter the employee screen and then move to the position screen. Employee identification numbers can be accessed through a name search function. The data for this function comes from a file that is stored separately from the HRIS database.

## B. Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted to pretest instruments described in the next section. Fifteen users from various departments were interviewed and asked to rate their familiarity with nineteen different objects. Seven objects that were familiar to the users were selected for the main instrument. The objects were:

1. Position enquiry - a data screen.

2. Employee enquiry - a data screen.

3. The name search file - provides access to data via employee name.

4. The terminal or PC used to access the HRIS.

5. The student records database.

6. The programmers who wrote the HRIS programs.

7. The staff of the university's payroll office.

It was expected that users would agree that the first two objects were part of the HRIS, but that there would be variations for the other five.

First, respondents were asked to indicate the number of other systems they were using besides the HRIS. Second, a 10-item scale developed by Cheney and Nelson [4] was used to measure IS ability. Each item asked respondents to rate their skill in a number of areas from 1 (very low ability) to 5 (very high ability). The responses were averaged for each individual.

## 3. Validity scale

A three-item validity scale was included with the ability instrument. The validity scale identified subjects who either did not read the items carefully or who provided inaccurate information. The validity items used the same format as the ability items. Users rated their ability in three non-existent areas: (1) “Use dynamic compression software (e.g., FAS, Dy-Comp II),” (2) “Use network recognition systems (e.g., NWork, XWR),” and (3) “Use division indexing techniques.” Responses were averaged to obtain a validity score ranging from 1 (very low ability with the non-existent systems) to 5 (very high ability with the non-existent systems).

## 3. Results

The survey was mailed to all 153 users of the HRIS. A reminder was mailed two weeks later.

Ninety-two usable replies were received, for a response rate of 60%. Table 2 shows a frequency distribution for the validity scale. Respondents with an average score greater than “Low” on the non-existent skills (corresponding to a value of 2) were removed from the sample. This eliminated 11 observations. Another respondent failed to complete the ability instrument, so a validity score could not be calculated. In the interests of conservatism, this subject was also dropped. A total of 12 respondents, or 13% of the sample, failed to pass the validity test.

One respondent stated that Position Enquiry was not part of the HRIS, while another stated that Employee Enquiry was not part of the system. Their responses were not reasonable according to the judgements of other users, all of whom agreed that these objects were part of the HRIS. Their responses might reflect a misunderstanding of the items rather than a true difference in definition. We decided to exercise caution and eliminate these responses from further analysis (re-analysis including these observations did not materially affect the results). The single respondent who believed that Name Search was not part of the HRIS was not eliminated, since a very knowledgeable user might know that name search data was taken from outside the HRIS database. This left 78 observations in the data set. There was some missing data, so the sample sizes listed in the tables below vary. Table 3 gives the means and standard deviations of all variables.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

The results confirm that definitional variations exist for the HRIS. Most respondents were sure of their opinions, even though they disagreed with one another. This is consistent with literature on the false consensus effect ([19]), showing that individuals often overestimate the extent to which others share their perceptions. As discussed earlier, beliefs held with certainty are (1) more likely to affect behavior and (2) relatively difficult to change. Definitions seemed to be related to IS experience and ability. Experts tended to include fewer objects in the HRIS than novices. However, it was overall experience that was important, not experience with the HRIS itself.

Several possible implications of definitional variations were mentioned earlier. Consider these in the light of the results, particularly the finding that variations appear to be related to experience and ability. First, boundary variations could lead researchers to draw invalid conclusions from empirical results. For example, Nichols and O'Connor [17] found that “Technical users are less satisfied with MIS than administrative users” (p. 479). Perhaps their findings partially reflected systematic differences in the way technical users (who are relative experts) and administrative users (who are relative novices) defined systems.

Second, definitional variations could hinder communication between users and analysts. Systems analysts typically have more IS expertise than users, so they may have more restrictive system definitions. Before discussing an IS, perhaps analysts and users should first agree on what the system is. Although tools such as data dictionaries provide standard definitions for data elements, standard definitions of systems might also be useful.

Third, definitional variations complicate the interpretation of users' evaluations of an IS. This is because the target of the evaluation is defined differently by different people. For some people, the evaluation may be influenced by events that are not only beyond the control of analysts, but are not related to their work at all. Before decisions are made based on these evaluations, the exact cause of dissatisfaction should be determined. It may be that the problem is not with the IS, but with some object in the system's environment. Similarly, user evaluations of IS personnel should be treated with caution. The evaluations may not accurately reflect the IS group's true performance, since users may not be familiar with the limits of the IS group's responsibilities.

It will be difficult to overcome definitional variations by simply presenting users with a definition at the time of evaluation and asking them to evaluate the newly defined object. The way in which overall evaluations are formed will make this procedure ineffective. Users, particularly very experienced users, integrate a large amount of information in forming evaluations. The information is not necessarily processed at the time at which the evaluation is requested. Much of the information will be processed at the time at which the events that generated the information occur. For example, suppose that in January a user has problems with his or her terminal. The user thinks that the terminal is part of the Sales IS, so the event affects the user's belief about the reliability of the system. In June, a questionnaire gives the user a definition of the Sales IS that does not include the terminal, and then asks the user to evaluate the system's reliability. Although the user can recall his or her overall judgement of the Sales IS, he or she cannot necessarily recall all of the events that influenced this opinion, such as the incident in January. The user cannot simply generate a new judgement based on the new definition, since the specific information that would be needed is unavailable.

There is support for this idea in the literature. According to current theory, people often store summary evaluations in memory separately from the beliefs that led to them ([18]). This is an efficient strategy, since the individual can simply recall the overall evaluation, rather than having to infer it each time it is needed. However, this means that a change in beliefs about how an IS is defined will not automatically lead to a change in the overall evaluation of the system.

This suggests that, when user evaluations are important to a system's developers, the developers ensure that each user has a consistent definition of the IS from the time of the individual's first contact with the system. Users will then be able to apply the definition to understand their own experience. Whether this would be effective is a theoretical question. It is important to align the system definition with the developers' responsibilities. For example, if data entry is the responsibility of the sales department, make it clear to the users that the system boundary excludes data entry. If printed reports are sent from the machine room to the mail room for distribution, make sure that users are aware of this.

There are many opportunities for further research. First, there are unanswered questions about the generalizability of the findings. Only a single IS in one organization was studied. The results should be confirmed in other settings. Second, the way in which system definitions are formed could be investigated. Ideas from the sociology of knowledge $[2]$ , organization behavior $[20]$ , and communication $[10]$ might be useful. For example, after the installation of an IS, users might all share a common view of the system instilled during training. Over time, definitions may become more idiosyncratic as users are exposed to different information within the social-and task-related structure of their individual departments.

To summarize, IS are complex, abstract objects. There are differences in the way people define systems. Even users who are certain about their definitions disagree with one another. Users with more IS-related expertise adopt more restrictive system definitions than those with less expertise.
