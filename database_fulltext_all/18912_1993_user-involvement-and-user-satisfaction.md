# User involvement and user satisfaction

Kwasi Amoako-Gyampah and Kathy B. White

University of North Carolina - Greensboro, Greensboro, NC, USA

User involvement is of interest to researchers because of its potential impact on the success of systems. This paper examines some contingencies of user involvement and user satisfaction (a surrogate for system success). Using the results from a survey questionnaire, we show that user perception of level of involvement has a direct, positive and significant impact on user satisfaction. The results also indicate that managers should become aware of the nature of the interactions that occur among user groups and system development groups. We show that user perception of the system development group's attitude toward involvement has a positive effect on user satisfaction.

Keywords: User involvement; User satisfaction; System development; System success; Project team; Contingency model; Project team attitudes; User-project team interaction.

## 1. Introduction

The impact of user involvement in system development and implementation is still an issue of considerable debate. Involving users in systems development and implementation has sometimes been found to be positively related to system success (however measured $[2]$ ), and other times has not been found to be related at all. In their review of previous research on user involvement and MIS design and implementation, Ives and Olson $[10]$ noted that the benefits of user involvement had not been convincingly demonstrated and thus more research on user involvement is useful.

Most studies have focused on the involvement of the users at the system initiation and design stages without considering the extent or impact of involvement at the implementation stage. This is perhaps due to the perception that user involvement is more critical during the early stages of system development when commitment of resources is being sought and that involvement during the implementation phase might be less crucial to success.

It has also been observed that the impact and appropriateness of user involvement depends on the type of system being developed. For certain

In particular, Kim and Lee [13] noted that user participation may be more appropriate for complex systems during the feasibility and information analyses stages. Tait and Vessey [26] reported, from a study of user involvement in 42 projects, that increased user involvement reduces the risk of system failure in complex projects. Hirschheim [9] also observed that more user participation was undertaken by organizations when the systems were complex.

Mann and Watson [16] noted that no empirical link has been established between the information requirements of the system and the involvement of the users in its development. They argue that the value of user involvement might depend on the characteristics of the information systems being developed; for example, the task complexities. And Edstrom [4] noted that the need for user involvement is higher when the systems being designed involve low or unstructured tasks. It is therefore worthwhile pursuing studies that contribute towards the understanding of the phenomenon and also help managers understand the role of user involvement in the various stages of systems development.

This paper examines through a field study a contingency model of user involvement, user satisfaction (a surrogate for system success), and four antecedent variables of user involvement.

## 2. User involvement in systems development - theoretical background

User involvement has potential impact on the success of systems. In particular, user involvement during system design has been predicted to improve system quality by:

1. Providing a more accurate and complete assessment of user information requirements [17];

2. Providing expertise about the organization that the system is going to support; this is usually not held by the information systems group [15];

3. Avoiding development of unacceptable or unimportant features;

4. Improving user understanding of the system.

In addition, during implementation, user involvement is thought to lead to decreased user resistance $[1]$ , decreased implementation sabotage, greater user acceptance, usage and satisfaction with the system $[23]$ , more perceived control on the part of the users, development of a sense of ownership, etc. The work of Robey and Farrow $[20]$ indicates that the participation of users during early stages can lead to greater perceived influence during later stages. Based on this review, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1A: There is a positive and significant correlation between user involvement and user satisfaction.

H1B: Users' satisfaction with an MIS system will be directly affected by their perception of the extent of their involvement.

Support for these hypotheses requires examination of the questions: What affects user involvement? How can managers use it in the development of systems?

A major contribution toward understanding user involvement is the development of contingency models. These $[5]$ $[7]$ have been developed to relate the roles of users in development to system success and serve as a framework for organizing knowledge. Some of the factors examined in previous contingency models include:

1. Top management support and commitment [3]

2. User attitudes [22]

3. Source of system origination [18]

4. System complexity

5. The supporting function of the MIS [16]

6. The resource constraints

7. User ownership and responsibility for system

8. User leadership roles.

## 3. Model development

User involvement in systems development and implementation has been studied largely through perceptual measures. For example, all the studies reviewed by Ives and Olson that used self-rated questionnaires were based on perceptual measures. User perceptions of level of involvement in systems development may be influenced by the nature of the interaction with other groups during development, by previous experience, and also by user expectations. It has been argued that the quality of the interaction between users and system developers is a key factor in determining system outcome [4] [21].

In the design and implementation of complex systems, users frequently interact with the project team, the project manager, and the MIS group. Their perception of level of involvement may be influenced by interactions with these groups. For example, Ginzberg [6] viewed the implementation of systems as an interaction process between users and designers and noted that the quality of the implementation outcome depends on how well interaction is managed. And Vertinsky et al. [27] posited that the quality of user-designer interaction will affect the success of system implementation. Other similar studies can be found in the literature [e.g., 24].

Most of the studies on the examination of user-designer interactions have focused on factors such as the attitude of the user towards the system being developed and towards the MIS group [19]. A further contribution can be made by examining the attitude of the system development groups towards the users and their involvement. For example, Ives and Olson suggested that the attitude of the systems designer towards the users could serve as a rich source for examining the role of user involvement on MIS success. We propose the following hypotheses:

H2A: The perceptions of the users regarding the attitude of the project team toward user involvement will be positively correlated with perceived user involvement.

H2B: Users' perception of their satisfaction with the system will be influenced by their perception of the attitude of the project team towards user involvement.

A key role of project managers in achieving project success is successful coordination of the activities of all the key groups. To achieve this requires effective communication. The appointment of steering committees, frequent indepth meetings between the project manager and the groups, the distribution of project meeting minutes and progress reports have been used for this purpose. However, the effectiveness of the communication techniques is highly dependent on the communication abilities of the project manager: this will affect user perception of the extent of involvement. Further, if users believe that the project leader sees their input as critical to the system's success, satisfaction will increase. We therefore propose these hypotheses:

H3A: Users' perception of the nature of their interaction with the project manager will be positively correlated with their perception of their level of involvement in systems development.

H3B: Users' perception of the nature of their interaction with the project manager will have a direct and positive effect on their satisfaction with the system.

In addition to the project team and the project manager, users frequently interact with the MIS group during the stages of systems development. This is particularly true when the role of information systems is being redefined. The extent of communication between MIS team and the users and their perceptions of the capability of the MIS team in fulfilling the IS role will affect the perception of their level of involvement and satisfaction. Further, users sometimes see a new system as a threat to take over their work, an intrusion into their world, a criticism of their work, or even that the system will add to their work load $[11]$ . Efforts of the MIS group directed towards minimizing any perceived threat or lack of understanding could contribute to user satisfaction with the system. The following hypotheses are proposed:

H4A: Users' interaction with the MIS team will be positively correlated with perceived user involvement.

H4B: Users' evaluation of the MIS team will have a significant effect on their satisfaction with the system.

Users' expectations about their involvement may be affected by their seeing well developed written plans for the development. These plans might include: the project mission and goals, plans for involving the users, the level of training to be provided, etc. Further, it is often thought that the existence of well developed plans encourages user involvement, especially for user-managers. However, Doll reported significant negative correlations between the existence of overall plans and user management participation. Thus, we need more studies investigating such an association. Accordingly, we propose:

H5A: The existence of an overall written plan for systems development will lead to an increase in the perception of user involvement.

H5B: The existence of an overall written plan for systems development will have a direct impact on user satisfaction.

## 4. Research method

Data for this study was collected from a large U.S. based organization which recently implemented a new multi-million dollar information system which impacted 12 different departments and over 280 users at different geographical locations in the United States. To the extent that this study was confined to one organization it represents a single-system study. Precedence however exists in both the MIS [e.g., 28] and implementation literatures [e.g., 14] for single system studies.

In fact several of the studies in the review by Ives and Olson were single system studies. These have one major advantage: they allow the researcher to maintain better control over sample selection.

In our study, preliminary interviews were conducted with some users and managers in the organization, thereby allowing survey questions to be modified and tailored to the system. This improved user understanding of the questions and also increased the internal validity of the measurement instruments. One criticism of single-system studies is that the system itself may be inappropriate for evaluating the relationship between user involvement and development success. The system was, in fact, large and complex, costing over \$15 million. It impacted several departments in multiple locations and was designed for an unstructured and not-well-understood environment. The system was conceived as a means of enhancing the global competitiveness of the entire company, which had previously only used MIS for basic structured support functions. At the time of the study, the system had been in use for only four months.

The method of data gathering was through survey-questionnaires using previously validated and tested instruments. The use of such instruments and the testing of their reliability have been suggested as ways of overcoming the shortcomings of previous user involvement research. The following subsections describe the instrument sources for our questionnaire.

## 4.1. User satisfaction

The surrogate measure of system success in our study was user satisfaction and the instrument used was that from Baronas and Louis. It consisted of 9 items focusing on the accuracy, reliability, control, responsiveness, capabilities, etc. of the system. System usage has sometimes been used as a surrogate for system success; however in situations such as this, where usage is mandatory, system usage is not appropriate.

## 4.2. User involvement

The instrument for user involvement was developed from that of Schultz, Slevin and Pinto [25] and Hawk and Aldag [8]. It consisted of 8 items. The questions focus on user roles in setting objectives and providing input, the extent to which users are kept informed of changes and system progress, and the adequacy of training provided to the users.

## 4.3. Attitude of project manager

Users were asked to assess, using a seven point Likert-type scale, the perceived attitude of the project manager towards user involvement and the extent to which the project manager was considered the focal point of communication.

## 4.4. Attitude of project team

A two-item Likert-type scale was used to measure the attitude of the project team toward user involvement and the concern of the project team for the users.

## 4.5. Evaluation of MIS team

The instrument used to evaluate the MIS team required the users to rate, on a seven point Likert-type scale, the level of communication between the users and the MIS team and also user confidence in the team; i.e., could they effectively lead the system development effort.

## 4.6. Existence of a system plan

Users were asked to indicate their awareness of an overall plan for system development, and if so, their perception of the nature and value of this plan.

The attitudinal, perceptual, and evaluation measures were similar to those used by Baronas and Louis, and Tait and Vessey. The questionnaires were mailed to 60 randomly chosen manager and operator-users in the organization. The organization would not permit more than 60 of its employees to participate in the study. $^{1}$

## 5.1. The $A$ hypotheses

The zero-order correlations between the variables are shown in Table 3. The table indicates that user involvement is significantly correlated with user satisfaction, the surrogate for system success, and hence hypothesis H1A is supported. The correlation of 0.43 obtained is typical of other values from similar studies using self-reports [8]. The attitude of the project team towards users is significantly correlated with user involvement and H2A is supported. Users' perception of their interaction with the project manager is significantly correlated with user involvement; supporting hypothesis H3A. Users' evaluation of the MIS group is also significantly correlated with user involvement. The existence of an overall plan for system development is negatively (but not significantly) correlated with user involvement and hence hypothesis H5A is not supported.

## 5.2. Testing the B hypotheses and contingency model

To test the B hypotheses and the contingency model developed earlier, path analysis was used to assess the direct and indirect effects of each variable on user satisfaction. Path analysis is a technique that allows one to provide explanations of observed correlations by constructing models of cause and effect relations among variables. The indirect effects were assessed with user involvement as an intervening variable between the other variables and user satisfaction. The path analytic procedure involved conducting two multiple regression analyses. The first used satisfaction as the dependent variable with user involvement, perceived attitude of project team toward users, users' perception of project manager's attitude, existence of an organizational plan for system development, and evaluation of the MIS group as the independent variables. The second analysis had user involvement as the internal variable with the others (attitude, etc.) as the external variables. The model with its path coefficients is shown in Figure 2. The path coefficients are the standardized regression coefficients [12].

## 5.3. Modified contingency model

The results were somewhat surprising, considering the fact that interviews conducted before and after the administration of the survey questionnaires seemed to indicate a lack of positive feeling about user roles and user interactions with the various systems design and implementation groups. With the exception of the variable used to measure the existence of an organizational plan for systems development, all other variables had positive effects on user involvement. However, as noted above, these effects were not significant, indicating mixed reactions on the part of the users with regards to their interaction with the system development groups. We therefore propose that the users thought of the system development groups as a unit when considering their interactions with these groups during interviews, but that the users were not sophisticated enough to make distinctions between the subgroups when responding to the survey questions. We found it necessary to assess the effect (if any) that users' perception of the attitude of the whole system development group toward user involvement had on user involvement and satisfaction. Stated formally, the hypotheses were as follows:

H6A: Users' evaluation and perceptions of the attitude of the systems development team as a whole towards user involvement will be positively and significantly correlated with perceived user involvement.

H6B: Users' evaluation and perception of the systems development team with regards to user involvement will have a positive and significant effect on perceived user involvement.

H7A: Users' evaluation and perception of the systems development team as a whole regarding user involvement will be positive and significantly correlated with user satisfaction.

H7B: Users' evaluation and perception of the systems development team as whole toward user involvement will have a significant effect on user satisfaction.

As before, hypotheses H6A and H7A were tested through an examination of the zero-order correlation coefficients while the other hypotheses were tested through the development of a revised path analytic model. The individual items for the attitude of the project team measure, the evaluation of the MIS team, and the perception of the project team were all combined into one instrument, as reported in the literature [29]. This measure was called systems team evaluation. The Cronbach reliability alpha for this combined instrument was 0.73, which is considered acceptable for an exploratory measure.

## 6. Conclusions

Understanding user involvement and its role in systems development success requires examination of factors that may influence user perceptions of level of user involvement. We have examined some contingencies of user involvement and user satisfaction with systems.

The strongest relationship in each of the contingency models occurred between user involvement and user satisfaction. The results show that user perception of level of involvement has a direct positive and significant impact on user satisfaction. Thus, one more support for user involvement in system development is provided.

The results further indicate that managers should become aware of the nature of the interactions that occur among user groups and other systems development groups. During systems development, especially for large complex ones, users interact with different system development groups. These groups serve as change-agents to co-opt the users and create a sense of perceived involvement that can lead to user satisfaction.

The perceptions of the users and their evaluations on the nature of their interactions with the separate project groups were found to be positively associated with user involvement and user satisfaction. As indicated earlier, user involvement is a perceptual issue. If users believe that the project groups (including the project manager) want and value their input, they will perceive their involvement as high and therefore, they are more satisfied. Even if they are given the opportunity to participate but they have concerns regarding the attitude of the project teams, users will not feel positive about their level of involvement; hence their satisfaction with the system is likely to be reduced.

A significant impact on user involvement was observed when user perception of the attitudes of the project groups as a whole was combined into a single measure. Thus, user overall perception and interaction with various system development teams (as a whole) is more important than user perceptions of the separate project groups. Therefore, we lend further support to the finding that the quality of user-system development group interaction has a direct effect on user satisfaction through the intervening variable of user involvement. Consequently, it seems important for managers to foster positive attitudes and open communications between the users and other groups to assure users of the importance of their role in system development. Although most of the variables cannot be said to influence user involvement or user satisfaction directly, their effects on perceived user involvement are meaningful and worthy of further study.
