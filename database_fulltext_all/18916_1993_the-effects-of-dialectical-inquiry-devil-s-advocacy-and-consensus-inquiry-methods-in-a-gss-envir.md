# The effects of dialectical inquiry, devil's advocacy, and consensus inquiry methods in a GSS environment

Lai Lai Tung \* and Alan R. Heminger
Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, USA

Much research has been done in the past few years on Group Support Systems (GSS). Laboratory versions have been created and studies have reported improved group outcomes, when compared with similar manual sessions. More recently, commercial systems have been introduced. Most of these have been created around a common approach to group work, which can be characterized as consensus. However, research in the manual group literature suggests that a consensus approach does not always produce the best outcomes. Structured conflict may provide superior performance when the issues under consideration contain multiple or unclear underlying assumptions. This paper describes a preliminary experiment to compare a consensus based approach (C) to two different conflict based approaches, devil's advocacy (DA) and dialectical inquiry (DI). The study was intended to help determine whether or not consensus based approaches are always the best choice. Results found no differences among the three inquiry methods in terms of process or outcome. Possible reasons are discussed.

Keywords: Group decision support systems; GDSS; Group support systems; GSS, Dialectic inquiry; Devil's advocacy; Small groups.

A GSS has been described as “.. an interactive, computer based system which facilitates solution of unstructured problems by a set of decision makers working together as a group.” [12]. As such, they are designed to minimize or overcome process losses of manual meetings, while seeking to enhance process gains. While there is evidence in the literature to support the benefits of GSS $[11,14,20,34]$ , most of it does not directly address the varying processes that it can support. Thus, it is not clear what types of processes are best employed within a GSS for various types of groups and group tasks. Specifically, it is not clear whether conflict or consensus based approaches are likely to be preferable for particular sessions.

At the same time, research in the area of manually supported groups has demonstrated that conflict, if managed well, can improve decision quality in manual group processes $[15,17,19,29]$ . Evidence has shown that effectively managed conflict can often improve decision performance in ill-structured and highly uncertain environments, compared to non-conflict based approaches $[30]$ . This appears to be because conflict based approaches challenge and test underlying assumptions and inferences, leading to better solutions. It would be valuable to know if the results of this research with manual groups are directly applicable to GSS supported sessions.

To address this issue, we studied the use of two major conflict based group processes and compared them with a consensus based process in a GSS environment. The two methods are devil's advocacy (DA) and dialectical inquiry (DI).

DA is based on developing a position in a given area of interest, followed by generating criticisms of this set of recommendations and its assumptions $[24]$ . Typically, a DA implementation might involve dividing a working group into two sub-groups. The first develops assumptions and strategies to address a given problem. The other, (the devil's advocate group), does not take part in this process. Instead, after the assumptions and strategies are developed, it strives to find fault with them. After listening to the devil's advocates present their case, the first group re-thinks its position in light of any perceived valid criticism, modifying its assumptions and recommendations as seem appropriate. Then, the devil's advocates again criticizes the result. This process can proceed interactively until each side agrees to accept the outcome.

DI uses debates based on diametrically opposed sets of recommendations and assumptions to surface important issues for the group's consideration [3,18]. A typical use of this technique might involve dividing a larger group into two subgroups. One would surface a set of working assumptions and the set of recommendations that follow from them. The second would be assigned to assume counter-assumptions and follow them through to a different set of recommendations. Then, an extended debate would be held by the two groups, with each supporting its position. At the end of the debate, the entire group would be asked to reach agreement on which assumptions and strategies had carried the day.

The consensus approach (C) focuses on creating a shared understanding of the problem and its preferred solution. Rather than dividing the larger group into adversarial sub-groups, everyone works together through a common process to solve the group's problem. Emphasis is placed on shared understanding, with the GSS technology being used to make information available to all. Typically, process facilitation is employed to help the group work smoothly together. It is this model that is frequently implemented in reported GSS sessions [22].

Both DA and DI have demonstrated that they can help decision makers avoid some of the negative effects of consensus based (C) approaches to decision making in manual meetings. Among these negative effects are groupthink $[21,33]$ , avoidance of uncertainty, and prematurely smoothing over conflict $[1]$ . On the other hand, there may be some negative effects of conflict based approaches. In a laboratory experiment comparing noncomputerized DA, DI and C approaches, group members using conflict based approaches experienced lower satisfaction with the group process, lower acceptance of the group decision, and possible negative long term effects on their working relationships with others. Conflicts may lead to negative outcomes, such as individuals feeling defeated and demeaned, increased distance between people, suspicion and distrust, and a breaking down of teamwork $[23]$ . To counteract these potential problems, a number of strategies have been suggested. These include:

(1) the use of post hoc discussions to try to remove some of the negative feelings;

(2) the use of DI and DA only sparingly and for only certain purposes;

(3) the use of DA instead of DI, since the emotional stake for the people proposing the counterplan in the DI method is higher than the people who merely critique the original plan in the DA approach;

(4) the use of structured meetings, so that the conflict is limited to a certain domain within the meeting.

Proponents of the DA approach argue that it should lead to less conflict than the DI approach. To the extent that participants have emotional stakes in the assumptions and recommendations they have developed, DI could lead to greater conflict, since it requires the development of diametrically opposed positions. DA requires only one set of assumptions and recommendations, and those who developed the critiques may develop smaller emotional stakes in their views than those who develop alternate recommendations. Hence, DA may produce better group performance and satisfaction than DI.

Proponents of the DI approach argue that in strategic planning, the debate and integration of plans leads to a higher level of understanding of the problems, issues, premises and assumptions facing decision makers in strategic planning. Constructive debates improve the planning process by creating an awareness of the complexity and interdependence of the issues involved. In addition, the extended debate in DI produces constructive alternatives, not just critiques, and therefore it should perform better than DA [27].

This study brought GSS, DA, DI, and C research together in a general setting. A GSS system was used to test the comparative effectiveness of two conflict based (DI and DA) and a nonconflict based (C) inquiry methods as well as assessing the comparative performance of DI and DA methods in a GSS environment.

## Research question

We sought to demonstrate whether conflict based GSS sessions can provide greater benefits than non-conflict based sessions for certain tasks. That is, within a GSS decision making environment, do groups using conflict based inquiry methods perform better, in terms of process and output, than groups using non-conflict based inquiry methods?

To explore this question, we examined a decision making process, measuring the quality of assumptions and recommendations that were developed. Performance was measured in terms of the quality of the groups' preferred recommendations and assumptions. Expert raters, composed of experts in the area of the subject matter, assessed the quality of recommendations and assumptions.

We were also interested in assessing the potentially negative aspects of using conflict based approaches, to see what their effect may be in a GSS environment. We wanted to learn whether the GSS environment would lower or eliminate some of these dysfunctional effects. In a GSS environment, is there a difference in perception between groups using conflict based and non-conflict based inquiry methods? Specifically, would the participants indicate differences in:

(1) the perceived quality of meeting;

(2) the level of satisfaction with decision process;

(3) the level of satisfaction with decision outcome;

(4) the perceived level of critical evaluation;

(5) the level of attraction to group?

The last refers to a group member's perception of being a part of his or her group, of moving to another group if given a chance, and of the interactions of group members in his or her group.

Finally, we were interested in finding out if one of the two conflict based methods (DA and DI) produces better outputs than the other. If the conflict based approaches do show better results than the consensus based approach, are those results uniformly better, or are there differences between the two conflict based approaches?

## Significance of the study

The potential significance of this study rests on the intersection of two important streams of research: the study of how groups work together to create shared outcomes and the exploration of GSS technology to support and improve group work. Research into the area of GSS has grown for the past few years. There are a number of existing academic GSSs, and we are beginning to see commercial systems based on them. Yet little is known about the relative value or importance of various inquiry methods that can be used within the GSS setting. If there are significant differences in the outcomes of the different approaches to group work in a GSS, this can have important impact on future designs and the use of these systems.

Researchers must systemically investigate subtle differences in the use and configuration of GSS so that contingency theories of design and use can be developed $[32]$ . The conditions under which GSS systems will be successful are still not yet well understood. DeSanctis et al. suggest that a determinant of success is likely to be the kind of task being performed. Others have suggested that the type of technological support for the group is important $[4,16]$ . There is a need to more precisely define and measure the technologies themselves, and we may discover that subtle differences in the systems count. While manual studies of the dialectical inquiry and devil's advocacy methods have been found to lead to better performance, this finding has not been studied explicitly in an electronic environment.

## Research model and method

The research model for this study focuses on the structural interventions DI, DA, and C (Figure 2). As noted in the model, group outcomes were measured by both objective and subjective means. Although the objective outcome of a group process is important, the participants' perceptions of the process is important. User perceptions impact such factors as the group members' commitment to the outcome and their willingness to use the system again.

## Research Model

The research method was experimental. Twelve small ad hoc groups composed of undergraduate business students used a GSS to generate solutions to various tasks using either DA, DI, or C methods. The students were randomly assigned to one of the three types of groups. Self report instruments were given before and after the sessions to assess participant reactions. In addition, data, such as the groups' final assumptions and recommendations generated under the different GSS conditions, were collected and evaluated.

When the subjects arrived for the experiment they were shown the facility and given instructions for using the GSS, based on the type of group to which they were assigned. After this, they were informed about the experimental task, which was a business case problem that required a recommended solution. Subjects then used the GSS and their assigned process to create a group recommendation.

## GSS system

We used GroupSystems as the GSS for this experiment. It was created at the University of Arizona Management Information Systems Department [22]. It is designed to be used in a conference room facility with micro-computers for each of the participants. The computers are generally arrayed around a “U-shaped” table, with a large public viewing screen at the open end with an additional computer for the facilitator. All of these computers and the public screen are connected via a LAN, so that they can pass and share data generated during the session. The facility used for the experiment is similarly equipped and arranged. Specific procedures for each of the treatment conditions were implemented by a facilitator who also assisted the group in its process.

## Task characteristics

The degree of task structure and inherent conflict were important considerations. If the task were too simple, or if the inherent conflict were too low, the group would not be expected to benefit greatly from the use of conflict based GSS approaches. For this study, a task was selected which allowed the participants to identify and operate from a number of opposing assumptions.

While various assumptions and recommendations were judged to be better or worse than others by a panel of experts, there was no best answer to the task.

## The devil's advocacy approach (DA)

The DA approach develops a solid argument for a reasonable recommendation, then subjects that recommendation to an in-depth, formal critique. The critique calls into question the assumptions and recommendations presented to the devil's advocate, and attempts to show why the recommendations should not be adopted. Through repeated criticism and revision, the approach leads to mutual acceptance of a recommendation. Proponents of this approach believe that good recommendations and assumptions will survive even the most forceful and effective criticism and that this approach is more likely to yield sound judgment or recommendations.

To implement DA, a group was divided into two subgroups. One was assigned the formal role of devil's advocate. Using the GSS, the other developed a set of recommendations and built an argument for them, supported by the key assumptions, facts, and data that the group identified. Then the DA group, also using the GSS, criticized the recommendation, underlying data, and assumptions of the first group. The criticism attempted to uncover all that is wrong with the recommendations, assumptions, facts, and data and to expound the reasons why the recommendation should not be adopted. The first subgroup then revised its recommendations and this was then put to a vote. If the group did not unanimously agree, the two sub-groups would continue to iterate until agreement is reached.

## The dialectical inquiry approach (DI)

This is also based on the manual DI manipulation of Schweiger et al. In the DI approach, a group was also divided into two subgroups. One, working on its own, determined a set of assumptions and recommendations. The second was then asked to determine counter-assumptions and to use them to create a counter-recommendation.

The two recommendations and their respective assumptions were subjected to in-depth, critical evaluation through a GSS supported debate between the subgroups. Using the same data, the debaters attempted to spell out the implications of each recommendation, reveal its underlying assumptions, and challenge (or defend) those assumptions as effectively as possible. Following this, the two sub-groups came to an agreement on which assumptions survived the scrutiny of debate and developed a recommendation.

## The consensus approach (C)

The C approach, based on Hall [13], relies on the use of the GSS to produce a thorough, open, and constructive discussion and examination of the recommendations and underlying assumptions developed individually by the group members. In the course of this process, each group member has the opportunity to present recommendations, their underlying assumptions, and relevant facts and data in as clear and logical a manner as possible. Through discussion and questioning, the group seeks a better recommendation than might be produced by a single person. Each person need not be completely satisfied with the assumptions and recommendations – only that each can accept them and consider them feasible. Consensus is said to exist when all group members can accept one recommendation and its underlying assumptions. During implementation of the C approach, the group was kept intact and worked together on the solution to the assigned task. The assumptions and recommendations were developed collaboratively.

## Quality of recommendations

A Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA test indicated that there were no significant differences among the three treatments (Chi-Square = 2.50, p = 0.29 corrected for ties). Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA for comparisons between two treatments (Figure 8) showed no differences on comparisons between treatments. For the recommendations for the 12 groups, we used a Kendall W test for inter-rater reliability. Kendall's W for the three judges showed that inter-rater reliability of the judges was high, W = 0.6022, p < 0.05.

## Discussion

The only significant finding of this study was that the validity of assumptions for DI groups was higher than for either DA or C groups. This is a somewhat surprising finding, given that other clear differences had been seen when similar studies looked at manually run groups. The limited findings in this study may reflect the actual lack of differences or the results may be due to other factors. Regarding the participant self assessments, possibly the methods were not able to bring out any differences. Similarly, the influence of the GSS may be such that effects of the method were not discernable. Alternatively the task chosen here may not bring out any differences.

Nonetheless, the fact that DI groups made assumptions that are assessed to be more valid than DA or C groups may have implications for GSS researchers, designers, and practitioners. The DI approach may well be preferable for situations where it is essential for the group to identify valid underlying assumptions. The results suggest that, within a GSS environment, when the validity of assumptions is of utmost importance, the DI approach may be superior to the other approaches.

It appears that the impact of GSS support is perceived by its users to be beneficial, irrespective of the specific process being implemented. It is possible that this effect is strong enough to mask differences. We cannot conclude that future GSSs should routinely make use of conflict based processes instead of a consensus based approach.

An important limiting factor in any such study of group behavior is the complex set of interactions that are a part of any group's process. Even with the structure provided in a GSS environment, there are a myriad of possible factors that can affect the group's process and outcome. For this reason, perhaps more than any other, any conclusions from a single study of this type must be viewed with caution.
