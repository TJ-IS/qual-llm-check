# Analyzing methodological rigor of MIS survey research from 1980–1989

Varun Grover, Choong C. Lee and Douglas Durand \*

University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC, USA

IS researchers are using survey methods as a means of determining and explaining practical phenomena. There is, however, growing concern over the rigor of the research performed by IS researchers. This study attempts to establish proper criteria and evaluate MIS survey research over the past decade. Specifically, the volume, trends and quality of research are described and discussed. The results indicate that while there is a need for more methodological rigor along the guidelines suggested, there are some indications of the maturing of the IS field.

Keywords: MIS survey research; Methodological rigor; Research quality; Research themes; Standards; Survey method

## 1. Introduction

The ongoing debate whether Management Information Systems (MIS) researchers are building a cumulative research tradition has included a growing concern about the rigor of various MIS research methodologies $[3,10,13,14]$ . Further, the quality of research methods used in the MIS field has been criticized $[e.g., 6,29]$ . After reviewing existing MIS research literature, many authors have advocated the development of guidelines or standards, to increase MIS research rigor $[7,31,32,41]$ . Some suggest the use of multiple methodologies (e.g., case study, field study, field experiment, and laboratory experiment) to complement each other, while others have devoted exclusive attention to particular methodologies

Similarly, Benbasat [8] and Lee [34] propose guidelines for conducting case research. The recency of these papers suggests a growing awareness of the “rigor” issue in MIS research. To help structure this, our study closely examines guidelines for defining rigor of a methodology that is growing in popularity and application, MIS survey research.

The MIS field has evolved from a concentration on technology to include managerial and organizational questions $[33]$ . This evolution has required methodological change to study different problems. The increase in volume of MIS survey research over the past decade, as reported by Farhoomand $[15]$ and Vogel and Wetherbe $[42]$ , is reflective of such change. However, with the increasing popularity of surveys, IS researchers should be cognizant of the need to set and adhere to certain standards in conducting the work. These should not only be applicable to the process of conducting survey research but also in the reporting of the results in our journals $[35]$ . This not only brings more rigor to scientific endeavor but facilitates follow-up studies, triangulation, minimum acceptable norms and consequently increased respectability for the discipline.

Our purpose, then, is to investigate the evolution of MIS survey research in terms of its volume and orientation. We also seek to compile a set of normative standards for conducting survey research. Published MIS survey studies are then evaluated for quality with respect to these standards.

## 2. Survey methodological attributes (SMA)

Schiltz [38] has shown that survey research faces special technical problems and challenges. He emphasizes the need for standards or norms for conducting surveys. Since surveys are among the most common methodologies for MIS research, this concern takes on special importance; it requires a clear articulation of survey standards. A movement to standards would help the evaluation of survey practice (with implications for all who collect, analyze, or read about survey data). To this end, a set of Survey Methodological

Attributes (henceforth referred to as SMA) was compiled to reflect desirable characteristics (or attributes) of survey research. SMA development required an extensive examination of research methodology literature. Books and articles that describe the necessary attributes and steps in the survey process were reviewed [e.g., 1,2,5,11,17,24,25,26].

It should be noted that when evaluating survey quality, it is important to consider the complete survey procedure, not just a few isolated aspects. Fowler [18] suggested that the quality of the sample, of the measurement questions, of data collection, and of the mode of data collection constitute a tightly interrelated set of issues and decisions. Still, in designing a survey researchers may accept tradeoffs between time, costs, and methodological rigor. Thus, we acknowledge that it may not always be feasible for researchers to address every attribute fully.

## Ideal Survey Methodological Attributes (SMA)

Ideal survey research includes the following attributes:

[1] Report the approach used to randomize or select samples: In reporting the details of a survey, a researcher should include all relevant details of the sampling procedure (i.e., whether the survey used simple random sampling, clustering, stratification, or unequal rates of selection among subgroups of the population [5,11,18]). Often, a random sample may be infeasible because there is no way to insure that selected organizations or individuals could be enticed to participate [35]. By describing the randomization or selection procedure, a researcher permits readers to decide the validity and representativeness of the sample frame.

[2] Report a profile of the sample frame: The sampling frame is the listing of the elements from which the actual sample was drawn. Fowler [18] pointed out that how well a sample represents a population depends on the sample frame. If the sample represents the population, it is essential that the sampling frame represents characteristics of all members of the population. Reporting the boundary of the sample frame should be the minimum that a researcher should provide to show how the sample frame corresponds to the target population.

[3] Report characteristics of respondents: Issac and Michael [27] state that survey researchers should describe the characteristics of the respondents. This practice permits readers to appreciate whether particular characteristics are representative of the sample.

[4] Use a combination of personal, telephone, and mail data collection: The selection of a data collection method is not simple. Since each approach has strengths and weaknesses, combining these methods should supplement their individual strengths [35]. Fowler suggests that combining these approaches may offer cost savings by reducing sampling or nonresponse bias.

[5] Append the whole or part of the questionnaire: By reproducing the questionnaire, the researcher provides readers with the exact wording of questions.

[6] Adopt a validated instrument or perform a validity or reliability analysis: As Lucas and Straub point out, MIS researchers must validate their research instruments. They contend that confirmatory empirical findings will be strengthened when instrument validation precedes both internal and statistical conclusion validity. Straub also warns that a large numbers of MIS researchers do not even test the minimal level of validation (i.e., reliability).

[7] Perform an instrument pretest: A pretest is a tryout, and its purpose is to help produce a survey form that is more usable and reliable [17,39]. Pretesting helps refine the instrument.

[8] Report on response rate: Response rate and nonresponse error have been a major issue in survey research methodology [43]. Simply obtaining a large sample would be meaningless unless a researcher seriously considers the response rate. Reporting on response rate should be a minimal requirement.

[9] Perform a statistical test to justify the loss of data from nonrespondents: The popularity of survey methods increasingly has been accompanied by problems of nonresponse bias, produced by small proportions or bias in the response. One clear step is to improve the response rate. However, other remedies can be applied as posterior methods. Those suggested by Hartman, Fuqua, and Jenkins, include compensation, elimination, estimation, trend projection, and resampling. Aiken further describes the use of interval estimation and a test of difference between proportions and means.

These desirable survey method attributes are used to represent the SMA score for evaluation of survey research. Four major criteria were used in the selection of these:

1. Unit of Analysis: The unit of analysis for the methodological attributes is the use of the methodology as reported in the research paper. That is, the unit of analysis is the research paper. By using the published article as a proxy for the method actually used, there is an implicit, albeit reasonable assumption, that any methodological attribute performed would be reported. It would be surprising that sampling procedures, validation, etc. were not reported if they had been performed appropriately.

2. Measurability: Care was taken to ensure that the attributes described were measured objectively. To do that, dichotomous attributes were chosen, where the presence of the attribute was deemed “good” and its absence was judged “bad.”

3. Independence: The attributes chosen were independent (i.e., one might occur independently of the other). Each attribute measured a different facet of methodology and reporting.

4. Exploratory vs. Explanatory: Given the early evolutionary stage of the field, there has been a tendency to use survey methodology descriptively, rather than to test hypotheses. Attributes were chosen to fit both types of studies. Descriptive surveys should not compromise the rigor of either administration or reporting.

Despite occasional discussions of normative and specific criteria for assessments of scientific quality, Gottfredson [19] pointed out that judgement criteria for research quality usually have been vague or intuitive. Therefore, we tried to provide objective criteria. Though our approach included a risk of undercounting the implicit content of the survey methodology, we believe that it provided a meaningful approach to quantifying and evaluating the methodological rigor of survey research. Using the quantified survey method attributes as a guide, we formed research questions to direct our analysis.

## 3. Research questions

The following questions guided our evaluation of MIS survey studies from 1980 to 1989. The questions can be categorized into three distinct sections: Volume, Research Themes and Quality.

## 3.1. Volume

On a purely descriptive note, the number of published MIS survey studies was examined. The objective was to test the contention that the use of the survey methodology has been increasing in the MIS discipline [e.g., 22]. The question is: Has the use of survey methods in MIS research increased? Two generic types of survey are descriptive and explanatory. $^{1}$ It would be expected that more mature disciplines would tend to emphasize the latter, where the focus is on establishing and testing relationships between constructs. To evaluate the use of each type of survey over time, the following questions are examined:

\- Which of the two popular survey methods – descriptive or explanatory (hypothesis testing) surveys – are more extensively used? Has there been a change over time?

\- Is there a difference in their quality, as reflected by the SMA score?

## 3.2. Research themes

Triangulation or the use of multiple methodologies to examine similar problems, is often advocated [18]. The domination of any one methodology in the investigation of a problem type or topic is reflective of the match between the problem and the methodology or the convenience of the researcher. In the MIS field it would be interesting to examine if the survey methodology is used equally across topical areas, or if its use is biased toward certain themes. The question is: Which research themes most often use the survey method?

## 3.3. Quality

The major focus of this study is to evaluate the quality of MIS survey research, as indicated by SMA scores. Based on the criteria, evaluation of survey papers should yield insight into specific weaknesses of MIS survey work. Such insights could have prescriptive implications for MIS researchers. Therefore: Which aspects of MIS survey research have the greatest methodological weaknesses?

Also, using the SMA score, it is possible to evaluate whether the past decade has seen significant improvement in the quality of MIS survey research. A measurable increase in methodological rigor bodes well for the evolution of the field. Thus: Has there been a significant improvement in the methodological quality of MIS survey research?

Non-MIS journals (like Management Science, Decision Science, Communication of the ACM, Academy of Management Journal, etc.) have their origins in “more mature” disciplines and are often exemplified as having more rigorous methodology standard $[6,31]$ . This proposition can be tested using the SMA score as a quantifiable measure of rigor: Is there a significant difference between MIS and non-MIS journals regarding the quality of MIS survey research?

## 4. Study design

Data for the study were based on MIS articles using the survey methodology from selected MIS journals. A standard coding format was developed for the SMA (see Appendix A). A dichotomous “yes/no” rating of each attribute produced the SMA score. The demographic data included such items as journal name, year published, type of survey (explanatory vs. descriptive survey), etc. The total SMA score for each article was computed by summing across each of nine reported categories. Total coding activity took approximately 120 hours. (an average of almost 30 minutes per article).

## 4.1. Journal selection

Eight journals and one conference proceeding were selected for the study (see Table 1). Ten years of published MIS survey studies (1980–1989) were investigated (except for the Journal of MIS which began publication in 1984). The selected journals were separated into two groups, non-MIS and MIS journals. Journal selection for this study was consistent with similar prior studies, and represented widely accepted high quality outlets for MIS research articles [13,14,37,42].

Using this definition, studies were excluded that used unstructured interviews (e.g., protocol analysis) or simple observation to produce qualitative data. Studies using secondary data (i.e. citation analysis) and unobtrusive measures were also excluded unless these were used as a small portion of overall results. Such criteria do not exclusively identify survey-only research; e.g., occasional case studies, field studies, and field experiments could be included so long as the survey method dominated the research methodology and conformed to our selection criteria.

## 4.2. Sample selection criteria

For MIS journals (MISQ, ICIS, I&M, JMIS), the entire collection of articles was evaluated; the articles using survey methodology were identified. For non-MIS journals (CACM, CS, MS, DS, and AMJ), keywords and titles in each article were used to classify MIS articles. Then, identical selection criteria were applied for survey research.

## 4.3. Statistical analysis

Two hundred and twenty-seven (227) journal articles were classified as using the survey method; they subsequently were coded using SMA scores. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics and measures of difference and change. Significant change and trend difference were analyzed using the Chow and t-test respectively. The Chow test is a reliable statistical method for testing for differences between two or more regressions [21]. For measurement validity, an item-analysis using the Chi-square test was used.

Articles also were classified by research themes. Three independent raters $^{2}$ coded the 227 article abstracts on their research themes. Each article was rated on a four point scale to represent its contribution in each of 20 predefined research themes. A rating of “3” indicated that the rater was very confident that the article addressed the designated theme, “2” meant moderately confident, “1” implied unsure, and “0” meant that the reviewer considered the article to be unrelated. A maximum of three themes was identified for articles with multiple themes. Then, the ratings of the three raters were summed. Research themes that received a total rating score of less than 6 were discarded. An article was classified as possessing a theme when the aggregated score exceeded six. If multiple themes satisfied the criterion, then the theme was weighted proportionally (i.e., if two themes satisfied the criteria, then each received half the article count). Similar methods have been used in prior research (e.g., [20], [28]).

## 4.4. Reliability and validity

Due to the fact that the instrument consists of several “strata” of categories or dimensions, the use of coefficient alpha to measure reliability is open to question. Rather, it is presumed that at least a small, but important, degree of unique variance is associated with the categories [16]. Measuring reliability using coefficient alpha was abandoned and the predictive validity of each attribute was measured under the assumption that the total score is a linear composite of all attributes.

To determine the SMA validity, an item analysis was conducted. Item analysis is a procedure to increase the pragmatic (predictive) validity of the total score; each test item is evaluated separately to find if that item discriminates in the way intended. The results of item analysis could determine whether any item should be discarded, based upon its level of discriminating power. The smallest item analysis Chi-square statistic was 5.269 (p < 0.01). This showed that all nine items have had favorable discriminating power and contribute to the measurement validity.

Of the MIS articles collected, the proportion of survey articles was 17% of the total. Table 2 presents a tabulation of the articles selected from each journal over the ten-year period (1980–1989). The highest number of survey articles was published by I&M, followed by MISQ. Even JMIS's short publication period (23 articles over 5.5 years) reflects a significant number of survey studies in the MIS field.

## Research themes

To identify the major themes of MIS research, we modified the Brancheau and Wetherbe [9] listing of IS issues to reflect a taxonomy of research themes (see Table 5). Eighteen articles (out of 227) could not be classified on these themes. The 209 remaining articles, were classified on the 20 research themes by three researchers. The top four themes comprised 51% of the total survey articles. These themes were: IS human resources/IS personnel (15%), system development (14%), end-user computing (11%), and IS effectiveness (11%). Figure 3 describes the proportion of survey studies dealing with each research theme for the two halves of the decade: the profile of studies is different for each period. Specifically, survey work has been higher in the areas of strategic planning for IS, and end-user computing in the 1985–1989 period, while studies on IS personnel and IS development have declined. Some themes have had very limited survey work, either because they are not easily amenable to it (e.g., Expert Systems) or because they are relatively newer themes that at this stage are not pervasive enough for widespread data collection (e.g., Information Architecture).

The results discussed above raise some interesting questions. For instance, does the change in research emphasis over the two five year periods reflect the changing knowledge profile of the topics? Or, is it indicative of the increase in managerial orientation as discussed earlier? Or, more disturbingly, is it reflective of the ephemerality of much IS research? In other words, is IS research, especially survey-based works, driven by the “fad-of-the month”? While our data do not provide insight into this, we would tend to suspect that new technologies or technologically based phenomenon provide impetus for many IS studies.

## Quality

This seeks identification of the methodological attributes that are most ignored by MIS survey researchers. Table 6 presents the percentage of MIS surveys that contain various desirable attributes of survey research. The attributes are presented in descending order of incidence.

In general, MIS studies deviated extensively from the defined ideal. For example, only 16 studies (7%) performed a statistical test to justify the nonresponse bias. On average, 90% of the reviewed surveys failed to contain: (a) justification of nonresponse error, (b) use of a combination of data collection methods, and (c) reporting on the sample randomization or selection procedure. These three are the most frequently ignored attributes by MIS researchers. These often require work that goes beyond that of simply administrating an instrument. Testing for non-response bias generally involves additional data collection (by interview) of a sample of non-respondents and this means that the privacy of individuals who choose not to respond may be violated. Use of multiple data collection methodologies involves additional effort in administration and cross-validation of the methods. Sampling involves the selection of appropriate populations and appropriate probability sampling to determine the target sample for survey administration. This often comes at the cost of convenience.

Other attributes, such as validity analysis, instrument pretesting, and questionnaire disclosure are missed or ignored by about 2/3 of the studies. While disclosure of the questionnaire might be difficult due to page restrictions, it is desirable (and often an editorial mandate), as it facilitates evaluation and replication of the study. Validity analysis and pretesting however, are essential to ensuring that the constructs are appropriately measured and the relationships are meaningful.

To test for changes in the quality of MIS survey research methodology throughout the last decade, the SMA scores from the MIS 1980–89 survey articles were analyzed using the Chow test. Ten years of sample data were divided into two five year subperiods and the Chow test was used to test for difference in the two periods. The results in Table 7 do not indicate significant differences between the periods (p > 0.05). This finding implies that the quality of MIS survey research has not changed, but the aggregate number of MIS surveys has consistently increased over the period.

To test the difference in quality for survey work accepted in MIS vs. non-MIS journals, we replaced the Chow-test with a simple t-test, because the assumption of homoscedasticity is violated between the non-MIS group and MIS-group [40]. Due to their more rigid standards, we assumed that survey research in the non-MIS journals would show higher methodological rigor.

## 6. Discussion

Overall, the study evaluated MIS survey research in terms of volume, theme, and quality over the past decade. A methodological yardstick, called the SMA, was compiled, in an attempt to “measure” the rigor in the application of this popular methodology. While the individual attributes are not new to survey research and can be found in much of the normative literature on the methodology, careful compilation of them into a set of prescriptive guidelines (the SMA) provides value for future research. The increasing use of surveys in the MIS field, as indicated by this study among others and an increased awareness of methodological rigor, makes it important to establish norms for MIS survey research.

The major assumption is that the attributes are measured as reported in the paper. In other words, unreported procedures were assumed to be either “not performed” or “unsuccessful.” A limitation of the dichotomous measure however, was that it did not capture the richness of some variables. For instance, there was great variation in length of the description of the sample frame, and this was not captured. Also, Survey Methodological Attributes (SMAs) were based on a list of attributes that may not have been exhaustive. While these were analyzed individually, the overall SMA score assumes that they had equal weight. However, since our intention was to measure the comparative (not absolute) quality of survey research methodology over a specified period, aggregated measures probably are appropriate.

Our results revealed that explanatory surveys have become more popular than descriptive surveys. Their quality, as measured by the number of attributes reported, is higher than those in descriptive surveys. Though these two survey types share the same attributes, the increase in explanatory surveys suggests maturation of the field along this dimension, as the “data fishing” qualities often associated with exploratory surveys are avoided. Further, as discussed by Nachmias and Nachmias [36], the research process should ideally deal with inferential rather than descriptive data gathering, and this is an indication of the maturity of a field. Using a research model or/and applicable theory to derive the relationships or deriving a model inductively from the data facilitates the structuring of knowledge in the field.

The results also indicate that more than half the surveys conducted in MIS over the 1980–1989 period concentrated on four research themes. In contrast, the other half were spread thinly over 16 themes. The strong preference of IS researches to use surveys for certain topics might indicate a lack of maturation along this dimension. It has been suggested that more mature fields tend to address similar problems with a “balance” of methodologies, without domination by any one [23].

Table 9 provides a frequency table depicting the number of MIS survey studies by Survey Methodological Attribute scores. None of the survey studies contained all the ideal attributes. Generally, most studies did not contain even half the ideal characteristics. Moreover, MIS survey studies did not show increased quality through the decade. Since most of the suggested survey attributes are fundamental, many shortcomings of the survey method could be remedied by devoting special attention to these attributes. Thus, the generally low methodological rigor and reporting of survey research in the field (as measured by the SMA score) should serve as a clear call to MIS researchers for improvement. For example, only 7% of the studies showed any analysis of nonresponse bias. This may be approximated by simply comparing the answer patterns of early respondents to those of late respondents. Considering the importance of response validity and the minimum measurement effort required, it is an important practice for MIS survey researchers to follow.

Straub's study found that 17 percent of survey studies relied on previously used instruments. Similarly, our findings indicated that $12.3\%$ of MIS survey researchers adopted instruments that previously had been used and validated. From Straub's results, about $30\%$ of previously used instruments were themselves apparently never validated. The adoption of a previously used instrument, therefore, should be based upon the validity of the adopted instrument - not its prior use.

It should be pointed out however, that attention to each ‘standard’ described in this paper will not guarantee good survey research. That is still contingent on the topic, theory, contribution, interest, and organization. However, adherence to standards will add in the methodological competence of the paper.

Interestingly, the technical and methodological problems of survey research have not been restricted to the MIS field. In the education field Schiltz argues, “The survey is vulnerable because it is a subtle, complex activity that belongs to no particular academic discipline and is governed by none". This vulnerability, he continues, should be supported by explicit standards, not by circulated implicit peer-acceptance.

In the MIS field, Keen pointed out that the “lack of standards” in MIS research fails to provide a clear idea of what is “good”. In a broad context, standards provide criteria that help individual researchers develop their work and allow readers to render their judgement. As Schultz explains, standards provide the “rules of the game”; they form a necessary prerequisite to communication and confidence between researchers and readers. Articles dealing with methodological rigor and standards should have a positive effect on the quality of future MIS empirical work. This process cannot be only the duty of a few of the field’s gatekeepers. Rather, it is the responsibility of every researcher. We hope that our evaluation criteria will encourage this.
