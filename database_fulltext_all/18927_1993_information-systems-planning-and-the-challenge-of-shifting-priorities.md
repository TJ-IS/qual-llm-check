# Information systems planning and the challenge of shifting priorities $^{1}$

Albert L. Lederer

Oakland University, Rochester, MI, USA

Aubrey L. Mendelow

Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA

Establishing and maintaining priorities for information systems (IS) development is a challenge that has made IS planning an enduring issue. During structured interviews, senior IS executives from 20 diverse organizations discussed reasons why it is difficult to establish and maintain priorities. The executives also discussed the ways they are attempting to overcome such problems. An analysis of the interviews provides IS practitioners with insight into the difficulty and some possible actions to deal with it.

Keywords: Information planning; Information management; Priority setting.

The potential of information to contribute to an organization's performance has grown dramatically in recent years; today's managers must therefore be able to develop a portfolio of the organization's appropriate applications. They must then be able to prioritize these applications and maintain the priorities as they are implemented.

In fact, the key elements of planning are the setting of objectives and priorities. Much attention has been given to the importance of identifying IS applications that are consistent with organizational objectives $[14,22,23,28]$ . However, less attention has been given recently to the priority with which each application should be developed. This process is especially complex because it considers business objectives and financial and intangible benefits plus technical complexity $[5]$ .

Research has consistently found improved IS planning as the key issue facing IS executives $[1,4,6,10]$ . More recent research has identified reasons why IS planning has remained an enduring issue $[15]$ . Such reasons include IS management's inability to identify top management's objectives $[16]$ , to convince top management of the strategic potential of IS $[17]$ , and to coordinate IS objectives with business objectives $[18]$ . This article reports the results of a study to better understand the process of establishing and maintaining priorities for IS development. The study sought to identify the problems of the prioritization process, as encountered by senior IS managers, and to determine the techniques used to overcome the problems.

## Background

## Dealing with limited resources

Organizations generally possess limited resources. Management's challenge is to allocate these resources among critical projects within the organization's goals [3]. To do so, managers must set priorities. This means that they must first cull the critical projects from all that are proposed. They must then establish the order in which to complete the critical projects. As Drucker pointed out, “Nothing is ever accomplished unless scarce resources are concentrated on a small number of priorities [7].”

Two practices, the use of cost-benefit analysis and the use of steering committees, have generally guided establishing and maintaining IS priorities. Cost-benefit analysis has long helped IS managers identify projects with high returns $[8,12,13]$ . However, because the benefits of IS applications are often difficult to quantify, the incorporation of intangible factors makes cost-benefit analysis uncertain $[21,30]$ . Nevertheless, cost-benefit analysis remains useful and popular.

Steering committees consisting of senior business executives have also long helped IS managers identify their organization's most critical priorities [2]. Priority setting by individuals may not be optimal for the enterprise as a whole [26]. The individual heads of any specific department are rarely the right persons to set organization-wide priorities, because they prefer an even flow of work for their departments. Individual heads might also set priorities to further their personal goals.

To be optimal for the entire enterprise, a group of business executives, such as a steering committee, can effectively shift the burden of priority setting from IS management [25]. A steering committee may also inspire user and management commitment to projects, a prerequisite for their successful implementation [9]. However, research has not shown that steering committees eliminate priority setting problems. Still they are widely prescribed and popular.

## Consequences of failure to follow plans

When changes in priorities occur they can be very costly. For example, delayed implementation of a strategic IS may give a competitor an advantage that will later prove difficult to overcome.

Moreover, changes in priorities may force managers to suspend one project to start another. This wastes resources devoted to the interrupted project, since, even if restarted, IS staff must relearn it. In turn, this can cause overruns in budgets and completion dates. Thus change in priorities can ultimately diminish the credibility of the IS department $[19]$ .

## Plans are often not scrupulously followed

A recent study of the execution of strategic IS plans verified the presence of changes in priorities and therefore the problems of establishing and maintaining priorities $[20]$ . Although the participating organizations on average had completed over half of their planning horizon, surprisingly, 38% of all initiated projects during this time period had not been identified in the strategic IS plan. This suggested that organizations were developing other applications despite the plan. Moreover, less than one quarter of the projects from the strategic plan had been initiated during that period.

The study also revealed that securing top management commitment for implementing the final plan was the most serious problem in implementing a strategic IS planning methodology. This is added evidence of the problem of shifting priorities. Such findings suggest the elusiveness of the IS plan in which project priorities remain satisfactorily constant from their inclusion to their completion. The findings further suggest that IS planning might be less beneficial than supposed. Therefore, it is important to identify techniques that might eliminate the difficulty.

## The study of IS priority setting

Given the absence of prior empirical work, a “direct” research approach involving structured interviews seemed the most appropriate precursor to a more conventional study $[24]$ . Moreover, this technique is good practice.

Since the research related primarily to the IS function, senior managers responsible for planning and managing their organization's IS were sought for interviews. A decision was made to include medium to large organizations in a wide variety of industries. The authors described the study to the potential subjects as an interview about their IS planning experience. All these managers agreed to participate. Table 1 contains profiles of the 20 participating U.S. organizations.

Although the subjects were chosen to represent a variety of diverse industries, several of the organizations and their IS executives are very highly regarded in their industries and the field of information management.

The interviews with each IS executive lasted approximately three hours. Subjects were promised anonymity. A written agenda guided an exhaustive discussion of a wide variety of IS planning problems, techniques, and philosophies. The following questions important to this paper were asked: Is establishing and maintaining priorities a difficulty that you have experienced? If so, why? If not, why not? What do you do (or have you done) to overcome this difficulty? The researchers also asked questions about the presence of specific, general, and IS management practices and techniques.

All interviews were conducted by both co-authors of this paper. Although each interviewer specialized in one portion of the questioning, the other was free to interrupt, seek clarification, and probe further. Both interviewers took extensive notes and transcribed them as soon as possible (usually within two days). With the subject's permission, they recorded all but the first interview on audio tape and later used this to resolve any discrepancies.

## Results of the study

The authors studied the transcripts of the interviews to identify why establishing and maintaining IS priorities is believed to be a problem for IS managers. Several interviewees gave more than one response. The responses were collated into Table 2. The techniques which the interviewees advocated to overcome the problem are listed in Table 3. The low “Number of Interviewees Mentioning” each reason or technique occurred because the responses were not prompted.

Seventeen of the 20 executives stated that they experienced difficulty establishing and maintaining priorities. We attempt to report their standard reasons dispassionately and save our interpretation for later.

## Reasons

R1: Duration A major problem is the extended duration of the systems development life cycle. The Vice President of IS of a consumer products manufacturer explained that “the user has to think two years ahead” to identify his or her needs but will only “see the system three years later.” During lengthy development stages, needs may change. New needs may also cause new IS priorities. Also, users may lose interest in a system designed to satisfy their old needs. The Vice President quoted an apathetic user-manager who said, “If I had known it was going to take so long, I would never have committed.”

R2: The user Many users feel that their own projects are the most important; some demand satisfaction without concern for others. According to the Manager of IS of an industry association, “somebody will present a desperate need and management will change the priorities.” The Vice President, Data Processing, of a retail drug chain observed that desperate users could sometimes convince a steering committee to interrupt an important project in their favor even though he felt that the important project should have been completed.

The Manager, IS Division, of an industry association summed up the situation as, “The winds change with each quarterly director’s meeting and we come back with a new set of signals.”

R4: Limited resources As the organization has insufficient IS resources to complete all proposals, only those with the highest priority will be completed. Hence, users may attempt to influence the priority setting process. R5: Bypass tactics When users find that their projects do not have the necessary priority, they may attempt to bypass the priority setting system. At a manufacturer of construction supplies, users worked around the approval process by approaching programmers with small requests for systems work without IS management knowledge. Thus official priorities were compromised.

At a coal producer, users broke a large project into several small requests to be evaluated by a lower-level and (more lenient) approval committee. Had all the small requests been approved, the users would have received their larger project despite its lower priority. An alert management noticed this, stopped the subterfuge, and maintained the appropriate priorities.

At a natural gas producer, users attempt to subvert the process by understating their requirements for a new IS. The IS Department initially estimated its cost at \$1.5 million. After detail design, it was estimated at \$3 million. Later, after \$500,000 had been spent on developing the system, the cost was estimated at \$7 million. The system was ultimately dropped.

R6: Planning subsystem The failure to integrate long-term and short-term plans can make it difficult to establish and maintain priorities. At a manufacturer of processed foods, the five year plan considered broad technological issues while the annual plan contained specific applications and priorities. According to the General Manager, IS, the absence of applications and their priorities in the five year plan allowed priorities to be “twisted and turned” during the creation of each annual plan.

R7: Government Government legislation can force new IS development priorities in regulated industries. It can also affect priorities in the administration of compensation, benefits, employment and taxation. The IS department must juggle priorities to accommodate the changes.

Finally, government regulations can alter the conditions under which an industry operates. For example, changes in U.S. interstate banking have exposed banks to out-of-state competitors with highly developed IS. Local banks must alter their priorities to compete.

R8: The IS department IS Department groups may set their own priorities. At a full service bank, systems analysts were assigned to business units and they set their own priorities. Programmers had difficulty completing projects because the analysts changed their priorities almost arbitrarily.

R9: Return on investment Management may raise the priority of new proposals with high return on investment. Tangible benefits are accorded a higher priority than “soft” benefits. The Vice President, Computer Services, of a natural gas producer quoted his chairman as saying, “I can’t pay dividends to shareholders based on intangible benefits.”

R10: Level of management involvement If exclusively low-level management set priorities, it can be difficult maintain them. They are often unable to see the impact on the business as a whole and top management may therefore change the priorities.

## Techniques

The major techniques used to establish and maintain priorities are:

T1: Use a steering committee This was the most commonly mentioned technique. Fifteen of the executives used such a committee.

For example, the General Manager, Computer Systems of a coal producer stated that his organization had the CEO and five executive vice presidents as a steering committee. In another case, the MIS Director of a construction management firm said that his organization employed a steering committee of users to decide “who wants what and when.” He viewed his role as that of a mediator.

The Vice President, IS, of a specialty food chain described how he initially set priorities which he then discussed with a steering committee consisting 5 other VPs. The IS VP could change the priorities if needed. As a final example, another MIS Director said that in reality, "me and my boss set priorities."

T2: Control IS resources The second most popular technique was to apply a control system to monitor the project approval and implementation process. This can be viewed as an ad hoc set of bureaucratic rules.

For example, the General Manager, Computer System of a coal producer explained that an attempt to circumvent the approval process within his organization was grounds for dismissal. Moreover, his organization requires a management review of projects over \$10,000. This involved almost all projects, since programmers were billed at \$5000 per week.

The MIS Director of a construction management firm stated that his staff received no credit for work unless it was on the priority list. Moreover, all requests for MIS service had to be in writing. At a steel producer, the General Manager, EDP Planning, explained that hiring outside consultants to complete work with low priority required IS department approval and that any changes in priority had to be approved by the VP of Accounting.

In addition, the executives discourage changes in priorities by warning users of the adverse consequences. For example, the IS Director of a chemical manufacturer pointed out the risk of stopping a project: “Users learn by experience” not to change priorities. The Executive Vice President of a full-service bank also made it clear that “a project put on the shelf costs 50% more to take off the shelf.” The General Manager, EDP Planning and Control, of a steel producer maintained that it was “nearly impossible for users to beat the plan.”

T3: Dedicate teams to functional areas When the IS department dedicates a team to each functional area of the business, the users in the functional areas may set their own priorities. This localizes the problems resulting from shifting to the team rather than the entire department. The problems, however, persist for individual teams.

T4: Prepare a good plan Formulating the most comprehensive plan is yet another means of establishing and maintaining priorities. In developing the plan, the IS Director of a chemicals manufacturer stated that he viewed change as a way of life and that one should “plan for the unexpected.” The MIS Director of a construction management firm observed that better estimating reduced the likelihood that priorities would need to change. The General Manager, Computer Systems, of a coal manufacturer pointed out that his plan meticulously included hardware matters because significant equipment changes could cause major changes in priorities.

T5: Promulgate priorities and reasons for them An IS department can minimize shifts in priorities by promulgating existing priorities and the reasons for them. The Director, Computing and Information Services, at a university explained that information on projects is open and publicly disseminated. The Manager, IS Division, of an industry association provides a monthly status report highlighting new priorities while the General Manager, EDP Planning and Control of a steel manufacturer described project sheets and monthly progress reports.

T6: Act dictatorially Some IS executives say that they could establish and maintain priorities effectively if they set the priorities dictatorially. The Vice President, IS, of a consumer products manufacturer sets the priorities based on his personal conviction and his negotiation with users. The Vice President, MIS, of a specialty food chain, added that this approach depended on the strength, personality, and stature of the top IS executive.

T7: Apply new technology In some situations, IS executives use new technology to shorten development time and even permit users to develop their own systems. This avoids the difficulty of establishing and maintaining priorities both by reducing the time during which the priorities can change and by shifting responsibility to the users. This is possible because many applications are now commercially available off-the-shelf at lower costs and can be tailored to individual users' needs.

T8: Reward completed projects By rewarding project managers for successfully completed projects, IS executives encourage completion on time and within budget.

T9: Obtain excess resources The need for prioritization stems from the lack of sufficient resources. The Director, Corporate Data Center, of a conglomerate, explained that he did not have problems, since his department did little new development and had extensive resources. Moreover, his staff could handle all requests without delay.

T10: Encourage teamwork The MIS Director of a construction company stated that the problem of shifting priorities can be reduced by encouraging selflessness among groups of users competing for the IS resource.

T11: Live with it When all else fails, the Vice President and Director of Data Processing of a savings bank stated that the only remaining response is to accept changes in priorities.

## Difference between firms

Only three of the 20 IS executives stated that they did not have a problem establishing and maintaining priorities. This prompted two separate attempts to discover why.

The first attempt was an examination of the reasons of the three executives. The Director, Corporate Data Center of a conglomerate explained that his organization had virtually ceased developing new systems and had excess resources. The General Manager, Computer Systems, of a coal producer explained that priorities were established by executives at a very high level, and thus he was not personally involved with the process and did not have the problem. The Executive Vice President of a full service bank noted that “each user manages his own priorities.”

Thus none of the three genuinely solved the prioritization problem. The first avoided the problem while the other two merely transferred it. In fact, the strategy of dedicating IS teams to each functional area was used unsuccessfully by four other IS executives. Likewise, the strategy of establishing priorities by executives at a very high level was unsuccessful for several steering committee users.

The second attempt assumed genuine difference between the groups and was a search of the interview transcripts for a pattern of IS management techniques which would distinguish the three companies. Thus for example, the researchers investigated whether the use of steering committees distinguised them from the others. However, all had steering committees. Similarly, all organizations used cost-benefit analysis.

In fact, none of the techniques completely eliminated the problems of shifting priorities. Many merely attenuated it or attempted to move responsibility elsewhere. Thus the techniques are simply palliatives.

## An interpretation

The failure to associate any techniques with the complete elimination of the problem demanded further interpretation. The prioritization process is probably much more complex than it appears.

Thus the authors suggest viewing the reasons in terms of two types of behavior: rational and political [27]. Rational behavior is exhibited when goals and preferences are consistent for all participants, with an orderly decision process based on rules and norms and decisions tied to outcome-maximizing choices. Political behavior is manifest when individual groups have their own goals and preferences. It is marked by bargaining and interplay among groups with conflicting interests. IS development has been analyzed in terms of these two types of behaviors [29].

Rational behavior is evident in several of the reasons. Changes in business strategy (R3) in response to competitive moves and new government legislation (R7) may militate changes in priorities that can be approved by all groups. Likewise, changes in the business environment may compel management to raise the priority of new proposals with better ROI (R9) to increase profits. These changes can occur where the lengthy duration of IS development (R1) is reasonable and the organization genuinely lacks sufficient resources (R4).

Political behavior is also evident in several reasons. For example, when individual users have their own goals and preferences, they may believe that their own projects are the most important (R2) and may attempt to bypass the priority setting system (R5). Similarly, groups within the IS department may set their own priorities (R8) because they have their own goals and preferences. Possibly top management's goals are visionary and long-term while department managers' goals deal with current crises or local optimization; such conflicting goals might produce poorly integrated long-term and short-term plans (R6). Also, if low-level management is involved in priority setting (R10), shifts in priorities may be easier to effect. Finally, a lengthy planning process (R1) provides more time for political maneuvering to take place.

Parallel to rational and political behaviors, external and internal factors may be significant with external factors largely responsible for rational issues and internal factors largely responsible for political ones. For example, external influences such as business competition and government regulation (R7) force changes in business strategy (R3) that can result in shifting priorities. Also, internal influences such as the belief by managers that their projects are the most important (R2) can result in shifting priorities.

This suggests it may be worthwhile to discover if one set of factors is more responsible for shifting priorities than the other. Despite the small sample size, we tallied the number of subjects mentioning rational (R1, R3, R4, R7, and R9) and the number mentioning political (R1, R2, R5, R6, R8, R10). (We counted R1 for both since it appeared for both.) The total was very close (18 for the external and 19 for the internal) and we concluded neither set of factors is substantially more important.

Nevertheless, the bifurcation of behaviors (and factors) helps recognize how the techniques might fail to prevent priorities from shifting. IS management techniques based on the rational approach alone may be insufficient to deal with political behavior. Thus steering committees (T1) may not prevent bargaining that can change priorities and wreak havoc with IS plans. Also, control mechanisms (T2) may not prevent users from bypassing the priority setting system. In effect, IS management may not wield sufficient power to enable it to negotiate satisfactorily with the politicos.

Similarly, IS technology might be insufficient to cope with the external forces that result in shifting priorities. New technologies to shorten the development life cycle and user developed systems (T7) (such as prototyping, CASE techniques, and fourth generation languages) will never prevent change in business strategy and new government legislation.

In the final analysis, the perfect IS plan, in which priorities remain constant from each project's inclusion in the plan until its completion, may not exist. Apparently uncontrollable events occur - whether due to upheaval in the external business environment or to the internal political machinations of organizational managers.

## Implications for practitioners

When researchers promise anonymity to IS executives, they assert that irrational demands, unrealistic expectations, political pressures, and their own shortcomings have much to do with the challenges of setting and maintaining IS priorities. This has two implications for practitioners.

First, it provides a view of the problem of establishing and maintaining priorities. Assuming the accurate assessment of the rational and political behaviors, Keen's [11] prescriptions apply: practitioners should decide whether a reprioritization proposal is based on rational or political behavior. In whose interest is the change – within the general interest of the entire organization or within some parochial interest of the user? IS managers should look for the rational behavior, in which genuine changes in business priorities require changes in priorities.

IS managers also need to recognize political behavior, in which proposers of changes have private agendas. IS managers need to evaluate the power of such proposers compared to their own and exercise this power as they negotiate for change.

## Implications for researchers

Improved IS planning is a key issue for IS executives. IS managers' difficulty in establishing and maintaining priorities is one explanation for this.

In studies, IS researchers should attempt to identify the conditions under which the challenge of establishing and maintaining priorities is more difficult by asking such questions as: Do organizations that are highly dependent on information have different problems than those less dependent on it? Do larger organizations have different problems than smaller organizations? Do larger IS departments have different problems than smaller IS departments? Do problems differ by industry? Are the particular skills and experiences of IS managers associated with specific problems?
