# Organized and strategic utilization of information technology

# A nationwide approach

Liang Thow-Yick

National University of Singapore, Singapore

The information wave is sweeping around the world and information technology (IT) is advancing at a breath-taking pace. Nations and blocs of nations are exploring its potential. Similarly, business organizations have been juggling with this ever-evolving technology. However, a strategic gap exists, in that the potentials of IT is not maximized. Recognizing that an organized and systematic approach to IT usage on a nationwide basis is essential, the Singapore government introduced a National IT Plan (NITP) in 1986. This study reviews the objectives of the NITP and its implementation strategy. The strategy is analyzed with reference to various Management Information Systems (MIS) frameworks, such as Nolan's stage-model, McFarlan's and McKenny's strategic grid model, and Porter's value chain model. The impact of the plan in certain areas and its transformation effect on the economy are examined and the first five years of experience are explored and shared.

Keywords: Information technology; Management information systems; National IT plan; Strategic utilization; Nationwide approach.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Information revolution

Dramatic changes have occurred in the last three decades due to the advent of information technology (IT). Since the first general purpose computer was built, nations, organizations and even individuals have been subjected to the influence of the information wave. An organization or a nation has to utilize the potentials of IT to compete. And IT will have to be strategically exploited if a user is to emerge as a victor [Parsons, 1983]. Apparently, the pace of the information revolution greatly exceeds that of the agricultural and industrial revolutions.

## 1.2. Impact on nations

The Japanese ‘The Fifth Generation Computer Project, State of the Art Report 11.1’, says that “society at large is loosely linked together by a living network of bonding information whose primary function is to coordinate the activities of each group of people so that the group behaves as an ‘organized’ unit with its own identity”.

In a recent paper, English [English, 1985] cautioned that “the potential of IT as a catalyst for industrial and social changes is enormous, yet Europe, handicapped by national boundaries, is letting these events overtake it, so losing out in the competitive race”. Thus it seems that even an industrialized continent is concerned with the impact of the IT revolution. In order to overcome these apparent handicaps against Japan and USA, some of Europe’s companies set up joint research with government support via ESPRIT institute to co-ordinate activities at a precompetitive level [Tomeski and Klahr, 1988]. This action endorsed even more strongly the fact that the economic performance of a nation or a bloc of nations in the next decade depends significantly on the strategic exploitation of IT.

## 1.3. Impact on organizations

For most organizations, IT has become one of the most important and critical functional areas. Dependence on IT and computerized information systems (CIS) is now spreading across organizations of all forms, whether they are sole proprietorships or multinational corporations, or whether they are in the business of retail sales, manufacturing, or services. The spread of IT is continual and rapid.

An IT plan becomes essential in most organizations. It must support the overall corporate strategic plan; indeed, Lucas 1987 says: "IT will continue to shape the strategies and management structures of corporations across the economy". However, because of the rapid growth of IT, a strategic gap exists between the technology and senior management.

## 2. Strategic gap

## 2.1. Universal observation

In all countries, including those on the technology frontier such as the United States and Europe, the gap between the opportunities created by IT and the effective utilization of this technology is expanding [Rockart and Crescenzi, 1984;]. Apparently, two factors have contributed to this gap [Benjamin et al., 1984]. They are that “functionality and cost performance”...“in effect is creating strategic opportunities” and “senior managers have little or no experience or background in managing information and telecommunications technologies”...“they do not have an experiential base to relate this new form of strategic opportunity to their business.”

## 2.2. Singapore's observation

In Singapore, although senior executives are increasingly aware of the benefits of IT, most do not understand its impact or know how to utilize it effectively. The following factors are identified as some of the undesirable attributes that are contributing to the widening of the strategic gap [Liang and Ta, 1988].

(a) A large proportion of top executives have little or no experience in managing IT products and services. An inertia or technological phobia exists.

(b) There is a lack of insight into the tremendous potential benefits that can be derived from IT. Top managers do not understand that some of its benefits are difficult to assess in advance.

(c) There are no commonly accepted guidelines for measuring the contributions of IT. Top managers feel insecure when they cannot quantify the benefits that can be generated from IT applications.

(d) There is insufficient planning in utilizing IT and there is a wide disparity in the emphasis organizations place on its importance and utilization. In general, organizations concentrate on basic technology and avoid newer technology.

(e) Many organizations fail to realise the advantage of IT as a competitive strategic weapon.

## 3. Needs for planning

In Singapore, IT is normally utilized in organizations only in traditional areas. An organized plan does not exist and the idea of strategic exploitation of IT is not practised. Although awareness of IT's potential is expanding, its use so far is relatively low and expertise on the part of employees is scarce. A strategic gap exists between technological advancement and application. The signs and symptoms manifested are similar to those of Nolan's Expansion Stage [Nolan, 1974 & 1979].

Even though Nolan's stage-model was first proposed to explain the growth of electronic data processing, the growth of IT applications appears to have a similar profile. By providing an organized, systematic, and exploitative approach, minimizing the strategic gap, and identifying niches swiftly, the government is attempting to boost the fortunes of the entire IT industry. The expansion should also improve the entire economy.

This nationwide lift is worth examining. The thought and planning processes undertaken, the mustering of support from IT experts and users, and the grooming of an IT culture through awareness-building and education can be shared.

## 4. The study

This study examines Singapore's National Information Technology Plan (NITP) using Management Information Systems (MIS) frameworks, such as McFarlan's and McKenny's strategic grid model, Porter's value chain model and, in particular, an attempt to determine whether a transformation has occurred from the Expansion Stage to the Formalization Stage, and whether the niches and initial critical success factors (CSF) [Rockart, 1979] have expedited the plan.

## 5. The national IT plan

In 1985, Singapore was facing a recession. An economic committee was formed to review the progress of its economy and, more importantly, to identify new directions for future growth. IT was recognized as a significant resource for improving Singapore's business efficiency, productivity, and competitiveness in the world market. Several representatives from government bodies were requested to map the NITP.

An analysis of the plan indicates that its implementation strategy can be categorized into five strategic levels that provide an integral approach to IT utilization. These are:

(a) Strategic Building Block

(b) Industry Strategic Grid

(c) Industry Value Chain

(d) IT Risk and Opportunity Grid

(e) Extra Infrastructures

## 5.1. Strategic building block

An integrated seven-pronged approach was adopted; each prong is a strategic building block that serves as a catalyst to achieve the objective of exploiting IT for economic and social gain. These blocks, the first-level strategic frame, are: the IT industry, IT application, IT manpower, the information communication infrastructure, IT culture, the climate for creativity and entrepreneurship, and coordination and collaboration (see Table 1).

A strong IT industry is recognized as the main driving force needed to push the economy towards a higher level of performance. There is therefore a need to improve the local IT industry. The three strategies formulated to achieve a stronger IT industry are: (i) to establish Singapore as a centre for the development of niche IT products for the world market; (ii) to develop the indigenous capability of the local IT industry; and (iii) to encourage more IT multinational companies to set up their regional operation headquarters in Singapore.

In the area of IT application, all economic sectors must be encouraged to exploit IT to the fullest. Business organizations should automate and computerize their operations. Three strategies were developed to spearhead this operation: build up an extensive information infrastructure to enhance the value of IT; promote IT as a means of increasing productivity and business competitiveness; and extend the public sector computerization programme to include users in the private sector.

## 5.2. Industry strategic grid

Not all sectors of the economy can exploit IT in the same manner. The second-level strategic frame places the different industries into their respective strategic grid positions [McFarlan and McKenny, 1983]. The details are illustrated in Table 2.

In the strategic grid, there are four cells showing the relationships between industry dependence on IT and the impact of IT:

(a) Industries in the “Strategic” cell are highly dependent on IT as a CSF. For them day-to-day operations are associated with IT usage. An organization with better IT will have a competitive edge. In Singapore, such industries include computer, financial, industrial electronics, and telecommunication services.

(b) Those in the “Factory” cell are highly dependent on IT support for smooth operation. IT is essential for survival but it does not provide a competitive edge. IT is a way of life for these organizations as well as their competitors. Examples are the insurance, petrochemical, and pharmaceutical industries.

(c) Industries in the “Turnaround” cell do not depend on IT use to achieve their objectives at present, but their newest IT applications are expected to be vital for their strategic success; that is, their future competitiveness and survival depend on IT. In Singapore, the industries that are in this stage of development include advertising, market research, publishing, and legal services.

(d) Those in the “Support” cell do not and are not likely to depend on IT for survival. Examples are the textile and real estate industries.

## 5.3. Industry value chain

The next strategic frame identifies the value chain of the various industrial sectors. The model adopted is that of Porter's value chain model which examines the business operations for IT applications (Porter and Millar, 1985; Porter, 1985). An illustration of this approach in the manufacturing sector is shown in Table 3.

Three principles are adopted in the value chain model. First, organizations are analysed as a series of interrelated activities, where each activity contributes value to its portion of the business operation. Second, there are nine value activities and each is examined to determine how it uses IT to support its operation. Thirdly, because the value activities are interdependent, the way that an activity is carried out affects the cost and performance of the others. By managing these linkages, competitive advantages (such as lower cost, differentiation of product and service, and bigger market share) can be created.

## 5.4. IT risk and opportunity grid

IT has numerous technologies that are potentials for investment in the commercial sector, or appear rewarding enough to be investigated in research laboratories. The risk factor and potential opportunity of the various technological areas can be analyzed and their potentials weighted. The areas examined by the NITP include microelectronics, mass storage, optical fibre, input-output devices, CAD/CAM, cellular mobile radios, value-added-networks, local area networks, artificial intelligence, operating systems, software productivity tools, and smart cards.

## 5.5. Building extra infrastructure

Substantial extra efforts are needed to ensure that the NITP is a success. Huge amounts of funds, attractive incentives, and research and development are introduced to complement the plan. Some of the extras that form the five level strategic frame are illustrated in Table 5. First, in April 1986 the Singapore Information Technology Institute was set up with a fund of S\$21.5 (US\$12) million to be used over the first three years for training, research, and consulting on IT factors.

Second, in 1987 a small enterprise computerization programme was launched to provide incentives for small local firms to computerize. The scheme includes a one-year tax write-off for computer equipment, grants for computer consulting, and cheap loans for the purchase of computer hardware and software. Third, in 1988 a national IT research and development committee was established to plan and integrate the overall development and progress of IT research in Singapore. An intelligence unit was also initiated to monitor global developments and trends in this fast evolving field.

## 6. Impact of the NITP

The NITP is a comprehensive plan mapped to transform Singapore's newly industrialized economy into one that is high-tech-based. The systematic strategy furnished by the government has rendered the transformation smoother. In general, the acceptance and implementation of IT by organizations has become more organized and rapid. This is particularly true in the more traditional areas, such as CIS. The users have moved from the Expansion into the Formalization Stage.

On the newer frontiers such as Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Computer Integrated Manufacturing (CIM), progress has been slower [Liang and Ta, 1990; Liang, 1990]. However, there are some users that are beginning to actively exploit the newer IT [Teo, 1991]. The emphasis on the strategic exploitation of newer technologies has increased substantially during the past years. It is usually large organizations and government-related agencies that take the lead.

The five-level strategic frame approach in analyzing the NITP has thrown greater light on the impact of the plan. The identification of building blocks, the creation of the industry strategic grid, the determination of the industry value chain, the creation of the IT risk and opportunity grid, and the introduction of additional support incentives and infrastructures are the key factors contributing to the success of the NITP. The plan has better prepared business organizations, both technically and psychologically, for their use of

IT. The study has also shown that the various MIS frameworks are useful tools.

## 6.1. IT manpower development

In 1986, there were only 4,000 IT professionals in Singapore; this represented a severe shortage of IT experts. The NITP specifically highlighted the problem and action was taken to rectify it. The number of professional increased to about 10,000 in 1991, a 150% increase in five years. This rapid multiplication of IT professionals is primarily due to the well-charted manpower development programmes taking place in tertiary and other training institutions. Many IT personnel are also trained overseas. At present, about 6% to 7% of the professionals have postgraduate qualifications, and a substantial number of them are working on the technology frontier, involved in research and development activities.

## 6.2. IT industry performance

The IT industry itself is one of the key components that the NITP is actively harnessing. The evolution of this industry directly affects the other IT-related industries, and therefore great attention has been given to it. With this extensive emphasis, IT growth rate has been extremely rapid, varying from 26% to 28% per annum in both software and hardware industries. Total industrial revenue was S\$561 (US\$ 320) million in 1985, S\$1.065 (US\$ 0.6) billion in 1988, and about S\$1.6 (US\$ 0.9) billion in 1991. Such a major increase in the IT industry's revenue is, to a large extent, due to the systematic and organized approach stipulated in the NITP.

## 6.3. Research and development advancement

In 1985, Singapore was basically importing most forms of IT. Now, a substantial amount of research and development is taking place in areas such as expert systems, artificial neural networks, multimedia IS, Chinese Language computing, supercomputing, voice processing, and image processing using funds provided by both the public and private sectors. Local commercial software packages in areas such as Chinese Language computing and music are now available, and AI systems of various forms are also making their ways into business corporations. Within a period of five years, the NITP has transformed Singapore from an IT importing nation to one that has great potential for export.

## 7. IT2000

Five years after the formulation of the NITP, a steering committee was again convened to map out an even more comprehensive plan, code-named IT2000, to facilitate an even higher level of IT usage. While the NITP has been perceived as conceptual, IT2000 is going to be action-oriented, with well-defined programmes and goals. Intensive needs analysis studies have already been carried out in ten major industrial sectors. Subsequently, more sectors will be included before a detailed plan is formulated.

To provide the necessary strategic thrust, IT2000 has established a series of basic aims, which include the following:

(a) To make Singapore a high value-added node in the global IT industry;

(b) To exploit IT extensively for sustained competitive advantage;

(c) To develop a rich mix of IT expertise;

(d) To commercialize IT innovations resulting from local research and development efforts;

(e) To implement IT projects to improve the nation's quality of life; and

(f) To develop an integrated network infrastructure to support one-stop, non-stop services'.

Such a mapping out of a new plan is a clear indication that the government is placing strong emphasis on IT as an economic generator. It is also an endorsement of the NITP's success. IT is and will be an economic strategic weapon for our small and internationalistic nation. Frequent updating of the IT plan is essential for Singapore to remain competitive worldwide. This new document is a national level IT plan from which new parts can be adopted into the strategic plan of the various industrial sectors. Similarly, business organizations can look forward to IT2000 for new directions, and map their corporate strategy with a better IT dimension.

## 8. Conclusion

This study was an investigation of the strategy adopted by the NITP on a nationwide basis with respect to a few MIS frameworks. It reveals that the awareness, impetus and performance of IT and IT-related industries have increased substantially from 1986 to 1991. Such a systematic approach at the national level is a quick and efficient means of utilizing IT; random or ad hoc utilization would be less advantageous.

Singapore's experience is an experiment that can be analyzed by other countries who wish to exploit IT. However, it must be noted that Singapore is a small country with a land size of about 640 sq km, a population of about 2.6 million, and a per capital income of about S\$16,000 (US\$9,000). Such a situation may not occur elsewhere. However, a state or provincial unit may still be able to gain from such a experience.
