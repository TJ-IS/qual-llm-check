# Determining information system usage: Some issues and examples

Bernadette Szajna

Texas Christian University, Fort Worth, TX, USA

While the utilization of an information system (IS) is widely regarded as an indicator of its success, effectiveness, or acceptance, past research has found inconsistent associations between usage and other measures of system success. There are a number of potential factors that could influence the usage of an IS and there are unique problems in its measurement; these make validation of the measures difficult. One area in particular has apparently been neglected: establishing the relevance of the way of measuring usage to the task or study. Data obtained from a laboratory experiment on user expectations illustrate the necessity of choosing a utilization measure that is relevant to the task involved. The data also indicate that behavioral and perceptual variables of IS evaluation ought to be considered separately when determining the level of IS success.

Keywords: Information system usage; Information system performance; Information system satisfaction; Decision performance; User attitudes; Information system success; Information system implementation.

In both the practical and research arenas, use of an information system (IS) is widely regarded as an indicator of its success, effectiveness, or acceptance. But past research indicates that there are inconsistent associations, or even none, between use of an IS and other measures of system success $[29]$ . While much attention has been given to the construction and validation of attitudinal measures of system success $[e.g., 1]$ , little notice has been paid to a valid measure of system utilization with a few exceptions.

Use of an information system is necessary before the success or failure of a system can be determined. Lucas [18] encouraged the measurement of IS usage, because if a system is not used, it cannot be considered successful. Likewise, Fuerst and Cheney [10] claimed that unless a decision support system (DSS) is used, it could have no benefit to an organization. Yet, it does not follow that a used system should be considered successful nor that a used DSS would benefit the organization. It is easy, but not necessarily correct, to assume that usage is indicative of success: e.g., Bruwer [4] acknowledges that one of the most attractive indicators of system success from a measurement standpoint is system use, but he also admits that there are times where considerable usage is not a sign of success.

Using data obtained from a study of the effects of users' expectations on their performance and perceptions, this paper (1) outlines the problems associated with the concept and measurement of system utilization, (2) addresses the importance of finding a relevant measure of system usage and, (3) suggests the merit of evaluating an IS from both the perceptual and behavioral/performance standpoints separately [29].

## Potential effects on IS usage

Ginzberg [11] claims that use, by itself, is an inadequate measure of effectiveness. In a study of 30 IS projects, Ginzberg found that one third had dissatisfied users and only information generated by the system was available. He concluded that when users were dissatisfied, the IS should be considered unsuccessful. Cheney and Dickson [5] echo this concern and suggest that there ought to be alternate means of obtaining the information if usage of the IS is to be defined as an indicator of system effectiveness. Related to such alternative sources of information is the relative accessibility of the information. Culnan [6] and O'Reilly [22] were able to identify accessibility as being related to usage. Therefore, it may be desirable to consider the accessibility of the information when measuring usage.

Other reasons for the use of an IS include political and social pressures. Managers may use the IS to please superiors, in answer to a demand or even force, or to conform to the behavior of their peers $[28]$ because of fear, envy, or curiosity $[24]$ . Even the qualifications of a superior were found to be a determinant of IS usage $[20]$ . Mere self-protection in justifying decisions or the level of environmental uncertainty are two other factors that could affect IS usage.

Some individual characteristics of IS users have been empirically shown to be associated with different levels of information or information system usage. Experience with information systems $[10,23]$ and training were found to have a positive effect on the level of IS usage while computer anxiety had a negative association with usage $[12]$ . A number of IS investigators have indicated, without much consistency, that the cognitive style of IS users leads to different types and amounts of information usage $[6,28]$ . Age and educational level have also been identified as possible influences.

## Difficulties involved in usage measurement

## Level of usage

Besides the factors which can influence the level of IS usage, unique difficulties are encountered in measuring it. One of the questions the practitioner or researcher must confront is: "What is the ideal, or at least sufficient, level of use for a successful or effective IS?" For instance, an IS may be used once a year but save millions of dollars. The life cycle of the IS should also be considered: during the early, learning period, usage would probably exceed that at a later time, as the user becomes more proficient. However, these factors are not generally incorporated when reporting IS usage.

## Voluntariness

Lucas's [19] notion that use of an IS should be voluntary if used in IS evaluation has been a much-referenced claim. Although an organization's policy may be that IS use is voluntary, if no alternative information source is available, or political/social influences exist, then the decision to utilize the system may not be truly at the user's command.

Robey [25] stated that use of information was voluntary in his study of sales personnel, and that use was measured as the number of times a salesperson updated his/her accounts in the year. The salespeople were, however, “encouraged” to update their accounts on a regular basis though they were required to update their customer accounts only once per year. Consequently, the researcher might find it necessary to question the degree of freedom in use of the IS because of the political and social implications of the word “encouraged.” Even in DeSanctis’s [8] controlled laboratory experiment where subjects were led to believe that use of a decision support system was voluntary, up to 20% of the responses were omitted from the analysis because subjects felt that usage was not voluntary.

Kim and Lee [14] did recognize the value of establishing voluntariness and included it in their measure of IS usage. They determined usage as the product of the frequency and voluntariness of use as self-reported by the user.

## Relevance

An important point seldom considered is precisely what measure is relevant to the study or the decision-making task $[15]$ . A number of studies measure usage by self-report, but this can produce a “halo” effect when other perceptual measures are involved [16]. Ettema [9] found that self-report usage correlated with two other measures at 0.29 and 0.34, leading him to conclude that self-report and actual usage were related but not measures of exactly the same thing. Pentland [23] found that self-report usage strongly overstated actual usage; therefore, such data would tend to be less accurate than objective usage data.

Studies also measure usage objectively by such methods as, the number of reports requested, connect time, and frequency of computer use. Benbasat and Schroeder [3] claim that sometimes, as usage (e.g., the number of report requests) increases, the data processing cost and the time to analyze them increase. Consequently, with some usage measures an increase in usage can increase the time spent in decision making and decrease the efficiency of the process. Table 1 is a sample of methods of measuring IS usage employed in empirical research: it is important to note their variety. The existence of a variable in an empirical study is usually rationalized by the researcher, but the particular method of measuring usage is seldom shown to be relevant to the task or study. Trice and Treacy [30] make a strong case for taking into consideration the goal of the study when system usage is employed as a dependent variable: if an empirical study involves a decision-making task, then the measure should be relevant to it. The importance of this notion will be illustrated in a laboratory experiment.

## Methods

A longitudinal laboratory experiment was performed using 158 undergraduate business students enrolled in a required MIS course. The main purpose of this study was to assess the effects of user expectations on their perceptions and performance but the data gathered in this study is also relevant to several issues of IS utilization.

Over a two-week period, the subjects, playing the role of regional sales managers, made three separate budget allocation decisions using a computerized information system. In each of the three decision periods their usage, decision performance, perception of decision performance, and user satisfaction were measured. This data is used here to emphasize some important issues of IS usage.

The decision making task was based on one used by Benbasat, Dexter and Todd [2] in a series of three experiments: the “Brand Manager’s Allocation Problem” [21]. An assigned promotional budget must be allocated by the subject to three sales areas in an attempt to optimize the total profit for the three areas (the regional profit). Each of the sales areas has an independent response function that relates promotional dollars to profit in the form:

$$
P R O F I T = t _ {\min} + t _ {s} \left(a x ^ {b} / \left(1 + a x ^ {b}\right)\right) - x,
$$

where x = promotional dollars allocated to the sales area, $t_{min}$ = sales in the territory when x = 0, $t_{s}$ = saturation sales minus $t_{min}$ in the territory and, a, b are parameters.

Each decision-making scenario had a different promotional budget and response function parameters, and hence a different optimal solution. For the optimal solution the subjects were to allocate the assigned promotional budget to the three sales areas in order to obtain the highest possible regional profit.

The information system for the decision-making task was an application developed using Lotus 1-2-3. It was menu-based system and provided both a display of information and a way of entering the decision. The information system contained two sets of data in two presentation formats: tabular and graphic. The historical data consisted of past decisions on promotional dollars, sales, and profit. When a decision was made, it was added to the next period's historical data. The “functional” data was the expected profit for each of the three sales areas, based on the number of promotional dollars allocated to that region. The purpose of this data was to allow the subject to determine the optimal allocation of promotional dollars.

Decision performance was measured using the formula:
PROFIT

## (subject allocation profit-zero allocation profit)

## $= \frac{1}{(\text{optimal allocation profit - zero allocation profit})}$

Thus, when a subject achieved the optimal allocation, the decision performance variable was 1.00. User satisfaction was measured with a 15-item questionnaire, a modified version of the Jenkins-Ricketts [13] user satisfaction instrument. It exhibited reliabilities for the three decision periods that were 0.88, 0.92, and 0.93, respectively. Perceived decision performance was measured with a 5-item questionnaire based on an instrument developed by Sanders [27] to determine the success of a decision support system. The reliabilities for the perceived decision performance instrument for the three decision periods are: 0.89, 0.92, and 0.92, respectively. Information system usage was tracked by unobtrusive computer logging. The information system was designed to record which reports were used for how long.

## Data analysis

## Usage and decision performance

The ultimate goal of an effective information system is to provide support for quality decision making. Decision-making performance is difficult, if not impossible, to measure in a practical sense. Therefore, surrogate measures of IS success are often used, one being IS usage. However, the relationship between decision performance and usage can be complex. Lucas [17] suggests that, in some cases, use of a system may determine performance, and in other instances, performance may be a determinant of use.

Two correlations are provided for each decision period: (a) usage with performance in the same decision period and (b) decision performance with usage in the subsequent decision period. The correlations of two measures of IS usage (the number of reports used and the time spent using the reports) and decision performance are shown in Table 2. There is only one significant correlation (at p < 0.05) between the number of different reports used and decision performance. For the decision-making task used in this study, it would not be expected that decision performance would be related to the number of reports used. On the other hand, for all three decision periods, a significant correlation existed between decision performance and the amount of time spent using the reports. However, this particular measure of usage does not convey much knowledge about usage patterns or the data selection behavior possible in this information system.

One important consideration about the relevance of the usage measure to the task is the actual use of the information generated by the system in the decision-making process. Robey and Markus [26] note that a report may have little value for decision making, but a weighty report sitting on a manager's desk may appear to be evidence that a decision was based on hard, computer-generated information rather than on intuition. Barkin and Dickson [1] went one step further in this analysis, noting that outputs may not be examined even if the decision maker receives them. Moreover, even if the outputs are examined, they may not have influenced the action of the decision maker. Therefore, it becomes desirable to measure IS usage in decision-making scenarios where the information is used in the decision-making task.

Information value in decision making is a difficult variable to assess, yet, it is the measure most relevant in evaluating the effectiveness of the IS. Cheney and Dickson [5] claim that system utilization refers to the incorporation of system-generated information into the decision-making process. The method they have used to measure system utilization includes a self-assessment of the use of information. It was an attempt to employ a measure of IS usage that is more relevant to a decision-making study. Barkin and Dickson also subscribe to the decision-making view of utilization. They make a case for using data selection patterns as the usage variable.

For this experiment, the decision makers could choose either historical data or functional data. The data selection pattern needed to demonstrate use of the IS in this decision-making process is quite easy to determine. While the historical data provides a record of past performance, it does little to aid the decision maker in achieving an optimal solution. On the other hand, the functional data supplies the relationship between the promotional dollars allocated and the profit to be achieved. Therefore, use of the functional data is necessary for an optimal solution. Table 3 presents a correlation analysis of decision performance with the amount of time spent using the historical data and the amount of time spent using the functional data.

The amount of time spent on the reports (as in Table 2) is merely the sum of the time spent on the historical data and the time spent on the functional data. But when the time spent on the historical data and functional data are investigated separately, it becomes clear that a specific data selection pattern is crucial to performance in this task. The time the subjects spent using the historical data suggests a relatively weak negative association between historical data usage and decision performance, while the time they spent on the functional data demonstrates a significant positive relationship between functional data usage and decision performance.

Lucas [18] experienced similar results when he measured usage of an online information storage and retrieval system. By measuring the number of sessions and displays accessed by a librarian, he found that usage was negatively associated with information-user attitudes; however, when he measured usage by the number of sessions and displays accessed by the information user, he discovered a positive association with user attitudes. If Lucas had chosen to use only one of these usage measures, much knowledge of IS usage and its relationship to user attitudes would have been missing. Likewise, in this study, the result of counting only different reports as a usage measure, would have yielded a conclusion that there is no relationship between usage and decision performance. Without careful analysis of the usage measure the researcher might have concluded that usage is not associated with decision performance or is negatively associated with user attitudes.

To further investigate the relevance of usage to the decision-making task, Table 4 shows the correlation of decision performance with the percentage of the total time spent on the historical and functional data. In the correlation analysis of historical data time with decision performance, the correlations were negative but tended to be weak (as in Table 3). However, when the correlations involve the percentage of the total time spent on the historical data, they are all negative and relatively strong. This approach to the analysis of IS usage leads to a stronger conclusion: use of the historical data is negatively related to decision performance, particularly if it is the percentage of the total time spent on the historical data. Significant positive associations exist for decision performance with the percentage of time spent on the functional data.

## Usage and perceptions

An abundance of research attempts to correlate the use of an IS with other measures of system effectiveness. While one of the most prevalent measures of system effectiveness investigated is user attitudes (e.g., user satisfaction), the relationship between user attitudes and use of an IS has not been established. Some studies support a positive relationship [e.g., 3], some find no relationship [5], and others even indicate a negative relationship [e.g., 26]. Srinivasan notes that users who spend long periods of time at each decision-making session with the information system tend to see the system as contributing unfavorably to their decision-making task. He therefore concludes that researchers have to be cautious when using surrogate measures of system success, because with certain classes of systems a strong positive association may exist between the use and attitudes, while in others this relationship may be non-existent. Robey claims that IS usage is dependent on the user's perceptions of its impact on performance. In our experiment, both user satisfaction and their perceptions of decision performance were measured.

Table 5 shows the associations between all the usage variables and the perceptual variables of user satisfaction and perception of decision performance. Few significant associations were found. While IS usage, user satisfaction, and perceptions of performance have all been employed as surrogate measures of information system success, there are inconsistencies in their interrelationships. It is because of these kinds of inconsistencies that Srinivasan suggests that it may be wise to evaluate the results of the behavioral (i.e., IS usage) measures separately from the perceptual (e.g., user satisfaction) measures of system effectiveness.

## Conclusion

The practitioner or researcher who wishes to use a measure of system usage as a surrogate of system success, effectiveness or acceptance, should be particularly cautious in choosing a relevant measure of IS usage. Different independent variables will influence IS usage in different ways. Further, when measuring IS usage, it would be wise to include a measure of the users' perceptions. Variables can then be analyzed individually in order to arrive at a statement of system success. If it is found that both are favorable, then statements about system acceptance are valid. On the other hand, if either one is unfavorable (e.g., low levels of usage, dissatisfied users), the researcher should investigate the relevance of the methods used to measure the variables and analyze other factors, external to the task, that may be influencing the outcomes of the variables.

For such an analysis it may be necessary to consider what alternate sources of information are available and their accessibility. The investigator should be aware of any political or social influences that might exist influencing the use or non-use of the IS. Individual characteristics of the users ought to be considered potential influences. If use of the information system is not seen as voluntary, the validity of employing usage as a dependent variable is questionable. The ideal, or at least sufficient, amount of usage necessary for decision effectiveness may also be a factor to consider.
