# Chargeback and maturity of IS use

Craig A. VanLengen and James N. Morgan

Northern Arizona University, Flagstaff, AZ, USA

A survey of IS executives was conducted to assess their use of chargeback systems and the maturity of IS management and use. The majority of respondents reported the use of some form of chargeback system that recovered the costs of providing IS services. Findings indicate a positive relationship between sophisticated chargeback systems and most measures of maturity of IS management and use. However, a significant negative relationship was found between level of chargeback and measures of strategic use of IS.

Keywords: Chargeback; IS maturity; IS management; IS cost recovery; Value of information; IS stages of maturity; Levels of chargeback; Strategic IS; IRM; Strategic management; End user involvement.

## Introduction

Chargeback mechanisms for information systems services are now widely used. It has been argued that the use of chargeback systems leads to improved efficiency in the use of IS resources and to more active end user involvement in the IS function. Several researchers have proposed models of the development of chargeback systems that are related to Nolan's $[17,18)$ theory that organizations go through stages of maturity in their use of IS. It has been argued that chargeback systems become more useful and more sophisticated as the organization's use of IS matures.

However, several researchers have expressed concern that the use of chargeback systems might tend to stifle experimental and innovative uses of IS technology, e.g. [6]. If true, this could suggest that chargeback systems are not supportive of two important components of mature organizational IS use: effective use of emerging IS technologies and their strategic use.

Attempts to verify that complex chargeback systems are most likely to be found in firms whose use of IS is sophisticated or “mature” have been quite limited and inconclusive. This paper examines the relationship between the use of chargeback systems and of other IS management methods.

## Chargeback systems and the maturity of IS

Many researchers have indicated that one of the major benefits of chargeback systems in IS is that they cause increased communication and interaction between users and the IS staff. Benefits expected from this increased interaction include causing: more efficient and effective use of organizational resources in the provision of IS $[14]$ , users to take more responsibility for ensuring that IS generates the expected benefits $[13]$ , and users to gain a better understanding of the costs and requirements necessary for the use of IS. Empirical studies have generally found that the use of chargeback for IS does tend to increase user involvement $[7,8,15]$ .

Some user knowledge of IS technology is needed for chargeback systems to be effective. Unsophisticated users may be hindered in their use by sophisticated chargeback systems that do not fit the organizational environment $[16]$ . However, ideally chargeback is instituted and modified to provide more sophisticated information for managerial control as a natural evolution, occurring when the user is ready and the previous system is no longer effective in the decision making process $[3]$ .

McKinnon and Kallman [16] developed a systematic model relating the evolution of chargeback to that in other areas of IS management and use. Chargeback systems are described as evolving from allocation, to average cost, to standard cost, and finally, to flexible pricing. Allocation systems provide very little control or accountability for costs, but simply inform the user and IS management of the cost of providing services after they have been delivered. The average cost is a simple direct-chargeback method where information systems costs are included in the users budget. In a standard cost system, charges or prices are based on estimates of costs at a reasonable level of activity. User and IS management must work together in planning levels of activity and service. Finally, flexible pricing is a market-based approach, where prices are used to regulate demand and provide cost recovery.

This evolution is related to evolution in IS technology, MIS maturity, and the role of the chief information officer (CIO). As use of IS technology evolves organizations are described as using increasingly complex forms of technology and becoming increasingly dependent upon that technology. MIS maturity may be described in terms of Nolan's stages from initiation through maturity. Finally, the role of the CIO is described as evolving from reactive to proactive.

McKinnon and Kallman argue that evolution in all of these areas tends to be parallel. Thus, a flexible pricing system would most likely be used by an organization that employs complex IS technology, is at the maturity stage of MIS use, and has a proactive CIO. On the other hand, no chargeback or an allocation chargeback system would be likely to be used by an organization that employs relatively simple IS technology, is at the initiation or contagion stage of MIS use, and has no CIO or a CIO acting in a reactive mode.

A number of other researchers have supported the idea that chargeback systems evolve from primitive to more sophisticated systems without formally tying that evolution to a stage model of IS use. Henry [10] sees chargeback as evolving through different levels of maturity starting with use sensitive data and costs and maturing to a level where information services guarantees a certain level of service and quality. Choudhury, Sircar, and Rao [4] agree that at the maturity stage an organization would most likely use a profit-center transfer pricing approach to chargeback.

While levels of chargeback systems have been discussed or alluded to in many papers, Allen [1] provides the most complete and well documented set of classifications and forms the basis for the classification of chargeback systems used in this paper. His classifications, listed in order from the lowest level of evolution or sophistication to the highest are:

1. No chargeback for IS costs,

2. IS costs allocated along with corporate overhead,

3. Use based memo billings, but IS costs not actually charged to departmental budget,

4. Billing for “fair share” (based on use) of costs at the end of the accounting period,

5. Billing based on budgeted rates with period end adjustments for over/under allocation,

6. Billing based on budgeted rates without period end adjustments,

7. Billing based on standard rates and negotiated prices,

8. Billing based on functional prices (price per unit).

The ordering of this classification scheme is based on the usefulness of the information provided in managing and controlling IS use. The assumption is that the information provided becomes progressively more useful as: the charges become “real” enough to encourage meaningful use of the system, the rates are known in advance and not subject to modification, and the rates are expressed on a per unit basis. Although, Allen’s model provides the most detailed set of classifications available, the structure of his classifications is supported by the work of many other authors $[11,12,24]$ .

The expected relationship between the use and level of chargeback systems and maturity of information systems does not have clear cut empirical support. Benbasat et al. [2] reviewed studies relating to Nolan's stage model of the development of the IS function in organizations and found little empirical support for Nolan's hypothesis. The Benbasat study specifically found a lack of statistically significant correlation between the use of chargeback systems and measures of organizational maturity.

In addition, several authors have suggested that chargeback systems may tend to limit the ability of organizations to use IS strategically. If true, this would represent a serious limitation, since strategic management of IS is an important and necessary component of mature IS use. Concern has been expressed that chargeback systems may restrain requests and development of experimental and innovative systems, which have higher risks $[20,23]$ . Using information systems for competitive purposes as part of a strategic initiative is very risky but it may be very necessary; strategic use of information systems should be based on competitive advantage and not on the user charges. Young $[25]$ feels that if information services become only a service-for-hire, they it will not be able to lead the organization in the competitive use of information systems. Experimental or innovative ideas might not be developed since there is too much risk involved, making for low expected payoff.

Despite these criticisms, most researchers feel that chargeback systems develop in an evolutionary manner and many continue to tie the development of chargeback systems to maturity in organizational IS use.

## Measures of mature IS use and management

A variety of features or measures could be used as indicators of organizational maturity in the application and management of IS. In our empirical work, six factors are used as measures of IS maturity. The factors selected were adapted from the McKinnon and Kallman model. These include:

a. the presence of a strong CIO,

b. the management of information as a corporate resource,

c. the effective management of new technologies and their diffusion within the organization,

d. involvement of the IS department in strategic planning and strategic use of IS,

e. active end user involvement in IS planning and development, and

f. effective functional management of the IS function,

In addition, each of these features has been described as an element of information resource management (IRM) or advocated as an element that should be added to the IRM concept. A review paper by Guimaraes [9] finds that various authors see IRM as either the management of information as a resource, the effective functional management of the IS function, or the management of emerging IS technology in the organization. He also notes that the importance of the presence and organizational placement of the CIO has been an important concern in the IRM literature. In addition, recent IRM related articles [5,19,22] have argued that strategic management needs to be an important element of IRM. Zijlker [26] argues for a view of IRM that focuses on decentralization, end user support, and the effective management of IS resources in an environment in which both the resources themselves and the responsibility for managing them are decentralized. Other recent articles have focused on end user concerns and responsibilities with respect to IRM [21] and IRM as a coordinating mechanism between IS departments and end users. Thus, the six features are not merely components of a particular stage model of IS development, but are seen as key elements of the IRM concept in its broadest context.

An empirical analysis of chargeback and maturity of IS use

In the Spring of 1990, a survey and follow-up were mailed to IS executives representing 87 organizations in the Phoenix, Arizona area. All are members of the Society for Information Management. Thirty nine usable responses were received, representing a response rate of 45 percent. Responses to a set of demographic questions included in the survey indicate that the respondents are mostly IS executives (84%) who have been in the IS profession for over 20 years (69%) and work for organizations with over 1000 employees (67%). A very diverse set of industries is represented among the respondents.

Beyond demographics, the survey asked respondents to categorize their chargeback systems, if any, for IS services. ## Analysis of survey results

Table 1 summarizes the types of chargeback systems used by the respondent organizations. The first question was designed to categorize respondents by their type of chargeback system, based on Allen's classification system. For the purposes of analysis, responses to this question are assumed to represent an ordinal ranking of the evolution of chargeback systems. That is, higher numbered responses to this question are assumed to represent chargeback systems that are more mature or in a later stage of evolution than lower numbered responses.

Among respondents to this survey, several had no chargeback system and about a third had only an overhead allocation or memo system that does not actually charge divisions according to their use. About half of the respondents used some form of average cost pricing or a functional pricing system.

For organizations with chargeback systems, the vast majority base their charges on full costs while very few use priority charges or off-peak discounts. This perhaps reflects the changing nature of IS expenses. Early chargeback systems were designed primarily to manage the shared use of large centralized computing hardware and covered only operating costs. Differential peak and off-peak pricing is particularly important to the effective allocation of large-scale centralized hardware resources. As this type of expense has become a relatively less important component of total IS costs there is less incentive to impose differential pricing schemes and more to cover full costs. The final question was designed to determine whether organizations are beginning to charge departments for a share of the costs incurred by others in collecting and maintaining organizational data. This type of charge is appropriate if the organization treats information as a corporate resource and wants the information to be appropriately priced. Clearly this type of charge is not in wide use.

Respondents were also asked to use Likert scale responses to rate their organizations' utilization of a number of management techniques and organizational structures; results are summarized in Table 2. Respondents indicated whether they strongly agreed – SA, agreed – A, were neutral – N, disagreed – D, or strongly disagreed – SD with each statement. The statements were designed to evaluate the degree to which organizations have implemented policies or activities associated with the six measures of IS maturity. The distribution of responses is shown.

The distribution of responses indicates that most of the measures are widely used. Over half of the respondents either agreed or strongly agreed with every statement except that dealing with the establishment of a distinct group within the IS staff for the evaluation and introduction of new IS technologies (\#5). While many of the statements describe the types of management procedures and structures used without directly measuring their effectiveness, the generally positive responses indicate that most of the respondent organizations are using IS management procedures associated with IS maturity.

The final column indicates the correlation of responses to the statement with the type of chargeback system used. The statistic is Spearman's rho, which is appropriate for ordinal data. For the purposes of this analysis, responses to the statements were converted to a numeric scale with affirmative responses receiving a higher numeric rating (SA = 5, A = 4, N = 3, D = 2, SD = 1). The responses are correlated with those to the first question of Table 1, which was designed to provide numerical ratings proportional to the level of evolution or sophistication of the chargeback system. Higher numeric values for the chargeback type represent more highly evolved systems. Thus, if more highly evolved chargeback systems are a characteristic of mature IS utilization and management, there should be a positive correlation with the type of chargeback system.

The results show uniformly positive correlations for three of the IS maturity measures. There is strong support for the idea that highly evolved chargeback systems are correlated with higher levels of end user involvement in IS management. Positive correlation coefficients are found for all four statements in this category, and the correlation is statistically significant at the 0.05 level for the use of steering committees and the level of end user involvement in those steering committees. Positive correlations are also found for measures of management of new technologies, although only the presence of a “systematic procedure for monitoring and evaluating new IS technologies” is significantly correlated, and only at the 0.1 level. The two statements relating to management of information as a resource also have positive correlation with more evolved chargeback systems, but these correlations are not significant.

Negative correlations were found for some statements associated with the three remaining IS maturity measures. The level of chargeback system was slightly negatively related to both the presence of a CIO and having a CIO who reports directly to the CEO, but neither was statistically significant.

With respect to the use of strategic management, the heads of IS in organizations with highly evolved chargeback systems were slightly more likely than the others to be heavily involved in the strategic planning process in their organizations. However, the level of chargeback use was strongly negatively correlated with the “development of strategic opportunities for the use of information systems” as a part of the strategic planning of the organization. This tends to support the concerns that chargeback systems might tend to limit the innovative uses of IS technology that are most likely to provide strategic advantage.

With respect to functional management in the IS department, results are mixed. Advanced chargeback systems were found to be positively correlated to the use of three common functional management techniques: project management, financial budgets, and quality assurance. However, advanced chargeback systems were negatively correlated with the use of planning models and the use of strategic planning within the IS department. This would seem to suggest that chargeback systems, while tending to support or require the use of measures for cost control and quality assurance in the management of IS projects, also tend to create uncertainties for the IS department. This may make it difficult for them to plan and may particularly hamper long run, strategic planning.

Table 3 examines how the level of chargeback system correlates with the structure of IS and its relationship with other departments in the firm. These include: the degree to which the firm uses outside service centers to provide IS service and the degree of centralization of its IS function. Highly evolved chargeback systems may be more necessary in firms which allow users to purchase IS services externally; they must be able to choose which services to acquire externally and which to acquire internally. Similarly, there may be more need for advanced chargeback systems in firms whose IS resources are somewhat decentralized. End user involvement and choice will be greater and thus, perhaps, there will be a greater need for a sophisticated chargeback system.

In general the respondent organizations contracted for very few external IS services. Only 3 of the firms contracted for more than 10 percent of their IS services. The correlation between these measures and the type of chargeback system was as expected. The level of chargeback was positively correlated with the percentage of IS services contracted with outside service centers, and significantly negatively correlated with the percentage of processing centralized within the organization. Thus, the study supports the idea that advanced chargeback systems are more likely to be used in firms that allow user departments to contract with outside service centers for IS services, and in firms whose computer processing is decentralized.

## Summary

This paper presents the results of an empirical study designed to evaluate the extent to which the use of advanced chargeback systems is associated with mature IS use and management in organizations. The results generally tend to support this association with some important exceptions: firms with advanced chargeback systems are less likely than others to use IS technology strategically, and less likely to use planning models in managing the IS department; also firms with a strong CIO may be less likely than others to use advanced chargeback systems.

However, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The sample size is small and limited in scope, and many of the correlations found were not statistically significant. In addition, the survey reflects only the views of IS executives. User managers might have very different views.

The results do suggest a potential problem with chargeback systems. They may inhibit effective long term planning in IS and may make it more difficult to win approval of strategic uses of IS technology. Also, there may be a need to modify chargeback structure in ways that ensure that IS departments have the long term commitments from users required for effective planning of the IS function.
