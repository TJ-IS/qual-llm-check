# Reducing bias in users' evaluations of information systems

Kieran Mathieson

Oakland University, Rochester, MI, USA

Systematic biases occur when users evaluate an information system (IS). Such biases can have serious consequences for those involved in the development and acquisition of an IS. Some simple techniques are discussed for reducing evaluation bias. By using these techniques, system developers and managers could improve the quality of their decisions.

Keywords: Evaluation; Attitudes; Beliefs; User satisfaction; Bias; Accuracy.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers have studied how users form opinions about, or evaluate, information systems (IS). The evaluations serve two main purposes. First, they help individuals select the IS they use. Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw [2] and Mathieson [5] found that users' evaluations affect the adoption of IS. The more positively an individual evaluates an IS, the more likely that person is to use it. Moreover, their opinion of an IS may also determine whether others adopt it. For example, a manager might choose a word processor for his or her department based on an employee's opinions.

Second, evaluations help guide IS development. Prototyping, for example, is based on a cycle of user evaluation and system modification. A developer builds a prototype, and hands it to a user for evaluation. The user then suggests changes to the prototype. The developer alters the prototype and returns it to the user, and the cycle continues. Clearly, user evaluations influence the direction of the project. Other development techniques also rely on user evaluation. For example, in Martin's [4] Rapid Application Development methodology, user teams evaluate everything from system requirements to general design to the final IS.

Errors in evaluations can cause expensive mistakes. Consider the following scenario:

A manager asks an employee with significant computing expertise to evaluate several spreadsheet packages. The employee recommends a package that he or she feels best meets the task needs, given his or her particular skills. On adopting the package, the manager finds that other employees require extensive training before they can use it, and some are so intimidated that they avoid the package when they can. Further, while the package offers good support for tasks performed by the employee who originally evaluated it, it is apparently poor for tasks that other employees perform.

In this example, the employee's evaluation was valid from his or her own point of view. If the objective had been to choose a package for use by that employee alone, the evaluation would have been useful and accurate. However, that was not the manager's objective. He or she wanted a spreadsheet that would be suitable for the entire department. From the manager's point of view, the evaluation was incorrect and led to an expensive mistake.

This example involved system acquisition, but the same problems can occur in IS development. Suppose an analyst designs reports based on the requirements expressed by only one individual. While the reports help that person, they may be difficult for others to use. They might need expensive modification, increasing the total cost of the IS and reducing the credibility of its developers.

Firms that gather user evaluations generally report improvements in their IS and in the relationships between users and IS professionals [1]. However, many firms do not make systematic evaluations of their IS. The information they gather may be biased, that is, it may not accurately represent the quality of their IS. This can have serious consequences for firms, users, and IS professionals.

Past research has identified the causes of bias in evaluations. For instance, some people may criticize an IS when they try to use it for tasks that it was not designed to support. Others may praise a system because a superior was involved in the project, even though the final IS is not really useful. Still others might criticize an IS that automates a valued skill, even though it is effective. In fact, the more effective the IS is, the greater the threat, and the more they may attack it.

Many of these problems can be controlled. While it is difficult to eliminate bias, it can be reduced by using some fairly simple techniques. IS professionals who currently measure user evaluations or are considering doing so, should apply the methods discussed below.

## 2. How users evaluate information systems

Consider a situation where a “rater” (a single individual) examines an “information system” and produces an “evaluation” that is reported to the “sponsor.” An “information system” (IS) is a set of computer programs designed to help its user perform tasks, or a model of such a set (e.g., a prototype). An “evaluation” is a subset of a rater’s beliefs about an IS. Beliefs can be specific, such as “the regional sales total should be highlighted,” or general, such as “the system is somewhat useful” (Mathieson and Ryan [7]).

Raters report evaluations to the “sponsor,” the person or group who will use the information. This might be a developer constructing a new IS or a manager choosing a system for his or her department. When an individual evaluates an IS he or she is considering adopting, the rater and the sponsor are the same person.

Raters are intelligent and goal-directed, able to predict the consequences of their actions. There are a number of goals that raters pursue when evaluating an IS. These goals relate to the outcome of the evaluation process (the evaluation) as well as the process itself. First, raters could be interested in accurately judging the system's support of particular tasks: these are task-support goals. If the IS is good, raters are willing to say it is good. If it is poor, they will say it is poor. Before the evaluation session begins, the rater is unbiased, having no proprietary interest in the outcome.

Second, raters might be biased towards a particular evaluation even before examining the IS: this an outcome goal. Some raters make an evaluation that is in their best interest. They might prefer either a positive or negative evaluation, depending on their motivations. For example, a rater who feels threatened by a new system might decide to evaluate it negatively in the hope that it will not be adopted. The rater makes this decision before even seeing the IS. He or she enters the evaluation session determined to provide a negative evaluation. In other words, the rater has a particular outcome in mind.

There are three types of outcome goals: social adjustment, value expressive, and protective. Raters with social adjustment goals want to establish identity with various groups. For example, a rater who socializes with members of the development team might want to evaluate an IS positively. The rater is pursuing group favor, not evaluation accuracy. Value expressive goals cause raters to behave in a manner that is consistent with their underlying values. For example, a rater who is pro-technology may evaluate an IS positively, even if it is ineffective. Finally, protective goals cause raters to respond to threats to their self-esteem and self-worth. They result in both positive and negative bias. For example, someone who feels threatened by an IS (perhaps because it automates a valued skill) may be inclined to reject it. On the other hand, a rater who has publicly supported an IS could evaluate it more positively than is justified.

Figure 1 presents an overview of the evaluation process. Normative beliefs are the rater's opinions about attributes that the IS should possess. They are the rater's expectations for the information system and depend on:

(1) what the rater has learned about the tasks and IS, and

(2) the rater's task-support goals.

Task-support goals guide the rater's attention towards certain IS features. For example, an engineer evaluating a word processor for the task "help to write technical reports" might focus on the program's ability to generate tables and import graphics. An administrator evaluating the same program with different tasks in mind might consider features such as mail merge and document version control.

Descriptive beliefs are the rater's opinions about attributes that the IS does possess. While normative beliefs guide raters' examination of the IS, the information they gather determines their descriptive beliefs. The rater notes discrepancies between the normative and descriptive beliefs and forms an evaluation. Finally, the rater reports the evaluation.

## 3. Reducing bias

Sponsors should ask some questions before conducting evaluations.

## A. Why is the evaluation being conducted?

This is the first and most important question. The design of evaluation sessions depends on the objectives that the sponsor wishes to achieve. One objective could be to encourage recalcitrant employees to use an existing IS. In this case, the sponsor wants the IS to be evaluated positively. The sponsor might emphasize how easy the IS is to use, how it helps complete tasks quickly and accurately, and how much more valuable the employees will be when they have acquired IS skills. Another objective could be to encourage a group of senior managers to fund a project. The sponsor might focus on the project's high return on investment and minimal risk. Sponsors should choose their objectives with care. Their efforts to create positive evaluations could also create unrealistic expectations. In both of the examples, the sponsors want to manipulate evaluations to achieve their own ends.

However, evaluations can also provide valuable information about the quality of an IS. This information can be used to make good business decisions. In particular, we consider here cases where sponsors want either (1) to select the best IS for a particular application, or (2) to gather information to guide IS development. Given these objectives, a useful evaluation accurately reflects the extent to which an IS improves user performance. Although one might argue for other criteria, we emphasize evaluation accuracy.

## B. What is being evaluated?

There are three sets of issues here. First, consider the focus of the questions being asked. A sponsor might simply ask raters to “evaluate the IS,” even though this is often inappropriate. Second, the specificity of the questions (the level of detail) should be decided. Third, the sponsor should understand when an investment in an expensive but realistic prototype is justified.

## 1. Focus of the evaluation

When conditions are right, raters can make fine distinctions between concepts. For example, people's opinions about behaviors are different from their opinions about objects involved in the behaviors. A rater's opinion of “the accounting IS” could be very different from his or her opinion of “my using the accounting IS.” The rater might think that the IS is good overall, but that it is not useful for the particular tasks he or she performs.

Knowledgeable sponsors can take advantage of raters' intelligence and conceptual flexibility. Sponsors can focus raters on exactly the issues they want to know about. But unfortunately, if sponsors are not careful, they risk gathering information that is difficult to interpret. A constant theme throughout this paper is that sponsors should decide what information they want and ask raters for exactly that information. In many cases, the appropriate focus of the evaluations will be:

(1) the extent to which the IS helps perform certain tasks (this is particularly important when selecting an IS), and

(2) how the IS could support the tasks better (this is particularly important when developing an IS).

Other questions may be appropriate given other objectives, such as encouraging use. However, it is assumed that raters are asked to examine the system's support of tasks.

The sponsor should explicitly identify the tasks that are of interest, and tell the raters what they are. Mathieson, Olfman and Ryan [6] showed that rater behavior is heavily influenced by the tasks they consider important when examining an IS. Left to themselves, raters will probably focus on their tasks. While this will often be appropriate, raters and sponsors may be interested in different tasks. For example, suppose a manager wants to choose a word processor for secretarial tasks. He or she asks a programmer to simply “compare these two packages and tell me how good they are.” The manager has not specified which tasks to consider. The programmer therefore evaluates the packages’ support of tasks that he or she performs (such as editing code), rather than their support for tasks a secretary performs (such as printing mailing labels). Sponsors should therefore decide the types of tasks of interest and inform the raters of this. Otherwise they risk gathering information that will not help in making decisions.

## 2. Specificity

A second issue is evaluation specificity. Raters can be asked to provide summary judgments (e.g., “the IS is somewhat good at the tasks”), general task-specific judgments (e.g., “the IS is very good at task 1 but poor at task 2”), or detailed task-specific judgments (e.g., “data entry is difficult for task 2, but the reports are OK”).

Two recommendations are appropriate. First, sponsors should seek information that helps them achieve their objectives. For example, general opinions may be useful early in a project, when the emphasis is on basic functionality. Later, when the design is being finalized, sponsors will need more detailed information.

Second, the raters should know, before the evaluation, what level of specificity will be required. The level of specificity that raters originally are asked to provide affects their evaluations. In one of our experiments, raters were asked to evaluate a system's support of three different tasks, some of which were well supported, and some of which were not. There was evidence of a halo effect among raters who were initially asked to provide an overall evaluation. That is, raters did not differentiate between the parts of the IS that performed well and those parts that performed poorly. However, raters accurately evaluated the system's support of tasks when asked to focus on specific tasks before the evaluation session started. It seems that raters may have difficulty providing information that is more specific than originally requested.

Finally, if there is a particular issue about which sponsors want information, they should specifically ask for it. A problem is that raters might infer that an issue is important when it is not. For example, if a sponsor asks a rater whether the background color of a screen should be black or dark blue, the rater might assume that background color is important. Even though it is a trivial detail, the sponsor's apparent interest might make the issue appear to have greater weight than it deserves.

Evaluations are conducted to give sponsors information for making decisions. If information on a specific issue is needed, the sponsor should ask for it, rather than risk not receiving any information on the topic. The sponsor could identify issues that are particularly important, so that raters could allocate their effort appropriately.

Thus sponsors should (1) decide on the specificity required, (2) identify any specific information needed, and (3) tell the raters of this. Asking for too much detail will be burdensome. However, at a minimum, raters should be asked to evaluate the system's support for each task separately.

## 3. Prototype realism

Prototypes vary on two dimensions: cost and realism. Unrealistic prototypes, perhaps consisting of text screens linked together by a simple menu program, are cheap to develop. Realistic prototypes that actually perform some computations are more expensive to build, but give raters more information. They allow raters to develop detailed mental models of the IS, and thus to better understand what the final system would be like to use.

Sponsors should consider three factors when deciding how much to invest in a prototype. The first is the sponsor's objectives. During the early stages of a project, it may not be necessary to provide a detailed prototype, since the sponsor will usually be concerned with basic IS functionality. A more realistic prototype may be justified later, when details are more important. Second, raters can only absorb a limited amount of information. When they first see a prototype, so much is new that raters may be overwhelmed with detail. The extra investment in a realistic prototype may be unwarranted, since an unrealistic prototype will provide as much information as raters can handle. The realistic prototype can then be of more value later in the project.

Third, raters with significant IS experience may be able to build accurate mental models of systems more easily than novices. An expert shown an unrealistic prototype might be able to imagine how the final IS would operate, while a novice may find this impossible. Therefore, less investment in realistic prototypes may be needed for expert raters than for novices. This is, however, speculative.

## C. Who are the raters?

There are a number of criteria that should be used to select raters. First, they should know enough to judge the system's support of a task accurately. This implies that they should know:

(1) how to perform the tasks that are to be supported,

(2) different methods that an IS could use to support the tasks, and

(3) what constraints will be faced by the users of the IS.

Task expertise is not enough, since lack of IS expertise limits the conclusions raters can draw. For example, a rater who has only used command-line interfaces might rate another command-line interface very highly, even though a menu-based interface might be more appropriate. Further, a rater could be unfamiliar with the constraints under which users operate. The rater might overestimate their expertise or resources, recommending an IS that is inappropriate for their circumstances.

Second, raters should be motivated to provide an evaluation. This is easiest if the tasks are personally relevant; e.g., a purchaser might be the best person to evaluate a purchasing IS. Such a rater should have a high level of task expertise and be familiar with user constraints.

Third, raters should not be biased by outcome goals before they evaluate the system. That is, they should not be predisposed to a particular evaluation before they examine the IS. It is impossible to eliminate outcome bias, but problematic situations can be identified and avoided. Some examples will serve to illustrate this. First, a user who socializes with the system development team might feel social pressure to evaluate the IS positively. Second, a user wishing to gain management approval for an IS project might overestimate a task's value. Third, someone who disparages his or her colleagues for relying on IS would be a poor rater. His or her basic values might prevent an accurate evaluation. It is impossible to describe every situation in which outcome goals cause evaluation bias. However, sponsors familiar with the affects of outcome goals on evaluations will be better able to identify poor raters.

D. How should evaluation sessions be administered?

Many evaluation sessions are uncontrolled. For example, they occur in a rater's office when he or she examines a program used by a colleague. However, there are opportunities for formal, planned evaluation sessions during development, such as the usability testing, as described by Prasse [9]. This section provides guidelines for these situations.

The first objective is to focus raters on the exact questions that they should consider. If the sponsor is interested in task support, the raters should be told what tasks they should evaluate, how specific their evaluations should be, and whether there are any particular issues that they should address. This should be done before the start of the evaluation session.

Second, raters should be given the resources they need to complete their evaluations. For instance, they should be given enough time. Decision makers under time pressure rely on overly simple decision rules and ignore some of the information presented to them $[3]$ . They should also receive realistic test data. It is possible to select only test cases on which an IS performs well, giving the impression that the system is more useful than it really is. If sponsors want realistic evaluations, they should require (or provide) realistic test data.

Third, sponsors should try to motivate raters. People who think their decisions will have personal consequences spend more time and effort making those decisions than people who think there will be no personal consequences $[3]$ . Sponsors might explain to raters that their evaluations can impact their working lives. This is another reason for using raters who currently perform the tasks to be supported by the IS; they are the ones most affected by a new system. Further, people who know that they will have to explain their decisions are more careful than people who know that their evaluations are anonymous [8]. This implies that the session administrator might express an interest in following up on evaluations. It would also be valuable to have the raters' supervisors recognize and support their efforts.

Fourth, sponsors should avoid introducing outcome biases. In particular, raters should also know that evaluations are anonymous, as far as the sponsor is concerned.

There might seem to be a conflict here, since evaluations should be anonymous to avoid outcome biases, yet raters are more motivated when evaluations are not anonymous. However, anonymity exists with respect to a certain group. Social biases occur when raters think that their evaluations are known to a third party who has a vested interested in the evaluations. Protective biases occur in similar situations, when the third party has some power over the rater. Raters are not unintelligent. They are likely to predict the consequences of their actions. A rater might evaluate the IS negatively to protect his or her job assignment. The actual quality of the system is less important for this rater than self-preservation.

Such problems can be reduced if evaluation sessions are administered by someone who does not have a vested interest in the outcome. The administrator should also hide the identity of raters from those who have a preferred outcome (such as a developer or a superior with a prior opinion of the IS). At the very least, the session administrator should be independent from both the sponsor (e.g., the development team) and the raters' functional areas.

The administrator should know the identity of the raters, and work with them to resolve any incompleteness and inconsistencies in the evaluations. This should motivate the raters to spend effort evaluating the IS. The administrator should disguise the source of evaluations before delivering them to the sponsor. This should reduce outcome biases. This method will be effective only if raters believe that the administrator can truly protect their anonymity. It may require creative approaches to evaluation. For example, if only a few people will evaluate an IS, the administrator might ask to choose the raters, rather than receive names from the sponsor.

The sponsor should decide on a case-by-case basis whether the cost of such measures is worthwhile. If the sponsor needs information that is as unbiased as possible, and suspects that biases might occur, then using an independent administrator could be justified.

If raters believe that an IS is a threat, it will be particularly difficult to guarantee accurate evaluations. When the threat is perceived rather than real, raters might be persuaded that there is no reason for concern, but it is difficult to tell whether the attempt was successful. Perhaps the best option is to avoid using such raters.

(7) Have evaluation sessions administered by someone who is seen as impartial. The administrator should not be a member of the development team or of the rater's functional area. The evaluations should not be anonymous to the administrator, but may need to be disguised before being sent to the sponsor.

Managers and system developers should consider the role that user evaluations should play. If evaluations provide important information, then evaluation procedures should be designed to discourage bias. If the information is worth having, then it is worth taking a little extra care to make sure that it is valid.

## 4. Conclusions

This paper suggests methods for controlling bias in user evaluations. The main recommendations for sponsors are:

(1) Decide on the objective. In particular, decide what tasks are of interest.

(2) Decide how specific the evaluations should be. In the early stages of a project, general information is probably the most valuable. As the project progresses, more specific information becomes important.

(3) Tell the raters what tasks should be addressed and how specific the evaluations should be. If there are any special issues, the raters must be told to consider them.

(4) Choose raters who are familiar with the tasks to be supported, who have some IS experience, and who know the constraints under which users operate.

(5) Avoid raters who might be affected by social adjustment goals, protective goals, or value expressive goals. If such people must be used, allow for biases when interpreting their evaluations.

(6) Give the raters the resources they need, including time and test data. Be careful to choose test data that is unbiased.
