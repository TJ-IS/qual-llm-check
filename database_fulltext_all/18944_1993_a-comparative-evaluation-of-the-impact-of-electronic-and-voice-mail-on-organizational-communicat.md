## 1. Introduction

As organizations evolve, the structure and complexity of an individual's work environment is in a constant state of transition: From smaller, relatively autonomous units to larger, necessarily interdependent operations. In the struggle to keep pace, information technology (IT) has found its way into the hands of managers and other professionals. Perhaps no other form of IT stands to impact the user community, and ultimately the organization, more than computer-mediated communication systems (CMCS). Indeed, CMCS are allowing managers to reshape and redirect their most time-consuming activity.

The purpose of this paper is to explore the way in which two specific forms of CMCS, voice mail (V-mail) and electronic mail (E-mail) change individuals' perceptions of the communication process in an organization. This evaluation is conducted along four dimensions: Scope of communication, patterns of communication, communication task and message content, each of which have been examined previously for E-mail but not for V-mail. In addition, the two technologies are compared with one another. As pointed out by Rice [36] “research aimed at understanding human benefits from new (communication) media should focus on the unique strengths of each medium.”

This exploratory study is based on a field survey of users of both V-mail and E-mail. In spite of the perceived similarities between them, the differences in their interface technologies, and fundamental differences between the processes of speaking and listening versus reading and writing lead us to predict that the technologies will have different impacts on organizational communication. Thus, the goal is to understand the impact of these technologies so that they can best be deployed in organizations. Understanding how the technologies differ should assist organizations in making investment decisions (see for example $[2,14,46]$ ).

## 2. Communication via voice and electronic mail

Two computer-mediated communication technologies, E-mail and V-mail, have emerged as the leading candidates to complement traditional communication methods, such as face-to-face meetings, memos, and the telephone. An E-mail system is defined as a computer-based system that facilitates the transmission, receipt, and storage of written correspondence, messages, and text-based information. A V-mail system is defined as a computer-based system by which the user of a standard telephone can send, receive, store, or redirect voice messages.

Both V- and E-mail share a common purpose: supporting asynchronous, or one-way, organizational communication with common functions of message transmission, retrieval, and storage. A recent survey of information technology experts noted that V- and E-mail would probably have a profound effect on the way organizations exchange information [49]. Within the literature it is not unusual for the two to be grouped together and treated as homogeneous in application and effect [16,17,23,25,37]. In general, it is agreed that the two technologies would have similar impact on organizational communication [31,33,34,44,45]. Consistent with the academic literature, the trade and practitioner literature shows that V- and E-mail are perceived to share many common advantages and disadvantages. Table 1 summarizes some of the advantages cited for both E-mail and V-mail. As can be seen, there are few items that are unique to either media.

We have found no research which compares the two in terms of organizational applications. However, given the differences in interface technologies and the different processes, it would seem reasonable to expect some differences in terms of how they influence communications ([4,5,15,22,50,56] provide evidence of the difference between communication modes under a variety of task and technological conditions).

## 2.1. Electronic mail (E-mail)

There is considerable literature on the impact of E-mail on communication processes. This review focuses on the use of E-mail for asynchronous, typically point-to-point, communication which is viewed as the most common form of computer-mediated communication [55]. This analysis does not consider the literature on interactive computer conferencing and computer-supported group decision making [18,21].

The primary purpose of E-mail is to improve the process of communication, thus supporting management $[32]$ . E-mail is also intended to streamline managerial communication, freeing the manager from the constraints of a centralized work environment by allowing him or her to work outside the normal time and geographic boundaries of traditional office work. This review explores the ways in which E-mail influences organizational communication patterns by examining the scope and pattern of its communication, the tasks for which it is employed, and the content of its messages.

Scope of communication Scope refers to both the breadth and capacity of a communication network. Thus, it considers such things as the number of participants in a message system, the number of interconnections between them and the volume of message traffic flowing through the network. E-mail has been noted to expand the volume of communication in organizations [47], while acting as a substitute for alternative media such as face-to-face and telephone. The expansion takes on two forms. First, E-mail results in more frequent communication with others in a pre-existing communications network. Second, the use of E-mail leads to new contacts in the organization, resulting in communication that might not have otherwise occurred $[20,38]$ . Such an expansion should lead to a larger number and greater variety of people participating as information sources in decision making. Turoff $[55]$ views the establishment and enhancement of such informal information exchange as one of the key organizational benefits of computer-mediated communication.

Communication patterns In addition to increasing the scope of communication, E-mail has altered patterns of communication [6,26,48,51]. In general, studies have found such changes as an overall increase in vertical communication [1], e.g., increased communication from subordinates to superiors and increased diagonal communication across traditional divisional relations [57]. These changes may be due to differences in social presence associated with E-mail; being a less personal form of communication it may be used to make novel or risky contacts. Crawford [7] found that E-mail was perceived as less intimidating than making a phone call to a superior but more attention grabbing than a memo. Similarly, the lower social presence of E-mail may make it appropriate for new and informal communication across organizational boundaries. In general, as social distance between sender and receiver increases, issues of status become less important to communication: E-mail may be viewed as an 'equalizer'.

Communication tasks Several studies have examined the tasks for which E-mail is used or perceived to be most appropriate. It improves both coordination and control tasks $[8]$ . Trevino et al. $[54]$ concluded that E-mail was primarily used to send a message quickly and efficiently, often to people who are busy. Others have noted that E-mail tended to be the preferred medium for messages that were well defined and required routine processing by the receiver $[35]$ . Trevino et al. also speculate that the relative newness of E-mail limits its symbolic power (i.e., the medium itself carries no message) and that it will typically be employed for structured tasks. Turoff points out that over-structuring E-mail can reduce its effectiveness to the level of an automated inter-office memo system. When this occurs, none of its increased communication benefits are likely to occur.

Message content Finally, the use of E-mail has been observed to influence the content of messages. Sproull and Kiesler [47] found that E-mail messages contained less social context and tended to be more self-absorbing than more traditional media. People were also more inclined to send bad news over E-mail and be uninhibited in message transmission relative to traditional media. Crawford noted that messages were perceived by some E-mail users as being formal, sterile and terse. Other research has indicated that E-mail messages may contain high socio-emotional content [39]. Some users also report receiving a high proportion of non-task related ‘electronic junk’ as E-mail. Message content has also been noted to vary with changes in organizational context, such as entering a crisis period [12].

## 2.2. Voice mail (V-mail)

The literature on V-mail is much less developed than that for E-mail; very little empirical work has been conducted to assess the effect of V-mail. In addition, while the trade literature seems to indicate that there are many advantages to using V-mail, existing case studies are contradictory. A study by Wang in 1981 found a number of positive attributes related to the implementation of V-mail, while an AT&T study indicated that successful implementation was far from certain and that user training was critical [3]. In the latter study, V-mail was perceived as a barrier to communication when it was used to prevent interruptions.

They reported on the adoption of V-mail in a highly decentralized setting and showed the importance of training in these systems. Users who were provided with only limited training sessions did not find the system very useful. In addition, the authors found that use of the system across departments underscores the need to develop a critical mass of users throughout the organization. Interdependent departments had less favourable ratings of the system than those that were more autonomous. The interdependent groups found that others with whom they needed to communicate did not use, or infrequently used, the system.

Rice and Shook [40] present data on the use of a voice messaging system at a large insurance corporation. They make an important distinction between the use for voice messaging (i.e., active use of the channel to send messages) and voice answering (i.e., as an answering machine). They note that active users of V-mail tend to perceive greater benefits than those who only use voice answering features. In an overall evaluation of the system, the most common reaction was that it had no effect on communication patterns. However, when the messaging features of V-mail were employed, users had greater ability to distribute and obtain information, thus, achieving the benefits afforded by this technology.

Overall, the case studies show that V-mail is far from being a guaranteed success in organizations. Although, much of the trade and practitioner literature tends to tout their advantages, benefits are contingent on implementation factors, such as training and the overall utility of the system. V-mail has the potential to alter and improve communication processes, but improvement will only occur if the system is properly matched to organizational structural factors and task contingencies.

In general, our knowledge of V-mail centers on the antecedent conditions that influence implementation success. Little is understood about how V-mail is used, and how that use influences the communication process. However, the fundamental differences in communication modes between V- and E-mail and the way the two technologies differ in information richness or social presence, coupled with our understanding of the impacts of E-mail on communication processes lead us to predict how the two should differ in their influence on communication scope, patterns, task and message content.

## 2.3. Voice versus electronic mail

While some people have predicted that the telephone system will result in a natural advantage for V-mail and that it will eventually replace E-mail, others have argued that the two are largely complementary technologies [27]. In assessing the differences, it is useful to look at the nature of the interface technologies. To the extent that reading and writing are different from speaking and listening, V- and E-mail would be expected to have different effects on the communication process (e.g., [43] provides useful comparisons of communication modes in the context of CMCS).

The only empirically based comparison of V-mail and E-mail to date is provided by Rice and Steinfield [41]. They point out that while there are common uses, there are also differences. For example, they note that there appears to be less social and broadcast use of V-mail than E-mail. Overall, they note that the manner in which these new technologies change communication constraints, bandwidth, interaction, and network attributes imply that they should not be evaluated as substitutes for traditional media, but in terms of how they change the way that people communicate.

In line with these suggestions, we propose four areas of investigation: Scope of communication, patterns of communication, communication tasks, and communication content.

Scope of communication E-mail has been observed to have an impact on the volume of communication, both by increasing contact among those in pre-existing communication networks and by facilitating the creation of new linkages within and across organizational boundaries. Though the evidence for V-mail is much more limited, there is no indication of increased communication as the result of it. Therefore, we assert the following proposition.

Proposition 1 (PI). E-mail will be perceived to increase the overall scope of communication more than V-mail

This will result in users perceiving that, relative to V-mail, E-mail leads to the following.

P1a. More total messages being exchanged; P1b. An increase in the number of people with whom an individual communicates.

Patterns of communication There are three general patterns of communication: Peer-to-peer, superior-to-subordinate, and subordinate-to-superior. For peer-to-peer communication, E-mail leads to new contacts. No such effect has been noted for V-mail. In addition, Rice and Williams postulate that there will be cross divisional communication as a result of E-mail usage, i.e., communication that crosses traditional organizational or departmental boundaries. E-mail may also result in message broadcasts to potentially thousands of users at little or no cost to senders $[24]$ . Sproull and Kielser note how such broadcasts can subsequently lead to new contacts and ideas. The increase in social distance between sender and receiver may lead users to make ‘risky’ contacts, while the more direct and personal communication resulting from V-mail may be likely to inhibit such contacts. Therefore, we expect that the following is true.

Proposition 2 (P2). E-mail will be used to a greater extent than V-mail for peer-to-peer communication.

Subordinate-to-superior contact may also be better facilitated by E-mail than V-mail. The increased social distance makes it likely that subordinates will be less intimidated when contacting superiors, possibly even indirect superiors. Such upward communication has been noted in a number of studies of E-mail (see for example $[6]$ ). Williams $[56]$ has also suggested that as social distance increases, issues of status become less important in communication. In addition, Beswick and Reinsch $[3]$ say that V-mail can be perceived as a barrier to communication with superiors who choose to ignore incoming messages and calls; no similar observation has been noted for E-mail. Therefore, we expect that Proposition 3 holds.

Proposition 3 (P3). E-mail will be used to a greater extent than V-mail for subordinate-to-superior communication.

The final communication pattern is superior-to-subordinate. The literature does not indicate that either E-mail or V-mail has any impact on this type of communication. Downward communication might rely on richer media to ensure accurate transmission of orders, thus neither V nor E-mail may be particularly appropriate. However V-mail, as a richer medium, may be preferred. Based on this, we expect that the following is true.

Proposition 4 (P4). V-mail will be used to a greater extent than E-mail for superior-to-subordinate communication.

Communication tasks E- and V-mail may also be compared across task types. Our focus is on two task areas: Co-ordination and control. A third generic class of tasks, collaborative work, is more appropriately supported by richer, interactive technologies such as group decision support systems and video conferencing. Co-ordination and control tasks, which tend to be routine and structured, should fit better with the asynchronous, low capacity characteristics of V- and E-mail.

In situations of interdependence, concerted action comes through co-ordination [53], which can be defined as the process whereby two or more individuals create and/or use existing decision rules that have been established to deal collectively with their shared task [42]. Coordination activities include scheduling meetings, assigning responsibilities to groups of co-workers, or allocating resources to different projects, activities or groups [28]. Information passing in this context most closely resembles directives or orders in which there may be little need for mutual exchange or interaction.

Advantages for CMCS arise from being able to remove time zone and geographical barriers and to distribute messages to many people simultaneously and asynchronously. The simultaneous distribution of information helps to organize project groups, schedule meetings and more generally enhance information exchange. Both V- and E-mail can facilitate coordination; thus it is not obvious whether one medium can be viewed as more appropriate than the other for these tasks. It might be argued, however, that the richer V-mail channel would make it the more appropriate medium. Also, to the extent that the amount of information exchanged for co-ordination activities, such as scheduling, is low, V-mail may be preferred. However, E-mail apparently improves the process of co-ordination relative to more traditional media. Therefore, it is difficult to assert that either technology will be viewed as being more useful for co-ordination tasks, thus we have no a priori expectation regarding the null hypothesis.

Proposition 5 (P5). There will be no difference between V-mail and E-mail with respect to their usefulness in supporting co-ordination tasks.

As a result, an exploratory test will be employed.

Control tasks are like co-ordination, except that they involve monitoring ongoing activities and reporting intermediate results. Such communication tends to involve one-way reporting, where information moves up the organizational hierarchy. This is an important difference between control and co-ordination which requires communication in all directions: To peers, superiors, or subordinates. Only in the event of an exception is there a need for control information to flow in two directions. As a result, less rich media (such as written reporting systems) may suffice in supporting routine control tasks.

E-mail is appropriate for routine, structured communication that is the hallmark of the control process. In addition, to the extent that control information represents deviation from expectations, it may often be viewed as 'bad news'. Sproull and Kiesler [47] noted that E-mail was more likely to be used than traditional media in communicating bad news. Based on these differences we would expect the following.

Proposition 6 (P6). E-mail will be perceived to be more useful than V-mail for supporting control-oriented tasks.

Message content There are a variety of ways that media may influence message content. E-mail has been noted to result in messages that are sometimes viewed as formal, sterile, and terse. V-mail messages, on the other hand, are typically longer, though intended to convey the same information; E-mail messages also tend to be self-absorbed and somewhat impersonal, with little emphasis on the receiver. E-mail is frequently used for non-work communication or transmission of electronic junk mail.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no reported content analysis of V-mail messages. It might be expected that the richer voice channel would result in more inhibited behaviour than E-mail. Thus, voice messages seem more likely to conform to traditional conventions via telephone communication.

Based on these findings we expect that Proposition 7 holds.

Proposition P7a. E-mail messages will be perceived as being shorter than V-mail messages;  
P7b. E-mail messages will be perceived as being more terse than V-mail messages;  
P7c. E-mail messages will be perceived as being less personal than V-mail messages;  
P7d. More E-mail messages will be perceived as being non-work related than V-mail messages;  
P7e. More E-mail messages will be perceived as being junk mail than V-mail messages.

## 3. The study

## 3.1. Instrument development

Questions were developed to examine the four areas for which E- and V-mail might be thought to have the previously discussed differential effects: Scope of communication, communication patterns (peer-to-peer, superior-to-subordinate, and subordinate-to-superior), communication tasks (co-ordination and control), and message content. All questions used a seven-point Likert scale, where 1 represented strongly disagree and 7 strongly agree. The questionnaire is outlined in Appendix A.

All items were pilot tested using a sample of twenty-five academic users of both V- and E-mail. Pretest participants were asked to comment on the questionnaire's content and clarity; problems were later corrected. A second pretest was then undertaken with the revised version, which was completed and evaluated by some IS academics. Based on their comments the questions were again modified and some items were added. None of the additional items related to this study. The pilot tests indicated that the questionnaire was reasonably easy to follow and could be completed in a reasonable amount of time.

In total, the questionnaire had over sixty items, examining also such issues as ease of use and usefulness of E-mail and V-mail. All items were ordered randomly throughout the questionnaire. Definitions of the terms computer-based message system, E-mail, and V-mail were provided to respondents when completing the questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into two forms with the same questions for both V- and E-mail. Respondents were asked to complete either one or both sections depending upon their use of the technologies.

## 4. Results

Analysis of the data was performed using t-tests to compare the mean responses for E- and V-mail systems. The results are summarized in Table 3. Individual t-tests of composite scale items are included. All items tested conformed to the equality of variance assumption.

Communication scope E-mail is viewed as expanding the scope of communication to a greater extent than V-mail (P1). E-mail is perceived to increase the total number of messages sent to a greater degree than V-mail (P1a). E-mail is also perceived to be more important as a primary mode of communication than V-mail (P1a). E-mail is also seen as increasing the number of individuals in the organization with whom people communicate (P1b) and is more likely than V-mail to lead to new contacts within the organization (P1b).

Communication patterns E-mail is used more than V-mail to communicate with peers (P2); with superiors (P3) and with subordinates (P4) than is V-mail. Note that the result for P4 is opposite to the direction we anticipated.

Communication task E-mail was viewed as improving co-ordination within the organization to a greater extent than V-mail (P5). E-mail was also viewed as improving control to a greater extent than V-mail (P6). Of the two items that comprised the control scale, increased control over work was significant while control over subordinates showed no significant difference.

Message content V-mail messages were more likely to be viewed as too short (P7a) and too terse (P7b) compared to E-mail, this result is the opposite to the direction anticipated. E-mail messages are viewed as more impersonal than V-mail messages (P7c). More E-mail messages are received which are not directly relevant (P7d) and E-mail was a more likely source of junk mail (P7e).

## 5. Discussion

An overall interpretation of these results is that E-mail is considered a more useful and versatile communication medium that is perceived as dominant over V-mail for intra-organizational communication. E-mail has the effect of generally expanding the scope of communication in an organization while V-mail does not. An examination of the means reported in Table 3 indicate that the E-mail results tend to be greater than four and thus on the agree side of the scale while the V-mail results tend to be less than four. In addition, E-mail is reported as being more widely used for communicating throughout the organization. Again, the mean responses, with the exception of peer-to-peer communication, indicate that E-mail is considered to be a tool that augments communication while V-mail is not perceived as having a substantial impact. This result is consistent with the case findings reported by Rice and Case [38] and Sproull and Kiesler [47] for E-mail and with the case report by Manross and Rice [29] for intelligent telephone systems. Moreover, the general positive perceptions about the use of E-mail show up in spite of the fact that the results for message content indicate that E-mail messages have several negative qualities relative to V-mail.

The results indicate that V-mail may not be viewed so much as a distinct communication channel but rather as little more than an answering service. An additional item from the questionnaire appears to support this contention. Respondents were asked if they used V- or E-mail only when they could not reach someone by telephone. The mean responses were 5.3 for V-mail and 2.3 for E-mail, where 7 equals strongly agree (t = -9.5; p = 0.0001). Thus, V-mail is viewed as a backup to telephone communication while E-mail is seen as an alternative communication channel. Users of V-mail systems are less likely to actively compose and transmit messages. E-mail use, on the other hand, is active and leads to new communication and more contacts within the organization.

E-mail might be seen as a mechanism for improving organizational effectiveness, by expanding the range of information and expertise available to organizational decision makers. V-mail, on the other hand, may only facilitate the timely and accurate exchange of messages in an asynchronous mode. While this may streamline the functioning of certain operations and perhaps reduce costs, it is unlikely to have a significant impact on organizational decision making. Consistent with this interpretation E-mail was rated more useful overall than V-mail in improving work effectiveness (E-mail mean 5.7, V-mail = 5.2; t = 3.05; p = 0.003).

There are several possible explanations for the perceived dominance of E-mail over V-mail. First, our sample consists of users of both technologies, but respondents reported having an average of 21 months experience with E-mail and 28 months experience with V-mail, indicating that they had actually spent more time using V-mail (t = -1.87; p = 0.06).

Given the importance attributed to training in previous research $[3,29,30]$ , each respondent was asked to report how many hours of formal training he/she had on the use of each technology. Respondents reported having significantly more training on E-mail than on V-mail (2.1 versus 0.7 hours on average, t = 3.31; p = 0.001). On the other hand, there is no difference in perceived ease of use (measured using scales adapted from Davis $[13]$ ) for the two technologies (t = 1.36; p = 0.42). The mean responses (5.7 for V-mail versus 5.9 for E-mail) indicate that both technologies are viewed as relatively easy to use. Training, however, may have influenced the respondents understanding of the systems. Respondents tended to rate themselves as more knowledgeable in the use of E-mail than V-mail (t = 2.73; p = 0.008).

It is also possible that V-mail may have less overall impact because of its direct and obvious linkage to telephone technology. Users interacting with a V-mail system may view themselves as interacting with a telephone and they may make little use of its features. Such an explanation is consistent with the concept of functional fixedness which asserts that it is difficult for individuals to use highly familiar objects in unusual or novel ways.

The phone is essentially a mechanism used for synchronous two-way communication; using it to generate and distribute asynchronous messages may seem unnatural. On the other hand, a computer is viewed as a versatile, general purpose processor supporting a wide variety of tasks. In addition, its relative novelty means that strict patterns of use will not be as well developed as they are for the telephone.

The specific results about the applicability of

V- and E-mail to different managerial tasks are also of interest. While the means for co-ordination indicate that both V- and E-mail are considered useful in supporting this function, E-mail is viewed as superior. In addition, the results indicate that E-mail provides users with greater control over their own work environment.

## 6. Concluding comments

The results of this exploratory study provide several implications for management. The clearest result is that E-mail is perceived as a tool that augments communication capabilities and expands the scope of communication in an organization. Thus E-mail is more likely to enhance organizational information processing and decision-making capabilities, while V-mail is likely to provide some increased internal efficiencies. E-mail is therefore the clear investment choice for intra-organizational communication. However, our analysis focused only on internal communication. V-mail may be a very valuable tool in supporting inter-organizational communication. In fact, it was rated as being used more often for those outside the organization than E-mail (t = 9.26; p = 0.002). Also, V-mail would appear to be more appropriate for keeping in touch while away from the office and for inter-organizational communication. In this sense, the two products might be viewed as complementary.

In spite of the dominance of E-mail, evidence indicates that it was not without its problems. E-mail messages were perceived as more impersonal and less work relevant indicating that organizations should provide users of such new technologies with protocols that help to develop advantageous patterns of use as part of training programs. At the same time it is important not to design procedures to reduce informal ties and information exchange. Such restrictions can reduce the value that message systems provide.

Our recommendations should be mitigated because of the limited and exploratory nature of the study. First, all our data is based on self-perception of system usage. It could be that measures of actual usage and usage patterns would differ. For example, Sproull and Kiesler [47] report that users overestimate the number of messages they send and receive. However, as our interest is in the comparison of technologies this inflation is of less concern. Second, our data is based on a cross-section of users of both technologies. Perceptions and actual use of either V- or E-mail may differ when only one technology is available. Users may more readily adapt to novel uses of V-mail when E-mail is not available. Indeed, an institution in our pilot study that had good V-mail but a poor E-mail system, indicated that users can make active use of V-mail to distribute messages.

Our focus has been on the effect of V- and E-mail on organizational work. Many researchers in the area of computer-mediated communication have noted the importance of considering affective and social cues in assessing the impact of technologies. Studies that more closely model the antecedent conditions for V- and E-mail usage would be of interest.

In summary, the results of this survey indicate that V- and E-mail cannot be treated as homogeneous technologies, though they share many common functions. E-mail was clearly viewed as having a strong impact on communication processes, V-mail was perceived as being little more than an adjunct to the telephone.
