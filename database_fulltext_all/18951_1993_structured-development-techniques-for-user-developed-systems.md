# Structured development techniques for user-developed systems

Linda Salchenberger

Loyola University of Chicago, Chicago IL, USA

The demand for individual control over information and processing is increasing with the availability of inexpensive hardware, user-friendly software, and greater numbers of sophisticated users. Since it is well-known that structured methods can improve productivity and system quality, they should be adapted to aid in end user computing. In this paper, a set of guidelines for applying a structured methodology to end user developed applications, based on established structured tools and techniques, is presented with an implementation strategy. Two cases in which the methodologies and tools were successfully applied by end users with information systems staff support are discussed.

Keywords: End user computing; Structured methods; End user developed systems

## 1. Introduction

The emerging role of end users as developers of business microcomputer-based systems is generating a new set of challenges for management. Since application development software is easy to use, people are anxious to develop and implement models to solve problems. Unfortunately, they tend to do so without first performing requirements analysis or developing a systems design. Such systems may be unreliable and/or produce inaccurate results, leading to poor decision making by the user. Worse yet, the effects of poor quality systems may be widespread if they interface with other organizational systems. While much has been written about the phenomenon of end user computing, the practical problem of providing organizational support for user developers has received substantially less attention. User developers are confused about how to handle their new role as system developers and frustrated by their inadequate preparation for this new task. In addition, since systems development is not their main function in the organization, user developers lack the time to learn the required new skills.

In this paper, we provide a structured approach to the analysis and design of user developed systems (UDSs). The ability to develop small-scale information systems will be required on an on-going basis by many end users as we move further into the 1990s. At the same time, it is neither necessary nor possible for users to have the same level of understanding of formal structured methodologies as analysts. We attempt to adapt those elements of structured analysis and design crucial for the development of logically correct systems into a structured methodology that can be easily understood and implemented by end users. The approach for systems development is task oriented, rather than tool oriented: It should be generally available to users regardless of the development tool used. Application to user developed systems (UDSs) are illustrated with examples taken from two system designs where this methodology was utilized.

## 2. The end-user developers

The CODASYL end user facilities committee [3] defined three categories of users: Indirect, intermediate, and direct. The first group includes those who use computers by directing other people, the second includes those who specify information requirements for systems developed by others, and the last includes end users who actually use terminals and develop their own systems. Another classification scheme developed by McLean [7] differentiates between DP professionals and DP users. The latter category includes DP amateurs (typically engineers, financial analysts, or corporate planners who write simple programs and use packages) and non-DP trained users (individuals with no knowledge of programming). Rockart and Flannery [11] observe a finer-grained classification of end users in an empirical study of end-user computing and define six different categories. These end user categories were: Non-programming, command-level, end-user programmer, functional support personnel, end-user computing support personnel, and data-processing programmer. The first three include users who are not part of the data-processing staff, while the last three included DP professionals. Non-programming end users access data through software provided by others; command level users utilize report generators to perform simple queries, and end user programmers utilize both command and procedural languages for their personal needs and develop applications for use by others. Functional support personnel are sophisticated programmers supporting other users within their functional areas, while end-user computing support personnel assist users and develop software and they are most often located in the information center. In a more recent empirical survey of end-user computing [2], end users were broadly categorized is those who develop applications but are not personnel of the information systems department.

We define end-user developers as individuals who produce microcomputer-based systems to solve business problems at the operational, tactical, or strategic level for their personal use or for use by a limited number of others within an organization. This individual's role would coincide with that of the end user programmer identified by Rockart and Flannery [11]. However, we make the distinction that they develop systems without necessarily programming in the traditional sense. Also, the scope of their projects may be broader, and so they are system developers rather than programmers. The user developer is not a member of the information systems staff and has traditionally been an end user. The diversity of end users is more significant than their commonality. They differ with respect to formal training, experience with computers, computing needs, problem-solving skills, and cognitive styles.

## 3. Characteristics of user-developed applications

Empirical studies have revealed that user-developed applications can range from highly complex systems to simple report generation or query-based systems. Although UDSs are often characterized as specialized applications designed for one-time only use, many have been integrated into the business environment and may support operational, tactical, or strategic planning and control. Typical applications of end-user computing include internal monitoring and control systems, project planning and scheduling systems, and systems which support sensitivity analysis $[12]$ . These systems download organizational data, generate customized reports, and are used for decision making at all levels.

End-user systems are most often developed using 4th generation tools, spreadsheets, database systems, or other modelling tools. A recent survey of the use of 4GLs [8] concluded that they provide a higher-level programming environment and arc casier to debug and maintain than equivalent COBOL programs. The rapid development cycle which can be achieved with the use of 4GLs means that end-user developers tend to bypass a traditional project life cycle when developing systems.

## 4. Quality of user-developed systems

The quality of UDSs should be judged on ease of use, reliability (logical and mathematical correctness of the model), auditability (ability to determine source of data values in the model), and modifiability (ability to change or update the system as needs change). Poorly structured systems can specify models incorrectly and be difficult to use and maintain. Some specific problems with spreadsheet models include mistakes in logic, incorrect range specifications, and incorrectly copied formulas. It becomes increasingly important. to establish software engineering standards to assure quality in UDSs, since these systems are becoming more widespread, involve multiple users, and access and manipulate corporate data.

## 5. Reasons for developing a methodology

In a recent study, lack of user education was cited as the most important problem that organizations face in end user computing $[5]$ . An evaluation of user education that is currently being provided revealed user dissatisfaction in the existing training programs; for the most part, the training is tool oriented rather than task oriented. In addition to supporting the user-development process, managing UDSs after the development was identified as a major concern. It is clear that users require training in the use of tools, and need to be exposed to development strategies and methodologies that have only been accessible to information systems staff members in the past.

The application of the principles of software engineering to UDSs can improve system quality, based on experience with large-scale systems that have been developed by DP professionals. A methodology can provide a checklist for the user, identifying all the deliverables necessary to specify the resulting system. By following a graphic or linguistic syntax for the system specification before development takes place, crucial questions are asked and problems are identified early in the development cycle. The methodology can provide a measure of provability for checking the solution. Through the consistent application of structured design principles, users may perceive similarities among systems that have been developed and some re-usable subsystems can be generated.

Qualitative deficiencies can be detected by evaluating designs against standards before the programming phase of the development cycle. Finally, efficiency in program development and testing should be achieved through the application of structured analysis and design principles.

While benefits of these principles are widely acknowledged by IS professionals, introducing them to end users presents a new challenge. Resistance to a formal methodology will be likely for many reasons. Since UDSs can be easily implemented using non-procedural tools, like the programmers of the 60s and 70s, users often neglect preliminary analysis and are anxious to begin development. User developers wish to see the tangible results of the development process immediately. UDSs are perceived as ‘easy’ to modify, so users are not motivated to utilize a formal development methodology. Finally, lack of experience in developing systems prevents users from understanding the need for a methodological approach.

## 6. End user computing support

Organizations must assume control of end-user computing (EUC) by first clearly defining the roles of the end-user developers and the end-user support staff. Studies have revealed that end-user developers need support from information systems staff and yet, desire the independence required to develop their own applications $[6]$ . In fact, in a recent survey by Rivard and Huff $[9]$ , user satisfaction with support provided by the DP department was the factor most closely related to overall satisfaction with end-user developed applications. End-user developers must accept responsibility for application development and maintenance while IS professionals should provide advice and training, communicate development standards and guidelines, provide information about hardware and software, assist with data extraction and system integration, and assist with trouble-shooting and debugging.

Providing user training in systems development techniques is an important component of a comprehensive EUC strategy and should result in reducing the risk to the organization introduced by unreliable or inaccurate systems. Studies by Batra et al. [1] show that user training can significantly impact the ability of novices to translate business information requirements into conceptual models and thus build quality systems.

## 7. The methodology

The methodology focuses on five areas of the project development cycle; preliminary investigation, requirements analysis, functional analysis and design, data analysis and design, development and testing. Key steps and techniques identified for each phase are listed in Table 1 and each is discussed in detail.

## 7.1. Preliminary investigation

Early investigation requires tasks that are somewhat different than those performed when developing a large-scale information system. The user developer's major function in the organization is not to develop computer-based systems and thus time should not be spent overcoming technical obstacles. Therefore, an appropriate strategy during preliminary a investigation is the use of critical success factors [10]. These are critical to the success of a project and are identified at the start; thus if any difficulties cannot be resolved, with the assistance of the information systems staff, the project itself must be abandoned.

The problem should be carefully documented, and the scope of the system, its interfaces, and potential users should be identified. Based on these and other organizational factors, the developers, with the assistance of the information systems staff, should determine if the project is feasible for end-user development. In Table 2, a set of characteristics of systems appropriate for user development is provided. Each organization should establish its own criteria for determining which are suitable.

Users must assume responsibility for overall project management when they undertake the development of a microcomputer-based system. Because they are anxious to begin working on the system, it is easy to neglect this aspect. While decisions about the production environment of large scale systems are usually deferred to a later stage, these issues must be addressed as part of the investigation. This allows the user to ascertain if the proposed project is feasible, given the technological and resource constraints of the development environment. The developer must investigate this and carefully consider the use of application development facilitators, e.g., EXPRESS for Rbase. Napier et al. [8] show that these can greatly improve the reliability of UDSs and reduce the need to learn to use more procedural development tools. As part of the development methodology, the user should establish, at this early stage, a strategy for managing the development effort. User developers are not professional systems developers and therefore, the work done on these systems is often sporadic and does not proceed continuously. Careful management of the development environment will result in more efficient use of the developer's time. For example, procedures for managing backup and working versions of the system should be established. To avoid confusion, only one current and one backup version of the production system be maintained and these be appropriately named. Portability of the system should be investigated so that the user may work in different environments with different equipment (e.g., printers and monitors).

The overall design should be determined at this time, although in the traditional systems development life cycle (SDLC) applied to large-scale systems, all design issues are deferred to a later stage. For spreadsheet-based systems which require that macros reside in the spreadsheet itself, an overall design positioning data entry screens, report screens, and macros along the diagonals is recommended $[12]$ . This allows rows and columns to be inserted and deleted in each separate area without affecting the rest of the spreadsheet. Efficiency is maintained by the software using compression techniques so the large amount of ‘blank’ space is not a concern. These areas should then be named to facilitate movement throughout the worksheet and identify regions for printing.

Before the user view of the database can be specified, the source and destination of the data should be determined. In many UDSs, data are downloaded from the corporate data base. In some cases, technical problems in transferring data are insurmountable, and the project may be abandoned. It should be recognized that keying-in the data manually seriously increases the risk of bad data. Word processing packages may be used to intercept data in ASCII format and modify them before loading into the system. Utilities like 1-2-3's IMPORT command can also be used to download data from mainframes to spreadsheets. Naming conventions for data items, files, databases, and spreadsheet ranges should be documented using the development software. This does not mean that data items and files are actually named: Rather, that the user establish the mnemonics (with prefixes or suffixes) to be used to reference the contents of a file, record, or data item.

Finally, integration issues should be resolved and system interfaces designed so that the feasibility of developing interfaces with other systems is ascertained before further work is done. Again, a decision to abandon the project may result at the end of this part of the investigation.

## 7.2. Requirements analysis

The importance of determining and documenting preliminary systems requirements cannot be overlooked in UDSs. Nevertheless, a rapid development life cycle changes the way in which requirements are specified. During requirements analysis, a preliminary systems analysis parallels the development of systems requirements.

The problem definition is the starting point. Even though a system is developed for personal or limited use, a simple English narrative containing a description of the problem should be recorded. This should explain the system objectives, its scope, its boundaries, and its interfaces. Users should determine a small number of critical success factors that will be used to determine whether the system meets expectations.

The development of a main menu or control program and the use of data flow diagrams are two methods for determining system requirements. In addition, user developers relate very well to prototyping during requirements determination.

## 7.2.1. Main menu in requirements analysis

Since most end-user systems are menu-driven, an initial version of the main menu can be developed to assist in requirements analysis. The user determines which major functions are needed to specify the main menu and its control logic. The user's initial expectations of system behavior and its essential features will be documented through this process. Only control logic for menu navigation is developed. The user is satisfied, because he or she is performing some development work, while the menu is actually a vehicle for specifying the essential requirements. By developing the main menu, the essence of the system's performance is established and the user developer documents expectations about the system performance by specifying the main functions as menu options.

## 7.2.2. Data-flow diagrams in requirements analysis

An overview of the system can be produced using data-flow-diagramming techniques. Dataflow diagrams (DFDs) can be exploded to give more detail, as activities are developed in more detail. Data-flow diagrams depict data flows, processes, and data stores to show how events precipitate system behavior [4]. Data-flow diagrams can play an important role in UDSs. While other techniques have been suggested, we found that users understood and could use DFDs effectively for requirements analysis.

Since the outputs from UDSs are often event triggered, one approach to using data-flow diagrams for requirements analysis is to begin with the selection of key events $[13]$ . This approach is easy for user developers since they often formulate their expectations of system behavior based on anticipated system responses to events. Events are occurrences which require a response from the system and generate data flows. External events take place outside the system boundaries, internal events are triggered by the system itself to implement a response, and temporal events are time-triggered events which cause activity within the system. Stimuli are data flows which act as system inputs when an event occurs and responses are data flows from the system to an entity or from the system to an internal activity in the system.

## 7.3. Functional analysis and design

Structure charts, data-flow diagrams, and presentation graphics are among the many tools used by professional systems analysts to support systems analysis methodologies. These formal methods generally require the expertise and experience of a systems analyst and have been shown to improve system quality. UDSs can benefit from similar analysis tools without employing the same degree of formalism.

Using the requirements analysis documents (e.g., main menu, data flow diagrams, narrative), a more detailed analysis of the system functions and the data interfaces can be determined. This may involve further development of the main menu or control program, context diagram, event diagram, data model, or application prototype. A traditional functional decomposition takes place here and the approach is top-down. The use of consistent enumeration of the modules is important to document the model, since the hierarchical structure is difficult to perceive when the menu is displayed on the screen or printed. The principles of a balanced design with respect to scope and span of control should be applied as the menus are developed. If a data flow diagram serves as the requirements determination document, the next step is to develop minispecifications, i.e., explode functions in more detail. Using a friendly syntax to replace the structured English used by professional analysts can make this technique more accessible to the user developer [4].

## 7.4. Data analysis and design

The first step in data analysis and design is to specify the entities, the objects about which data must be stored to achieve system goals. Some entities can be identified by examining first-cut data-flow diagrams for data stores. The individual data items (object attributes) can be further determined and named, from report and query requirements. The value of the names are an indication of the users' ability to understand their model and we so include this identification process as part of the data analysis. Data item names should be descriptive, but not too long; abbreviated names might be used for development, but these should be replaced by a more self-documenting version at installation time. Many DBMSs and spreadsheets restrict the number of characters that are displayed, though more are stored. Therefore, data item names should be established so that the first several (6–10) characters uniquely identify the data element. Unique identifier or key fields should be established for each record. Some keys with embedded structures may have to be split up into separate columns to support queries.

Next, relationships between entities can be identified by examining natural or inherent associations between the entities. When a relational database model is used, the entities and relationships are represented as tables. The essential concepts of normalization should be presented so users can develop tables that avoid anomalies and provide query flexibility and efficiency. The creation of secondary indexes is supported by most DBMSs and adding indexes where appropriate can improve efficiency. Users must be aware that the cost of building indexes is increased storage requirements, and additional update time and should be encouraged to carefully select the fields which are indexed.

## 7.5. Development and testing

System development will be top-down and bottom-up, i.e., while the major development work will begin with higher level modules and calling routines, some service modules should be developed in parallel. In particular, utilities which enhance the production environment should be developed and tested first, followed by top-down development of modules. For example, a test macro is essential so that individual modules can be tested before installation. Administrative functions like backing up files, recovering data, editing data, and combining data from various files will result in a more supportive production environment. Processes implemented by modules of UDSs are often routine with respect to logical construct and many are similar. Therefore, an excellent development strategy is to select a portion of the system which is essential and develop that module first. This may then be useful elsewhere.

For macro development, a good strategy is to write ‘thinly’ (put few keywords on a single line) so that features can be added easily to a macro as the system is developed. A guideline for maintaining module cohesion is to limit the number of steps for any macro to between 10 and 20.

The code-testing and specification-testing strategies used in large-scale systems development can be adapted for user-developed systems. Code-testing examines the logic of the routines or programs by testing every set of conditions that is handled by the routine. Specification testing evaluates the performance of the system by developing test cases which correspond to situations discussed in the specifications. Systems testing should be performed in the early stages of system development to determine if the control logic (often directed by the menu navigation) is correct. If possible, a sample of the actual data should be used as test data. Experience has led us to conclude that all potential users of the system should be involved in testing, since the user developer alone may be qualified to evaluate test results for reasonableness.

## 7.6. Documentation

Documentation is an essential component of UDSs and should be considered an integral part of the system itself. The development work in UDSs often proceeds at an uneven pace and documentation can reduce startup time required by the user to reacquaint themselves with the state of a system in progress. In addition, it is not unusual to develop a system which is only used infrequently. Therefore, some information about how the system performs its functions is required. One suggestion is to provide a description of programs and spreadsheets at the top of a worksheet so the file becomes selfdocumenting. Program files, program modules, data files and data items can be self-documenting when named appropriately. Using a five column form for macros is recommended so that the third, fourth, and fifth columns provide space for including comments that describe the function of the macro. If the system is to be used by others, the developer should be especially conscientious about including documentation.

## 8. Benefits and Limitations of the Methodology

One of the benefits of exposing user developers to structured methods is that such training should increase user awareness that software development is process oriented. As user developers become more task oriented and less tool oriented, they will be able to apply general analysis and design principles to applications which require different development environments. This will become increasingly important in the future because the emphasis will be on quality in the requirements definition and high-level design, as development tools that support detailed design and implementation become widely available. Other benefits which can be attributed to structured analysis include the reduction of errors, improvement of system quality, and increase in productivity. An accelerated approach to analysis and design has been recommended to accommodate the rapid system development cycle typical of UDSs.

Unfortunately, there is no single integrated tool to support the application of structured methodology to UDSs and the approaches provided in this paper are somewhat dependent upon the tool used. User training is required and overhead is added to the development effort when structured methods are used. Nevertheless, the increase of UDSs and the importance of decisions made with these systems provide the motivation for introducing structured analysis and design principles and techniques to end user developers.

## 9. Applications

Two cases in which users were trained in this systems development methodology are presented to illustrate potential benefits. We first present background information on each of the cases.

## 9.1. The statistical analysis project

Technical staff in a market-research organization were limited by a mainframe statistical analysis system which was unable to produce the customized reports that clients had requested. Business was lost to competitors and employees were frustrated with their inability to view and manipulate data. Statistics from market data were computed manually by the market analysts and individual reports were type-written to accommodate each client's request.

An attempt was made to develop a microcomputer-based system that would accept data from the mainframe, perform statistical analysis, and generate customized client reports. Unfortunately, there was no formal support for end-user computing in the organization and the market analysts were novice microcomputer users. An attempt to build a system to solve this problem using a spreadsheet model with macros was abandoned after two months.

Management intervened and a consultant trained a small group of end users in the structured analysis and design techniques presented in this paper. Consequently, a spreadsheet system was successfully developed by two market analysts who participated in the training program. The system was developed in top-down, modular fashion, with custom menus that made the system user-friendly so that clerks rather than analysts could generate the reports. The cost of the development effort included the spreadsheet software and the market analysts' time. The benefits included flexibility, since the microcomputer system could be used to support to special requests and gains in productivity, since the market analysts could devote their time to other tasks. The system developed using structured design techniques proved to be easy to maintain and subsequently, more formal statistical analysis was added to the spreadsheet model and more specialized reports could be generated for the clients.

## 9.2. Using structured tools in an inventory system

A second case where users learned and applied structured methods and CASE (computer-aided software engineering) techniques involved an inventory system. A retail computer store belonging to a national chain was experiencing difficulty in managing its manual inventory system, customer tracking system, and repair and maintenance system. A mainframe system that maintained inventory data for the entire chain of stores could not be modified to accommodate new requests and provide information needed to automate in-store systems.

Several employees completed course work in structured methods at a local university and worked with other staff members on the design and implementation of a microcomputer-based inventory and customer system. A database software package with an application generator was chosen as the development tool so that the majority of the development effort was devoted to analysis and design rather than programming. Structured tools (e.g., data flow diagrams) were used to facilitate communication between the user developer and the store manager during the analysis and design process. The total implementation effort took about 100 hours of work, including analysis and design. The end-user developer played a dual role, since he was involved in the development and was also a system user.

Using a modular design, the inventory subsystem was developed and tested before other systems were added. Once this key component of the system was completed, lessons learned by the user developer facilitated the development of the other modules. The customer tracking system was added, and plans for developing the repair and sales performance/commissions tracking systems were made.

Management support throughout the project contributed greatly to its success. Existing computer hardware was used, and plans to expand the system on a local area network were realized a few months later. Required resources included: The end user developer's time, and database and word processing software. The greatest problem encountered was the downloading of mainframe master inventory data into the microcomputer-based system which was eventually accomplished using a utility. The store staff and salespersons were active participants in design and development through the use of rapid prototyping. This meant that they could see a working version of the system and provide early feedback on the format of data entry screens and reports. The effort to produce these report formats was minimal using the database system.

## 10. Conclusions

While arguing for greater structure in user-developed programs, we have provided specific tasks to be performed during preliminary investigation, requirements determination, functional analysis and design, data analysis and design, development, and documentation. User developers can benefit from applying these techniques because an emphasis has been placed on requirements determination (traditionally difficult for users to articulate), good documentation and development strategies which support sporadic development, and the integration of structured analysis and database design. We offer concrete suggestions, and demonstrate success based on practical experience, on one approach to the issue of end-user training. To date, experience with applying principles of structured methods to UDSs has been limited to specific cases. However, the extensive use and development of UDSs suggests that user developers can benefit substantially by employing structured methods and relational techniques in systems development.

The cases provide evidence of some preliminary success with training users in structured systems development methodologies. These experiences suggest that this approach to user education may be beneficial to the organization in the long run because it provides the user developers with skills that are not tool specific and are transferable.
