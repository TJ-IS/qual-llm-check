# Information systems development and communicative action theory \*

Marius A. Janson

University of Missouri-St. Louis, St. Louis, MO, USA

Carson C. Woo

University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC, Canada

L. Douglas Smith

University of Missouri-St. Louis, St. Louis, MO, USA

Effective communication among developers and users underlies the success of designing computer-based information systems (CBISs). We propose that Habermas' theory of communicative action is useful for investigating the CBIS developmental process. To support this we analyze our experiences in constructing two different DSSs. Communicative action theory is used to uncover and explain the unexpected failure of one and the unanticipated success of another. The two case studies show how appropriate communicative action can be used to respond to design uncertainty and user resistance. The cases also suggest that a lack of appropriate communicative action during the developmental process can cause premature system failure. We perceive the problems in constructing these DSSs to be representative of the difficulties experienced in developing different CBISs. This implies that insight arising from investigating the cases using communicative action theory may also be useful in designing other CBISs.

Keywords: System development; Communication; Communicative action; Speech act; User-designer relationship.

## 2. Language and information systems

Recent studies indicate that over 50% of practitioners consider active user involvement and participation the most important factor for successfully implementing an information system. User involvement and participation, however, require analyst-user communication. Because information systems affect the users' social environment in important ways, the participants in this process use language to achieve multiple, often conflicting, objectives, including expressing information requirements, influencing one's opponent, and effecting change; e.g., by bringing about consensus through negotiation. Because language is an instrument for effecting change and not just a vehicle for disclosing thought, it must be analyzed and interpreted in terms of the speaker and the hearer's social environment.

The application of language in a social context is the essence of Habermas' communicative action theory [13,14,15]. In elaborating his ideas, Habermas builds on a speech act theory that originated with Austin [3] and was further elaborated by Searle [31, 32]. A central assumption in speech act theory is that: to communicate is to perform an act, such as stating facts, making requests, making promises, or issuing orders. To illustrate, by making the statement, "I will complete the job tomorrow," the speaker commits to a future course of action, which in turn affects the hearer. Hence, by uttering the sentence the speaker says something, does something in saying the sentence, and affects the hearer by saying the sentence. Speech acts are performed to make factual statements, to request someone to do something, to make promises and commitments, to effect change, and to express a personal feeling: they are called assertives, directives, commissives, declaratives, and expressives respectively.

Speech act theory originally attempted to analyze verbal utterances. However, theorists have extensively expanded it during the last two decades; its current applications include analyzing verbal discourse, written discourse [4], and textual material [28].

These concepts can be illustrated by analyzing several of the cryptic messages that are part of the exchange between a bank customer who withdraws cash from an account and an automatic teller machine (ATM). Because the purpose of this communicative interaction is uniquely goal oriented, this example accentuates performative aspects of language.

It also follows that on successfully enunciating the sentence, the card is canceled. No particular follow-up actions are required.

The last speech act type, the expressive, seeks to express a certain psychological state by the speaker or by the transmitter of the message. The utterance “We Apologize For The Inconvenience” has the specific function of apologizing by expressing concern. The implicit goal is to make the inconvenience more acceptable to the hearer.

The orientation toward a goal suggests specific validity criteria for assessing the efficacy of the speech act. A successful assertive implies propositional truth, a successful directive implies suitability or appropriateness for the situation, a successful commissive calls for sincerity and the ability to deliver what is promised, a successful declarative requires that the speaker possesses the authority to effect the action, and a successful expressive necessitates that the speaker be sincere.

Habermas uses speech act theory to develop a communicative action typology that focuses primarily on the social aspects of communication. He identifies three action types: instrumental, strategic, and communicative action (Table 2).

Instrumental action is singularly success oriented. It is evaluated using technical criteria, which implies assessing the efficiency by which success is achieved. Successful instrumental action necessitates accepting the implied truth validity claim. To illustrate, the statement “Machine not in service” succeeds when it is accepted as a truthful description of the ATM’s state of readiness (Table 3). Instrumental action is appropriate in a nonsocial setting. Because it aims to describe a state of affairs or an object, instrumental action uses assertive and directive speech acts.

Strategic action seeks to influence decisions of a rational opponent (Table 2). It therefore has a social application domain and is evaluated in terms of the efficiency of achieving its goal. Successful strategic action requires that the hearer accepts the speaker's actions as suitable with respect to interpersonal authority and power relations. For example, the statement "There will be a bus-to-block DSS in this garage" succeeds when the hearer accepts the strategic act's legitimacy because the speaker has backing from upper management (Table 3). Strategic action implies stating facts, requesting others to supply information, ordering others to behave in certain ways, and making commitments and promises. Operating in the social domain implies that strategic action requires a more varied use of speech acts than does instrumental action.

Reaching understanding takes priority over rational efficiency and efficacy (Table 2). Successful communicative action implies veracity as the primary validity claim. Because the goal is reaching understanding, communicative action calls for candor, truthfulness, and sincerity. For example, the communicative act “I understand your concerns about disruptions during DSS implementation, perhaps, you can suggest how I might minimize potential problems” succeeds when the hearer accepts that it reflects the speaker's true intentions (Table 3). Communicative action is firmly anchored in the social domain and requires using a variety of speech acts.

Speech act theory not only defines the three action types; it also allows analysis and classification of conversations in practical situations. For example, using frequent declaratives indicates strategic action, or using expressives is an indicator of communicative action, or usage limited to assertives typifies instrumental action.

The action typology allows one to assess the degree of difficulty of each action type. Progressing from instrumental action through strategic action to communicative action implies validity claims of increasing complexity. It is more difficult to operate in the social domain (i.e., claiming suitability or veracity validity) than in the nonsocial domain (i.e., claiming truth). Alternatively, getting agreement through negotiation (i.e., satisfying veracity claims) is more difficult than achieving it by enforcement (i.e., claiming suitability with respect to interpersonal power relations). Of the three validity claims, veracity is the most encompassing and, thus, most difficult to achieve. However, meaningful user involvement and participation in which the individuals act as equals occurs only when the veracity requirement is satisfied.

## 3. Communicative action and DSS development

DSS requirements are often difficult to define and major design changes are frequently necessary. The DSS has to be adapted to the user's evolving decision-making needs $[6,21,38]$ . Hogue and Watson $[17]$ rank DSS development methods on a continuum, with the standard development life cycle and evolutionary approaches located at opposite ends. Their investigations indicate that over two thirds of the developers favor an evolutionary DSS design approach, whereas fewer than one third prefer design approaches similar to the standard development life cycle.

The standard development life cycle may be divided into three stages: analysis, design, and implementation [33]. The analysis stage includes defining the decision-making problem, assessing economic and technical feasibility, planning system development, studying the current system, identifying system requirements and constraints, evaluating alternatives, and providing a high-level logical design for the proposed system. The detailed development of software, hardware, data structures, and operating and training procedures occurs during the design stage. The implementation stage comprises system conversion, operation and maintenance, and evaluation.

The evolutionary approach aims to shorten the systems developmental cycle. An initial system is designed and implemented quickly and then modified in response to user feedback. DSS development, to this point is similar to developing any information system $[7,23]$ ; however, the level of uncertainty about what is actually needed tends to be higher, thus several loops of feedback may be needed. The design approach must therefore reduce development cost because several intermediate design products may be needed before convergence on the final version. The number of times each stage is revisited can be expressed as the degree of intensity of communicative action use. We will investigate communicative action strategies by focusing on the individual DSS analysis, design, and implementation stages.

Table 4 shows, for various developmental activities, the primary communicative action type, its primary validity claim, and its domain of action; it also shows clusters of speech acts characteristic of the different communicative action types.

The first task in the analysis stage is to assess the current situation. This requires achieving an understanding of and, possibly, affecting the business environment, organizational culture, and users' decision-making style; it requires discussion between the analyst and the users. Therefore the communicative behavior occurs in the social domain. Strategic action is indicated, because the implicit or explicit objectives are to obtain and record information. Directives and assertives are used to request and record this information. Furthermore, the analyst who attempts to affect the situation in some way may resort to using commissive and declarative speech acts. The primary validity claim in this case is not truth, but the analyst's dependability and reliability as perceived by the user. If in the past, the user has found that the analyst makes good on commitments, then that knowledge will be more convincing than the strictly propositional truth of the speech acts being used.

The second task in the analysis stage focuses on the need to change the current situation. This requires negotiating consensus on issues involving the business environment, organizational culture, and users' decision-making responsibility. These activities exemplify communicative action. Because negotiation occurs between people, the communicative action domain is distinctly social. The process involves stating facts, requesting information, asking others to act in specified ways, and making promises and commitments. An additional feature of negotiating agreement is that the participants reveal personal biases and preferences. Also, negotiating a successful agreement depends on bringing about an alignment of individually held normative systems. The participants act as equals and try to reach consensus by rational discourse that, to be successful, calls for honesty, truthfulness, and integrity. Veracity is thus the primary validity claim of communicative action. These tasks are accomplished by assertive, directive, commissive, and expressive speech act types.

During the DSS design stage, all communicative action types come into play. It is possible, for example, that the specific DSS design necessitates modifying the DSS generator's software [39]. An important component of computer software is the set of procedures that control the requisite computer operations. Because computer software consists of data and instructions, it can be viewed as a collection of assertive and directive speech acts. The communicative action type is primarily instrumental because the focus is on nonsocial technical issues.

Designers spend considerable time in solitary activity as they transform DSS specifications into an operating information system. This activity calls for assertive and directive speech acts for specifying design constructs and creating computer programs. The communicative behavior is best characterized as instrumental action occurring nonsocially.

Other design tasks require strategic communicative behavior that occurs socially. For example, periodic design review meetings are held to share project progress, to discuss design problems, and to provide information needed by others to complete their individual design assignments. Often, however, these activities go beyond sharing information, especially if the actors attempt to influence one another. For example, DSS designers may attempt to keep users uninformed about project delays by withholding information. Designers may well succeed in this attempt, especially if past design projects have been completed on schedule. Thus, the validity claims of strategic action in this situation are the user-perceived reliability and dependability of the designers. The essential speech acts here are of all types. Disagreement, however, often arises with respect to design issues, solutions to design problems, and time schedules. Reconciliation of these personal differences based on mutual respect and understanding calls for communicative action.

The communicative behavior during the implementation stage consists of a mixture of strategic action and communicative action occurring in a social environment. Strategic action, in the form of assertive, directive, commissive, and declarative speech acts, instructs the users in the day-to-day operation of the DSS. Often, however, other issues come to the fore. For example, users may discover inconsistencies between the organizational and the DSS's problem-solving style. After reaching understanding, the designer then proceeds to discuss, with the user, tradeoffs required to reconcile these differences and to negotiate agreement about the desired course of action. This implies communicative action and the application of assertive, directive, commissive, and expressive speech acts. Furthermore, in cases where the users resist the system, the designer may appeal to a higher authority and thus enforce user compliance. Alternatively, the designer may resort to using action by promising continued system support. This suggests shifts in behavior among instrumental, strategic, and communicative action, as the needs dictate.

## 4.1. A marketing DSS for a regional insurance company

A DSS for marketing research and planning was constructed for a regional insurance company whose business concentration was in personal lines (life, property, and casualty insurance). It serves a 13-state area in the lower midwest. The project was initiated by the company's president, who requested that the director of planning and research construct a computer based system for developing plans to improve market penetration. The project received strong support from the vice-president of marketing who regarded the system integral to greater rigor in developing and executing marketing plans. The DSS, therefore, served the strategic interests of the company's president and its vice-president of marketing and the tactical interests of its director of planning and research. The combination of upper managerial commitment and the computer expertise of the director were viewed by the developers as enhancing the prospects of successful system implementation.

## Analysis

The initial request from the president to the director of planning and research called for analytical support to identify locations at which agents should be added and for the capability to convey this information on maps of their marketing area. The president's request is best described as strategic action using primarily assertive, directive, and declarative speech acts. The director of planning and research approached the authors for technical assistance in satisfying the request and for detailed Census data required for studies of market penetration. A series of meetings were held with the director of planning and research and with the marketing vice-president to understand the business needs to be satisfied by the DSS. Strategic action consisting of assertives, directives, and commissives dominated these deliberations. Contacts between the developers and the company's president were directed at assessing upper management's support for the project; they consisted of a mixture of strategic and communicative action.

Discussion moved quickly to specifying the DSS's technical requirements, because the director of planning and research readily understood the analytical potential of a DSS constructed using the modules of a DSS generator. The discussion consisted chiefly of a mixture of instrumental and strategic action types. Instrumental action consisting of assertives and directives served to work out and record technical aspects of the proposed DSS. Strategic action was used to acquire an initial understanding of the users' information requirements, to communicate system design alternatives and, when necessary, to convince users of the preferred course of action.

## Design

The DSS contained mapping routines for depicting market penetration, concentration of agent force, frequency and severity of claims, and other descriptive statistics. It emphasized easy-to-use statistical tools for consolidating data from public and corporate data bases and featured multivariate statistical analyses for studying factors affecting market penetration, agent productivity, and loss rates from claims. In constructing the DSS for supporting marketing research, we employed the mapping modules from an existing DSS. Its generator relied on the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) [10,20,29,30] for providing data base, model base, and user interface functions. Additional SAS macros had been created for report generation and computer cartography [35]. Hence, at the start of the marketing DSS we possessed an SAS-based DSS generator that enabled the rapid construction of a DSS prototype for the insurance company at minimal cost [36].

After creating an analytical data base, several day-long meetings with the director of planning and research took place; during these we demonstrated the operation and decision-making features of the DSS prototype, refined prototypes, and instructed a new analyst in using the tools. Statistical models were formulated and validated for studying factors affecting insurance claims, for studying agent productivity, and for assessing corporate performance in each county of the marketing area as a function of the resources deployed. Strategic action, using assertive and directive speech acts, helped to gain understanding of the business environment; it was conducted informally between the model builder and the director of planning and research. Strategic action pertaining to technical aspects of the DSS and its potential applications was dominant, however, during the concentrated work sessions.

Discussions with the vice-president of marketing centered on demonstrating prototypical applications of the DSS and presenting results of statistical studies to address specific problems in the location of agents. Contacts between the DSS builders and the president focused on ensuring continued upper management support for the project, on sharing general project progress reports, and on exchanging occasional pleasantries. Hence, during these contacts with upper managerial personnel, strategic action predominated with a frequent use of assertives, directives, commissives, and some occasional communicative action in the form of expressives. The conceptual models of the business and its environment differed considerably among managerial personnel and few comprehended or displayed any significant level of interest in the analytical constructs used in the DSS. Thus, the use of strategic and communicative action with upper management personnel was not extensive.

Access to the DSS generator from a previous project enabled us to develop tools and to implement them quickly and at much less cost. It also, however, caused us to move quickly to consider the technical aspects of DSS design, and possibly to spend less time that we should in acquiring a fundamental understanding of the business environment and the corporate culture. That is to say, communication in the form of strategic and communicative action may have been cut short prematurely in favor of instrumental action.

## Implementation

Senior management personnel perceived that the use of the tools was for identifying opportunities for expansion of the agency force. When the company entered a period of retrenchment and allowed attrition to reduce the number of agents, it refocused its efforts on changing underwriting rules and rate structures to improve profitability. The president and the vice-president of marketing could have used the DSS to generate information helpful for tactical decisions during retrenchment. The director of planning and research, however, was the only person familiar with this potential, but it had not been communicated to the upper management team. Departure of the director of planning and research, who left for a position at a different company, led to underuse of the DSS and its eventual demise. More extensive strategic and communicative action would have been required with upper management to convince them of the full potential of the DSS, and for us to design the system in such a way that it could have served the broader interests of senior personnel, moreover lower management personnel played no role in the project.

## 4.2. A DSS for bus operations in St. Louis's mass transit system

A DSS was developed for the Bi-State metropolitan transit agency which serves greater St. Louis. The system enabled garage personnel to park and dispatch buses so that bus operators could drive, as much as possible, the same bus each day, thus becoming familiar with the equipment and enabling the drivers to cooperate with maintenance personnel to keep the fleet in good working order. With the DSS, the transit agency has improved the operating condition of its fleet, minimized mechanical failures on the road, reduced maintenance expenditures, and achieved a 95% likelihood that bus operators drive the same bus each shift [37].

The DSS provides continuous real-time support to garage personnel on a 24-hour basis. The software modules for data maintenance, for generating regular assignments of buses to runs, and for controlling daily garage operations are all written in FORTRAN. The modules form a DSS generator that can be tailored to cities other than St. Louis with different operating environments.

The deputy general manager for transit asked his research staff members to investigate the feasibility of developing such a system. Superintendents of maintenance at each of the garages were highly skeptical about its feasibility. The climate for introducing the system is best described as hostile. After short visits with a member of the research staff and the maintenance superintendents at one of the garages, we suggested doing a computer simulation of garage operations and fleet movements to determine the proposed system's feasibility.

## Analysis

Data were collected to enable us to model realistically the deviations from the schedule that occur when buses return to the garage, and to model stochastically the refueling and maintenance operations. On the basis of simulated performance via a complex SIMSCRIPT II.5 model $[25]$ , we concluded that the DSS could be successfully implemented if the necessary computer system were installed. Technical specifications for the system were prepared by the transit agency and conveyed to the DSS builders in a legal contract. A few meetings were held for negotiating necessary changes in operating practices. During the system analysis phase we used instrumental action to record and describe the outcome of the simulation runs to Bi-State staff members.

We used strategic action to learn about day-to-day operating procedures and the application environment from middle and lower managerial personnel, and to assess the resistance to the proposed system. Cooperation of middle and lower management members was brought about by establishing a working relationship based on meaningful user participation. Hence, cooperation was a result of a process of communicative action. Finally, we used a mixture of strategic and communicative action to negotiate a preliminary contract with upper management personnel and an agreement to proceed with a simulation study prior to embarking on a fully fledged system design effort.

## Design

Development of the DSS's conceptual model was entirely the responsibility of the DSS builder, because there were no existing models in industry. Using insight gained from experiments with the simulation model, we developed and implemented a prototype at a garage where the director-in-charge was sympathetic to the project and where one of the three maintenance supervisors was convinced that the project had merit. It performed successfully for six months and had favorable effects on fleet maintenance. An enhanced version of the DSS generator was then used to develop specific DSSs at each of the transit agency facilities.

Contacts between the developers and middle and lower managerial personnel involved instrumental and strategic action. We used instrumental action to record system specifications and design alternatives and we used strategic action to acquire knowledge about technical aspects pertaining to garage operations. We used communicative action to convince the supervisors that cooperating with us on the proposed system would ensure they would be the first to enjoy its benefits. We acknowledged the disadvantages inherent in being a test site, expressed understanding about disrupting garage activities, and promised extensive on-site help during the implementation and shakeout period. Thus there was frequent use of communicative action in the form of assertive, directive, commissive, and expressive speech acts.

## Implementation

During prototype implementation, operation, and testing, management personnel used a strategic action strategy with a predominance on assertive and directive speech acts to educate the DSS builder regarding operational procedures and constraints, and to establish additional performance specifications, such as the percentage of time that an operator would need to receive the correct bus and the maximum permissible computer response time.

After implementing the prototype, the DSS builder was involved in the actual operation of the system to identify problems, train users, and effect changes to improve performance. The high degree of structure in the operating environment and the need for essentially instantaneous decisions gave rise to large numbers of assertive and directive speech acts. This communicative process is thus best described as strategic action.

Most interaction with the system's users during implementation was in the form of instrumental and strategic action, characterized by assertive, directive, commissive, and declarative speech acts. Instrumental and strategic action played important roles in recording and familiarizing the users with the day-to-day system operating procedures. Because system development and implementation occurred in a hostile environment characterized by overt and covert user opposition, we relied frequently on declarative speech acts to bring about user compliance. Some personnel were told at times, “There will be an automated bus-to-run system in this garage,” and “You will have to use this system.” The developers could use declaratives because garage operators were aware that the managers considered the project top priority. Employing declaratives is definitely not a preferred method of achieving successful system implementation. However, because of the very short time allotted for implementation, and to minimize disruptions of daily operating procedures, declaratives were often unavoidable.

Interestingly, after the system was finally proven to perform successfully at the test site, communicative action occurred because managers of other garages, who were initially skeptical about the DSS, sought to negotiate earlier implementation in their garage. Low-intensity communicative action characterized the interaction between the system builder and the company's upper management personnel while negotiating a proper disengagement from the project.

## 5. Discussion

The two DSSs differ significantly with respect to sophistication, organizational rank, number of users, extent of managerial and user participation in system construction, developmental environment, application risk, implementation outcome, and the communicative action types employed during system development.

The marketing DSS was initiated by upper management personnel to support decision making at the strategic and tactical levels of management. The system's primary user, the director of planning and research, actively participated during all phases of system development. He had realistic expectations of the DSS's potential for developing corporate marketing strategies and shared a similar cognitive style with the designers. Contacts between this manager and the designers were at all times collegial and free from interpersonal conflict. The designers were well acquainted with the management science techniques needed to support user decision making. These factors were expected to enhance the prospects of successful system implementation; thus developing the marketing DSS appeared to be a low-risk assignment. However, despite these initial promising indications, the DSS fell into disuse when the director of planning and research for whom the system had been designed left the firm to join a different company.

The mass transit DSS was initiated by upper management personnel primarily to support decision making by many managers at the operational level. Personnel at the tactical and operational managerial levels participated in the development to differing degrees, depending on the stage of design completion. The users did not appreciate the potential contribution of a DSS in improving their work environment, job effectiveness, and benefits in the form of lower fleet maintenance cost. The cognitive style of the users and the designers differed greatly. The designers formulated the design assignment from an engineering perspective and focused on the aims of management and technical aspects. The users were concerned with different issues that revolved around the day-to-day working environment and how it would change as a result of implementing a DSS. The interpersonal relationship among managers, users, and designers was often strained: many were either skeptical or openly hostile to the prospective DSS. The designers were initially unsure about the technical feasibility of a DSS as proposed by upper management personnel. These factors reduced the prospect of successful system implementation; developing the Bi-State DSS appeared to be a high-risk assignment. However, despite these initial negative indications, the DSS became a resounding success.

An explanation for these disparate results can be formulated, we believe, in terms of the relationship between participation and communicative action. Participation has three dimensions: structure, content, and process [26]. Participative structure is direct when the individuals affected by the information system are personally involved in decision making. It is indirect when those affected by the information system are represented by a spokesperson. Participative content refers to the issues that are open to participation and the issues that are excluded from participation. Participative process refers to the techniques used for acquiring the knowledge for making informed decisions.

Table 5 maps communicative action into the developmental phases of the two DSSs. The table serves to explain the relation between communicative action, participation, and the success of the Bi-state DSS and the limited success of the marketing DSS.

In the case of the marketing DSS, the participative structure was, primarily, direct. It involved the director of planning and research who was, in fact, the system user. Individuals who could have benefited from the DSS were excluded from direct and indirect participation. Participative content involved, on the whole, technical issues. Participative content focused to a much lesser degree on managerial issues. The participative process was characterized primarily by a use of instrumental and strategic communicative action types. A similarity of approach to problem formulation between designers and users, designer familiarity with the application area, and the absence of interpersonal conflict led to the implementors cutting short communicative action prematurely and spending insufficient time acquiring a fundamental understanding of the business environment and the corporate culture. Thus the DSS, although serving the director of planning and research well, was narrowly designed and unable to adjust to changing business conditions. When the director of planning and research left the firm, the marketing DSS fell into disuse.

In the case of the Bi-State mass transit DSS, participative structure was primarily direct and, secondarily, indirect. It involved individuals at all managerial levels. Participative content centered on managerial, operational, and technical issues. The participative process was characterized by use of all three communicative action types with varying degrees of intensity. Using communicative action for achieving understanding, agreement, and consensus in turn led to negotiating the participative structure and its content. Thus, communicative action impacted not only the participative process; it also influenced the participative structure and content. This enriched the total participative experience for system users and designers. It resulted in a broadly conceived DSS that successfully answered the varied and changing information needs of multiple users at several managerial levels.

The two disparate outcomes, one negative and the other positive, suggest that design uncertainty and user resistance are not counterproductive but, instead, they can play an important role ensuring system success. A sensitivity of designers to other-than-technical issues, accompanied by an appropriate communicative action strategy, encourages meaningful user participation, results in an understanding and alleviation of user resistance, and enhances the prospects for successful system implementation.

## 6. Conclusion

The problems encountered during the construction of two DSSs are similar to problems experienced during development of other CBISs. The need for effective communication is a recurring theme in MIS literature. Applying communicative action theory to system development provides important insight into the cause of “people problems” and enables one to formulate an appropriate system development strategy. Thus communicative action theory makes a significant contribution to information system development because, as pointed out by Lyytinen [24], “people problems” are among the most frequent causes of systems failure.

Our findings suggest several other problems, such as: what makes a difference between high and low rated system developers? Do they differ on the use of communicative action types? Does this help explain differences in developer effectiveness? Also: can analyzing developer-user communicative action be a tool for selecting information requirement acquisition methods best suited for the design environment? Although we have found some indication that this may indeed be possible [19], it is largely conceptually based. We hope that our case-based study contributes toward a better understanding of the developmental process.
