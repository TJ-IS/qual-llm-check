# Advantages and disadvantages of electronic data interchange An industry perspective

Steve Scala

IBM Corp., Tampa, FL, USA

Roger McGrath, Jr.

University of South Florida, Tampa, FL, USA

This study is designed to explore, from discussions with experts, the perceived advantages and disadvantages of EDI. By identifying both, some conclusions may be drawn as to why EDI's growth is booming in some firms, while many companies refuse to implement it. Using the Delphi Technique, experts assisted us in gaining a greater understanding of the pro's and con's of EDI, and allowed us to identify the top ten associated with EDI's usage. The advantages complement common business strategies while the disadvantages identified common shortcomings.

Keywords: Electronic data interchange; EDI; Inter-organizational systems; IOS; IS management; Corporate IS.

## Introduction

Corporate America is spending about \$300 billion a year on computing and business communications. The goal is to integrate the hardware, software and people into information collection, processing and delivery structures. The perceived benefit of this structure is access to higher quality data, that travels faster and is more accurate for less cost than traditional methods. These information structures allow companies to exchange data electronically via intra-company and/or inter-company networks. Electronic links are used to communicate memos, documents, price lists, orders and inventory status; electronic transportation of business related information is known as Electronic Data Interchange (EDI). It has three basic components: “the application software, which allows the computer to perform business tasks such as accounts payable, accounts receivable, purchasing and order processing; the translation software, which converts data into a standard format; and the communication network, which delivers the information to the proper trading partner” [5].

EDI is a subset of the Inter-Organizational Systems (IOS) concept, which has significantly contributed to enhanced productivity, flexibility and competitiveness in many companies. IOS is an information system shared by two or more computers. “The players in a system are either participants or facilitators. An IOS participant is an organization that develops, operates, or uses an IOS to exchange information that supports a primary business process. Participants can be competitors, organizations in the buyer-supplier chain, or a combination of these. An IOS facilitator is an organization that aids in the development, operation, or use of such a network for exchange of information among participants.” [1].

IOS replaces the traditional paper intensive communication channel to reduce the costs while reducing time for information to travel between companies. In an effort to reduce the times and costs, corporations have explored alternative ways of transporting information. The result has been an explosion in FAX machines, overnight mail services and EDI.

The benefits can be considerable because “at least 70% of one computer’s input was another computers output. And 25% of a transaction’s cost is data entry and reentry.” [3]. The U.S. Department of Defense recently converted to EDI for bills of lading and freight billing. Robert A. Moore, director of defense transportation policy says “that substituting EDI for the paper version of the government bill of lading will save between \$10 million and \$17 million a year.” [2].

It is difficult to assess how many companies are currently using EDI, and to what level. A 1988 Forrester Research report stated that less than half of the Fortune 1000 corporations were using EDI. But EDI is expected to grow rapidly. Information regarding EDI is limited and the EDI articles that are published are brief. In simple terms, the ramifications of EDI are not yet understood.

## Focus

If its growth continues, EDI will be in almost every office by the end of this century. Its history and momentum warrant an in depth view on how companies will be impacted by EDI. Most of the reviews of EDI have been in trade periodicals and are thus not formal studies. Many corporate studies show that EDI is not cost effective. However, because reduction in computing and messaging costs is likely to continue while labor costs increase, even if EDI is not cost effective now, it soon will be.

The introduction of new technology often solves many old problems but introduces new ones to be managed. This study shows that EDI has solved some company problems and introduced some new ones.

The forces that hinder and help the growth and usage of EDI are a matter of opinion. It should be noted that the majority of EDI literature cites why companies should use EDI. It is evident that many companies are convinced that EDI is a competitive tool and that it must be incorporated into the company's overall strategic plan. Yet EDI has still not been introduced. By gathering opinions of consultants and business leaders responsible for the evaluation of EDI within a company, we have compiled a list of perceived EDI advantages and disadvantages.

## Methodology

To determine how managers responsible for EDI made their decisions the Delphi Method was used.

## The Delphi method

Delphi is a group process that utilizes written media to solicit and aggregate the judgments of a number of individuals. “Its aim is to improve the quality of the group’s work. Essentially, Delphi is a series of linked questionnaires. Starting with an open-ended questionnaire, succeeding questionnaires feed back group responses to the preceding questionnaire and ask for further information. The process stops when consensus among individuals has been reached or when sufficient information has been exhausted.” [6].

The Delphi process offers three major benefits. First, it draws on the experience of experts. Second, it documents facts along with personal preferences and expectations. Finally, the technique avoids the pitfalls of face-to-face interaction. The Delphi technique is not new, it was used in 1949 when the Air Force asked the Rand Corporation to estimate the number of atomic bombs the Russians would need to destroy the military muscle of the United States.

## Participants

Representatives charged with marketing EDI software and services were used for contacting and distributing the surveys. The participants were either EDI managers (e.g., individuals currently in charge of a company's EDI implementation) or MIS managers given the mission of evaluating a firm's use of EDI.

Over 150 surveys were handed out but there were only 28 responses. Of these 28 respondents, only six had no direct experience in running EDI applications. The other 22 have been using EDI for a combined average of 4.5 years. The limited number of respondents, particularly the small number of those who have considered EDI but not implemented it, suggest that the results of this study may not represent industry-wide views.

The data was collected in two phases. During the first, an open ended survey asked MIS managers and EDI consultants to identify the most significant impacts of EDI implementation on their business. They were also asked to list major disadvantages of EDI in their business. The first round identified the individual in the firm to whom succeeding rounds were to be mailed.

In phase two the respondents were asked to rank the advantages and disadvantages identified during the first phase. Advantages and disadvantages were listed only if suggested by at least three independent responses. The respondents were then asked to rank the top ten in order of importance.

## Round one

Our respondents had many ways of communicating the same advantage or disadvantage without using the same words or phrases. This required us to interpret responses. To help ensure they were grouped correctly, several researchers independently grouped the responses into categories. After reviewing the researchers' differences, the categories for round two were created if three or more individuals agreed. We therefore had a listing of the perceived advantages and disadvantages of using Electronic Data Interchange. The list of advantages contained 13 categories as shown in Table 1 and disadvantages shown in Table 2 respectively.

## Findings

The findings tended to corroborate the benefits touted by vendors and found in trade journals. Perhaps more interesting were the disadvantages, especially since EDI literature does not include such information.

## Top ten advantages

The order of stated EDI advantages are now discussed in order of preference.

1. EDI improves the accuracy of information and reduces errors. Corporate America has spent billions of dollars to develop and acquire applications that control order entry, accounts receivable, accounts payable, shipping notices and invoicing. Most of these applications need high reliability, high data integrity and high availability. Automation has reduced human intervention, increased the speed of process, reduced the costs and reduced errors. The concept of EDI is to expand these applications, using a common set of data structures: thus data can be shared without ever changing its medium, while allowing trading partners to eliminate the resources previously required to interpret and reenter information.

2. EDI reduces data reentry. The new order entry process allows companies to receive orders electronically in a standardized format. An application can be programmed to interpret this data and enter it into the order entry system. EDI allows order entry to be fast, cost efficient, and error free without the need for manual reentry.

3. EDI speeds the transmission of information between organizations. The value of delivering something to a destination faster must have greater benefit than its cost. In essence, the speed up of information can help a company gain a competitive edge. EDI allows corporations to receive information as soon as a trading partner makes it available.

4. EDI reduces inventory and inventory carrying costs. The ability to reduce and even eliminate order lead times has had a significant impact on inventory and inventory carrying costs.

5. EDI enhances our relationship with customers and suppliers. The implementation normally benefits both parties and strengthens the bond between manufacturer and retailer. The implementation of EDI allowed both parties to gain a net cost savings, while providing a better solution: a win, win implementation.

6. EDI complements and enhances our company's marketing effort. The goal of marketing is to deliver a product or service that meets the consumers expectation which includes both price and time frame. Each expectation can have a different priority; at times these can change. One consumer may pay a premium to have a flexible delivery schedule and yet another may shop price regardless of delivery schedule. In either case a manufacturer would like to be the least cost supplier. Companies that have implemented EDI, do so to help control costs while providing a better service.

7. EDI improves productivity. EDI has helped displace many of the labor intensive duties associated with collecting, sending and receiving information. This has allowed companies to an increase information handling activities without increase in labor cost and allowed salespeople to spend more time selling rather than collecting routine orders. EDI has helped companies automate tasks, relocate people to other tasks, and gain a productivity edge.

8. EDI reduces the paper flow between organizations. Reduction in paper handling is one of the many benefits. The volume of paper flowing in and out of companies can be massive: Bergen Brunswig, the second largest pharmaceutical wholesaler in the USA receives between 5,000 and 6,000 invoices a day in envelopes that must be opened by hand. Bergen-Brunswig has negotiated with suppliers to receive these invoices electronically. The United States Postal Service now charges at least \$.29 for first class mail, while “...the cost of a single EDI transmission usually runs around \$.10, and sometimes can be as low as \$.07 or \$.08.” Displacing paper flow with electronic communications can provide efficiencies in cost, speed and ease of use [7].

9. EDI standardizes programs and procedures. The introduction of industry standards has been one of the largest single factors attributed to the growth of EDI. Not having to negotiate transaction sets with each trading partner improves interoperability, though start up costs may still be high. Depending on the size and level of integration, the on-going cost of supporting and growing EDI volumes is minimal. The majority of EDI costs are incurred when integrating EDI into internal systems.

To date there is no global agreement on data standards. However the majority of EDI users use one of the following three standards: American National Standard Institute (ANSI) X12, Uniform Communication Standards (UCS), or Electronic Data Interchange for Administration, Commerce and Transportation (UN/EDIFACT). EDI users continually lobby their respective industry standards board for new and common transaction sets.

10. EDI allows for the reduction in personnel. None of the literature discusses layoffs as a result of implementing EDI. Yet our survey clearly indicates that the gains in productivity have allowed companies to displace existing labor. Maybe the worker(s) has not been released from the company, but reassigned a new task, and that the reductions are being sought through attrition rather than dismissal from the company.

## Top ten disadvantages

The order of EDI disadvantages are now discussed:

1. EDI requires high level management commitment to be successful. “EDI is less a technical concern than a business issue that requires senior levels of nontechnical management as EDI applications become more complex and involve internal constituencies.” [8]. The introduction of EDI can only be successful if senior management has dictated that all functional areas embrace the concept. The entire process of modifying applications and pressuring trading partners on the supply side and customers on the demand side all needs a concerted effort that is well orchestrated.

2. EDI lacks a common understanding and there is limited education. There are still many companies that do not have a good fundamental grasp of EDI. We speculate that this is both at the conceptual level and technical level. The pressuring of trading partners to review the potential use of EDI will force companies to seek out available documentation and education, while creating a demand. Through education, companies will begin to understand and relate to the concepts of EDI.

3. It is hard to quantify the return on investment using EDI. Despite all the claims, there seem to be several benefits that EDI promises, each of which impacts a different functional area of the business, making it difficult to quantify the costs. John McWilliams, a director of financial services at Bergen-Brunswick Corp states that “No one knows how much money EDI saves. If they do say they know, they are lying.” [4].

The inability to provide information into this cost benefit analysis may delay the mass implementation of EDI.

4. EDI requires a high initial capital expense. Depending on the complexity and level of involvement, a company can anticipate spending from \$1,000 to \$500,000 to implement EDI. Much of the cost will be incurred in the modification of existing applications. Although the data is usually accessible, extensive effort is often required to pull the information together. Software packages to assist in the translation and communications, in addition to the computer hardware, must be acquired if not already on hand.

5. EDI requires high volumes before the benefits are attained. The goal of many companies is to substitute EDI for the manual paper and/or verbal based communication system of today. In order to provide for an orderly substitution there must be a migration plan. A company's old paper or verbal based system cannot be replaced or reduced until there is a substantial shift in the use of the systems. In order to provide this shift, significant EDI volumes need to be reached. Some companies such as Premark International Inc., have obtained transitional benefits after migrating just 15% of their total purchase orders to EDI. They state that they save \$360,000 a year by transmitting 18,000 purchase orders electronically to 13 suppliers. The firm estimates that they have reduced the cost of a purchase order from about \$25 to about \$5.

6. There are too many vendors providing connectivity. Two options are available. The first is a system involving two trading partners. A line simply carries bits and bytes from one to the other. Nothing else is involved. Frequently, however, more than two trading partners are involved. To accomplish this usually requires a Value Added Network (VAN) which provides the mechanism that allows one trading partner to send information to another.

The introduction of VAN's has created a logistical nightmare for many firms. Until recently, trading partners were forced to use the same VAN in order to communicate. This often required a contract with several VANs in order to reach all trading partners. The number of VANs currently inter-connected is limited and the service between them is isolated to only a few data standards.

7. EDI standards are still in a state of change. Some industries have been involved with EDI for many years, and their participation at standards committees has ensured a set of transactions for their product group. The process of developing new transaction sets and modifying existing ones is continuous. The lack of standards has forced some companies to take a wait and see attitude, while others, such as K-Mart, have forged ahead creating their own. K-Mart developed its proprietary transmission software and protocols in 1976. and implemented EDI before the development of standards. In time, the public developed standards, but K-Mart chose not to use them, because they were not comprehensive enough. Since those early years K-Mart has adopted both the UCS and ANSI X12 standards.

Many reluctant companies believe that once the major thrust to use EDI is over, all industry groups will have come to a common understanding and the changing of existing standards will slow.

8. EDI impacts organizational structures, procedures and controls. EDI represents an extension of previously internal activities and therefore a constant reexamination and realignment of functional and business unit roles are required. If the

EDI implementation does not include executive level sponsorship the process of realignment may stall and internal groups may find themselves competing in highly political situations.

EDI impacts organizational structure and also the procedures and controls already established in the communication of business information. Applications need to be changed and new procedures established to accommodate this paperless society.

9. EDI causes significant impact on the organizational culture. According to Bob Martone, accounting manager for Shell Oil's Products Organization, cultural considerations are one of the most serious stumbling blocks on the way to implementing EDI. It can introduce dramatic cultural change and curtail personal contact between the companies sales and purchasing people, and those of its trading partners.

Other cultural impacts hinge on the company's human resources. Companies must be prepared to deal with the immediate excess of personnel and the addition of computer-conversant employees. Employees may be required to cross train in order to introduce EDI to other functional areas.

10. The majority of our trading partners do not use EDI. The most limiting factor is the nature of a company's trading partners. Not all trading partners are ready to commit to it. Without cooperative suppliers and customers, EDI capability essentially is worthless. In an effort to reduce this effort many large customers having been taking a "big stick" approach with suppliers and a "soft sell" approach with customers.

## Summary

Clearly the most common theme in advantages and disadvantages of EDI is costs/savings. “It is hard to quantify the return on investment using EDI.” This problem is not unexpected since getting to the bottom line is always difficult when dealing with complex systems.

To the manager trying to decide whether or not to use EDI the findings that:

(1) EDI requires a high initial capital expense and

(2) EDI requires high volumes before benefits are attained

are critical issues and may explain the high ranking of the disadvantage “EDI requires high level management commitment to be successful.”

On the savings side it was noted that:

(1) EDI reduces inventory and inventory carrying costs;

(2) EDI improves productivity;

(3) EDI reduces the paper flow between organizations; and

(4) EDI allows for the reduction in personnel.

Standards continue to be a major issue. The value added network vendors continue to attempt to work out interconnectivity issues as a response to market pressure. When more firms have implemented EDI standards, they will encourage more firms to convert.

The overall synergistic effect of EDI, is reflected in comments such as: “EDI enhances our relationship with customers and suppliers” and “EDI complements and enhances our company’s marketing efforts.” This perceived competitive advantage may be the major driving force in the future.
