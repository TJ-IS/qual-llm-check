# The impact of purchasing policy on electronic markets and electronic hierarchies

David Rosenthal, Shashi K. Shah and Baichun Xiao

Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ, USA

Markets and hierarchies are two mechanisms used by organizations to coordinate steps through the value chain. For the last twenty years information technology has been used to enhance both of these coordination structures. It has been suggested (Malone et al.) that the improvement of information technology price/performance will have a more profound effect on markets leading to an increase in transactions coordinated by markets. This paper investigates the feasibility of electronic markets in light of the purchasing policy dictated by corporate strategy. Specifically, we will discuss principles of purchasing which would impede or prevent the adoption of electronic markets. We have supplemented our discussion with results from a market research survey which sought impressions of issues involved with an electronic market.

Keywords: Electronic market; Electronic hierarchy.

In addition to the effect on market structure, electronic markets will cause a fundamental change in organization structure. This research analyzes the viability of electronic markets from the perspective of purchasing policy. This point of view, not previously examined, is warranted as the use of electronic markets/electronic hierarchies has significant ramifications on how the job of purchasing is performed.

The analysis is divided into two parts. The first part identifies principles of modern purchasing policy that favor the use of hierarchies. These principles are the outgrowth of contemporary business strategies. The principles are examined to see if advances in IT make them available within an electronic market environment. The second part is an empirical study of the chemical industry, which supports ideas expressed in our theoretical analysis. ## 2. Background

Markets and hierarchies are two mechanisms used by economies to coordinate transactions through the value-added chain. According to Malone et al., "Market forces determine the design, price, quantity and target delivery schedule for a given product that serve as an input into another process. The buyer of the good compares its many possible sources and makes a choice based on the best combination of these attributes" [14].

Hierarchies coordinate the flow of materials through managerial decisions. “Managerial decisions, not the interaction of market forces, determine design, price, quantity and delivery schedule. Thus buyers do not select a supplier from a group of potential suppliers; they simply work with a single predetermined one” [14].

As noted in [14], the variants that exist between these two categories can usually be classified as primarily markets or hierarchies.

Electronic markets and electronic hierarchies use IT to facilitate transaction coordination. An electronic market (hierarchy) is a market (hierarchy) that is implemented using electronic media, such as computers and communication systems.

Malone et al.'s analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of markets and hierarchies is based on relative production and coordination costs. Production cost is defined as the cost of producing the goods. "Coordination cost includes the transaction cost of all the information processing necessary to coordinate the work that produces the goods" [14]. For markets, this process includes selecting suppliers, negotiating contracts, and so forth. For hierarchies, this involves managerial decision making, accounting, planning and control processes.

Malone and Williamson [14,24] conclude that because of economies of scale, production cost is lower in a market environment than in a hierarchy. However, because of the need to gather and analyze data from many potential suppliers, the coordination cost for markets is higher than it is for hierarchies.

Williamson [26,27] further identifies two major components that affect transaction cost and production cost: asset specificity and complexity of product description. He defines a good as being asset specific if it cannot readily be used by other firms because of site specificity, physical asset specificity or human asset specificity. An example of a physically specific good is a specialized machine tool designed for a single purpose. Malone et al. [14] assert that asset specific goods are more likely to be coordinated within a hierarchy because of the time/cost it takes for the buyer and the supplier to coordinate and produce the good that meets the buyer's specifications.

Complexity of product description refers to the amount of information needed to describe the good in sufficient detail so that vendors can determine whether they are able to supply it. Common stock is an example of a good with low complexity of description. The specification of a computer program is a good with a high complexity of description. It is generally agreed that goods with low complexity are more suitable for markets, while those with high complexity are better handled within a hierarchy. This is due to the high coordination cost of transmitting large amounts of information associated with complex product descriptions. Consequently, a firm that buys goods with a high complexity of product description is constrained to use hierarchies. This limits the number of suppliers to which the information is sent.

The result of the improvement of IT price/performance provides facilities to coordinate the transmission of information economically. This effect will be particularly strong for those goods that have a high complexity of product description. IT innovations such as databases and high-bandwidth communications allow for more efficient processing of description data between buyers and suppliers [15]. This should lower the data gathering and analysis component of the buyer coordination cost. Eventually this coordination cost will decrease to the point where using the electronic market will provide the good at a lower overall cost. This will make electronic markets the more attractive coordination mechanism as buyers can take advantage of lower production costs without incurring high coordination costs.

These ideas lead to the following conclusions [15]. First, organizations will be inclined to shift from hierarchies to markets. Second, the use of vertical integration, which is a special case of hierarchies, will also diminish in favor of outsourcing.

Gurbaxani and Whang [5] analyzed the impact of IT on organizational structure using agency theory and transaction cost economics. Their goal was to see the effect of IT on firm size and allocation of decision authority. They found that although IT increases external communication efficiency, thus leading to a potential increase in horizontal integration, the improvement of internal communication due to IT may also cause effective vertical integration. Therefore, “a firm’s use of IT can result in increase or decrease in either the horizontal or vertical dimension of firm size” [5].

A conclusion contrary to Malone et al. is reached by researchers of purchasing policy. A review of the purchasing literature, both academic and practical, shows that it will be more advantageous to reduce the number of suppliers. This notion is well described by Newman [18].

"The last decade has produced a significant shift from the traditional way in which purchasing has performed its major task of awarding business contracts. That shift has been most pronounced in the area of sourcing. The traditional approach of utilizing a number of sources for the same commodity has given way to the use of the single source of supply. The rationale for this change is simple. Buying organizations are seeking the obvious benefits of reduced paperwork, better quality and supplier innovation".

It is the preponderance of research results from this area that has motivated us to examine the claims of the applicability of electronic markets.

## 3. Purchasing policy issues

As previously noted, prior research did not address issues within purchasing policy to consider the effect of electronic markets. Operationally, purchasing would be impacted by a change from hierarchies to markets. Given the uniformity of opinion among purchasing researchers to move to a hierarchy, it seems prudent that the arguments for this policy be explored in light of the workings of an electronic market.

In order to give this analysis structure, we will evaluate purchasing policy as an implementation of corporate strategy. Specifically we look at the general business strategies/goals of quality, cost control and product differentiation to see:

a. how each is currently implemented within the purchasing function;

b. the rationale for current implementation;

c. if electronic markets would provide all the features required to attain the goal;

d. if IT will change the ability to implement these goals within an electronic market or an electronic hierarchy.

## 3.1. Quality

Sparked by superior reliability of Japanese products and growing global competition, firms have incorporated product quality as a principle that the entire organization must strive to meet. It is clear that this trend is not a fad as competition and improved manufacturing techniques have raised the acceptable quality standard.

The purchasing decision plays a large role in the quest for quality. This has been substantiated by a number of different researchers. Crosby [22] estimates that $50\%$ of a company's quality nonconformances are caused by defective purchased material. Ishikawa [7] attributes $70\%$ of the blame for defective purchased material to the purchasing organization. Deming [3] maintains that the practice of buying based primarily on price must end and instead that purchasing policy must concern itself with quality.

The basic strategy employed within the purchasing decision to attain high quality standards is tighter integration with supplier planning and scheduling systems $[4]$ . This approach requires the supplier to become more actively involved in the manufacturing and planning processes of the buyer's organization. This type of involvement is most productive if it is initiated within the planning phase of product development. In that way, assumptions about the technical aspects of a purchased raw material can be substantiated. This contrasts with the method of designing a product without regard for the availability/cost of the component parts.

The idea of more actively including the supplier in the design/manufacturing process is part of a shift in buyer/supplier relations. In the past, suppliers were viewed as adversaries. Current research indicates that viewing them in a partnership role leads to quality improvement for the buyer [10,17]. There have been studies that show that poor communications between buyer and supplier is a fundamental weakness that adversely affects the goal of quality improvement. The problem stems from the adversarial relationship, not from the lack of efficient communication technology.

The implementation of a quality driven purchasing policy will incorporate the following ideas:

a. Identify one or a small set of suppliers for each purchased good. These suppliers will be qualified by a rigorous evaluation process. This process examines the supplier's manufacturing and financial status. Attention must be paid to the quality controls the supplier has in place.

b. Establish a close rapport with the supplier. The supplier should be informed of goals and methodologies. Whenever possible, include the supplier in the design process.

c. Foster a quality improvement relationship. This might include providing the supplier with quality training or the installation of a statistical process control (SPC) program.

d. Monitor supplier performance.

To lower coordination costs, current research suggests that the total number of suppliers be reduced. By doing so, the cost to implement each part will be reduced. Our empirical study shows that most companies deal with less than three suppliers per purchased good in a relationship that is hierarchical.

It does not seem that improvements in IT price/performance will affect this process to allow selection from a larger set of suppliers. The tasks associated with steps a, b and c are unstructured or interpersonal and therefore unlikely to be affected by IT. Task d would gain from IT advances. However, it is not clear that the cost savings gained would be sufficient to change purchasing policy to work in an electronic market.

## 3.2. Cost reduction

While minimizing cost has always been a corporate goal, this idea has been emphasized more in the current business environment. In answer to the push for cost reduction, purchasing policy has changed to embrace two ideas: better inventory management and lower material costs.

## A. Inventory management

The ultimate goal of inventory management is to minimize total inventory cost while meeting some constraints, such as service level. This has led to many inventory management systems, with

Just-in-Time (JIT) as the one which has become increasingly popular. A company switching to JIT will need to alter its purchasing policy from the traditional method. These changes are most notable in the areas shown in Table 1 [23].

The table highlights two issues with respect to the use of markets and hierarchies. The first is that the thrust of supplier selection is towards reduction of suppliers. As with quality, the reason for this decision is that the level of coordination requires a non-adversarial relationship that is not typical in the traditional purchasing method.

The second issue involves the key to JIT, which is the ability to receive frequent shipments of small quantities. For this to occur reliably, the distance between source and destination should be minimized. While this is not a necessary condition for supplier selection, it does impose asset specificity (site) on the material. As Malone suggests, goods with asset specificity are more suitable for hierarchies. In the case of JIT, the site specificity is not a characteristic of the good but of the delivery process.

The implementation of JIT requires commitments of both the buyer and supplier. Based upon estimates of the purchaser's needs, the supplier is required to keep inventory available. The purchaser is required to buy the estimated amount over time. As a result of this commitment, the purchaser would be limited to a small set of suppliers. This suggests that electronic markets would not be applicable.

Clearly, the increase of IT capabilities would not allow JIT to be workable within an electronic market as non-adversarial relationships and site specificity are not affected.

## B. Material costs

While the emphasis on cost as the primary factor in the purchasing decision has diminished, there is still a requirement to minimize it. Theoretically, purchasing decisions weighted towards lower production costs would use markets as their coordination mechanism. Although we are not able to refute this assertion, we will show that hierarchies can be structured to facilitate lower production and coordination costs and that these reductions can be passed on to the buyer.

There are two strategies a buyer can use to achieve lower costs while working within a hierarchy. The first revolves around the idea of cooperation between buyer and supplier. Rubin and Carter [20] show that it is possible for a buyer and a supplier to compromise on an agreement with the result that both parties improve profitability. Research in purchasing refers to this agreement as “win-win” [16]. (Porter [19] shows that in a hierarchy, buyer and supplier can achieve improved profitability through “joint optimization”). An example of a “win-win” agreement is when buyer and supplier agree on the price of material as a function of the supplier’s costs. In this arrangement, the supplier is guaranteed an established mark up over documented cost. If the buyer can coordinate purchases with the supplier in a manner that lowers the supplier’s cost, then this savings would be passed on to the buyer. Obviously the buyer benefits from lower costs.

In general, there are two ways that the buyer can lower costs incurred by the supplier. The first is to include the supplier, if possible, in design discussions. This would allow the supplier to suggest better (in terms of quality or cost) alternatives to proposed raw materials $[17]$ . The second involves better coordination between buyer and supplier. Specifically, the buyer tries to create a schedule of material delivery that fits better into the supplier's schedule $[9,17]$ . Under some conditions it is possible that a particular order quantity would be superior to the economical order quantity (EOQ) for the buyer and economic lot size for the supplier $[1]$ .

The second strategy used by a buyer in a hierarchy to receive better pricing is quantity discounts. Within markets or hierarchies the policy of discounting prices as a function of the quantity purchased (or committed to purchase) is standard. Those buyers who split purchases among many suppliers based on lowest current price forfeit the chance to lower overall price by concentrating purchases with a few suppliers.

A buyer purchasing within a hierarchy increases the effect of quantity discounts. While there are many methods for pricing within a hierarchy, all are predicated on the buyer committing to purchase some agreed amount of product over a period of time. The overall amount is used as the quantity on which to establish the price. Clearly, this would lead to larger discounts as compared to material purchased on individual orders.

Aside from possibly lowering price, hierarchies can also provide for price stability. The primary benefit of electronic markets is that they provide an efficient mechanism for establishing the “best” price for the buyer at any time. The down side for buyers working in a market is that they have to absorb all price fluctuations over time. For some companies, these fluctuations are not acceptable. These companies can assure themselves of less volatile pricing by establishing a contract price with a particular supplier.

## 3.3. Product differentiation

Firms that seek competitive advantage through product differentiation influence the purchasing decision in a variety of ways. Landeros and Monczka [9] maintain that conventional price-driven purchasing will hinder manufacturing firms who emphasize product differentiation. They posit that suppliers should be selected on the basis of the relevancy of the supplier's research and development with respect to the buying firm's product line.

Two other considerations that impact the purchasing decision need to be discussed. The first is the need for material specifications that exceed the industry standard. The second is the need to maintain security of material purchased. These two concerns and the point made by Landeros and Monczka, are best addressed by buyers purchasing within a hierarchy.

## A. Material specialization

Industrial goods can be segmented into two classes: commodities and specialties. A commodity is a good that can be purchased from many suppliers, where there is very little difference in the characteristics of the good from supplier to supplier. A specialty is a good that has few suppliers and/or the difference in its characteristics from supplier to supplier is large.

In some industries, buyers are blurring this distinction. In the chemical industry, for example, buyers are turning commodities into specialties by specifying product acceptance criteria that exceed the industry accepted standard for that product.

The following are examples of this behavior:

\- Some buyers set quality control specifications that exceed the standard acceptable range. In the chemical industry, an example would be when a purchaser specifies that a raw material have a pH value in a range that is narrower than the one generally accepted.

\- Even though an item is classified as a commodity, some purchasers will only buy the commodity manufactured by a specific supplier. Their rationale is that there are slight variations of the commodity from one supplier to another. In industries such as chemical manufacturing where a new product is designed in the lab using sample materials, it is desirable to minimize any variations in production from the laboratory formulation. This means using materials from the same manufacturer who supplied the sample. Some buyers may even require that the material be from specific lots.

These requirements are better served in a hierarchy than in a market. In order to satisfy the buyer's requirement of tighter quality controls or manufacturer specific material, detailed knowledge and coordination are needed between buyer and supplier.

For the purchase of specialty goods, the use of markets seems limited. First, if there are few suppliers, the information gathering cost is low even without an electronic market. Second, it is unlikely that once a specialty is incorporated into a process, a substitute for the specialty could be used without extensive testing. Third, since a specialty has no industry standard name, it would have to be described through some language. We suspect that it is not possible to create a universal language for all industries. In fact, it is likely that a unique language would be required for each industry. (Within the EDI domain, specialization of the same business document was required for different industries.)

## B. Security

The user of an electronic market transmits to all participating suppliers the requirements of the materials being purchased. These requirements include material specification, quantity, delivery schedule, etc. In general, any company that wishes to join the electronic market could receive this information. That is, if the electronic market was administered by a third party, then the only restriction to accessing this information would be that a company pay the fee for joining and using the market.

In some industries, the knowledge of the names and the quantities of the raw material purchased is proprietary. (For example, in the pharmaceutical industry, it is possible to deduce a formula for a proprietary drug given a few of its ingredients.) Within an electronic market this information would be available to a competitor if the competitor subscribed to the electronic market as a supplier.

We can think of three ways that electronic markets could be structured to address the security problem. All three methods impose restrictions that would severely limit the electronic market's utility. The following describes the methods and their restrictions.

(1) Allow buyers to submit requests anonymously. This solution has the drawback that suppliers typically will not quote unknown buyers. While it seems counter-intuitive that a supplier would be reluctant to inform a potential customer of its products, this does happen. For fear of providing competitors with useful information, some industrial organizations will standardly request background information from prospects. The information is checked for its veracity. If the review of the prospect's information is suspicious, either general information or no information will be sent. (Our empirical research supports this.) Additionally, if the sales department is given only general information about the prospect, it is likely that standard or list pricing would be given. The prospect would have no chance to negotiate a better price based on promises for continued business.

(2) Restrict the electronic market to only those suppliers who have demonstrated that they are capable of supplying the materials for which they are receiving requests for quotations. This solution means the electronic market provider needs to validate the production/distribution capabilities of all supplier members of the market. In addition, the provider would have to periodically update this validation. For a market that lists many products, this task would be difficult and expensive.

(3) Allow the buyer to select the set of suppliers that would receive information. This solution reduces the effectiveness of the electronic market as the buyer is essentially selecting approved suppliers. This method would address the security issue but would sacrifice the ability of the electronic market to provide the best price, as the lowest price supplier may not be included in the set of approved suppliers or is unknown to the buyer. In the current environment, firms with this security concern tend to limit the number of suppliers with which they deal. They do so in order to reduce the possibility of unauthorized disclosure of their purchasing information.

In summary, the issues of quality, material costs and product differentiation impose constraints on the purchasing decision. Current re-

## 4. Empirical study

In this section we will discuss results from an empirical study that focused on issues pertinent to electronic markets and hierarchies. The study conducted was a market research survey constructed to measure the likelihood that electronic markets would be a viable method of buying and selling within the chemical industry. This survey was commissioned by a firm that had implemented the technological components of an electronic market. That is, they built a computer and communications system that would allow for the creation, sending, receiving, responding and reviewing functions of an electronic market. The firm targeted the chemical industry as their first market for this product. Prior to the survey, it was thought that the chemical industry was dominated by industrial purchasing and commodity items. These two characteristics indicate that an electronic market would be commercially viable.

A market research firm [21] was hired to conduct the survey. Three questionnaires were designed and sent to three segments of the market – purchasing managers, sales managers, and company executives. Each version of the questionnaire was mailed to 500 respondents (a total of 1500). The names of respondents were obtained from subscriber lists of chemical industry magazines. Table 5 exhibits that even under special situations, at least 70% of the companies surveyed still do not buy frequently from alternative vendors.

It appears that hierarchies are the dominant coordination mechanism in the chemical industry as represented by this sample. The actual selection between market and hierarchy is determined by many factors. Since the survey questionnaire did not address most of these factors, we are not able to address the relevance of these factors based on the survey data. However, the nature of the products in the chemical market is clearly an important factor in favor of hierarchies.

Products in the chemical industry fall into the commodity/speciality categories discussed previously. The results of the survey show (based on the response of sales executives) that 46% of the companies sold more than 70% specialty chemicals, while only 31% of the companies sold more than 70% commodity chemicals. In addition, 24% of the companies surveyed said they sold 100% specialty chemicals and only 12% of the companies sold 100% commodity chemicals. The average sales of specialty and commodity chemicals for all companies are 53% and 40.6%, respectively.

The following null hypothesis was tested and the result is shown in Table 6.

A majority of the companies sells more commodity chemicals than specialty chemicals.

The above null hypothesis is rejected at the 1% significance level. In other words, in the chemical industry represented by this sample, most companies sell more specialties than commodities.

Since most chemicals sold are specialties, the statistical results reported confirm the theory that hierarchies are the preferred coordination channel if complexity of product description is present, other things being equal.

It seems likely that if suppliers are reluctant to release their product information to unknown buyers, the utility in a buyer sending an RFQ through electronic markets would be greatly diminished. $Q_{11}$ attempted to measure this resistance. The proportion of those who were very interested in this electronic database service is significantly lower than 30%. When asked if they would like to use the electronic market service, the intensity of interest for the companies was relatively low. Less than 50% of companies said they were somewhat interested in obtaining this service, and less than 15% of them showed a strong desire (see Table 7). ## 5. Conclusion

## Gurbaxani and Whang [5] note that

“... information systems should be assessed and compared with regard to specific managerial contexts. Business functions, market conditions, industry characteristics, and organization cultures each constitute different dimensions in which to evaluate an information system; a monolithic application of IT concepts at the firm level without regard to these factors is likely to be incomplete and incorrect”

Our paper adopts this idea by investigating the impact of IT on coordination structures, while limiting the scope of the effects to the business function of purchasing policy.

Our theoretical analysis produced the following conclusions:

(1) The business goals of quality, cost reduction and product differentiation influence purchasing policy towards the use of hierarchies as the preferred coordination mechanism.

(2) The above three goals would be compromised by a firm that switched from a hierarchy to an electronic market. The effects of IT on the three goals are limited within an electronic market.

(3) The advances in IT can result in adoption of either electronic markets or electronic hierarchies, depending on the nature of the firm or industry.

The empirical study of buying and selling in the chemical industry produced the following results:

(1) The bulk of the purchasing is currently done in a hierarchy. This was surprising as our intuition had been that most chemicals are commodities and as such are amenable to being purchased within a market.

(2) There was not an enormous interest in using an electronic market to transact chemical purchases. It was thought that the reason the chemical industry is hierarchical is because a satisfactory market mechanism does not exist. The market research indicates that even if an electronic market were available, its usage would be low.

(3) Suppliers would be reluctant to distribute product data to unknown prospects. This is a significant barrier to a firm attempting to create an electronic market. If buyers are unable to get reliable data through the electronic market, there would be little incentive to use it. If buyers stopped listing purchasing requests in the electronic market, suppliers would have little incentive to participate.

The results from the analysis of purchasing policy contrast sharply with those presented by Malone et al. [14]. Specifically, their notion of a generic shift towards electronic markets is not corroborated by our results. It is our belief that Malone's prediction is more applicable in consumer oriented purchasing (American Airlines Sabre system, Chemical Bank's Pronto home banking) and is less applicable in industrial purchasing.

Predicting the effects of IT is precarious. It is especially difficult with respect to this topic as there is a scarcity of data relating to the component costs of coordination. In addition, it is quite likely that the coordination costs vary from industry to industry. It would be useful if future research tried to capture these coordination costs for companies using different coordination mechanisms within the same industries. It would then be possible for a more quantitative analysis of this problem.
