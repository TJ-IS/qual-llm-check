# Current practices in management information systems

Mary C. Jones and Kirk P. Arnett
Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS, USA

The results of a nationwide survey of U.S. systems analysts chosen from firms listed in Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives are presented. Respondents are highly experienced analysts who work with large-scale information systems projects in mature IS environments. Technical, organizational, and intrinsic facets of the information systems development process are assessed. Findings of the study indicate that while these facets of the development process are changing, the changes are not occurring as rapidly as the literature often suggests. Systems flowcharts continue to be the most widely used systems development tool. Relatively newer products such as CASE tools are used by over one-half of analysts, yet their benefits are not being fully exploited.

Keywords: Systems analysis and design; Systems development methods; Structured methods; Design tools; CASE tools; Automated tools; Programming languages; Fourth generation languages; Information systems professionals; Information systems practices

## 1. Introduction

Over several decades, information systems (IS) have rapidly evolved as a catalyst for organizational problem solving $[23]$ . IS support today involves both problem solving at various levels in the organization and also the promotion of information technology as the primary weapon for competitive advantage. Management consulting activities, such as systems design and development, have even ushered nontraditional IS firms (e.g., Andersen Consulting, American Express, Dun & Bradstreet, Ernst and Young, and Price Waterhouse) into Datamation's list of the top 100 U.S. IS revenue producers $[5]$ .

IS activities are being outsourced and developed by end users at an increasing rate; however, the system development life cycle (SDLC) remains the primary method for IS development in many organizations. Thus, the efficiency and effectiveness of IS design and development are crucial to the survival of an organization. Both general managers and information systems managers agree that the delivery of effective information systems is a key issue for the 1990s [28].

Several problems arise in the design and development process; if these are not solved, they may

In order to provide support, the IS analyst must serve as a liaison between users, who have some understanding of the organizational aspect of the IS, and technicians, who have an understanding of its technical aspects $[13,30]$ . “The analyst must make a match between what is currently possible … and what is worth doing for the organization…” $[15]$ .

A review of the literature indicates that several studies have assessed the IS development process in industry $[11,19,24]$ . Three streams of investigation flow from this. Of the studies that address technical aspects, the focus has been on the tools/techniques and methodologies used in industry $[4,21,26]$ . Studies that assess user related aspects including user satisfaction and user involvement promote improved quality of IS based on user acceptance $[2,3,20]$ . The third area addresses intrinsic, psychological factors that may impact the final IS $[1,6,16,29]$ . However, these studies focus primarily on the user. There is little empirical work that assesses intrinsic factors that are related to either the systems analyst or the IS project manager $[8,9]$ .

Because of the liaison role of the analyst in the development process, he/she has a substantial impact on the system. The IS project manager also has a significant impact on the decisions that determine the outcome of the final system. Therefore, factors related to these individuals will influence the development process.

In addition, organizational factors may impact the analyst role and the success of the IS. These factors include the age of the IS department, the organizational level to which the IS manager reports, and level of the IS department with respect to the CEO/President of the organization. The literature suggests that the rank and location of the executive responsible for the management of an organization's IS is related to the usefulness of the system.

Perceived usefulness tends to be greater the higher the level of the MIS department. “The likelihood of a successful MIS effort declines rapidly the lower the rank of the executive to whom the MIS chief reports and is (quite small) if the executive responsible is more than two levels below the chief executive of the particular organization which the MIS serves” [12]. In addition,

Ein-Dor and Segev suggest that an MIS effort is more likely to be successful if the responsible executive is “not identified with any specific functional area.”

Another organizational characteristic that may impact success of an IS is the age of the IS department. Franz and Robey [14] provide support for the proposition that the older the IS department, the more useful the systems developed.

This paper examines the IS function in industry to determine how the liaison role is being performed and provides information about current practices in IS development. Several factors are assessed, including intrinsic and organizational factors as well as technical factors. Factors examined include (1) the educational background and experience level of IS project managers and systems analysts; (2) organizational characteristics of IS departments; and (3) the extent to which analysis and design techniques are used in practice.

## 2. Survey questionnaire design

The questionnaire was designed to capture perceptions of main-line systems analysts in the organizations. A filtering question was used to select only responses of systems analysts who had worked on large-scale systems development projects. The survey first defined a systems development project as one that requires at least two person-years to develop. Respondents were then asked the number of systems development projects on which they had worked. Those respondents who reported zero were eliminated from further analysis; presumably this eliminates end-user and novice developer responses.

The remaining survey results therefore report only on experienced IS professionals. Descriptive statistics seem to support this. The questionnaire, then, is designed to measure the technical, organizational, and intrinsic factors that affect the IS development process as seen by systems analysts who are experienced in large-scale IS development efforts.

## 2.1. Data collection

The survey instrument was mailed to a systematic sampling of the top IS manager of 730 organizations representing a cross-section of industries listed in Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives. The majority of firms in the directory do not list an IS manager, therefore we feel this sample reflects those firms that place a high priority on IS. A two-stage survey distribution was used, whereby the IS manager was asked to deliver the survey to a systems analyst in the same department. The adjusted response rate was 12%. The response rate is relatively low, but the sample size was judged to be sufficient for analysis. The low response may be due, in part, to the use of a two-stage survey distribution method and the stringent experience requirement for respondents.

## 2.2. Technical factors

A survey of the literature indicates that there are several techniques available to facilitate the development of an IS at various stages of the process. These include tools for structured analysis, structured design, and structured programming $[7,10,32]$ . The degree of familiarity of a respondent with the techniques and the extent to which they are used are requested in the questionnaire. Other technical aspects that are addressed include use of prototyping, fourth generation languages, programming languages, and type of hardware on which the IS is implemented.

The literature also suggests that there is value in automated approaches to IS development, such as CASE tools $[17,27]$ . A survey conducted in 1987 indicates that only 29% of the respondents utilize automated tools to support systems development. However, Necco et al. (1987) anticipated that the use of such tools would continue to increase. Therefore, the extent of respondents' use of CASE tools is also examined.

## 2.3. Organizational factors

Three factors are assessed: (1) age is measured as the number of years that the IS department has existed; (2) position to which the IS manager reports is measured as either the CEO/President or the vice president of the area of which IS is a part; and (3) organizational level is measured as the number of levels between the IS department and the CEO/President.

## 2.4. Intrinsic factors

Intrinsic factors that are examined include the educational level, educational background, and experience level of both the analyst and the project manager. Educational level is defined as the highest degree obtained (e.g., high school, bachelors, etc.). Educational background is defined as the primary field of study (e.g., management information systems, engineering, computer science, etc.). The experience level of the analyst is defined as the number of years working in the IS area and the number of IS projects with which the subject has worked. The experience level of the project manager is defined as the number of years of experience in IS.

## 3. Results

We first determined the characteristics of systems analysts, project managers, and characteristics of the organization; then addressed the practical application of systems analysis tools and techniques.

The third most popular was Warnier/Orr Diagrams, with 14% of the respondents ranking this as their first or second choice. While a majority of the analysts use 4GLs and prototyping to some extent (68% and 70% respectively), they are interested in learning more about them. Some authors have predicted the demise of 4GLs and the imbedding of 4GLs within CASE tools [25,31]; results of this study indicate that this is unlikely or premature.

3.4.2. CASE tools Table 6 gives a profile of the use of CASE tools to support various IS development activities.

Only about thirty percent of the respondents indicated that they use CASE tools, and this is consistent with similar studies. However, one author suggests that “it is hard to pick up any computer periodical these days without reading about CASE, and that CASE technology is one of the hottest areas in computer science where well over 100 vendors vie for purchases” [25]. Thus, it seems that the use of CASE should be more extensive.

The relative lack of CASE use might be explained by the relatively high percentage of maintenance activities today. They still constitute more than 70% of the activities in many shops [22]. The largest application seems to be use of CASE for data dictionaries and documentation rather than for software reengineering when converting existing spaghetti code into CASE models.

3.4.3. Hardware & languages. Table 7 shows types of hardware used for IS applications in this study.

The majority of respondents indicated that mainframes are used for over 40% of the IS applications in their organizations. This seems a reasonable result as the survey deals with mature firms who have probably had critical transaction-oriented applications running for some time. The next most common type of hardware is minicomputers. Corporate lifeblood applications executing on PC platforms, while certainly increasing in number, continue to be a rarity among mature IS shops.

The languages used are profiled in Table 8. As expected, the most commonly used language is COBOL. Well over one-half the respondents indicated that they use COBOL for over 40% of their applications. There is no new news here. There are 70 billion lines of COBOL code in IS shops today [18]. However, this study's 58% COBOL usage pales in comparison with an earlier report that showed that COBOL was used in 95% of the responding organizations for “some use and heavy use combined.” The sheer volume of existing code, coupled with improved standards and new maintenance tools, will keep COBOL a dominant force for some time.

Analysts were also asked to indicate which languages they expected to be using in five years. Once again, well over one-half indicated COBOL (58%) as their first or second choice, followed by C and RPG (both at 24%). Many analysts (27%) indicated that they anticipate using another language (other than those listed): they are primarily 4GLs or code generation products.

There is a slow movement away from the use of the more traditional procedural languages. Clearly, the move will take place, but it is not likely to be as dramatic or rapid as some of the literature and vendor claims would have us believe.

## 4. Summary and conclusions

Several surveys have reported on various facets of the systems development life cycle. Our survey took a broad brush approach in an attempt to capture most of the aspects covered in other studies. Two factors make our work distinctive. First, the respondents are systems analysts, rather than IS managers; indeed, the respondents may be more in touch with their work environments than are managers, particularly in the usage of tools, methodologies, and techniques.

Next, respondents answered questions that identified them as persons who work on large-scale systems development projects with resource requirements greater than two person-years. Almost one-half (49%) of the responding analysts had over 15 years experience in information systems, and 45% had worked on 10 or less IS projects. These statistics support the notion of a highly experienced group of analysts working with complex and large-scale information systems projects. By design, this group contrasts sharply with systems analysts who quickly develop less complex applications and who have been the subject of other studies. The results here come from a highly skilled group of systems analysts who represent a large, though often hidden, segment of the IS profession.

The organizations reported in this survey possess several interesting characteristics. They indicate highly placed and mature IS departments; each has an IS manager listed by name in the Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives. Most organizations do not name the IS manager. Just about one-half of the responding organizations have IS managers who report at the Executive VP level or higher, and 89% of them are no more than two organizational levels from the CEO/President.

Systems flowcharts remain the most familiar systems tool. CASE tools are used primarily for documentation and data dictionaries: they are not used to any large extent in mature IS shops where mainframe computers are used 62% of the time to develop more than 40% of the IS applications. These shops still rely heavily on COBOL. It is the only language used by more than 50% of the organizations to develop at least 40% of their applications.

System development methodologies and new tools and techniques are being applied by the analysts responsible for liaison activities. The tools are not, however, being used to take over all systems development activities. Analysts who want to remain on the leading edge of their field will familiarize themselves with new technologies. Managers should recognize the gradual nature of changes in IS methods and encourage learning and experimentation with new products. Change will come: backlogs will be reduced; user satisfaction will improve; and maintenance activities will consume smaller portions of IS resources. But the changes will be evolutionary, not revolutionary.
