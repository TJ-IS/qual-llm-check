## Introduction

Most Expert System (ES) research has focused on the technical aspects of ES: the language used to write the software, the type of reasoning, the way the knowledge base is organized, etc. Measurement of the benefits of ES is now needed to ensure their continued use and proliferation. Although a few recent studies have addressed the impact of ES on decision makers and organizations [23], there remains a paucity of research. The purpose of this paper is to show potential impact on the individual decision maker: the augmentation of the user's domain knowledge by past use of an ES.

Many of the ES in use and under development share the goal of providing consistent, expert-level knowledge to individuals who lack experience in an area. Many studies $[21,29]$ attempt to measure whether or not the intended knowledge is actually represented in the knowledge base of the ES. They consider the ES successful if it is determined that this knowledge transfer does, indeed, occur.

The lore of ES suggests that the system will help the non-expert to attain a level of performance closer to that of a domain expert. In some instances, it is not crucial that they retain the knowledge portrayed by the system (although it would be a desirable second-order effect), since they are required to use it for all their problem-solving. Yet, not all novices will have continued or constant access to the ES. Some ES are designed as Intelligent Tutoring Systems for inclusion in training programs $[12]$ . This study examines the incremental benefits of ES upon problem-solving characteristics of novices.

## Literature review

Good managers and other professionals are those who can make good decisions quickly [2]. For the purpose of evaluating ES it is critical to study their contribution to the professional's performance in terms of better decision quality, shorter decision making time, and greater confidence in the decision.

## Decision quality

There are two dominant schools of thought on good decision making: one emphasizes the process, the other mainly considers the outcome. In practice, we note that an effective decision maker is one who makes good decisions, that is, a high proportion of correct outcomes $[33]$ . In this study, decision quality is operationalized as the ratio of correct solutions to the total number of problems presented to the subjects. However, we will also collect two (gross) surrogates of process by measuring confidence-in and time-to-make decisions, recognizing that there are many positive and negative influences that determine time.

ES technology is not intended to replace the entire decision making process [11]. In point of fact, there currently are no ES that perform all of the steps in the decision making process. Furthermore, some of these steps will probably never be fully fulfilled. However, since ES do provide consistent solutions to given problems, they may enhance the non-expert's decision quality.

## Decision speed

In many cases a decision must be made quickly; e.g., a decision to invest in stock may prove disastrous if carried out too late, because the price of the stock may have changed. Studies have demonstrated the relationship between a person's knowledge base and decision-making time. Park and Lessig [22] found that the more the subjects were familiar with a variety of product brands, the faster they decided which brand was best. An expert is expected to make a decision in less time than a novice. In fact, Wiedenbeck [35] found that expert programmers made decisions much faster than novice programmers.

It would follow then, that a technology that aids in reducing the time spent in decision-making would be a valuable asset to an organization, ceteris paribus. ES may provide this asset, particularly for domain novices. Lamberti and Wallace [15,16] measured problem solving time of experts and novices, when using and not using an ES. They found that low skilled programmers using ES shortened their problem solving time, to a greater degree than highly skilled programmers.

Rathburn and Weinroth [24] also noted a favorable improvement in a study of two groups of student novices. The one using an ES solved problems faster and with greater accuracy than the group who had to rely on a textbook; the study said nothing about performance differences after the Intelligent Tutoring System was removed, so that there is no indication of a long-term effect of the ES. Our study was intended to measure the difference in decision making time that can be attributed to previous exposure to an ES. Since the decision maker will no longer be constrained to solve problems in the manner mandated by the ES, we measure only that decision making time that is under the subjects' control: the time that elapsed from the moment the problem had been presented to the time the decision maker reached a decision.

## Decision confidence

Confidence in a decision is the degree to which a decision maker believes that the decision will bring about the desired outcomes $[14,17]$ and the greater the probability that the decision will be accepted by others $[8]$ . People perceive confidence as credibility $[34]$ .

If people learn from the ES they use, we should see greater confidence of the users in their decisions. This confidence is distinct from the users and managers confidence in the ability of the ES to give high quality, consistent recommendations. Our study attempts to see if ES users have greater confidence in their decisions than non-users, and if their confidence increases over the period of exposure to the ES.

## Expertise

Expertise is the skill and knowledge that some people possess; it results in performance that is far above the norm. Expertise often consists of massive amounts of factual information, coupled with rules-of-thumb, simplifications, rare facts and wise procedures all compiled in ways that allow the expert to analyze specific types of problems in an efficient manner. These include chunking $[20,27,28]$ , examining the “big picture” $[6,18,31]$ , reliance on base rates $[30,32]$ , hypothesis formulation $[4,10]$ and use of heuristics $[13]$ .

Organizations could benefit significantly more from an ES if it increased their employees' domain knowledge. Research is needed on the role of ES in enhancing the expertise of users without personal experience. In a case study of the users of a computer configuration ES, Woherem [36] states that 22 out of 24 users felt that use of the Configurer led to increases in their output, accuracy, and competence.

In this study, we attempt to measure the benefits resulting from personal knowledge base enhancement. We would expect a measurable increase in the quality of the decision, as well as a corresponding reduction in decision-making time and increased confidence of novices.

## Learning and ES

There is some evidence that using ES by novices narrows the gap of expertise between themselves and experts. No study has yet cited any long-term learning effects of ES on users in terms of prolonged expertise augmentation.

Empirically, learning is measured as correct and/or timely performance following an activity that is expected to improve performance. Such activity is instruction $[9,26]$ or practice $[5,19]$ . The most common example of measuring learning following teaching is school examinations.

In experiments that measure learning as a result of practice, subjects are assigned tasks in one or more sessions, and then their performance is measured in a similar session. To see if learning occurred, either the performance of a later session is compared with that of an early session, or the performance of the test session of a group with a certain treatment is compared with that of a group with another treatment. Learning effects are measured by comparing performance before and after practice sessions.

## The experiment

## Statement of the problem

A primary goal of ES is to increase expertise in an organization, as measured by better and faster decision making. ES are built to make expertise more available to non-experts. To date, this has been recognized as a major advantage. This study explores the possibility that an individual who routinely uses an ES is affected by the system beyond the actual sessions with it, i.e., a non-expert user may “learn” from the system. This would mean that the system provides an effective means of training and dissemination of expertise. We propose the following hypotheses to test these assertions.

## Hypothesis 1:

Subjects who solve problems with the aid of an ES improve their decision quality, over time, more than do subjects who do not use an ES.

## Hypothesis 2:

Subjects who solve problems with the aid of an ES shorten their decision making time, over time, more than do subjects who do not use an ES.

## Hypothesis 3:

The confidence in the decision of subjects who use an ES to solve problems increases, over time, more than does the confidence of subjects who do not use an ES.

## Experimental design

The experiment is based on a two-factor design. One factor is ES use vs no ES use, and the other is time. The experiment consists of two groups of domain novices, tested in five sessions. Users and non-users (serving as the control group) solved domain problems. In each session, the users solved the first three problems with the aid of the ES, and the last four problems without it. The non-users group solved the same seven problems every session (35 problems in all) in the same order. The continued use of the ES by the users (over sessions) is assumed to have created an effect similar to everyday use by their professional counterparts.

To test the hypotheses, only the data collected on problems solved without the aid of the ES in the first and last sessions were analyzed to give a total measure of relative improvement in each group. Since the user group was aided in the solution of the first three problems in each session, only the last four cases could be used to compare non-ES behavior between the user and non-user groups. For each subject, change in performance (decision quality and decision making time) and confidence was measured as

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {4} \left(S _ {i + 1} - S _ {i}\right),
$$

where S = the sum of the scores of decision quality/decision time/confidence, respectively, of the last four cases of the session (i = Session (i = 1, ..., 4)). The expression is equivalent to S5 - S1, where S5 is the fifth session, and S1 is the first session. However, the above expression emphasizes the cumulative nature of the learning effect over time. Figure 1 illustrates the experimental design.

The independent variables are the factors ES-use vs no-use, and time (over sessions). We measured three dependent variables: decision quality, decision making time and confidence in the decision. As reflected in the literature, a person's

This formula allowed us to quantify the accuracy of the decision on an ascending five point scale, and to determine the remuneration for the participants.

Decision making time was measured as the total time that elapsed from the moment the subject started to read the case to the time a solution was reached, as recorded by the subject. The start time of the first case and the finish time of the last case were monitored by one of the authors.

Decision confidence was measured using a questionnaire developed by Aldag and Power [1]. It uses three 7-point Likert scale questions and has a Cronbach's alpha of 0.84, indicating adequate reliability.

## Expert system

The system used in this research was ESRISK. It was developed by Citicorp to evaluate the risk in the development and implementation of financial information systems. The system consists of 70 if-then rules covering 17 risk factors. Each question allows the user to choose an alternative from a menu. At the end of this process, the system presents an expert evaluation of the risk involved in the project at hand: very high, high, medium, low, or very low. It also presents the risk level associated with each of the five issues that have to be considered in order to determine the overall risk: preliminary (the project cost and length), technology, financial, customer impact, and project management risks. Each category required the consideration of a number of factors. The explanation facility was disabled in order to eliminate a potential source of feedback to the user group. One could argue that disabling the explanation facility dilutes the generalizability of the experiment. However, in at least one field study, it was observed that most ES users never use the explanation feature, either because they did not have the time or interest to do so, or because they did not understand the explanation when they tried to use it [25]. Thus, the trade-off between experimental control and external validity appears to be minimal.

We chose ESRISK because it deals with semi-structured problems that require expert advice, is easy to operate, takes only a few minutes to learn to use and a typical session takes only a few minutes. This allowed the subjects to solve multiple problems, one after another, and enabled us to gather multiple data points per subject per session.

Another advantage of using ESRISK is the possibility of quantifying the quality of the decision reached with it. The system reaches a solution which can easily be assigned a numerical value, according to the overall risk level.

## Subjects

Thirty-two volunteers participated in the study. All were management students who were paid for their participation. They had novice-level knowledge in the domain. None had any experience in the development or implementation of financial computerized systems.

An oft-cited disadvantage of using students is that they do not experience the daily environment of professionals. However, the purpose of the study is to investigate the learning of novices. If students (domain novices) learn from the ES, then it is reasonable to assume that novice professionals also would. There is an additional advantage of using students: their level of prior expertise is more controllable and this allows the researchers to select a relatively uniform group of novices: it was imperative to use true novices in the experiment, in order to measure learning effects.

To ensure that the participants could solve problems in the domain, we selected only management students who had taken courses in finance, accounting and information systems. Before accepting the volunteer, we conducted a brief test to ensure understanding of the terms used in the cases that the participants had to consider. ## Material

The material for the experiment was 35 short cases written by the authors. They were read and corrected by an expert at Citicorp. The expert agreed that the cases described realistic and diversified situations. He also believed that the cases were similar in difficulty.

The correct solutions were pre-assessed by the expert. He solved the cases twice: without the aid of the ES and with its aid. Thirty-four of the expert's solutions were identical to those of the ES. There was a discrepancy of only one risk level in only one of the cases. The correct solution in that case was defined as the solution that the expert reached with the aid of the ES.

## Pre-tests

Two volunteers were asked to participate in a preliminary pre-test. As a result of these sessions, minor changes were made to the introduction. Twelve novices participated in the full-scale pre-test in a PC laboratory. Each case was solved manually by at least three different participants. When all of the participants finished solving, we asked them to solve five other cases with the aid of the ES. We also asked them to make comments on the introduction and the cases. In addition to testing the methodology and the cases, the pretest results were also used to determine a difficulty ranking for each case. The difficulty ranking was used to ensure similar difficulty level across sessions.

## The task

The participants met in the university PC lab. They were told that the purpose of the experiment was to test their ability to determine the overall risk of development and implementation of financial information systems. After the instructions were given, we indicated that they would be paid according to the closeness of their indicated risk level to the correct one: \$6 if they indicated the correct level, \$5 if their level deviated one level from the correct one, \$4 if it deviated two levels, etc. This payment acted as an incentive for the participant to make a sincere effort when solving the problems. To create a realistic situation where one cannot use an unlimited amount of time to make a decision, we told the participants that their compensation might be affected by the time taken to make the decision.

The participants were instructed to indicate their confidence in the decision on the questionnaire that was attached to each case. To encourage honest answers, we assured the participants that their confidence in the decision would not affect their compensation.

Participants were randomly divided into two groups of 16. The users group was told that they would have to solve the first three cases each session with the aid of an ES. We explained what an ES was and how ESRISK worked. The participants then practiced with the system. When a user finished the first three cases using ESRISK, the next four cases were given. The participant solved those cases without the aid of the ES. Meanwhile, in another room, the non-users were given comparable instructions, after which they received all seven cases and solved them.

This procedure, without the introduction and explanation, was repeated in four additional meetings. The two groups solved the same set of seven cases for a total of 35 different cases, and 20 of these formed the basis of our analysis. At the end of the fifth meeting, the users filled out a concluding questionnaire to elicit information on the users' feelings and beliefs about the ES.

## Measurement of dependent variables

Decision quality was measured as the absolute difference between the correct risk level and the one indicated by the subject, for each case, on a scale of 1 to 5. Obviously, some of the correct solutions should be 3 on a scale of 1–5. One might think that, to minimize their risk, the subjects would tend to prefer this level over the extremes. However, since the overall risk level is determined by the aggregate of seventeen different factors, it might be easier to determine an extreme risk level ('1' or '5') than to decide on the median ('3'), because it may be the result of many contradictory factors. Indeed, there was no significant difference between correct subject solutions to cases with risk level '3' and correct subject solutions to cases with other risk levels. A t-test of the differences among subjects' scores was computed and was not significant (p = 0.4555). Thus, participants did not prefer the '3' solution over the others.

Decision-making time was measured as the difference between the start and finish as recorded by the subject. Because the cases were solved sequentially, the sum of these times is equal to the length of the session for that user, as monitored by the experiment leader.

Confidence in the decision was measured as the aggregate score of the confidence questionnaire for each case. A Cronbach alpha value of 0.86 was computed for this measure.

## Data analysis

To test the hypotheses, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures was applied. The factor (independent variable) is ES use vs no-use. The repeated measures are decision quality, decision-making time, and confidence in the decision for each of the hypotheses. The measures refer to the first and last sessions only. The following expression describes the measures considered in the ANOVA for each of the three variables:

1st Measure: C4 + C5 + C6 + C7
2nd Measure: C32 + C33 + C34 + C35, where $C4$ is the subject's score for the 4th case, $C5$ is the subject's score for the 5th case, etc.

## Findings and interpretation

## Validation of the ES

It is first important to show that the system helps users to make decisions. To this end, the subjects' solutions to the first three cases of each session were analyzed. The purpose of the analysis was to see if the solutions reached with the system were significantly better than the solutions reached without it. A t-test comparing the quality of the solutions of the two groups was significant at the 0.0001 level. This is the only use to which data on these cases was put. All other analyses involved only data collected in the last four cases.

Relationships between demographics and dependent variables

Participants were assigned to the users' group and non-users' group randomly. For each group, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the demographic variables (age, gender, graduate/undergraduate status, number of years of experience with computers, and number of hours per week of computer work over the past year) and the difference of each dependent variable between the 5th and 1st session. There was no significant correlation between any of the demographics and any of the session differences at the 5% significance level. Hence, we conclude that none of the demographics considered had any effect on learning, either with or without the ES.

## Decision-making time

Hypothesis 2, concerning the shortening of decision-making time through the use of the ES was not supported. As can be seen from Table 4, the participants, as a whole, spent less time on their decisions in the last session than they did in the first session, but there was no significant difference between users and non-users, with respect to decision-making time.

As a group, it took the users more time to make their decisions than the non-users' group. This is true for all cases except one. Even though both groups were solving the cases manually, the users appeared to retain the structured approach to addressing the problems. The reader should note that the users' graph is not strictly monotonic; this is an aberration, and is not statistically significant.

## Discussion

Past studies of performance improvements of ES users have shown that users consistently outperformed non-users in the quality of their problem solving as well as in the time they take to solve problems. Our results show that the users acquire additional problem solving knowledge that can be ascribed directly to ES exposure. Over time, the users continued to achieve higher quality decisions than non-users, increasing their advantage in subsequent sessions.

Repeated work with the ES helped users familiarize themselves with the factors they had to consider: it served almost as a check list. In the concluding questionnaire, a few subjects wrote that the system was a good teacher. With the exception of one subject, all of the users felt their experience with the system had taught them how to solve problems better in the domain.

Further support for the presence of heuristic generation in the users lies in the way the subjects chose to make notations on the case sheets. Many more subjects did so in the users group. They underlined words and numbers in the case, and either drew arrows up and down, or wrote the words “low” and “high”, for low and high risk. This could help them sum up the “high’s” and “low’s”, and reach an overall risk level for the case.

A second finding shows that subjects with previous ES experience do not solve problems more quickly than their non-users counterparts when both are solving problems without the benefit of an ES. Although both groups continued to speed up their problem solving activities over time, the non-user group consistently solved problems at a faster rate. Over sessions, the users improved their solution speed, as did the non-users, but not to a greater extent. In five sessions, they could not close the time gap. Since the explanation facility was disabled, we know that the time measurement comprised actual decision making activity.

Although neither group had any kind of an explanation facility, there was still a difference noted in the amount of feedback each group received. The bow-shaped curve of the non-users in Figure 2 may be explained by the fact that the non-users did not have any feedback, whereas the users had feedback from the ES in the form of a suggested overall risk and the breakdown of that risk at the end of solving each case with the ES.

All subjects' confidence in their decision making improved over time. Users' confidence improved slightly more than non-users, although the differences were not significant.

## Limitations

Like any piece of behavioral research, this study is not free from limitations. An ES represents expertise of a person, or a group of people. It is therefore, subjective. What might be considered a correct decision by the experts of Citicorp, may be considered an incorrect decision by the experts of another financial institution.

As in other studies that involve the use of a single system, one must question whether this system is representative of the type of systems under study. It is not unreasonable to assume that the use of a different ES would produce different results. As mentioned before, the complexity and structure of the problem may also affect the improvement in decision-making.

A serious question arises about the domain. How similar is the domain under investigation to other domains? Obviously, decision-making in oil-exploration or internal medicine are not very similar to risk-assessment of development and implementation of financial information systems. Learning from an ES may be closely related to the particular domain in which the ES provides advice.

## Conclusion

ES are developed to provide non-experts with expert advice. However, they may contribute more than just expertise on demand. They may play an important role in the broadening of the user's knowledge. As such they may be considered training tools, whether or not they are intended for use in a training session.

In terms of decision making effectiveness and efficiency, this study has a clear message. ES can augment effective decision-making skills. Subjects who used the ES improved the quality of their decisions more than did those who did not use the ES. However, the ES they used did not enhance the efficiency of decision-making. Decision-making time of both groups, users and non-users of the ES, decreased over sessions, but, throughout the experiment, the users took longer to reach their decisions.

What we can say with a certain amount of confidence is that the ES users improved their decision-making skills more than the non-users. Organizations allocate considerable resources to the training of their employees. Better decision-making skills are highly beneficial to organizations. If ES can serve as on-the-job trainers and enhance people's decision-making effectiveness, much of the time human experts spend in basic consulting could be saved. Instead of assigning human experts to train novices, we can build ES to fulfill this task. The human experts can then devote more time to more complex decision-making. The organization saves even more resources, because this training is accomplished coincident to the productive work of the novice.

The study also corroborates previous IS research. The dependent variables tested in the experiment, decision quality, decision-making time and decision confidence, are the same [3] or similar to those measured in other IS studies [7]. Whereas those studies dealt with DSS, this study involves an information system that is knowledge-based.
