# The evolution of empirical research in IS A study in IS maturity

Myun J. Cheon and Varun Grover
University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC, USA

Rajiv Sabherwal

Florida International University, Miami, FL, USA

The Information Systems (IS) field has been subject to much introspection by IS researchers over the past decade. This study attempts to integrate this research systematically in an effort to assess the changing maturity of the IS field over the past 10 years. Data are collected on past empirical work and evaluated with respect to preestablished criteria. The results indicate that while there is little indication of change in maturity, there are definite directions toward which the IS field should evolve.

Keywords: IS research; Research topics; Research methods; IS maturity

## The maturity of the IS field

A field is a common ground on which members define the area and its important measures. Maturing of a field (for instance, IS) takes time, since fields do not usually evolve according to any pre-defined plans. This makes it difficult to evaluate the extent to which a field has matured. First, need to establish certain criteria for maturity, and then study the field's evolution with respect to these criteria.

Banville and Landry [5] have suggested that the maturity of a field can be evaluated on the basis of what its members say about it, its research methods, and its topics and variables. while some prior studies have focused on the IS field's members, this study focuses on the research topics $^{2}$ and research methods as criteria of evaluation. Arguments establishing these criteria are based on experience in other disciplines. Moreover, in a recent evaluation of the research on management of information systems Dickson and DeSanctis [17] examined the progress in terms of research topics, while pointing out that research methods also need to be considered for a more comprehensive assessment. Farhoomand [20] also examined research topics and methods in his study of the evolution of IS research.

In general, for the maturity of the field as a whole, a greater diversity of research topics and methods is needed. As discussed by Orlikowski and Baroudi [47]:

"One of the most pronounced features of contemporary social research – and by this we mean those disciplines concerned with human phenomena (individual and collective) such as anthropology, psychology, sociology, and their applied fields of administrative science, education, individual psychology and industrial sociology – is the great range of research perspectives that operate concurrently."

They go on to add that there is lack of diversity in information systems research. Similarly, Daft and Lewin [14] in their essay on organization studies, emphasize the diversity necessary in fields that deal with organizational contexts:

"If we accept that organizations are variable-rich, multidimensional, and perhaps chaotic, then we believe it appropriate for the field of organization theory to have settled on a limited set of conceptual boxes. ... One challenge ... is to encourage diverse perspectives and methods that pursue multiple organizational realities."

Therefore, relatively mature fields of research, such as administrative science and industrial psychology are characterized by a variety of topics and methods. Culnan [12] concurs with this but argues that the emergence of clear intellectual subfields within IS does represent progress. She suggests that “while MIS is still preparadigmatic, it has made progress, if one accepts the argument that MIS, like all social sciences, is a multiple paradigm discipline.”

The emergence of distinct research paradigms is considered one indication of a mature science [35]. The maturation of the IS field and the development of a firmer research paradigm may also influence the research topics. The examination of the existence of research paradigms can be done by analyzing the type of variables in a field [49]. For example, Brown and Gilmartin [10] compared articles published in the American Journal of Sociology in the periods 1940–1941 and 1965–1966 in order to find the existence of their research paradigms. They divided the research variables into three categories that roughly parallel the types of sociological paradigms.

Thus, it is suggested that the existence of a diverse set of variables is one indication of the maturity in the IS field. Such variety within a field indicates that research is not concentrating only on one or two topics. Moreover, it implies that the field is not being unduly influenced by transient “fads”, wherein researchers concentrate on one or two topics that might be considered important at that time.

Moreover, Benbasat [8], Evered and Louis [19], and Jenkins [31] have recommended that the choice of a research method should be based on the topic. Therefore, a discipline that has matured enough to be examining a diverse set of topics should also exhibit a variety of research methods $^{3}$ . Hamilton and Ives [23] have argued that it is necessary to establish a balance among the research methods in the IS field. In other words, balanced utilization of several different research methods, rather than excessive reliance on one or two, is an indication of the maturity of the IS field.

Furthermore, Nachimias and Nachimias [44] pointed out that the research process is the paradigm of scientific inquiry, in which ideal empirical research is inferential rather than descriptive or exploratory. Thus, the existence of less descriptive research methods and more inferential research methods may be considered as another indication of the maturity of the IS field.

Therefore, the criteria to evaluate the maturity of a field may be summarized as:

(1) A mature field integrates diverse sets of variables in an attempt to solidify a paradigm or a set of paradigms. Such diversity enables the field to gain a perspective on its relationships within and across its different domains. In contrast, less mature fields would have lesser diversity of topics, with fewer established relationships and consequently weaker paradigms.

(2) A mature field would use multiple methodologies, rather than be dominated by any one. In contrast, less mature fields would tend to have a limited repertoire of methodologies.

(3) A mature field would focus on explaining phenomena through hypothesis testing. The methodologies would be more generalizable and inferential. On the other hand, less mature fields would use more exploratory methodologies with an emphasis on describing and structuring phenomenon.

These criteria form the basis for evaluation of the IS field.

## Research topics

Some previous assessments of IS research [e.g., 22] have used topic categories which were neither mutually exclusive nor cumulatively exhaustive. This approach can be improved upon by using a well accepted framework to classify IS research topics [25]; research frameworks in any discipline are intended to be complete and encompassing, so as to provide a taxonomy of its topics.

Many frameworks have been developed for IS research $[21,30,37,39,42,46,50]$ . Ives et al.'s $[27]$ framework is adapted for this study, since it is the most complete $[6]$ and has been validated both by mapping 331 IS doctoral dissertations written between 1973 and 1979 into its research categories and by classifying 532 IS articles published from 1970 to 1979 into the research categories. In addition, this framework is the most widely endorsed for IS empirical research $[33,36,52]$ .

The framework is based upon the following definition of IS research:

“the systematic investigation of the development, operation, use and/or impact of an information (sub)system in an organizational environment.”

The definition identifies three basic sets of IS variables as information system environments, information system processes, and the information subsystem itself (see Figure 1).

Based upon this definition, the Ives et al. framework of IS research, with each variable in each group (see Figure 2) is as follows:

(1a) external environment considers legal, social, political, cultural, economic, educational, resource and industry/trade variables;

(1b) organizational environment consists of the organizational goals, tasks, structure, volatility, and management style and philosophy;

(1c) user environment consists of primary users who make a decision and intermediaries who filter or interpret the IS outputs for decision makers and their characteristics;

(1d) IS development environment includes the development methods and techniques, design personnel and their characteristics, and the organization and management of IS development and maintenance;

![](/api/attachments/VQEPYEBG/fulltext/images/685e41b972980a5b1572ba65ad2edd7f97b066fbebbd13d905b13e46d0c5ed20.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Five types of IS research.

(1e) IS operation environment consists of the resources necessary for IS operations, such as software, hardware, database, procedures and documentation, organization and management of IS operations, and the operations personnel;

(2a) IS content consists of dimensions of both the data and the decision models available through use of the IS;

(2b) presentation form includes the methods by which the information is presented to a user. The media selection, presentation format, and special features are considered;

(2c) presentation time considers when the information is presented to a user. Online vs. offline, reporting interval, and processing delay are considered.

(3a) development process consists of the selection and application of organizational resources within environmental constraints;

(3b) operation process considers the physical operation of the IS and focuses on a function of the operations resource, with interfaces at the boundaries of other environments in the form of personal interaction;

(3c) use process focuses on usage of the IS by the primary user.

The variable groups were used to derive five categories of IS research topics:

Type I research (RT1) involves variables within a single variable group – environment, process, or information subsystems (ISS).

Type II research (RT2) examines the relationship between one or more variables from the process group and one or more from the environment group.

Type III research (RT3) focuses on the relationship between the characteristics of the ISS and process variable(s).

Type IV research (RT4) examines the relationship between environmental resources/constraints and characteristics of the ISS.

Type V research (RT5) investigates the relationship between one or more variables from each of the three groups.

## Research methods

Empirical research captures the essence of research relying on observation. The empirical research process can be viewed as the overall process of scientific activities, in which researchers engage to produce useful knowledge. Based on empirical IS research methods classified by Van Horn [53] and the ecology of IS research proposed by Alavi, Carlson, and Brooke [1], we use five empirical research methods to classify IS articles.

These research methods are the same as those used by Farhoomand, except that he had classified field studies into either surveys or case studies.

Research method I (RM1) is a case study examining a single organization or part of an organization with no experimental design or controls.

Research method II (RM2) is a survey which is an exploratory field study in which there is no test relationship between variables.

Research method III (RM3) is a field study examining several or more organizations with experimental design but without experimental control.

Research method IV (RM4) is a field test studying one or more organizations with a specific experimental design and controls.

Research method V (RM5) is a laboratory experiment studying computer-organizational problems with an experimental design and high degree of control. There are four approaches: simulation, small group experiments, person-machine experiments, and prototype experiments.

Based on our criteria for maturity and these taxonomies, we propose the following three research propositions. In order to observe changes in trends, the decade was divided into two distinct periods. The propositions compare the first half of the decade (1980–84) with the second half (1985–89) in terms of research types and methods.

Proposition 1: Research types that examine (integrate) diverse sets of variables (types 2, 3, 4 and 5) will show increasing trends, while research types that examine a single set of variables (type 1) will show decreasing trends between the two periods.

Proposition 2: There would be a convergence of research methods, i.e., research methods that were higher in the first period would show a declining trend in the second period and visa versa. A direction of convergence (toward a balanced use of methods) is expected.

Proposition 3: Research methods that explain phenomena (research methods 3, 4, and 5) will show increasing trends in the second period while methods that describe phenomena (research methods 1 and 2) will show a corresponding declining trend.

Testing the three propositions based on the criteria gives an indication of the evolution of IS. Furthermore, by focussing on trends over time rather than at one time, the propositions present a dynamic view of the field. However, while the propositions deal with the 1980–89 period, the availability of data from the previous decade (1970–1979) allows us to compare proportion of research types and methods across the decades. However, due to the different method and sample, no statistical testing across the two decades will be attempted.

## Methodology

## Research sample

To obtain the empirical IS research for testing the propositions, six journals $^{4}$ were selected: Information and Management (I&M), Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Journal of MIS (JMIS), Communications of the ACM (CACM), Management Science (MS), and Decision Sciences (DS). Table 1 shows the characteristics of these six journals. This choice of journals is based on previous published research [23,24,25,28,54]. The selected journals are common among previous studies with the exception of Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS). JMIS is a relatively new journal but it has gained recognition and stature as a major journal in the IS field and has been included in some earlier such studies [28,55]. It ranked second among IS journals in a recent study [48].

The article selection procedure identified 500 empirical IS articles published over the period 1980 to 1989 in the selected six journals with the single exception of JMIS, which has been published only since 1984. The abstracts of all the 500 papers were thoroughly reviewed for selection as empirical research. A coding sheet (see Appendix A) was used to record data extracted from the articles to ensure uniformity, consistency, and completeness of data. Moreover, subjectivity was minimized by examining the sections of research questions and research hypotheses in each paper, and then using a simple coding questionnaire (Appendix B) to classify each paper into type I through V of research. Finally, the research methodology or equivalent sections were read thoroughly to classify research methods. Of the 500 empirical IS articles, 463 were used for statistical analysis, since the remaining 37, although empirical, were not clearly classified by the taxonomy (for instance, studies dealing with IS research, curriculum, etc.).

## Data analysis

Piecewise linear regression was used to investigate any differences in the trends in research types (proposition 1) and in use of empirical methods (propositions 2 and 3) over two periods (1980 to 1984 vs. 1985 to 1989). The unit of analysis is the proportion of IS articles published using a method or focussing on a type within a given year. The use of proportions standardizes on the change in article numbers over the years. Piecewise linear regression is a statistical technique that checks for different linear relations in some range of an independent variable (time) [45]. The form of the piecewise linear model is:

$$
Y _ {i j} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} X _ {i 1} + \beta_ {2} (X _ {i 1} - 5) X _ {i 2} + \epsilon_ {i},
$$

where $Y_{ij}$ is the annual proportion of empirical IS articles in the ith year with respect to the jth research method (or research type). $X_{i1}$ is the ith year, ranging from 1 (1980) to 10 (1989). $X_{i2}$ is 1 if $X_{i1} > 5$ and 0 otherwise. Since we are testing for change over two periods (1980 to 1984 vs. 1985 to 1989), we use a dummy variable, $X_{i2}$ , to check any significant change in the slope over the two periods. $\epsilon_{i}$ is the error terms. $\beta_{1}$ is the slope over the period 1980 and 1984. $\beta_{2}$ is the change in slope over the period 1985 and 1989. If there is a statistically significant difference in slopes between the two periods, we expect that $\beta_{2}$ is not 0. Therefore, by running the regression in “pieces” for two distinct periods, we can conveniently test for significant differences in trends.

## Testing the research propositions

Table 5 shows the results of piecewise regression on the proportion of articles on each research type over the two 5 year periods. The “change” column refers to the sign of $\beta_{2}$ which reflects the change of the second period over the first. The p-value indicates the significance of the change.

Proposition 1 states that if IS is indeed maturing as a field, research on multiple variables types should increase. The results do not show such an increase. In fact, among the multiple group research types (i.e., types 2–5) only type 5 shows a significantly increasing trend at the 0.10 level of significance. Also, research on a single variable group (type 1) seems to be increasing (0.10 level). All others show no significant change in trend.

Table 6 shows changes of research methods between the two periods 1980 to 1984 and 1985 to 1989. Proposition 2 predicted a maturing trend toward balance of research methods. The results do not indicate this. In fact, similar to the analysis between the two decades, we find a significant increase in the use of surveys. No other research method exhibits a significant change in trend. Thus, there is also no support for proposition 3, i.e., no increase in explanatory vs. exploratory methodologies.

## Recommendations for future is research

None of our three research propositions were supported. The progress has thus not satisfied any of our three criteria. Similar disturbing results have been reported by Farhoomand and Grover and Sabherwal, using a different criterion to assess progress: the correlation between the extent of research on an area and the importance of the area as perceived by IS managers. Together, these studies indicate a need for IS researchers to be very careful in deciding their future research topics.

An examination of the research topics in current articles showed that the most commonly investigated research type (I) concerns variables within a single group: environment, process, and the information system characteristics. IS research has thus not made much progress in diversity of topics. This result parallels the findings by Dickson and DeSanctis that empirical research on management of information systems (MOIS) has involved heavy activity in a few areas with virtually no work being done in others. According to them, most of the MOIS research is still not empirically-based and “the trend has been to evolve existing themes rather than to introduce radically different topics or methods into the MOIS area."

Future IS researchers need to place greater emphasis on research on the relationship between IS related processes and (a) the environment (type II), and (b) the information system characteristics (type III) and the relationship between the environment and the information system characteristics (type IV). An analysis of the research methods employed by the articles showed that the most commonly used empirical method is the field study and that the least used is field tests. In terms of trend between two periods (1980 to 1984 vs. 1985 to 1989) we are increasingly relying on exploratory field studies (surveys). Apparently, IS research has not made much progress since 1980 when Ives et al. and Keen criticized IS research for being largely based on narrative cases and atheoretical surveys and Vogel and Wetherbe found that IS research did not typically include field tests.

Exploratory field studies certainly have their value, especially for basic understanding of a phenomenon during the early stages of its investigation. However, they are less firmly rooted in existing theories than hypotheses-testing methods, and consequently make limited contribution to the building of a cumulative research tradition. Therefore, increasing use of exploratory surveys should be a matter of concern for the IS discipline. Initial exploratory research should be followed by theory-testing. In order words, greater emphasis should be placed on testing theories and constructing empirically based theories. In this context, Armstrong's [3] suggestion for avoiding excessive exploratory research is relevant. He recommended that we should specify: (1) a small number of causal variables, (2) the direction of each of the relationships, (3) the pattern of causality (4) the functional form, and (5) the magnitude of each relationship. This facilitates theory building and testing.

It is interesting to note, that as active IS researchers, our a priori belief was that we would observe an increase in the maturity level of the IS field. This was not the case. We acknowledge that there could be some disagreement over the criteria chosen, or the appropriateness of evaluating IS research by criteria found to hold true in other disciplines. However, according to our derived set of criteria, IS has not matured. Our recommendations for future research are in accordance with the concern, increasingly expressed by IS researchers, for more sophisticated attempts at theory-testing. For example, Jarvenpaa, Dickson, and DeSanctis [29] provided some recommendations to minimize the methodological problems common in experimental IS research, Straub [51] emphasized the importance of carefully validating instruments for confirmatory empirical research, and Baroudi and Orlikowski [7] provided some guidelines for use of the correct statistical methods in IS empirical research. Such suggestions, if adequately followed, should help future evolution of the IS research field.

## Limitations

This study may have been adversely affected to some extent by three limitations. First, the study focused only on a ten year period (1980–89). The study could have been improved if we had been able to examine the trends over a longer time period, say the past twenty years.

Second, our results are based on six journals that are commonly believed to be major publication outlets for IS researchers. In order to keep the research manageable, we had to exclude several other journals which present important outlets for empirical IS research, for example, Human Computer Interaction and International Journal of Man Machine Studies. Moreover, Harvard Business Review, Sloan Management Review, and Interfaces occasionally contain empirical IS research, especially case studies.

Finally, this study is limited by the fact that much research represents work done several years earlier. Moreover, due to the different backlogs and review times of the various journals, the time elapsed from research to publication differs across journals. However, this study is not alone in being affected by this limitation.

## Summary

In this paper we have assessed the current status of the IS field and the evolution of the field with respect to research methods and research types by collecting and analyzing IS empirical research. Much empirical IS research was conducted during this decade. This valuable source of knowledge has played a major role in our assessment of the evolution of the IS field in terms of research methods and research types. The results indicate very little change in maturity with respect to our criteria. On a positive note, however, the recent emphasis on methodological rigor and organizational level issues are signs that indicate maturing of the field.
