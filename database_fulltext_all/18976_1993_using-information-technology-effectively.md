# Using information technology effectively

S.K. Singh

Indian Institute of Management, Lucknow, India

Recently, information technology and computer-based information systems have emerged as key determinants of organizational effectiveness and competitiveness. However, organizations vary considerably in their ability to utilize information technologies. Having to deal with unfamiliar information and communication technologies that are advancing at an astounding pace, practitioners and academicians have been groping for appropriate approaches and strategies to help organizations harness the enormous potential inherent in this explosive growth. The paper reviews the major current models and proposes a comprehensive framework – the Comprehensive Organizational Preparedness Environment (COPE) which overcomes the limitations of prior models.

Keywords: IS planning models; Using IT effectively; Organizational preparedness models; Stage models; IS Strategic planning; IS Project management

## Introduction

Information Technology (IT) and computer-Based Information Systems (CBIS) are now major concerns in the organization. The need to use them to improve effectiveness and competitiveness is evidenced by the steady flow of literature on the subject (McFarlen, 1984; Parsons, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter and Miller, 1985; Benjamin et al., 1985; Gypeth, 1986; Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Miron et al., 1988; King et al., 1989). One of the earliest and basic rationale for the use of IT was provided by Leavitt and Whisler (1958) when they stated that “the most compelling reason” for using IT is “the pressure on management to cope with increasingly complicated organizational problems.” Over the years, with growing complexity of organizational contexts and increasing sophistication of IT, the rationale has not only remained valid but has become even more compelling. Enormous potential of IT based Information Systems (IS) notwithstanding, actual experiences of organizations with use of these systems have been less than satisfactory. The issue has been addressed by a number of authors (Lucas, 1975, 1982; Bostram and Heinen, 1977 a, b; Turner, 1982). Lyytinen (1987a) summarizes these concerns as follows:

"The IS community faces a paradox: despite impressive advances in technology, problems are more abundant than solutions; organizations experience rising costs instead of cost reductions, IS misuse and rejection are more frequent than acceptance and use".

To the extent that the survival and growth of both the organization and key individuals in it depends on results and results are quantifiable and measureable, the motivation for experimenting with new approaches and technologies will be strong. Ability creation has structural, procedural, and behavioural dimensions. Here, an attempt has been made to review the major efforts of helping organizations obtain benefits by exploitation of IT and present a comprehensive framework for organizational preparedness that overcomes limitations in the existing approaches.

## Growth stage models

These are based on the premise that the process of organization's adaptation and use of IT/IS is an evolutionary one involving organizational learning; it should, therefore, proceed through identifiable stages. If these stages and their associated characteristics can be identified, it can be used both for developing a blueprint for effective use of IT/IS by the organization and providing action guidelines for an orderly progression through various stages from the current one taking the organizational culture into account. ## Planning models

Planning models are directed towards producing an action blueprint to help an organization harness IT for enhancing its information/knowledge management capability. The models normally address issues at two levels: strategic, and operational. The scope of the model includes activity as well as resource planning. Boynton and Zmud (1987), based on analysis of major planning models, present a planning agenda of nine items and description of eleven distinct planning behaviours. According to them, IT planning must be viewed as a continual series of incremental efforts to first surface and then resolve or exploit business problems and opportunities. They conclude that, in future, projects aimed at better understanding of how to carry out an effective planning process are likely to be more significant than projects focussed on identifying additional issues to be addressed in the planning process.

Operational level planning models tend to be much closer to IS project management. Strategic level IS planning models vary considerably in their focus, scope, and use. Table 2 summarizes the characteristic features of well-known IS/IT strategic planning models.

Aharonian (1979) has proposed an Accomplishment Value Procedure (AVP) as a tool for project management in general and IS projects in particular. The quantification of the value of each task is central to AVP. As each task is completed, the project is credited with its value. There is no credit for a partially complete task. ## Limitations and Trends

Introduction or upgrading of a CBIS in an organization is an undertaking of significant dimensions affecting several persons, both within and outside the IS function, requiring commitment of sizeable organizational resources over prolonged periods of time. IS planning models are tools to help organizations manage such an undertaking effectively. Review of current literature dealing with management of IS efforts in organizations highlights several characteristic features. First, and perhaps most important, is the highly fragmented nature of and narrowly-focused approach to the IS planning and management effort. While the limited domain of application of growth stage, strategic planning, and project planning models is self-evident, prevalent composite planning models tend to give cursory treatment to implementation aspects. The second important feature is lack of generality.

While context-independence is assumed by most models, many have grown out of the experiences of a specific organization and invariably substantial changes have to be made before they can be adopted by other organizations. Third, lack of pragmatic orientation is evident in most models. Organizations have to make IS-related decisions in the 'here-and-now' situation characterized by their history, the present context, and the rapidly changing internal as well as external environments. Most models by themselves are of little help in deciding how to get started, how to adjust the plans to incorporate the latest technological advances, what organizational changes to make, when to abandon a system, and a host of other such questions. Emphasis seems to be on the use of techniques rather than on the solution of managerial problems related to the exploitation of IS/IT for enhancing organizational performance.

However, several helpful trends are also appearing. The scope of planning models is being continuously broadened, as evidenced by an increasing number of comprehensive IS planning models which incorporate strategic, tactical, and operational levels. Implementation is also being made an integral part of most comprehensive models. Most models also make provision for an external feedback loop (technology, competition, manpower) to help review and keep the plans current. A second helpful development is increasing automation of the IS planning and development function, which should help reduce the replanning effort and render feasible planning on a continuous basis. Third and perhaps the most significant development is the increasing involvement of top management and user managers in planning and management of IS functions, thereby helping them align themselves more closely with the organizational purpose and needs.

The helpful trends notwithstanding, there are still limitations in IS planning models for preparing organizations to derive benefits from IS/IT on a continuous basis. There is also the need for a more comprehensive and pragmatic approach to planning of the IS/IT function.

## One Organization's Experience

A large multi-division, multi-location company, which has averaged a healthy 20 percent annual growth in turnover, has been using computers for streamlining its data and information processing activities since 1971. Like most other organizations in India, computers were initially viewed as a replacement for accounting machines.

In the initial phase (1971–75), the Electronic Data Proceeding (EDP) Dept. was organized as a centralized Head Office function; applications were pre-dominantly accounting oriented, and there was little or no planning or cost justification for the computerization effort. The company had its own EDP staff but hired computer time. In the next phase (1976–80), which can be considered as a period of high growth of data processing activities in the company, the emphasis was on designing computer-based information systems for supporting mainline activities, like production and marketing. The company continued to use hired computer time but the EDP Dept. was re-organized into the corporate Management Services Division (MSD) and treated as a profit centre. As the applications profolio grew, so did the backlog. The dominant planning ethos was project planning using Gantt Chart and network techniques. Major problems were: user dissatisfaction due to excessive time overruns and lack of integration between the different applications.

The third phase (1981–85) was characterised by strong emphasis on integration of information systems at the company rather than the functional level, and decentralization of MSD functions at divisional and unit levels with the corporate Information Systems Division (ISD) retaining the responsibility for corporate computerisation policy and hardware/software acquisition. The company acquired six mainframe computer systems for the head office and the major units. The scope and level of planning was raised to the tactical level, and project level planning was sharpened and refined. However, the dissatisfaction at the senior managerial level continued on two counts: lack of flexibility and delay in responding to user requests.

The current phase (since 1986) is characterised by the introduction of personal-computers on a large scale, significant decentralization, and a fair amount of autonomy to the divisions and units regarding planning and implementation of information systems. Beginning in 1990, network-based distributed data processing systems were implemented. There is a significantly higher level of computer literacy among user managers and there is also increased user involvement in applications development/modification. The level and scope of IS planning has been raised to the strategic level with a formal statement of Corporate IS mission and strategy. The strategic plan of each division is required to include a long-term IS plan as one of its components. This is supported by tactical and operational level plans.

Prima facie, everything appears to be satisfactory; but the problems persist. In particular, time and cost overruns have not only continued but tend to increase. The strategic, tactical, and operational plans dovetail with each other at the beginning of the planning cycle but things go haywire as the implementation proceeds. Changes in plans at one level are not necessarily incorporated at other levels, leading to considerable confusion. Different divisions are progressing at different speeds with their computerization effort and, therefore, are at different stages of computerization. But stage characteristics are not being taken into account, because strategic plans, once prepared, are set in stone. Thus, while individual elements of an IS plan are present, there is definite lack of continuity and consistency, leading to a loss of credibility of CBIS and inability of the organization to derive greater benefits from use of IS/IT.

With the vantage of hindsight it can be seen that, at the beginning of the computerization process, the company did not possess the requisite IS/IT planning expertise or an adequate understanding of the benefits to be derived from it. Both were acquired incrementally over a period of two decades through a process of trial and error. In absence of a composite and enduring IS planning framework at corporate level, IS managers in divisions were not able to convince divisional heads to treat IS plan as an integral part of the division's strategic plans based on its potential for contributing to the profits of the division. The inclusion of an IS/IT component was primarily because it was a corporate requirement rather than the realization of its potential contribution. The exercise is mechanistic rather than organic in nature. The company would have started deriving benefits from use of IS/IT much earlier if it had access to a comprehensive IS/IT planning methodology and had made a conscious decision to use it from the very beginning of the computerization process.

## Proposed framework

Preparing the organization for making good use of IS/IT is both a complex and continuous process. It is complex because it is influenced by a host of socio-technical parameters whose behaviour and interactions are not well-understood. It has to be a continuous process to be effective, because of continual changes in both internal environment, which affect the information requirements, and the external environment, which influences technological, competitive, and manpower availability parameters. Complexity can be handled by identifying homogeneous groups of activities relevant to the different phases of the process and determining their inter-relationships, resource requirements, etc. Further, the process has to be a continuous one, with a degree of iteration. What is required, therefore, is a comprehensive approach, involving use of multiple models suitable for different stages of the process with built-in feedback and iterative capability.

The planning horizon is 5–10 years and re-formulation of the strategic plans is needed due to significant change in the current IS/IT environment (shift of origin) and experience in implementing the strategic plans.

Tactical Planning: Within the overall framework of the strategic plans, tactical planning aims at developing a portfolio of medium-term plans to facilitate resource acquisition, allocation, and control. The planning horizon is 2–4 years. Reformulation of tactical plans is necessitated due to changes in strategic plans and feedback in implementation of current tactical plans.

Project Planning: Project planning consists pf development of detailed schedules and budgets at the activity level to serve as a blueprint for action. The planning horizon is 1 month to 1 year. Reformulation of operational plans is needed because of changes in tactical plans and feedback from implementation of current operational plans.

Implementation and Control: Implementation is the most crucial and perhaps also the most neglected aspect of the IS effort in organizations. The implementation phase involves execution of operational plans on a day-to-day basis, followed by periodic progress review and control. The relevant time horizon is of the order of a week or so. Changes in this phase will follow both changes in operational plans and feedback from the evaluation phase.

Evaluation: Evaluation involves review of plans at different levels as well as their execution on a continuous basis to assess the extent of fulfilment of objectives for individual phases and the IS function as a whole. Accordingly, inputs to the evaluation process are the objectives of each phase, as well as the total IS function, plans and actual performance for each phase, and criteria/mechanisms for converting performance into the extent of objective(s) achievement. It should provide feedback input to all other phases. Frequency of evaluation effort would be dictated by the cost/benefit criteria of the evaluation effort itself.

## Concluding Remarks

There has been a significant gap between the potential for organizational effectiveness enhancement created by emerging the IT-based information/communication systems and its actual use by the organization. Even within the realm of actual usage, organizations vary widely in their IS/IT utilization capability. Planning for IS/IT has received scant attention in most organizations whereas information management, being the second order function (c.f. first order functions like design, manufacturing, distribution etc.) requires much more sophisticated planning. IS/IT planning models have been narrow in scope, slow in evolving, and still slower in implementation. Organizations that create a capability for adapting their information systems to the requirements of continuously changing organizational and environmental contexts and use the most appropriate information management tools and techniques are likely to secure significant strategic and tactical advantage over those that do not. The paper has presented a comprehensive model (COPE) to help organizations gear themselves for deriving maximum benefits from emerging information and communication technologies and systems.
