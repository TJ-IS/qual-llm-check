# The place and value of documentation in end-user computing

Gholamreza Torkzadeh and William J. Doll

The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH, USA

A central problem in the management of end-user computing is providing quality support in a cost effective manner for a variety of application needs and different levels of expertise and motivation. The end-user computing literature has emphasized training and consultation mechanisms whose nature and purpose are much the same in end-user computing as they are in a traditional data processing environment. In the end-user environment, documentation has assumed a new role as a “point-of-need” support mechanism that merits serious attention. This paper identifies the characteristics of early adopters and reluctant users, describes the diversity of their support needs, discusses the advantages of particular support mechanisms for meeting specific end-user needs, and examines the relationship between user documentation and end-user satisfaction using a sample of 326 end-users. Apparently, quality user documentation is an important determinant of end-user satisfaction, especially satisfaction with the information content of decision support applications.

Keywords: End-user support; User documentation; Documentation; Training; Consulting; Assistance; End-user satisfaction; End-user computing

## 1. Introduction

End-user computing (EUC) refers to direct interaction with application software by managerial, professional, and operating level personnel in user departments. End-users are a diverse group. Some of them may develop their own applications using data base or decision support software. However, they are not professional software developers. For the most part, they are nondata processing trained users who use code written by others in the course of their work (Rockart and Flannery, 1983).

EUC technology can allow individuals to learn new skills and develop new capabilities i.e., to participate in the design of their work as well as its execution (Alavi, 1985). EUC facilitates the human growth and development necessary for its creative and effective use. Zuboff (1982) describes a new world of “computer-mediated work”. She maintains that information technology can radically alter the way employees approach their tasks and offer managers a chance to develop new approaches to work organization. In this context, EUC requires substantial and sustained learning. Cougar (1988) cites strategies for transferring certain information system tasks to the user as the second most important human resource issue for the 1990s.

Most users need a considerable amount of information and help in order to become proficient in the use of end-user technologies. There are three general categories or mechanisms for user support: (a) consultation, including analyst/programmer advice and services, hot lines, consulting services through information centers, and assistance from functional support personnel or local experts; (b) training, whether provided by outside consultants or in-house systems personnel; and (c) documentation, including systems and programming documentation as well as end-user tool manuals and application oriented user documentation. The nature and purpose of these support mechanisms, especially user documentation, is changing as we move from traditional D.P. to EUC.

User documentation consists of written or visual explanations (e.g., manuals, procedures, films, tutorials, on-line help instructions, operating instructions, menus, etc.) about the application software, how it works, and how to use it. Increasingly, aspects of user documentation are presented via software interfaces. For example, on-line user help instructions are functionally equivalent to written documentation, only the medium is different. User documentation is an up-front investment that can decrease user effort in both tutorial and reference formats that reduce the cost of training and consultation and facilitate trouble shooting.

The literature on end-user support has focused on formal training (e.g. Kosinar and McAvoy) and consultation (Bergeron and Berube, 1988; Saaksjarvi, Heikkila and Saarinen, 1988; Carr, 1987; Crawford, 1986; Lee, 1986) with conflicting results. The nature of the computing environment as well as individual user needs and characteristics may moderate the efficacy of training and consultation. User documentation, which is less affected by these moderating factors, has been largely ignored.

## 2. User support in traditional vs. end-user environments

A number of psychological, technological, organizational, and social factors set boundaries that affect the influence of EUC on the cost and efficacy of support mechanisms. These factors include: the diversity of applications and software packages; the role of self-training; individual differences in reaction to computers and motivation for engaging in EUC activities; differences in EUC expertise or skill; task specific cost/benefit assessment; intermittent usage patterns; and the importance of point-of-need support. Figures 1 and 2 contrast user support mechanisms in a traditional data processing environment and an EUC environment. The intent of these figures is to illustrate that, in an EUC environment, analysts/programmers and operations staff are less directly involved in user support; users assume more responsibility for their own applications.

## 2.1 User support in a traditional environment

In the traditional environment, users interact with the computer resources (data files, programs, and computer systems) indirectly, through an analyst/programmer who is assigned to support the application, or through operations. Routine reports are requested via predefined transaction processing. For ad hoc or non-routine requests, an analyst/programmer assists the user. Users specify their information requirements (i.e., outputs) and the analysts select and implement models for data manipulation.

In the development of new applications, there is typically a separations of work planning from doing. The analyst, as a staff assistant to management, is often primarily responsible for designing how work is done using the new computer technology. The complexity of the technology and lack of user familiarity with this make it difficult to get users fully involved in creative and innovative uses of information technology. Opportunities for user learning during the systems development process are limited; they begin with training which is generally viewed as “preparation for use”.

The analyst/programmer is the user's primary link to computer resources; for the user, documentation often plays a minor role. Users seldom interact with the system directly thus, many applications are developed without full user manuals, but emphasized technical programming documentation for analyst/programmer reference. Analysts document logical and physical designs, primarily to aid in the development process (i.e., to ensure that the application is programmed as designed). Program documentation is motivated primarily by the cost of maintaining poorly documented applications.

## 2.2 Support in an end-user environment

In an end-user computing environment, users directly interact with computer resources (data base, model base, and computer systems) via an interactive software interface to enter information, manipulate data, and prepare output reports or graphics (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Martin, 1982; Sprague, 1980; McLean, 1979). Decision support and data base applications characterize the emerging EUC phenomenon. The nature and purpose of documentation changes emphasis from systems and programming documentation, for improving the development and maintenance costs to end-user tool manuals and application oriented documentation for improved user understanding and utilization. Other important support mechanisms include training and consultation.

Two aspects of the EUC environment, the diversity of applications/software packages and the dominant role of self-training, interact with user characteristics and support needs affecting the cost and efficacy of support mechanisms. Panko (1987) describes the variety of applications and software packages that characterize emerging patterns of EUC usage. He distinguishes between two offices. Type I offices include basic clerical work, such as transaction processing and records management; these rely heavily on access to centralized data bases, and typing/word processing and can be easily supported by centralized data processing. Type II offices entail non-routine policy making and professional work with much less reliance on centralized data bases; workers create much of their own information.

In Type II managerial and professional departments, no single dominant application emerges; usage is extremely varied and includes data base and decision support as well as desktop publishing, electronic mail, group decision support, and presentation preparation. Many of the desktop activities in a Type II office may be outside the scope of activities normally supported by traditional data processing. Specifically users assume increased responsibility for managing their information resources and, increasingly, find themselves on their own, without the degree of assistance they used to receive from professional analysts and programmers.

Self-training has always been the dominant source of computer related training for managerial and professional personnel (Brady, 1967; Benson, 1983). Amoroso (1986) found that “self-training” was the primary source of computer-based education for end-users. Nelson and Cheney (1987) report that most companies are unwilling and/or unable to spend the dollars necessary to meet the training needs of their end-users; employees end up teaching themselves the skills they need.

## 3. End-user characteristics and support needs

The EUC population includes many early adopters, users who are innovative and ready to try new tools and develop considerable skill in their use. However, there is a far larger population of reluctant users, individuals who lack motivation and have difficulty developing EUC. Especially among these reluctant or naive users, EUC skill levels tend to peak at relatively low levels. The early adopter and reluctant user have different psychological frames of reference in their use of computers.

Some people enjoy getting involved with computers, while others suffer from computer anxiety (Howard and Smith, 1986). Hill, Smith and Mann (1987) relate this and subsequent usage to beliefs concerning personal efficacy (i.e., the belief that it is always possible to master computer skills).

Many people may believe that they will never be able to interact successfully with computers or control them. Training, especially hands-on instruction, may help reduce computer anxiety and efficacy concerns among reluctant users.

Doll and Torkzadeh (1989) contend that value attainment (i.e., individuals believe that they will get what they want or attain their values) is the primary reason why users get involved in EUC activities. Hill et. al. (1987) also suggest that beliefs about job-related benefits from computers exert a strong influence on the decision to learn to use them; this is referred to as instrumentality (i.e., the belief that one will benefit from computers). Motivation for learning is shaped by their wants and whether they perceive EUC as a means of getting it. Thus, early adopters who view end-user activities as a means of accomplishing their goals are likely to be motivated to learn. In contrast, end-user support mechanisms may be less effective among reluctant users who do not perceive computer expertise as instrumental in achieving job-related benefits. This type of user may need advice or assistance in identifying a task-tool match. One way of achieving this match is to present users with ideas about the way computers might help in a realistic evaluation of possible applications and use of end-user tools.

Eason (1984) identifies two additional characteristics of end-users: intermittent usage (where many managers and professionals only occasionally need to use the tools) and task specific cost/benefit assessment (when willingness to use tools is based upon a series of assessments of “cost” as the personal time and effort it will take versus “benefit” measured in terms of utility for the task at hand). Intermittent usage is typical of both early adopters and reluctant users; however, reluctant users are likely to define benefits narrowly and be impatient with the time and effort to learn and use systems.

Intermittent usage has several consequences. First, intermittent users seldom receive formal, systematic training because they do not see it as worthwhile. Second, even if training does occur, users may forget many of the details before they use it. Formal training is valuable when there are close to full-time, dedicated users. Intermittent usage increases the requirement for point-of-need support mechanisms, such as instantly available consultation (e.g., hot line) or on-line user help.

Point-of-need support is good for all users but is crucial for reluctant users. Early adopters assume there is higher benefit in the use of technology, they are less likely to become frustrated or impatient as problems arise, focus on global/continuing rather than task specific/short-term objectives, and continue to develop their expertise over time. When problems arise, early adopters are likely to be capable of conducting experiments to isolate the problem, or identify alternative ways of meeting information needs. Manuals that describe software tools enable end-users to function independently and solve many problems on their own. Such mechanisms make point-of-need consulting assistance less critical for this group of users.

For the larger part of the working population who have only a marginal interest in technology, support is critical. Without support, the cost side of the cost/benefit assessment quickly becomes larger and usage stagnates. Beginning users need to be able to read something that will introduce concepts and information in a logical sequence and that will answer questions they do not know how to ask. More experienced users need a source to which they can go to answer specific questions (Nickerson, 1981).

Informal assistance from others in the same work group is also a cost effective way of handling simple queries. Many older or high status users might prefer to consult documentation (manuals) rather than display their ignorance before colleagues, especially subordinates. User documentation, especially tutorials, provides low-key, risk-free opportunities to get used to technology. Thus user documentation and informal assistance become important mechanisms for improving end-user understanding and utilization of computing resources.

## 4. The quality of user documentation

Many firms have made a substantial investment in computerized information systems. User documentation plays an important role in maintaining user satisfaction and improving user understanding (Doll and Torkzadeh, 1987). Effective data base and decision support systems require that users understand how their application works as well as “how to use it” (Ackoff, 1967).

Quality user documentation protects the investment in information technology by enhancing the value of the application to the user.

Organizations have generally failed to provide adequate user documentation. In a traditional data processing environment, only about half of the operational applications have good documentation. In an EUC environment, the situation has not improved. In contrast to their enthusiasm with PCs, users are significantly less satisfied with support manuals. Decision support systems differ widely in the quality of user support. Reimann and Waren (1985) suggest that user-oriented criteria for DSS software selection should include the quality of user documentation.

Doll and Ahmed (1985) found that good user documentation had a dramatic and continuing impact on user satisfaction. User managers with well documented systems found them easier to use, reported greater trust in information they received from them, felt that they better met the needs, and considered the applications more enjoyable to use. However, the study used only a single item, “Do you have good user documentation of the systems you are using?”, with only “yes” or “no” answer to measure the quality of user documentation in a traditional D.P. environment.

In an end-user computing environment, the major factors supporting the potential importance of user documentation include: the diversity of applications and software packages, the dominant role of self-training, intermittent usage patterns, and the importance of point-of-need support. Compared to training and consultation, user documentation appears to be a cost effective mechanism that meets many, but not all, of the continuing support needs of end-users. Thus the quality of user documentation should be associated with end-user satisfaction.

Locke and Schweiger (1979) describe three psychological mechanisms (cognitive, motivational, and value attainment) that affect individual satisfaction and productive efficiency. Two, cognitive and motivational, were used by Ives and Olson (1984) as a theoretical explanation of how user involvement affects MIS success. The mechanisms may provide a theoretical rationale for linking user documentation to EUC satisfaction.

Cognitive mechanisms refer to user documentation's enhancement of end-user computing information, knowledge, understanding and creativity. Enhancement occurs in two ways. First, by improved communication and better utilization of information. Second, by enhanced computing skills and better understanding of end-users. Motivational mechanisms (e.g., increased trust and sense of control) may enhance user acceptance of and commitment to the application. Thus, if user documentation improves the user's sense of control, it might improve user acceptance. Value attainment asks whether user documentation helps end-users get what they want, i.e., accomplish their objectives or attain work related benefits through EUC.

Hypothesis 1: Higher quality user documentation should be associated with increased EUC satisfaction with data base and decision support (DSS) applications.

Furthermore, DSS and their use are often more complex and difficult to master than data base management systems. DSS usually includes both data base capability and a model base for data manipulation and analysis. Thus, the need for user documentation may be higher in DSS than in data base applications. To the extent that data manipulation and analysis requires cognitive skills:

Hypothesis 2: User documentation should be more closely associated with EUC satisfaction in DSS than in data base applications.

## 5. The study

In order to test these hypotheses, a survey of end-users was conducted in over 40 firms. The sample was select rather than random. In participating firms, the MIS directors were asked to identify their major end-users of DSS and data base applications. Data were gathered by research assistants through personal interviews. These enabled the assistants to verify that the respondents were end-users who directly interacted with application software and made decisions in their work based on the output. The research assistants first conducted open-ended structured interviews and recorded the end-users' comments; then a Likert-type questionnaire was administered to each end-user to measure end-user satisfaction and perceptions of the quality of user documentation. Through this method, a sample of 354 end-users was obtained. The end-users were also asked their position, the type of application (i.e., decision support or data base), and whether the hardware system was a personal computer or a mainframe/mini.

## 5.2 Measures of end-user computing satisfaction

End-user computing satisfaction is assessed from the view point of an individual decision maker; it focuses on the utility of a specific application in decision making. The utility in decision making is enhanced when the outputs meet the requirements for information content, format, accuracy, timeliness and the application is easy to use. Ease of use or “user friendliness” is especially important in facilitating managerial use of data base or decision support applications.

The measure of end-user satisfaction included information product (Ives, Olson, and Baroudi, 1983) and ease of use items. To examine the discriminant validity of these two sets of items, the 27 item correlation matrix of EUCS (12 items) and user documentation (15 items) measures was examined. Discriminant validity was examined using the multitrait-multi-method (MTMM) approach by counting (for each item) the number of times it correlates more highly with an item of another construct than with items of its own theoretical variable. Campbell and Fiske (1959) suggest determining whether the count is higher than one-half the potential comparisons. However, in this case common method variances are present so it is unclear how large a count would be acceptable.

In this case, 360 potential comparisons existed (each of 12 EUCS items compared to 15 user documentation items, plus each of 15 user documentation items compared to 12 EUCS items). An examination of the measure correlation matrix revealed only 2 violations out of the 360 potential comparisons. Thus, the two constructs appear to be distinguishable. If EUCS and user documentation scales are correlated with each other, this correlation is not likely to be spurious (i.e., due to individual items of one construct being highly correlated with items of the other construct).

## 6. Results

## 6.1 Quantitative results

Table 4 summarizes end-user satisfaction and the perceived quality of user documentation by DSS and data base categories. End-users perceived the user documentation available with DSS applications to be slightly higher quality than the documentation for data base applications. However, the difference was not statistically significant. Apparently there is no significant difference between DSS and data base applications in either overall end-user computing satisfaction or any of its components. The quality of user documentation scores were not high, averaging approximately 51 on a 15 to 75 scale. Considerable improvement in the quality of end-user document is possible.

The hypothesized relationship between the quality of user documentation and end-user computing satisfaction is supported by the correlations of Table 5. Higher quality user documentation is associated with greater levels of end-user computing satisfaction (Hypothesis 1). This association is significant (p < 0.000) for both decision support and data base applications and for each component of the EUCS construct (i.e., satisfaction with information content, output format, accuracy, ease of use, and timeliness).

Not everyone saw user documentation as a partial substitute for assistance. One user, with an attitude familiar to many systems people, said: "User documentation is good for training, but after training, (we) just phone MIS for assistance."

This qualitative information provide useful insights. It supports the need for a new view of the importance of user documentation and suggests that training, consultation, and user documentation might be substitute support mechanisms for enhancing end-user learning.

## 7. Discussion

Providing an effective support system for a variety of users with different levels of skill and motivation is essential. The diversity of enduser support needs for managerial and professional personnel makes it difficult to provide timely as well as relevant instruction beyond the basic skill level. It is also difficult for information services staff to provide quality consultation services for a great variety of applications and software packages. Active end-users often quickly exhaust the knowledge of the consultant and must rely on user documentation and/or assistance from colleagues familiar with the application.

Initial training of full-time users is usually necessary; however, they also have a continuing need for support. Guimaraes and Ramanujam (1986) found that the nature of training currently provided to PC users is technical and limited in scope. Building upon this technical training, many PC users can train themselves if appropriate assistance is available. For intermittent users, training and consultation are not likely to be cost effective. Quality user documentation can provide continuing and often cost effective point-of-need support for both full-time and intermittent users. However, high-quality user documentation for a small number of users can be expensive, especially if an organization has to develop its own, rather than relying on the software vendor.

As end-user support efforts begin to focus on the reluctant user, it becomes clear that the major problem inhibiting further growth and penetration of computers in organizations is one of motivation. Among reluctant managerial and professional users with intermittent usage, training efforts should emphasize: (a) initial hands-on skills to reduce anxiety and get the user started; and (b) motivational seminars or demonstrations which help users identify applications which provide job-related benefits. Many users will rely primarily on self-training and often resort to user documentation. Research into the interaction between human beings and computers suggests that even if a user is well trained in the use of a system (or software tool), learning most often takes place while actually using the system. For this learning to occur, users must be supported by good documentation and human advisors.

## 8. Conclusions

Strategies for transferring certain information system tasks to the user will be one of the most important human resource issues in the 1990s. Desktop technologies will transform the work place by enabling individuals to participate in the design of their work as well as its execution. However, this transformation is unlikely to occur without cost effective enduser support. Quality of user documentation has the potential to be an important determinant of the success in an end-user computing environment. Substantial improvement in the quality of end-user tool manuals and application oriented user documentation is both possible and desirable. If this is to be achieved, software development and the design of user documentation should be simultaneous activities; early consideration of how the system and its use is described are essential.
