# An assessment of electronic meeting systems in a corporate setting

Maryam Alavi

University of Maryland, College Park, MD, USA

This article reports on the effectiveness of electronic meeting systems (EMS) in organizations. The findings of this field study were consistent with laboratory experiments and indicated that electronic meetings were highly rated in terms of both meeting task and process outcomes. The participants felt that, compared to traditional meetings, EMS led to generation of more high quality ideas in less time. Furthermore, the participants were highly satisfied with the electronic meeting process and perceived a high level of group cohesiveness during the meetings. Observation of group interaction processes during the electronic meetings and post-meeting interviews further suggest that (1) EMS do not replace but augment verbal communications in a face-to-face meeting. This increases the volume of information generated and communicated during the meetings; (2) user interface and ease of use of EMS software can compensate for low levels of participant computer literacy and for typing skills; (3) electronic meeting systems increase participants' concentration and enjoyment of the task; this may lead to increased creativity and productivity. As more organizations begin implementing and using EMS, additional field studies are needed to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the organizational implications of the adoption of these systems.

Keywords: Electronic meeting systems, Group decision support systems, Groupware, Computer-supported cooperative work.

## 1. Introduction

"A prevalent theme of modern industrial societies is that group activities are economically necessary, efficient as means of production, and reinforcing of democratic values" [10]. Although an organization is a collection of individuals, its effective functioning depends on groups of people working together to achieve common goals. Increasingly, work groups or business teams are becoming one of the primary vehicles for carrying out work in organizations [1]. Face-to-face meetings are the formal structures through which groups function and collaborate. It is estimated that managers and professionals spend between 40 to 70 percent of their time in meetings [4,14] However, meetings are often ineffective and unproductive [8,12,13]. Productivity losses in meetings may occur because a few members may dominate discussions, hidden agendas may influence the meeting outcomes, group members may miscommunicate information or some members may not participate in the group task [7]. Thus, enhancing meeting productivity provides an opportunity for improving group and organizational effectiveness. With recent advances in information technology (computers and telecommunications), serious efforts have been made to use this technology to enhance group meetings [9]. Electronic meeting systems (EMS) represent a particular form of information technology support for meetings. EMS environments typically consist of a number of networked computer workstations and a set of flexible software tools that provide basic communication and problem solving support, including brainstorming, information sharing and structuring, and alternative generation and evaluation. The emergence of EMS has led to a high level of research activity focusing on investigation of their effectiveness and impact on group meeting outcomes. However, most of the research studies have been conducted in laboratory settings that limit the generalizability of their findings to more complex organization settings. $^{1}$ Thus, although laboratory experiments in this area have been and will continue to be useful in creating a foundation for understanding the impacts of EMS on group meetings, as Pinsonneault and Kraemer [17] point out, there is a need to move from laboratory to field settings.

The primary objective of the current study is to assess the effectiveness of a series of electronic meetings conducted in an organization. More specifically, this study investigates the impact of electronic meeting systems on group processes and outcomes in organizational settings. The findings of the study are then integrated with the major laboratory findings in this area in an effort to provide a more complete understanding of the effectiveness of electronic meeting systems.

## 2. The study framework

The research framework employed to analyze the impact and effectiveness of EMS in organizations is depicted in Figure 1. This framework developed by Nunamaker et al. [14] is based on prior research and theory of-small-groups (e.g., the work of Hackman and Kaplan [6] and McGrath [11]). The framework defines six categories of variables and interrelationships considered important and relevant to the study of electronic meetings. One of the variable sets consists of group characteristics including group size, history, and composition; these impact the group's process and ultimately the meeting outcomes. Task refers to the group's substantive work. Task characteristics include complexity and type. Task type refers to the activities required to accomplish the work (e.g., idea generation, choice, and negotiation). Another variable set relates to contextual characteristics and reflects organizational and group environmental attributes, such as culture and climate, reward system, and organization structure features. Technology characteristics include hardware and software configuration and the level and type of electronic meeting support (e.g., electronic brainstorming or voting) provided to the group. Group process variables refer to group dynamics and interactions. This variable set contains attributes such as level of participation, group facilitation (technology or process facilitation or both), member anonymity, and communication and information exchange patterns. Two types of outcome variables are identified in the framework of Figure 1:

The objective of this field study was to evaluate electronic meetings relative to traditional (i.e., non-computer-mediated) meetings. A sample of the electronic meetings evaluated in the study are profiled in terms of the variable sets of the framework and are shown in Table 1. Entries are now briefly described.

Group characteristics. Twenty-three groups participating in electronic meetings were included in the study. All the groups were first-time users of electronic meeting systems. A total of 167 individuals participated in the study. The group sizes ranged from three to fifteen individuals (with an average of 8.43). Group members represented a range of different departments including: marketing, operations and facilities management, personnel, and legal. Participants in the electronic meetings held a variety of job titles including information systems specialist, vice president, manager, secretary, and administrative assistant. Both peer groups (consisting of individuals with the same organizational rank and status), and hierarchical groups (consisting of individuals with different organizational rank and status) were involved in the study. Some groups were ad hoc and temporary (e.g., project teams) while others were on-going and permanent components of the organization (e.g., marketing or quality teams).

Task characteristics. All tasks performed by the groups in the electronic meetings entailed idea/alternative generation and evaluation as the main performance process (a generative task in McGrath's task classification scheme $^{2}$ ) However, within this task type, a number of different task domains (e.g., marketing and human resource) were addressed. A typical electronic meeting session started with generating alternatives or comments in response to a specific question. The next activity consisted of discussions of alternatives and the rating of the alternatives on a seven point Likert scale. The electronic meeting tasks were limited to idea generation and rating in order to facilitate and encourage use of the EMS by reducing the training time and cost. Furthermore, since task activities determine the nature and level of technological support during the electronic meetings, limiting the task activities to idea generation and rating greatly reduced the need for extensive pre-meeting planning sessions to match task activities against different software tools. “Controlling” the group task type also allowed use of specific and similar task performance measures across different groups.

Contextual characteristics. Groups from throughout the headquarters were invited to use the electronic meeting system through promotional brochures and announcements in newsletter and other internal publications. By limiting the data collection to the single headquarters site, contextual variables (e.g., organization culture and climate, and the reward system) were controlled and did not present a source of variance in electronic meeting process and outcomes.

Technology characteristics. The electronic meeting environment consisted of ten networked PC/386 workstations arranged in a U-shape configuration, a laser printer and an overhead projector. The printer produced high-quality output on both paper and transparencies. Transparencies were projected on a white board for group viewing and discussion. The electronic meeting system software installed at this site was VISIONQUEST™ by Collaborative Technologies Corporation (CTC) of Austin, Texas. VISIONQUEST consists of an integrated set of eight software tools that support generating, communicating, displaying, and synthesizing information generated by groups during meetings. Since all meeting tasks consisted of idea generation and evaluation, two software tools – electronic brainwriting and rating – were used in all group meetings. The brainwriting tool enables group members simultaneously and anonymously to input ideas and alternatives. Each group member also sees the alternatives generated by other members as they are entered. The rating tool enables the group members to estimate the importance of the individual alternatives on a Likert type scale. The individual ratings of the alternatives are then pooled to prioritize the alternatives based on the group average rating scores. The alternatives are automatically “imported” from the brainwriting tool (where they were generated) to the rating tool (where they are rated and prioritized).

The same level and type of technology support was used in all the meetings. Thus, differing levels and forms of technology support were not a source of variance in group process and outcomes.

These electronic meetings were evaluated in terms of the two sets of task-related and group-related outcomes. More specifically, the electronic meetings were evaluated in terms of perceived quality and quantity of ideas generated and the time required to rate and prioritize them. Group-related outcomes consisted of group member satisfaction with the meeting process, and perceived group cohesiveness.

## 3.1. Data collection

Data were collected through multiple means: post-meeting questionnaires, interviews, and unobstrusive observations of the electronic meetings. The post-meeting questionnaire was designed to assess the task and group related outcomes and consisted of structured and open-ended questions. The structured questionnaire items used 5-point Likert scales and were a paper-and-pencil instrument. Task outcomes were measured in terms of:

perceived quality of ideas generated;

quantity of ideas generated;

time required to generate ideas;

time required to rate ideas.

Group outcomes were assessed in terms of:

group member satisfaction with the electronic meeting process, and

the perceived group cohesiveness.

Group process satisfaction was measured in terms of:

level of comfort in expressing ideas in the meeting;

level of perceived stress during the meeting;

ease of use of the computer;

willingness to use the electronic meeting system again.

The perceived group cohesiveness was assessed in terms of:

extent to which the individual felt a part of the group, and;

degree to which the group worked well together.

The open-ended question component of the post-meeting questionnaire stated: “Think of the computer assisted part of this meeting. Please tell us what really impressed you? What could be done differently or improved?”

In addition to the post-meeting questionnaire, personal interviews were conducted with the meeting leaders (a meeting leader was the individual who had called the electronic meeting). In some cases, the meeting leader was the group manager but in some cases a staff member played this role. The objective of the personal interviews was to obtain an in-depth understanding of the meeting task, group composition, established group norms, and the meeting leader's assessment of the electronic meeting process and outcome.

In addition to the questionnaires and interviews, the electronic meetings were also observed by the researcher. The focus of the observations was on the group interactions and the meeting process and dynamics.

## 4. Findings

Post-meeting questionnaires, interview data, and meeting observation logs were analyzed to assess the impact of EMS on the meeting task and group outcomes and processes.

## 4.1. Impact on task and groups outcomes

Descriptive statistics from the post-meeting questionnaire are displayed in Table 2. As indicated, the electronic meetings were highly rated in terms of both task and group outcomes. Participants in the electronic meetings felt that, compared to traditional meetings, the electronic meetings led to generation of more high-quality ideas. They agreed that it took less time to generate and rate these ideas. In response to the open-ended questions, several participants commented on the speed and efficiency of generating and rating ideas. For example, one respondent commented that “It (the electronic meeting system) was quick and very effective in accomplishing our goals.” Two other participants stated, “A large number of ideas were generated in a short time,” and, “The system was a very fast way to generate ideas.” The speed and efficiency of generating and rating ideas were also the most frequently mentioned benefit of the electronic meeting system in the personal interviews with meeting leaders.

The post-meeting questionnaire data also indicates that for the sample of electronic meetings included in the study, the meeting process was highly rated relative to traditional meetings. Participants felt more comfortable in offering their ideas, and perceived less stress during the electronic meetings. Perhaps the best indicator of user satisfaction with the electronic meeting systems was the willingness of almost all of the first-time users of the EMS to use it again for future meetings. When asked if they would like to use the electronic meeting system in the future, 98.2 percent of the participants responded positively. Furthermore, a high degree of group cohesiveness was experienced in the electronic meetings. Most of the meeting participants felt an integral part of the group and felt that the group members worked well together. These findings are consistent with the researcher's observation of group interactions during the meeting. Little conflict was observed among the members in any of the observed groups and a high degree of group cooperation was exhibited.

Kraemer [16] in their review and assessment of the empirical research on the effects of electronic meetings on groups concluded that laboratory research provides consistent results showing that electronic meeting systems increase task performance quality while decreasing task performance time. They stated that electronic meeting systems were associated with high levels of group member satisfaction with the group process. ## 4.2. Impact on group process

The major thrust of this study was to assess the impact and effectiveness of electronic meeting systems in an organizational setting and thus it is necessary to go beyond the user affective reactions to the electronic meetings. The post-meeting questionnaires containing the perceptual items were therefore, supplemented with data from unobstructive observations of meeting processes and follow-up interviews with meeting leaders. Qualitative data collected through the meeting observations and interviews developed a deeper understanding of the impact and implications of electronic meeting systems in organizations.

A substantial level of discussion and information sharing was observed during the electronic meetings. In addition to the exchange of ideas and information electronically through the networked computers, a relatively high level of verbal communication among the meeting participants took place. In a post-meeting interview one of the meeting leaders (who was also the group manager) stated that he was impressed by the level of verbal discussion in the electronic meeting relative to his regular staff meetings. This is an interesting finding in that the electronic communication channels did not seem to replace, but augmented and stimulated verbal discussion. This may in turn lead to increased information sharing and enhanced group decision making and problem solving. The following comments provided in the post-meeting questionnaire support this perspective:

"This method [the electronic meeting system] is great in expanding the thinking of an individual with ideas;" "I think this is a great system, a quick and easy way to gather data and develop alternatives;"

“Maximizes the group’s mental resources;”

Existence of both verbal and electronic information sharing and exchange in the electronic meetings seemed to provide an opportunity for interpersonal learning and task ambiguity reduction. This in turn suggests that the concept of electronic meeting systems effectiveness in organizational settings should be broadened to include an overarching evaluation of group process and should go beyond the simple and proximal task and group outcomes of a single or a few electronic meetings.

Most of the observed groups were intact groups, groups composed of individuals who had a history of working together, intend to work together in the future, and had a vested interest in the group activities and outcomes $[3]$ . These characteristics of the intact groups seemed to impact the electronic meeting processes and dynamics. For example, during one of the meetings, due to past experience and a history of working together, ideas entered anonymously via the electronic meeting system were attributed to the contributors by other group members. In another electronic meeting a relatively low level of discussion was observed. The post-meeting interview with the group leader indicated that the group had met prior to the electronic meeting to generate and discuss alternatives. Therefore, little discussion was necessary during the electronic meeting, which was primarily held to capture, record, rate, and prioritize the alternatives. In short, group interactions outside the electronic meeting sessions impact the group processes during the electronic meetings. It is important to consider the entire range of group task related interactions in evaluation of the electronic meeting systems in the field setting.

The meeting leader (or a technology facilitator) instructed the group during the meetings on the use of the EMS. Except for the meeting leader, none of the participants were trained in the use of the EMS software. During the meetings a wide variety in the levels of computer literacy and typing skills of the participants were observed. However, despite lack of software training and variation in computer literacy and typing skills, all participants were able to interact effectively with the EMS with minimal guidance during the meetings. This could be attributed to the ease of use and user-friendliness of the particular software at the research site; this minimized the key strokes for data entry through use of function keys and pull-down menus. This observation is consistent with the relatively high rating of the questionnaire item concerning the ease of use of the computer, where the mean score was 4.55 out of a maximum of 5.

Observations of the electronic meetings indicated high levels of participant motivation, engagement in, and enjoyment of the meeting. Furthermore, in the post-meeting questionnaire, several participants characterized their electronic meetings as enjoyable and “fun.” For example, an individual commented that “using the computer made the meeting fun even though we were discussing major topics and how we felt about people.” The high level of participant engagement and involvement in the task during the electronic meetings was also brought up by a number of meeting leaders who participated in the post-meeting interviews. For example, one leader mentioned that one of her staff members who is usually uncooperative, quiet, and uninvolved during the regular meetings had acted very enthusiastically and had been highly focused on the task in the electronic meeting.

This observation is consistent with the findings of Grohowski et al. [5] who report that group members stayed more focused on their task throughout the electronic meetings compared with traditional face-to-face meetings. Group members are likely to become more productive and creative when they are fully engaged in and enjoy the task. Therefore, future studies can benefit by investigating this phenomenon and its relationship to users' affective response to EMS and task outcomes (e.g., creativity).

The qualitative observations of the electronic meeting processes were extremely important to exploring phenomenon and group interaction patterns that would not surface from an analysis of the quantitative post-meeting questionnaire data alone. Such observations provide significant insight into issues surrounding application of EMS in the field.

## 5. Summary and conclusions

This field study evaluated the impact of EMS on task and group outcomes, and the meeting processes under varying group characteristics. Task type, contextual characteristics and the technology were maintained constant throughout the evaluation. The most interesting finding is the overall positive nature of the users' evaluation of the EMS in terms of task and group outcomes. The users felt that relative to traditional meetings, the electronic meeting lead to generation of more high quality ideas in a shorter time period. Participants in the meeting were highly satisfied with the meeting process and perceived a high level of group cohesiveness during the meetings. Thus, organizations implementing EMS can expect positive outcomes across different groups focusing on idea generation tasks. Knowledge of impact and effectiveness of EMS when other types of tasks (e.g. negotiations) are involved is limited and no generalizations in this area can be made from the current study.

Unobtrusive observations of electronic meetings provided further insight into issues surrounding implementation of EMS in organizational settings. The observations suggest that:

\- EMS do not replace verbal communications in a face-to-face meeting. EMS augment verbal communication channels increasing the volume of information generated and communicated during the meetings.

\- Organizational effectiveness of the electronic meetings should be evaluated in the context of the overall group task-related interactions both inside and outside the EMS session.

\- User interface and ease of use of EMS software can compensate for low levels of participant computer literacy and typing skills. Failure to consider these features of EMS software can lead to considerable training cost and time, which in turn may create a deterrent to system use.

\- Electronic meeting systems increase participants' concentration and enjoyment of the task which may lead to increased creativity and productivity. With a few exceptions [e.g., 2, 15], there is a dearth of field studies of electronic meeting systems. However, as more organizations begin implementing and using this technology, additional field studies are warranted to expand observations and integrate and synthesize aspects of field and experimental research.

Study of EMS in organizational settings is complex due, in part, to differences in technology, task, group size, and other contextual variables $[19]$ . The current study was limited to evaluation of single EMS sessions involving first-time user groups. Furthermore, the study addressed the EMS outcomes in terms of perceptual measures only. Future studies involving longitudinal observation of use and impact of EMS on group process and objective task performance measures are needed to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the organizational adoption and implications of EMS.
