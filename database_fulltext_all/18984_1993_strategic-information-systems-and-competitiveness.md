# Strategic information systems and competitiveness

# Are firms ready for an IST-driven competitive challenge?

Constanza Hagmann and Cynthia S. McCahon

Kansas State University, Manhattan, KS, USA

Strategic planning for information systems technology (IST) and its use for competitive advantage have been discussed for some time. However, such research focuses on the planning method and experience of firms that have been successful in their efforts to use IST for competitive advantage. Not much attention has been paid to less successful organizations and to what they are doing to prepare themselves for IST-driven competition. This paper presents a study conducted among small-to-medium size firms showing that a large fraction of these businesses place surprisingly little emphasis on planning for information systems (IS) and these business have a rather low level of awareness of the potential impact of IS planning on competitiveness. These results point to the fact that organizations need help in becoming more proactive in linking IS planning to their competitive strategy.

Keywords: Strategic information systems; Strategic information systems planning; Information systems as a competitive weapon

## Introduction

The factors leading to successful organizational use of information systems (IS) technology (IST) in establishing and maintaining competitive advantage have been discussed at length $[11,17,21,24,29]$ . However, there is no clear consensus on the connection between strategic planning for IST and its implementation and use for competitive advantage.

Even the definition of strategic information systems (SIS) has been subject to scrutiny: some authors $[4,13]$ define SIS as collections of planning processes that influence the organization's overall strategic plan; others $[9,14,15]$ prefer to classify systems as SIS if they simply alter the organization's approach to competition. The terms “Strategic Information Systems”, “Strategic Planning for Information Systems”, and “Strategic Information Systems Planning” [e.g., 1,20] are still used interchangeably.

Research concerning SIS planning focuses on the approaches and successful systems of very large and/or technologically innovative organizations [e.g., 18,25]. Thorough examination of what is being done by smaller and less innovative firms has not been made, even though the performance of appropriate IST planning might directly affect their ability to compete [27].

The purpose of this study is to examine empirical evidence of IST planning and its impact on the competitive activities of small (up to 249 employees) to medium (250 to 1400 employees) size firms by specifically looking at:

\- the IS portfolio these firms have implemented,

\- the type of planning (strategic/tactical) they perform for each type of system, and

\- the perceived effects of IST planning on potential competitive strategies.

The study is based on a survey sent to the managers of 300 Kansas firms. The survey instrument used Synnott's [28] Information Weapon model as a framework to obtain and analyze information on the firms' portfolios, planning, and their perceived impacts on competitiveness. The results show that many of these businesses are not prepared to face IST-driven competitive challenges. They need to increase their awareness and use of those IST planning approaches that provide tools and methods for examination of the impact of IST as a competitive weapon.

## Background and purpose

Information systems planning has gained steadily increasing organizational attention. However, a survey, conducted in 1991 among 245 of the 1000 largest manufacturing and service companies in the U.S.A., showed that 54.4% of these firms dedicated 2% or less of their IS staff efforts to planning, while only 9% dedicated 12% or more [23]. Current research in the area [e.g., 2,3,6,7] suggests that the successful adoption of integrated business/IST planning is still far from being commonplace. In fact, a study of very large organizations [10] indicates that the least effective data resource management function is the development of integrated, organization-wide data models and data architecture.

There is little scholarly agreement concerning what IST planning accomplishes. Different planning methodologies, such as IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP) and Information Quality Analysis (IQA), Andersen Consulting's Strategic

Information Planning, and Rockart's Critical Success Factors (CSF) frequently lead to significantly different planning results and decisions, because they use different techniques. BSP focuses on extensive analysis of business objectives and processes to create an “information architecture” for IS development, but CSF emphasizes that priority should be given to IST that supports the areas or factors critical to organizational success.

At present, a direct connection between strategic IST planning and as yet its implementation as a competitive tool is not clear, even among organizations which were the “leaders” in establishing such systems. An exploratory study of successful implementations $[19]$ found that, for the firms classified as innovators and leaders in competitive systems, the most important factors leading to success were the perceived need to improve internal operations, the experience obtained from their existing IST portfolio, and the stated objective of satisfying customer needs. Experience with IST applications allowed organizations to implement system improvements effectively and to modify and/or expand them when other sought to implement similar systems. The integration of IST and business planning was found to be very important for start-up businesses, such as Federal Express and USA Today, which incorporated IST into their strategy for success.

Nevertheless, the impact of IST innovations on entire industries, such as air travel and tourism, banking and financial services, hospital and pharmacy supply, publishing, and grocery distribution illustrates the need to prepare for possible changes caused by IST innovation in industry and markets. A case in point is the introduction of automatic teller machines (ATMs). ATMs eliminated the need for tellers to handle many simple transactions, provided extended service hours for bank customers, and gave the IST innovator, Citibank, an initial competitive advantage $[22]$ . This made it essential for other financial institutions to adjust to the new market situation to be able to compete.

Being a leader in IST innovation entails many risks, such as committing management to a new way of thinking; using a new and perhaps untried technology; investing a large proportion of organizational resources in IST and ensuring cooperation from other parts of the firm's internal and external environment [16]. Many organizations who choose a “follower” strategy may need to react quickly. Therefore, planning might be even more critical for these firms than for those at the leading edge of IST innovation.

Thus, existing planning models were not the primary force behind the initial use of IST innovation for competitive advantage. Experience with existing organizational IST applications was one of the main factors for success in this area, but quick reaction to a competitor's successful innovation is essential for proper adoption of new technology for non-leaders.

## IST as a competitive strategy weapon

Of the several models of the use of IST to gain competitive advantage, W.R. Synnott's Information Weapon (IW) model makes it possible to examine an organization's IST portfolio and its possible impact on strategic activities. The IW model classifies IST into three categories: systems that focus on innovation for competitive advantage, systems that use information itself as a weapon, and systems that increase productivity and thus lower the cost of production, marketing, or distribution. These form the strategies of innovation, information services, and productivity.

Innovation strategy centers around systems designed to anticipate and respond to customer needs: these are customer service or marketing planning systems. Here the firm might offer a new product with fairly non-imitable characteristics. One such product is Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account which offers cash withdrawal, credit, and investment options using a single account, thus simplifying these tasks for customers.

Information services strategy can furnish improved financial and statistical data and other necessary internal feedback, or provide customers with proprietary information that facilitates interactions with the firm. Financial planning, executive information, logistics, electronic data interchange (EDI), external database access, and expert systems can be considered information services systems. McKesson used this strategy with its ordering system which minimized customers' work in reordering by allowing them to link their systems to the distributor's.

Productivity strategy is usually implemented through operations support systems such as transaction processing, inventory management, centralized databases, production planning, personnel, and factory floor control systems. Operational productivity can increase by automating labor-intensive operations, or by improving quality and/or rate of output. For example, the use of CAD systems by Hitachi Ltd. hastened product design, production, and delivery, reducing rework and manufacturing costs.

These three strategies can be used singly or in combination. Thus, a firm can increase its productivity to become the low-cost producer and, at the same time, provide customer access to its database to make product selection and ordering simpler and faster.

## Methodology

The study was conducted through a survey sent to the managers of 300 small (up to 249 employees) to medium (250 to 1400 employees) Kansas firms. These firms were selected from the 1990 edition of the Directory of Kansas Manufacturers and Products. A total of 61 firms (20.3%) responded to the survey. The response rate is typical for this type of study. Table 1 gives a breakdown, based on total number of employees and type of firms for the organizations that participated in the survey.

When asked to report their own perception of their level of computerization compared with that of other firms of similar size in their industry, 14.8% saw themselves as leaders in computerization, 24.6% as having a very high level, 23% as having a high level, while 37.7% reported an average or below average level of computerization. There were no IS staff in 19.7% of these firms, 59% had between one and five people, and 21.3% reported more than 6. There was no correlation between perceived level of computerization and the size of the IS staff.

## Planning activities

Since experience with IST appears to be one of the main success factors in establishing competitive systems and facilitating quick responses to competitive challenges, IST planning should include a thorough study of a firm's applications portfolio [5]. Some of the items that should be examined are enumerated in Table 3. These items form part of an initial “environmental” study of the internal and external factors affecting the use of IST applications. Internal factors measure and define the organization's use of IT and potential improvements in its use. Documentation of the internal factors includes an analysis of the systems portfolio's efficiency and effectiveness, as well as its maturity (i.e., are the systems “old”, and can they evolve?). Factors external to each application include the interaction with other organizational systems and the current trends in technology.

A canonical correlation analysis using SAS [26] was conducted to investigate any potential relationship between a group of stated planning efforts for each IST system and a group of environmental factors. Canonical correlation analysis in general uncovers relationships between groups of variables. These are loaded onto the variate vector to maximize correlation. For this data, only the first canonical correlation was significant at the $\alpha = 0.01$ level using an F-test. The correlation between the groups was 0.75 (86% of variance explained). With a cutoff value of 0.30, the IST systems of statistical analysis systems, production planning, factory floor control, and transaction processing correlated positively with this first canonical variate. Expert systems correlated negatively. The environmental analyses of Efficiency and Systems Interactions were correlated at a 0.30 level or higher with this same canonical variate. It appears that those firms which include statistical analysis systems, production planning, factory floor control, transaction processing, but exclude expert systems will be more likely to perform the efficiency and systems interactions analyses. It can also be concluded that organizations that state they plan for IST do not apply a consistent set of analysis tools to their entire portfolio.

## Perceived impact of planning on IST-driven competitive activities

A list of possible competitive IST-driven activities was provided in the questionnaire to measure organizational awareness of the potential impact of IST planning on competitiveness. Respondents were asked to indicate, on a Likert-type scale, if they felt IST planning had “no impact”, “very little impact”, “some impact”, or “definite impact” on these activities in their organizations. Figure 4 provides a listing of the activities and a graphical representation of weighted average responses. It is evident from these responses that competitiveness issues are not part of the main criteria in their planning endeavors. The only activity that, on the average, came close to the “some impact” level was the utilization of computers for managerial decision support. Furthermore, no significant correlation was found to exist between the perceived impact of IST planning on these activities and their perceived level of computerization, their stated planning efforts, or their environmental analysis activities.

These IW model competitive activities were not listed in the questionnaire in any specific order. Therefore, it was of interest to see if the IST-driven activities naturally grouped into the framework (or factors) of the IW model. Factors typically represent the underlying processes which have created correlations among the variables. To uncover these factors, a factor analysis using an orthogonal varimax rotation was conducted. Three factors accounting for 87.7% of all variance were extracted as shown in Table 5. The factor composition closely follows the IW model classification, meaning that the IST-driven activities can be grouped into those supporting innovation, information services, and productivity strategies.

Categorization of IST-driven competitive activities is not mutually exclusive: an activity may support more than one strategy. Synnott suggested that managerial decision support systems could support both the information services and productivity strategies, but the factor analysis grouped them with the productivity oriented activities. This appears to indicate that organizations in the sample emphasize the productivity (or efficiency) aspects of managerial decision support rather than their informational content. He also suggested that placing terminals in customers' offices would be an innovation strategy activity, whereas the factor analysis placed it under the information services strategy. All the other factors matched the initial classification. The concentration of activities that establish links with the organizational external environment (i.e., terminals in customers' offices, EDI, utilizing external data bases, and selling software developed in-house to other firms) in the information services strategy factor indicates that respondents feel this emphasizes improvement of interorganizational rather than intraorganizational communications.

## Summary and conclusions

Even though the analysis is based on a small sample of firms, research indicates that the results are representative of those in similar type organizations.

The perceived organizational level of computerization is not an indication of planning activities in the firms participating in the survey: 62.4% reported a high or better level of computerization but 42% or less performed planning for any system in their portfolios; there was no correlation between their perceived level of computerization and their planning efforts. Furthermore, these firms gave relatively low priority to their IST function. Close to 20% employed no systems personnel and 73% had no separate information systems function. To achieve integration of IST planning and general business planning, IST should be included in all facets of the organizational planning process. This would be difficult to achieve for organizations that place a low priority on IST.

Most portfolio and planning efforts were concentrated on productivity strategy oriented systems. Participating firms would have a better chance to respond quickly to competitive challenges stemming from the use of this class of system than for the other two. Furthermore, their planning efforts were not consistently correlated with the environmental analyses, suggesting a lack of consistent planning policies and procedures across their entire IST portfolio. By not performing consistent analyses, they are missing the opportunity of pinpointing those systems that already show good potential or can be easily enhanced to provide organizational competitiveness.

Many of the participating firms are not well prepared for IST-driven competitive challenges. Despite the validation of the IW model, the firms perceived little planning impact on the activities that show implementation of the strategies. Furthermore, there was no correlation between their stated planning efforts for IST systems and the perceived impact of planning on the activities: apparently IST-driven competitive strength does not form part of their main planning criteria. This need not be the only criterion in IST and/or organizational planning; however, omitting consideration of this factor from the planning process can detract from the firm's capability to innovate and/or respond to potential challenges.

The rather low profile given to the IST function in most organizations, the inconsistency between their stated planning efforts and environmental analysis activities, and the lack of focus on the use of IS as a competitive weapon, increase the potential vulnerability of these firms. The challenge is to provide this type of firms with planning models and methodologies that are flexible and adapt to their needs, do not take an inordinate amount of time to incorporate into their planning processes, and address the possible use of information systems as a competitive weapon.
