Justifying and demonstrating the effectiveness of information systems is increasingly becoming critical as firms look to become more competitive and efficient. This study takes a detailed look at the ranking criteria and the overall effectiveness index developed by Computerworld. Researchers have alluded to this index as a significant development and Computerworld claims that it is useful for justifying information systems to top management. The broader objective of this study is to contribute to the measurement of the business value of information technology (IT). Towards this goal, a conceptual model of IT measures is developed.

Throughout the computer era there has been continuing difficulty in assessing the benefits and the impact of these investments $[7]$ . Developing methods and techniques for assessing the value of IT is a difficult and challenging task. In a recent review of past research, Kauffman and Weill [26] stated that “it is our observation in 1989 that we are beginning to build up ‘critical mass’ in this area of research.” Their conclusion is partly based upon the development of an index of a firm’s overall IS effectiveness by Computerworld (CW). Since 1988, CW has annually ranked companies based on their IS effectiveness and published its list of the Premier 100. The endorsement of the index and its legitimacy have been described as follows: “After our first ranking appeared last year, many IS managers used these measures to bolster their budget presentations to upper management.” [9]; “Each year, we get hundreds of reprint requests from firms wondering how they are doing” [10]; and “The Premier 100, now in its fourth year, has been developed and guided by information systems industry experts including academics, consultants and IS executives” [11].

This study takes a detailed and comprehensive look at the CW ranking criteria and overall index. The research design and methodology is based upon Ramanujam and Venkatraman [35] who had undertaken a critical evaluation of Peters and Waterman's [33] excellent companies. Both in terms of the data set considered and the analyses performed, this paper extends previous appraisals of the CW index, specifically Strassman [41] and Mahmood and Mann [30].

The broader objective of this research is to contribute to the measurement of business value of IT. Conceptual and methodological issues in assessing IT's business value have recently been addressed by a number of studies including Crowston and Treacy [13], Weill and Olson [45], and Kauffman and Weill [26]. However, most of the attention has been focused on the dependent variable – the appropriate performance indicators for assessing the impact of IT. In contrast, this research closely examines the independent variable and develops a three-level taxonomy of IT measures. It is argued that the results of past research are confounding because their IT measures were dissimilar. Thus, a large part of this study is devoted to building a foundation for measuring IT business value.

## Relationship between information technology and business value

This section reviews previous studies whose focus has been on measuring the business value of IT. As will be observed, the independent variables or their IT measures are not only dissimilar but founded on very different perspectives.

In one of the first studies, Cron and Sobol [12] sought to examine the impact of computerization on the performance of 138 medical wholesalers using cross-sectional data for the year 1979. They employed multiple measures of computerization as well as firm performance. Measures of computerization included the following: computer ownership (no computer, own/lease and time-sharing), number of application areas computerized and types of application areas computerized (accounting, management control and environmental links). Measures of firm performance included pretax profit as a percentage of sales, return on assets, return on net worth and five year average growth rate. The study found that firms making extensive use of IS were either very strong or very weak financial performers.

PIMS [34] and Strassman [41] investigated the impact of IS investment on a performance measure called management productivity. IS investments were measured as mission-critical information technology expense and management information systems expense. Data were obtained from a proprietary database called “Management Productivity and information Technology" which contains information about 35 manufacturing business units for the five year period 1978-1982. The study found that the impact of IS investments depends to a great extent upon the firm's strategic position. It was found-that among firms that had increased their IS investments annually, management productivity increased in firms with a superior strategic position whereas it did not for those with an poorer strategic position.

Turner [43] investigated whether IT expenditure in 58 mutual savings banks was related to organization size and organizational performance. IT expenditure was measured as a percentage of total assets while organizational performance as the percentage of net income to total assets. None of the relationships was found to be significant.

Bender [21] studied the relationship between IS investments and performance in 132 insurance companies. IS investment was measured by a ratio of information processing expenses to total general expense, and firm performance by a ratio of total general expense to premium income. By analyzing the correlation between the two ratios, the study concluded that the optimal value of information processing expense to general expense is between 20 and 25 percent for the insurance industry.

In a series of studies in the life insurance industry, Harris and Katz [21,22,23] examined the relationship between organizational profitability and IS capital intensity. Organizational profitability was measured as the ratio of total operating expense to premium income. IS capital intensity was operationalized as the ratio of information technology expense to total operating expense (IT expense ratio) and/or the ratio of total operating expense to premium income (IT cost efficiency ratio). The first study [21] examined the data for 1988 and found a positive relationship between profitability and IT expense ratio. The second study [22] analyzed data for the four year period, 1983 through 1988, and showed that a discriminant function based on the two IT ratios is a useful predictor of organizational performance. The third study [23] found that in firms considered IT leaders, organizational performance is positively related to IT expense ratio and negatively associated with IT cost efficiency ratio.

In an investigation of 30 manufacturing firms in the valve industry, Weill [44] examined the relationship between IT investments and organizational performance. IT investment was categorized into strategic, informational, and transactional. Organizational performance was measured using sales growth rate, ROA, and non-production labor. The results indicated that strategic and informational IT are not related to superior organizational performance in the long run. Only transactional IT was found to be associated with high performance.

Floyd and Wooldridge [18] studied the relationship between IT and performance using somewhat distinctive measures. Employing retail banks as the sample, the study assessed whether ROA was effected by the implementation of two categories of IT applications: Product IT and Process IT. Product IT included automated teller machines at bank and non-bank locations, and home banking. Process IT comprised online transaction processing, personal computers for managers, decision support systems, and online management reports. It was found that Product IT has a significantly positive effect on organizational performance.

The above review makes two points strikingly clear. First, the results are far from unequivocal; support for the IT-Performance relationship is positive in Bender and Harris and Katz, mixed in Cron and Sobol, Strassman, and Floyd and Wooldridge, extremely weak in Weill, and negative in Turner. Second, there is little evidence of a cumulative tradition in the measurement of IT; the measures and the underlying assumptions in different studies are fairly diverse. In fact, the lack of comparable IT measures may be the reason why the results are ambiguous and confounding, as has been the case in other IS areas such as user involvement, cognitive style, IS implementation, and information requirements determination [39]. Thus, a detailed look at the IT measures used in previous studies from a conceptual perspective is warranted.

## Measures of information technology: A conceptual model

For determining IT's business value, measures of IT can be categorized into three groups, as shown in Figure 1. Measures may focus on determining investments in IT as a whole. The typical operationalization is through ratios, for example, overall IT investments as a percentage of total assets, business operating expense, or premium income. In this approach, the major limitation is little agreement on what constitutes IT [41,45]. The lack of a standard definition of IT expense is attributed to the proliferation of IT and the decentralization of IT expenditure to functional departments. On the other hand, the advantage of using ratios is that they rely on quantifiable, objective data rather than perceptual measures.

Fig. 1. A conceptual model of IT measures.

The effectiveness paradigm implicit in the use of ratios is the goal approach $[6]$ which focuses on the accomplishment of goals, objectives, or “ends.” Specifically, IT effectiveness is presumed to refer to certain optimal values of the different IT ratios. Thus, studies in this area have aimed at determining benchmarks for the ratios under different contingencies.

The second category of IT measures segregates IT investments into different components. While IT expenditure remains the focus of analysis, the expenses are broken-down by different factors such as investment objective or embedded/mission-critical IT. One of the advantages of this approach is that tracking investment components is easier because organizations typically allocate expenses into different categories. Further, data can be more meaningfully compared across organizations. Additionally, the IT-Performance relationship can be studied more accurately because the impact of each IT investment component can be examined separately. Furthermore, precise, tailored performance measures can be used as dependent variables for different IT components. The major limitation of this method is that there are many diverse IT investment classifications but no selection criteria. Further, since information regarding IT investments in different categories is most likely obtainable only through perceptual data, problems arise related to the use of “key informants” in data gathering [24].

The effectiveness paradigm implicit in the use of IT investment components is a combination of the goal model and the system resource model (the latter defines effectiveness as the attainment of a normative state) [6]. That is because IT effectiveness is viewed as certain benchmark levels of IT investments in different categories; the goal model underlies the search for optimal IT investment levels whereas the systems resource model underlies the search for normative IT expenditure categories.

The third category of IT measures gauge the presence and extent of different IT components. The focus is on such factors as whether certain technologies are employed in organizations, the capabilities implemented, extent of actual use, functional areas affected, and so on. For example, Floyd and Wooldridge [18] measured IT as the extent of implementation of different Product and Process ITs. The major advantage in this approach is that IT measures can include not only quantitative variables but also qualitative variables. There is therefore great flexibility and versatility in IT definition and operationalization. The use of context-specific IT definition and operationalization enhances the likelihood of reliably ascertaining the existence and the strength of the IT-Performance relationship. This method's primary limitation is that there are potentially numerous IT factors associated with business performance. The problem is compounded by the fact that while some of the factors directly impact performance, others may have only an indirect impact. Another challenge is to distinguish between factors that determine and those that indicate performance [29]. Thus, the predicament facing this measurement option is how to select only those determinant variables that have a significant and direct relationship with business performance.

The effectiveness paradigm on which this approach is based is the system resource model. That is because IT effectiveness is viewed in such terms as adopting the “right” technologies, using them in “right” functional areas. Thus, there is an emphasis on attaining a normative state in the application and use of IT.

The measures used by CW combine variables from all the three categories above. In this regard, the method is innovative and novel.

## Computerworld's index of information systems effectiveness

Computerworld rates a company according to six criteria. A linear weighted sum of the scores on the six criteria yields an overall index. The six criteria and their weights are as follows:

(1) Annual IS budget as a percentage of revenue relative to the industry average (IS Budget) - 30% weightage,

(2) Currentness of IS equipment, measured by the current market value of equipment as a percentage of revenue (Market Value) - 15%, (3) Five year company profitability - 15%,

(4) IS staff costs as a percentage of IS budget (the lower the spending on personnel, the higher the effectiveness) (Staff Cost) - 10%,

(5) IS personnel training costs as a percentage of the IS budget (Training Cost) - 15% and

(6) Degree of user access to technology, measured by the ratio of microcomputers/terminals to total employees (User Access) - 15%.

It is interesting to note that criterion #1 relates to overall IT investments, criteria #2, #4, and #5 to IT investment components, and criterion #6 to IT components. Thus, unlike previous studies, the CW variables tap all three categories of IT measures. However, the limitation is a mixing of cause and effect measures, i.e., some measures are more related to determinants of IT effectiveness (such as #6) while others are more associated with effects (such as #1). But, as noted by Doll and Torkzadeh [15], in the early stages of research it is not uncommon for cause and effect items to be grouped together to describe a phenomenon, as seen in Bailey and Pearson's [3] work. Thus, it seems justified to use different types of measures.

However, there seems no clear rationale for including criterion #3 above as a part of the index, because it is not a measure of IT at all but of firm performance.

The measurement properties that are examined include: face validity, construct validity, internal consistency, and external consistency. A two-step procedure is followed. The analysis is first performed on the data for years 1988 and 1988. The results are replicated using data for 1990 and 1991. The objective is to enhance confidence in the results through a test-retest strategy. In its scope and analysis, this research is much more extensive and comprehensive than previous studies.

There have been two earlier assessments of the CW index and its constituents. Mahmood and Mann [30] correlated the CW criteria for the 1988 Premier 100 companies with six organizational financial performance measures. The presence of “weak and sporadic” individual relationships prompted them to use canonical analysis to find group associations. They concluded that the results were mixed because of unexpected findings. One limitation of the study is that its results are based on a single year’s analysis. Furthermore, no attention was paid to the overall index and its properties.

Strassman [41], on the other hand, assessed the validity of both the overall index as well as its constituent criteria. This was done by matching “them against an independent, objective measure,” specifically the ten year average shareholder returns from 1977–1987. This measure was correlated with the 1988 overall effectiveness scores and two criteria items: IS as % of Revenue and Employees-per-Workstation. None of the correlations were found to be significant. For 1989, a summary analysis was performed only for the overall effectiveness score which was found to be uncorrelated with the ten year (1978–1988) average shareholder returns. One drawback is that it seems rather limiting to validate the CS index using only one financial performance measure because there is no consensus regarding the latter [6]. Although it is justified that “Shareholder returns, rather than accounting profits, also reflect the reliability of earnings and the consistency of revenue growth as indicators of a firm’s strategic advantage,” a multidimensional assessment would seem to be a more careful and cautious approach. More significantly, however, some reservations can be expressed regarding the use of financial performance data spanning ten years. It is argued that “The long-term average offers the most realistic view of the systemic effects computers may have on company profitability. The long development lead-times for strategic applications dictate taking a long-term view of profitability.” While it is certainly appropriate to take a long-term view of firm profitability, there is some evidence to indicate that most IT investments show returns in about five years [36,44]. Thus, the ten year time frame may be too long.

Keeping in view the above limitations, a more exhaustive look at the index as well as its constituents especially using four years of CW data is worthwhile and hardly repetitious.

Assessment of measurement properties of the index

Face validity

One of the problems with the index is that the construct it operationalizes – IS Effectiveness – is not defined. Furthermore, little justification is presented for the specific weighting scheme used. Also, the assumptions behind the use of a weighted scoring model are not verified – statistical independence and compensatory nature of the individual measures. [17,27]. Additionally, while it is claimed that the index is based on carefully selected and researched criteria, a number of doubts persist. For instance, the inclusion of a measure of business performance (i.e., firm profitability) in an IT index is puzzling. Some potentially important omissions are equally perplexing. For example, when companies are attempting to free IS resources for applications development through means such as outsourcing [40], the extent of IS resources devoted to application development is potentially an important indicator of the extent to which IT is being leveraged for competitive purposes. It is not clear whether this and other similar factors need to be incorporated in the index.

Thus, evidence regarding the index's lace validity is not very persuasive.

## Construct validity

The construct validity of the index is examined by testing the following hypothesis: the financial performance of companies designated to be IS effective by CW will be significantly better than that of a benchmark set of firms. The basis for expecting superior financial performance of IS effective companies are such assertions as: IS resources in these companies “support revenue-production programs” whose emulation “provides a concise yardstick and a path to follow for success” [9]; and Premier 100 companies employ tactics to “make technology pay off” [11]. Another reason is the fact that one of the criteria (though weighted by only 15%) pertains to firm profitability.

Overview of the data analysis

Companies recognized by CW in 1988 and 1988 were designated as IS-effective firms. A set of benchmark companies was then carefully selected based upon two criteria: industry grouping and size of sales (this method is adapted from [35]).

In order to compare the performance of the IS-effective and the benchmark firms, a number of measures of organizational performance were considered. According to Woo and Willard [46], as many as 14 different performance measures have been used by past studies. Measures in the current study assessed the short-term as well as the long-term performance. They were reflections of two important performance dimensions: profitability and growth. Profitability was tapped by two measures: Return on Equity (ROE) and Return on Sales (ROS). Growth measures comprised of: Average five-year sales growth rate (Sales Growth) and Average five-year growth in earnings per share (Earnings Growth).

## Data collection

Forbes' Reports on American industry [19,20] constituted the main source of data for financial performance. It incorporates 1,031 and 1,116 companies for 1988 and 1989 respectively; their minimum annual revenue are \$400 million (\$475 million for banks). Forbes' definition and calculation of each performance measure is as follows:

ROE: primary earnings per share divided by common shareholders' equity per share at the beginning of the year;

ROS: net income as a percentage of net sales; Sales growth and earnings growth: calculated by the least-squares method.

All companies are classified into one of 31 different industry groups. For each industry group, Forbes additionally lists the median value of each performance measure, calculated by listing companies in rank order and selecting the middle value.

The sample of IS-effective companies was obtained from the 1988 and 1989 CW reports. In addition to the 100 premier companies, CW separately lists the top ten IS effective companies in each surveyed industry. For example, in addition to J.C. Penney Co., which is included in the premiere 100 in 1989, nine others are separately mentioned as IS effective companies in the retailing industry. As a result, a total of 136 and 146 firms are designated IS effective in 1988 and 1989 respectively. From this sample, 35 companies from the 1988 data and 20 from the 1989 data were subsequently deleted because they were not included in Forbes' report.

The 101 and 126 remaining CW companies were regrouped by Forbes' industry classification, because it is more comprehensive than CW's industry groupings. However, this classification mandates multiple industry groups for some companies. Specifically, for the 1988 data, 24 firms were included in two industry groups and 6 in three groups; for 1989, 19 companies were included in two industries and 1 in three different industries. Thus, the effective sample size (due to multiple groupings) was 284 companies, 137 from the 1988 and 147 from the 1989 data.

For each of the 284 IS-effective companies, a matched benchmark firm was chosen from the corresponding year's Forbes report. The matched benchmark firm satisfied two criteria: (1) it belonged to the same industry as the IS-effective firm, (2) the difference between its sales and that of the IS-effective firm was the smallest of other firms in that industry. In comparing sales, the percentage of sales a company derives from a particular industry was taken into account. While information about sectoral sales percentages is given in Forbes' reports, no information is provided about sales amounts for 1989. This information was subsequently obtained from another source, the Disclosure/Dialog database.

## Analyses and results

A first analysis compared the performance of IS-effective companies with their industry median. For each performance measure (five in all, including a measure Composite computed as the average of the four measures ROE, ROS, Sales Growth and Earnings Growth), differences were calculated between the score of each IS effective companies and its industry median. Table 1 shows the results of the t-tests which verified if the mean difference along each performance measure was significantly different from zero. The results show that except for Sales Growth, the financial performance of IS-effective companies is significantly better than the median for their industry.

A second analysis compared the performance of the IS-effective firms with that of the benchmark firms. To make the comparison as equitable as possible, the technique used by Ramanujam and Venkatraman [35] was adopted. The benchmark firms were further divided into two groups: high-performing benchmark (H-Benchmark) firms and low-performing benchmark (L-Benchmark)

firms, based upon the median value of the Composite performance measure. Then, using t-tests, the mean score of the IS-effective firms along each performance measure was compared with that of the H-Benchmark (results summarized in Table 2) and that of the L-Benchmark firms (Table 3).

As may be seen from Table 2, four of the five performance measures of H-Benchmark companies are significantly better than those of the IS-effective firms. Even the value of ROS, though not significant statistically, is higher for the H-benchmark than for the IS-effective firms.

On the other hand, Table 3 indicates that the

IS-effective firms perform significantly better than L-Benchmark firms on all the performance measures.

Thus, based on the above results, it can be concluded that the main research hypothesis is not supported. Empirical evidence therefore questions the validity of the index since the relationship of the index with organizational performance does not conform to expectations.

As may be seen from Table 4, IS Budget has a significantly positive correlation with Market Value and with User Access but not with Staff Cost or Training Cost. This suggests that companies spending a larger proportion on their revenues on IS do so more for updating equipment and for acquiring microcomputers/terminals rather than for hiring new IS staff or for training programs. As a sidelight, it indicates a disparity between what the organizations do and what they say; in the most recent Society for information Management survey [32], IS management ranked “Specifying, recruiting, and developing IS human resources” as one of the five most important issues for the next three to five years $^{1}$ .

Further, Market Value is positively associated with Staff Costs. This indicates that companies which have newer, more current technology spend greater amounts on staff salaries and other benefits. It can be reasoned that managing more state-of-the-art technology requires employees with higher qualifications and more experience and therefore employee salaries are higher. Alternatively, staff costs may be higher just because the management and maintenance of more current and up-to-date equipment requires a larger staff. Both these explanations conform with the observations of others such as Niederman et al. [32] who note that the increasing breadth of technologies today calls for greater and more specialized skills. As an aside, an interesting implication is that even strategic IT applications intended to reduce overall organizational costs and enhance efficiency $^{2}$ may increase some costs, such as those associated with IS staff.

Finally, Staff Costs is positively related to Training Costs. One explanation which seems reasonable is that companies with larger staff (and therefore greater spending) spend more on training compared to companies with smaller staffs. However, Staff Costs are a measure of efficiency of personnel spending in CW's definition. According to this interpretation, the positive correlation above implies that the IS staff of companies that invest more in training programs is not as efficient as the IS staff of companies that spend less on training programs! Thus, the observed relationship seems implausible when examined vis-a-vis CW's definition of Staff Costs.

It may therefore be concluded that while the criteria variables satisfy the internal consistency criteria, the meaning and explication of the variable Staff Costs (as proposed by CW) is ambivalent.

## External consistency of the criteria variables

External consistency was assessed by the correlations (shown in Table 5) between the criteria variables and the different organizational performance measures. The expectation was that all associations, except Staff Costs, would be positive.

The results for IS Budget, however, show that it is significantly negatively associated with ROE. Thus, companies spending a larger proportion of revenues on IS see a decline in short-term profitability. The explanation could be that these firms are spending beyond the industry optimal level $[2]$ leading to a loss of profitability. A similar implication is suggested by a recent review, which found that some of the highly profitable and growing companies have been able to keep a tight rein on IS costs, with their overall IS spending less than the industry average [16].

Like IS Budget, User Access is also negatively associated with ROE. It can be reasoned that greater spending on microcomputers/terminals for end users hurts short-term profitability due to the costs involved in its acquisition and learning. Perhaps the benefits occur mostly in the long run, through organizational changes such as enhanced innovativeness, flexibility, and responsiveness.

Unlike these, Market Value is not associated with any of the performance measures. CW's inclusion of this criteria is based on the rationale that new, current technology enhances firm efficiency. Furthermore, a firm's willingness to remain up-to-date with technological changes signifies a commitment to IS. However, such assumptions are not corroborated by the data.

The results for Staff Cost are also in contradiction with the original denotation of this variable. Staff Cost is positively associated with all performance measures, especially ROE. However, it is meant to be an indicator of efficiency which “awards more points for lower staff costs.” Thus, the observed positive (rather than negative) correlations run counter to the original specifications of Staff Cost.

## Summary and conclusion

Computerworld's criteria for IS effectiveness lack a strong theoretical or pragmatic basis. Empirical evidence that the CW index is not related to a firm's financial performance raises further questions about whether the index is actually measuring what it is supposed to - IS Effectiveness. On the other hand, perhaps it is too optimistic, even unrealistic, to expect IS effectiveness to be related to financial performance, because the latter is determined by many other more significant organizational and external factors. However, financial performance measures continue to be widely monitored by senior management [26] and constitute one of the main criteria for evaluating the performance of the IS function [38]. Furthermore, according to a survey of Fortune 1000 companies' CEO's, more than three quarters believe that the benefits of IS are quantifiable [37]. Thus, it is useful to try to discern if IS effectiveness measures proposed in the literature are directly related to financial performance. In fact, such an endeavor (which initially overlooks other factor influencing firm performance) is akin to Kauffman and Weill's [26] recommendation that the organization should work first with more “naive” rather than expanded models. Thus, evidence that there is little direct association between the CW index or its constituents and organizational performance suggests that, contrary to CW claims, much work still remains in the quest for measures that quantify IT’s business value.

CW measures may be deficient because it is too soon to measure IS effectiveness precisely. As pointed out by Kauffman and Weill [26], IT value research is still in its adolescence and thus the emphasis should be on theory building: the focus should be on such activities as identifying the appropriate IT variables, delineating their domain and definition, formulating operational measures, and developing a nomological net to understand their relationship with other constructs. The development of exact yardsticks and norms is best deferred to the future.

One guideline for acquiring a greater understanding of the appropriate IT variables is to use consistent measures. A cumulative tradition of research can be established for each of the three categories of IT measures described in this paper. This would make it possible to compare results across different studies. Furthermore, the relationships between the different IT measures and business performance would help explain how much and why IT creates business value; while measurements using IT investment (or investment components) would indicate how much and measurements using IT components would reveal why IT creates value.

As a part of the cumulative tradition, it may be useful to start to create an inventory of all the IT variables that have a demonstrated impact on business value. Such an endeavor would be similar to broader efforts aimed at compiling an archive of all survey instruments in IS $[31,47]$ . Some of the CW variables that can be included in the inventory are IS Budget, Training Cost, and User Access, since they generally satisfy the measurement criteria. Market Value seems an appropriate variable, but its relationship with performance indicators is not strong. On the other hand, Staff Cost must be completely re-examined vis-a-vis the original definition.

The search for the “best” indicators (of IT for assessing business value) is perhaps futile. Similar efforts have been unavailing in other disciplines which have a much longer history of research. For instance, Cameron [6] notes that there is little consensus on the best indicators in organizational effectiveness research. Therefore, a more constructive strategy is to acknowledge all pertinent variables and recognize their strengths and weaknesses. It is in this vein that this paper has explored the variables proposed by Computerworld.

Some of the interesting sidelights from this research are such results as the disparity between the importance and the extent of resource allocations for IS human resource management, the higher IS staff costs associated with companies having more current hardware and software and the negative organizational performance impact of above-average IS spending.
