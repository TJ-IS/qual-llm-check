# A citation analysis of business computing research journals

Clyde W. Holsapple
University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY, USA

Linda Ellis Johnson
and Herman Manakyan
Western Kentucky University, Bowling Green, KY, USA

John Tanner

University of Southwestern Louisiana, Lafayette, LA, USA

In the field of business computing systems, it is important for both researchers and those who evaluate them to have a clear, up-to-date picture of the forum available for scholarly discourse. Yet, the literature contains no recent, large-scale, objective studies of appropriate publication mechanisms. Here, we employ a citation analysis methodology to (1) examine citation trends for books, proceedings, and journals, and (2) determine a relative ranking of journals concerned with this research. Five base journals were determined by results of previously reported ranking studies. Our data collection produced over 25,000 citations for the years 1987–1991. The journal rankings generated are compared with those of earlier studies, both objective and subjective. Together, these rankings are used to identify core members for two top tiers of journals with each ranking serving as a filter on tier membership.

Keywords: Business computing research; Citation analysis; Information systems publications; Journal ranking; MIS publications

## 1. Introduction

Banville and Landry [1] have argued that “preoccupations about the present and future evolution of MIS as a scientific field” is an appropriate activity for scholars. One way of assessing the nature of a field is to examine the communication system available to scholars in the discipline. Books, journal articles, and conference proceedings serve as primary communication channels for researchers.

Periodically, it is important for researchers to take stock of the present channels available for communicating scholarly discourse and research. Several reasons have been given. Publishing research is a primary mechanism for evaluating an academic's scholarly performance for tenure at many universities. Another reason is that the field of business computing has changed over the past two decades [3,4]. Other reasons include targeting articles to appropriate forums, nurturing doctoral students awareness of journal forums, assessing the needs of practitioners, and reaching consensus on the relative importance of a set of widely used journals. It is important that academics as well as university administrators be familiar with differences among the forums available for publishing scholarly discourse and research.

Two sources that academics and administrators might find useful are Cabell's Directory of Publishing Opportunities in Business and Economics and the Social Science Citation Index (SSCI). However, because the field of business computing is relatively new compared to the more established disciplines in business, such as finance, accounting, marketing, and management, these two sources are insufficient. As a result, they as well as university administrators, may be unfamiliar with the various forums available for publishing business computing research.

The purpose of this article is to assess the relative importance of journals used by academics for research and scholarly discourse. While articles have appeared ranking journals, many have relied upon survey instruments that measure the perceptions of academics (and in some cases practitioners) toward these journals. This study employs a more objective means of ranking journals; it is based on the number of times a journal is cited. Ranking journals using this method is an unobtrusive means of measuring their relative importance. Although the motivation for this study is similar in nature to previous ones, this is different not only in the methodology employed, but the time frame studied. It has been argued that the discipline of business computing has changed in the last decade. This study examines research published between 1987 and 1991.

## 2. Previous studies

Studies have ranked journals publishing research and scholarly discourse in portions of the field of business computing (e.g., MIS and DSS). Table 1 lists several of these in chronological order. This list is not exhaustive, but representative of the diversity of approaches that have been used to examine this research question.

The studies are listed in chronological order because scholars have argued that the scope of research has changed over the past two decades. Since 1980, when Keen called for the building of a cumulative tradition at the First International Conference on Information Systems [9], researchers have investigated the notion of progress and maturation in the field of MIS, the “emergence of MIS as a distinct field of study”, and related research questions.

Of the seven articles, four of these studies employed a subjective methodology for ranking journals. While each of these employed a survey instrument, they differ most notably by the nature of the respondent. That is, CIS/MIS Department Chairmen [6], MIS academics [5,10], and business computing practitioners [16] were surveyed for their perceptions of different journals. A summary of the two using MIS academics has been published in [13]. Criticisms of the methodologies have been summarized in [10]. The studies mainly measure the perceptions of the respondents and provide researchers with one method for assessing the relative importance of journals publishing business computing research.

## 3. Related research in the disciplines

This diversity in methodologies is not unique to the field of business computing. Mabry and

Sharplin [11] have reviewed a number of methods employed in the finance discipline, as well as economics, psychology, management, and regional science. They conclude that, “The most common approach to comparing journal quality, however, is the citations approach.” Citation analysis has also been utilized to examine the role of books and journal article publications in the field of accounting [2].

There is no one “accepted” means of conducting a citation analysis study. A number of approaches appear to be popular. For example, in finance, Mabry and Sharplin [11] were able to collect data by inspecting the SSCI which indexes well-known journals in the field of finance. However, for the field of business computing, utilization of the SSCI would be incomplete, because it does not index many journals considered important by business computing academics and practitioners. Another approach has been to determine the major ten or fifteen journals, and then proceed by collecting data from each reference list in the set of base journals.

In most related business fields, not only are journals ranked, but tiers of journals are established according to some criteria. Establishing tiers (e.g., primary, secondary) is important, because they are often useful for related research efforts. For example, studies in related fields (e.g., finance and management) have investigated whether research is related to teaching performance. Research performance can be measured by quantity of research produced over a time period and the relative importance of journals contributed to by the academic. Pair-wise comparisons based on ranking schemes become infeasible for this type of study and hence, a number of these have relied on tiers [e.g., 12,15].

## 4. Methodology

Prior studies using a citation analysis approach in business computing have relied upon the authors to determine whether an article is related “enough” to the discipline to be included. We sought to avoid potential bias of this kind by carefully selecting journals from which all articles could reasonably be included. Therefore, no subjective method was employed to determine whether a citation was to a source, supporting literature, or a reference for further reading. In order to define a set of base journals, studies were examined to determine whether consistency could be found among existing ranking schemes.

The recent studies were chosen for close examination. MIS Quarterly (MISQ) and Communications of the ACM (CACM) were the top two journals identified in [10]. Decision Support Systems (DSS) and Information and Management (IM) were the top two journals identified in [14]. Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS) and MISQ were identified as the top two journals in [5]. The union of these sets yields a base set of five journals.

Every article's reference list appearing in each of the five journals during the years 1987 through 1991 was examined. Three categories of data were recorded for each year of the study. The number of citations to a journal from each base journal was gathered for each year. Citations to books and conference proceedings from each base journal for each year were tabulated. Dissertations were included in the count of citations to the book category. Technical reports, working papers, and personal correspondence were not included. Other than the inclusion of dissertations, the criterion for including a document as a book was that it be cited with a publisher and a year of publication.

## 5. Results

Interestingly, for the years 1987–1991, there were 27,543 citations to journal articles, books, and conference proceedings collected. Of these, 53.7% were citations to journal articles; these were in 1,366 different journals. Book citations comprised 34.8% and conference proceeding citations amounted to 11.5%. Further analysis of the results are divided into two sections.

Table 2 summarizes contributions made to the totals by the five journals. Variations across the base journals are notable. The reference lists in DSS and CACM have a higher proportion of citations to conference proceedings than the other three base journals. This may be because DSS and CACM emphasize articles that are more “technical” in nature and, hence, rely more heavily on results published in conference proceedings.

Variations in the base journals are also shown in Table 3. This provides the relative rank of the twenty-five most cited journals within the five base journals. Twenty-five journals are listed because these journals received 100 or more cumulative citations over the five year period from all five base journals combined. In essence, we used this as the criteria, because it means, on average, that the journal was cited four times or more in each base journal per year.

Interestingly, there are statistically significant variations (at the 95% confidence level) in the ranking of these journals by each of the five base journals. It was hypothesized that the ranks of the journals were the same. The Wilcoxon signed rank test [8] was performed on the ten pairs to test this hypothesis. The hypotheses were rejected for all pairs at the 95% confidence level except for CACM with respect to several of the other pairs. Therefore, the overall ranking of the journals is subsequently reported without CACM data.

All 1,366 journals were ranked based on the total number of citations they received from the five base journals over the period 1987–1991. Space limitations do not permit this list to be published in its entirety. Therefore, a criterion was established for inclusion. Journals receiving at least 25 citations over the five year study period are ranked in Table 4. The rationale is that on average a particular journal would be cited at least once in each basic journal for each year. It should be noted that citations were collected and recorded as published in the journals' articles. Hence, although Systems, Objectives and Solutions was incorporated into Information and Management, it is listed separately. Similarly, counts for the two distinct journals Data Base (an ACM publication) and Database (an ONLINE, Inc. publication) are as cited, even though some publishers and authors may adopt the convention of concatenating the two words. Such citations would confound the data for these two journals. Tied ranks are listed in alphabetical order.

## 6.1. Comparing studies

Table 8 compares the ranking with six other rankings from previous studies. All included CACM in their data collection efforts. In the interest of brevity, a journal had to meet either of two criteria to be included: it must have received more than 100 citations or it must have been ranked in at least a third of the seven rankings.

Two studies are not included. The survey of practitioner perceptions $[16]$ is excluded for two reasons: our focus here is on discourse among scholars and that study ranked a mere seven publications. There appears to be a consensus with respect to the importance of the top four journals. These were ranked in each of six studies. It is quite possible that the importance or impact of relatively new or specialized journals is understated.

## 6.2. Establishment of tiers

An alternative to a fully ordered ranking of journals is to assign them to tiers. One means of establishing tiers is to use citation patterns. Hamilton and Ives [7] implicitly argued for the establishment of tiers by reporting cumulative frequencies in their study covering the years 1970–1979, as Gillenson and Stutz [6] did explicitly by noticing “breaks” in the data collected by their survey instrument. Here, we introduce a broadbased approach to establishing tiers.

The diversity of ranking methodologies and results is clear. The four subjective studies cover the opinions of selected deans, department chairpersons, and academics. With respect to the latter, there are rankings of journals both as knowledge sources and channels for knowledge dissemination. The three objective studies range from a ranking of 20 journals based on 1970s citations to our ranking of 1,366 journals based on citations up to 1992. The seven rankings constitute a diverse set of filters for screening journals into tiers. Journals that pass through many of these filters with noteworthy ranks can be regarded as the core of a first tier. Those passing a less demanding screening make up the core of a second tier, etc. The user of resultant tiers is free to supplement a core with other (e.g., newer, specialized) journals of particular interest.

To establish tiers using this broad-based approach, we need to select screening protocols. There are numerous options for this; for example, a score could be calculated for each row (e.g., average rank, rank total), subject to determining a fair treatment of nr entries. Here, we adopt a very simple screening protocol for the first tier: accept each journal that has achieved a top 25 placement in over half of the rankings. The resulting journals are shown in Table 9. This is a quite rigorous criterion, in that it includes fewer than 20 of the nearly 1400 journals identified by the seven studies'. This criterion characterizes the longevity, visibility, usefulness, and importance of journals across a broad range of subjective and objective viewpoints.

To identify a second tier of journals, we accept those that achieve a top 25 placement in at least one-fourth of the rankings, or that simply appear in at least one-third. Certainly, there are well-regarded and well-known journals not appearing in either tier. Some of these journals are of more recent vintage and placement of these newer journals into one of the two tiers may arise over time as the journals are established and newer

## 6.3. Exclusion of CACM data

As previously noted, CACM appeared in the top five of all ranking schemes previously reviewed. However, it is often cited as a computer science journal. Over 27% of the journal citations tabulated came from articles in CACM. Because its scope extends well beyond business computing, including it may confound the results; Table 10 ranks 25 journals receiving the most citations with the CACM data excluded. The previous rank of these journals is found in the right-hand column. All top twenty journals in the prior ranking are still in the top twenty-five of this ranking. There is not a statistically significant difference in the ranking including all five base journals and the ranking excluding the CACM data based on a Wilcoxon signed rank test.

## 7. Conclusions

The body of knowledge comprising a discipline is most poignantly manifested through the communication channels available for reading and publishing research and scholarly discourse. This study differs from previous studies in that no subjective means was employed to assess whether an article or a particular citation should be included. Citations to journal articles, books, and conference proceedings were counted for all those that were published in the five base journals over a five year time period. Academics, administrators, practitioners, and doctoral students are impacted by changes in these channels. Variations across a set of base journals as well as variations across years were examined.

\- Journals that are more technical in nature seemed to rely more heavily on material from conference proceedings.

\- Over the years 1987–1991, a decreasing trend in citations was found for books.
