# End-user control environments and the accounting managers' perceived quality of the applications \*

Richard L. Jenson \*\*

Utah State University, Logan, UT, USA

The availability of powerful application development tools has raised important questions about the extent to which control can and should be administered over end-user developers. This study examines the relationships among four factors hypothesized to be relevant to the control of end-user computing and the quality of such applications. The variables examined were end-user application quality, the extent of management concern about end-user computing issues, the extent of the organizational control environment, and the impact of resulting applications. Management concern about end-user control was shown to have a positive linear relationship with application impact, control environment, and application quality. There was no support for the hypothesis that a more extensive control environment is related to higher application quality.

Keywords: End-user computing; End-user developed applications; Application impact; Application quality.

## 1. Introduction

It has been suggested that there are four reasons for the virtually exponential growth in end-user application development. First, users have become increasingly aware of the potential benefits of end-user computing. Second, technological advances have made computer resources more accessible. Third, increasingly difficult decision environments require a more compressed timeframe for information gathering. Finally, end-users have needs that are not satisfied through the traditional information systems (IS) organization [9].

Prior to the advent of fourth generation languages and microcomputers, application development responsibility resided primarily with technically oriented IS analysts and computer programmers. The technical knowledge needed to program applications in the mainframe environment presented a barrier to such activities for most end-users. Furthermore, access to the IS technology was restricted to data processing personnel. While many notable advances have been made in the mainframe and minicomputer environment, many system end-users continue to feel that the highly structured and centralized approach is unresponsive to their needs.

While many modern software tools may empower the end-user to develop sophisticated applications without use of the centralized systems staff, it is apparent that many end-user applications lack attention to control considerations, such as data backup, systems security, and program documentation $[4]$ . Moreover, the general decentralization and downsizing of computing operations has introduced computer security concerns including program and database integrity, and unauthorized access to company data and programs.

Although many researchers would appear to support the normative view favoring organizational management and control of end-user computing [1], there appear to be some practical limitations on it. In one study, attempts to implement rigid controls – such as independent review by a data processing professional – were seen as self defeating [8]; in fact some controls may work against the primary benefit of end-user computing, such as faster development. Similarly, a case study [3] states that user employees believed that getting a system operational was the most important part of the development process while ignoring planning, controls, standards, and documentation. Indeed, if there were more controls there was less user satisfaction. In a study to assess the types of organizational end-user policies and the degree of end-user satisfaction toward such policies, it was concluded that end-user policies should not hinder improvements to organizational processes. It was suggested that a company should only formulate policies that have potential for facilitating organizational growth. The company should allow end-users to participate in the formulation of such policies and also share responsibility with management for their enforcement [6].

## 2. Conceptual basis

While studies have examined selected control procedures practiced in end-user environments [2], none have empirically addressed the relationship between end-user control environments and the perceived quality of end-user developed applications (EUDA). Specifically, this study attempts to answer the questions:

1. To what extent are managers concerned about certain risks associated with end-user computing activities? The specific risks explored here are:

(a) unwarranted reliance on computer output

(b) lack of data security

(c) wasted application development resources

2. How do managers feel about the quality of EUDA developed within their organizations?

3. To what extent do organizations establish controls over EUDA?

4. What impact do EUDA have on the operations of an organization?

The variables are management concern about the risks of end-user computing, the impact of EUDA on company operations, the extent of the control environment pertaining to EUDA, and the perceived quality of EUDA.

Management Concern. Here, the risk associated with EUDA is viewed in three broad dimensions: (a) concern for integrity and reliability; (b) concern for preserving data security; and (c) concern for the efficient use of consumed resources.

Application Impact. The EUDA impact may be measured in two dimensions: scope, and organizational importance. Scope refers to the breadth of the application, from single-user, to departmental, or multi-departmental application. As the scope of an application broadens, the consequences of programming errors become more severe. Organizational importance refers to the criticality of EUDAs to the operations of the organization. This is somewhat of a departure from the taxonomy of Rockart and Flannery [9], who suggest that EUDAs be classified by purpose, such as operational, report generation, or inquiry and analysis. Similarly, Sipior and Sanders [10] characterized EUDAs by their function, such as transaction processing, planning and control, or decision support. The suite of applications considered critical for success could include any or all of these types. A payroll application might be characterized as a routine operational system, but errors in this application might cause more damage than a poor high level management decision due to programming errors in a DSS.

Control Environment. Alavi and Weiss [1] and others believe that management must take responsibility for controlling the end-user computing environment by establishing corporate policies. In this study, an attempt is made to measure the extent to which management exercises control over EUDAs. Application approvals, development guidelines, training in the proper use of end-user computing technologies are incorporated into the control measure. Also, an attempt is made to capture the extent to which EUDAs undergo post-development evaluation and testing.

Application Quality. The quality construct is also measured in three dimensions. The first dimension is application integrity: the application should be correctly specified and programmed. The second is data security: EUDAs should not allow data loss, unauthorized data access, change or disclosure. Finally, the EUDA must be cost-effective.

## The hypotheses

Certain hypotheses about the relationships among variables were tested. These included:

H1: A positive relationship exists between management concern and control environment.

Controls should raise the level of awareness and concern of managers about control issues and maybe managers with higher risk awareness and concern will push for more controls.

H2: A positive relationship exists between the organizational impact of end-user computing and management concern.

This suggests that managers will be more concerned about issues of application quality when such applications have greater potential impact on the organization. Similarly, integrity problems with mission critical software will potentially lead to more damage.

H3: A positive relationship exists between the control environment and application quality.

When an organization exercises a higher degree of control over EUDA, the quality of the resulting applications should be higher. Also, training and support for end-user developers should increase the quality of applications.

## 3. Methodology

## The sample

Questionnaires were mailed to 97 individuals selected from the directory of a state chapter of a society of management accountants. In order to maintain the homogeneity of the sample, only those individuals in management accounting practice were included; individuals in public accounting, educators, students, and retirees were excluded. Because the questionnaire attempts to measure variables from an organizational perspective, only one individual was selected from each organization. Of the questionnaires mailed, 50 were returned in usable form: a net response rate of 51.5%.

The use of management accountants as respondents in this study can be supported on the following grounds: (1) they interface with virtually all functional departments in the course of their data gathering activities and therefore have a unique opportunity to see many of the types of EUDA applications developed within the organization; and (2) they have a significant control awareness resulting from their training and experience. Management accountants should therefore be sensitive to the control implications of EUDAs.

Of the organizations represented in the sample, 42 had an information systems department. Table 1 shows the size of the organizations.

## Variable measurements

The questionnaire consisted of four multiple-item scales constructed for the study variables. Five-point Likert-type scaling was used. The score for each variable was computed by averaging the responses within the scale for each respondent.

Control Concern was measured by asking the extent to which management was concerned with: the integrity of EUDAs; data security; and the expenditure on EUDAs.

## Data analysis

The significance of the sample mean for each study variable was analyzed using individual two-tailed t-tests of the null hypothesis: mean = 3 (a neutral response on a 5-point scale). Hypotheses were tested by computing Pearson Product Moment Correlation Coefficients. Because all hypothesized relationships are directional, significance levels are for one-tailed tests.

## 4. Results

Individual t-tests of the sample means for each variable resulted in all four instances in rejection of their respective null hypotheses that the mean score is not different from 3 (i.e., a neutral response). Of these, only control environment had a mean less than three (2.42).

The correlation matrix for study variables is presented in Table 4. The first hypothesis (H1), in null form, is that there is no linear relationship between management concern and control environment. This hypothesis is rejected (r = 0.45, p = 0.00) and it can be concluded that a positive relationship exists between management concern and control environment. Hypothesis H2, which in null form states that there is not a linear relationship between application impact and management concern, is also rejected (r = 0.32, p = 0.01); this provides support for the existence of a positive linear relationship between application impact and control environment. Finally, the hypothesis that a positive linear relationship does not exist between control environment and application quality (H3) is not rejected (r = 0.03, p = 0.41). Therefore, the data do not support the existence of a linear relationship between end-user controls and application quality. However, a linear relationship appears to exist between application quality and management concern (r = 0.27, p = 0.03).

## 5. Discussion and implications

Although individual t-tests indicated that each sample mean was statistically different from a neutral response, these results must be interpreted with caution. First, it cannot be assumed that respondents equated a “3” response to neutrality for each scale. Secondly, it cannot be assumed that the sample means provide a basis for comparing relative response strength. Third, the sample is relatively small. Nevertheless, organizational impact had the highest mean response (3.77) and control environment had the lowest mean response (2.42). It would appear that although the end-user environments surveyed were not perceived as being highly controlled, managers were moderately confident of the quality of EUDAs produced.

The positive relationship between management concern and control environment suggests that the control environment may serve to raise the awareness of managers about control issues within the organization. As managers are educated about such control issues it is conceivable that a feedback effect occurs where managers suggest additional control procedures. This would suggest that educating user managers in end-user control issues may help win support in the control effort.

It appears that managers are more sensitive to end-user control issues when applications have a greater organizational impact. This is encouraging because it is important that the organization identify high impact EUDAs and ensure that they meet an acceptable level of quality.

However, there is no support for the hypothesis that a more highly controlled end-user development environment leads to better quality applications. Although this result appears contrary to conventional wisdom, it is not necessarily surprising. For example, Bergeron and Berube [5] found a negative correlation between the number of end-user policies and user satisfaction. One of the appealing features of end-user computing, at least from the end-user's standpoint, is that the developer no longer has to rely on the information systems department or be subject to their policies and procedures. Moreover, attempts to impose stringent controls on end-user developers are likely to reduce their incentive to develop new and innovative applications. Such a scenario might then contribute to lower quality applications. An alternative to stringent controls might be higher levels of support and training.

Care must be exercised in attempting to generalize the above findings. This study implicitly assumes that management accountants can make an unbiased assessment of the organizational control factors examined. Clearly, the possibility of such bias cannot be dismissed. It is also possible that the end-user computing environments influence the types of EUDA. It may be more natural for an accountant as an application developer to satisfy the three dimensions of application quality measured in this study (correctness, data security, and cost-effectiveness) than for another type of end-user developer with less structured application requirements.

## 6. Conclusion

Because of the pronounced trend toward decentralization and end-user developed applications, it is imperative that management recognize the risks that accompany this trend. This study suggests that while EUDAs are seen as making a significant impact within the organization, they are not perceived as being subject to stringent controls. Greater concern by management for EUDA risks is positively linked to higher levels of control. Similarly, EUDA impact is positively linked to management concern about EUDA risks. Nevertheless, this study provides no evidence that tighter controls lead to higher perceived application quality.
