# An organizational communications perspective on knowledge-based mail systems

Luvai Motiwalla

University of Hartford, West Hartford, CT, USA

Milam Aiken

University of Mississippi, University, MS, USA

Knowledge-based mail systems (KMS) have been developed to surmount the communication barriers of traditional electronic mail systems. However, little attempt has been made to ground these systems in organizational communication (OC) theory. We have reviewed 14 existing KMSs; they provide a framework for classifying these systems in terms of the mechanistic, psychological, systems interaction, and interpretive-symbolic perspectives in OC theory. This framework provides a basis for resolving e-mail usage problems.

Keywords: Organizational communications; Intelligent E-Mail systems; Organizational coordination; Distributed office information systems; Knowledge-based systems; Expert systems

## 1. Introduction

The integration of computer and communication technologies has increased the range of coordination and communication tools in organizations, one of which is electronic mail (or e-mail). An e-mail system is a computer-based system in which the sender transmits electronic information to the receiver without any intervening physical (hard-copy) document delivery [5]. E-mail systems have become an attractive means for inter- and intra-organizational communication because they offer speed, globality (geographical independence of the sender and receiver), asynchrony (sender and receiver are decoupled), and computer processing power [22]. That is, an electronic message can be communicated instantaneously from any place, at any time, and to any person without worrying about their physical location and presence.

Although organizations welcome the increased use of this new communication medium, like other new technologies, e-mail does not come without its bagage of problems. A meta-analysis study on the usage of all electronic media [26] places e-mail in the middle of an information richness/social presence scale because it can transmit very few non-verbal cues. This has resulted in usage problems such as, e-mail being used for exchanging routine information $[7,27]$ like announcements that require quick and massive distribution. This phenomenon also explains the increased demand for message distribution lists $[18]$ which provide an easy mechanism for creating electronic junk mail $[9]$ .

A review of studies on the impact of e-mail in organizations reveals that at least two reasons have hindered e-mail's growth as a communication tool. First, e-mail has a low medium richness because it can transmit very few non-verbal cues [26]. Second, e-mail can cause information overload [13]. The latter occurs because e-mail technology facilitates quick and massive distribution of a message to other people with little extra effort. These have motivated two divergent streams of research in the computer and information science disciplines with a common objective of enhancing e-mail technology to overcome usage problems.

To overcome the low medium richness problem, some researchers are focusing on multimedia e-mail systems, such as the Andrew Message System $[2]$ and ATOMICMAIL $[3]$ which allow users to increase the richness of their messages by incorporating graphic images, voice, and executable programs. On the other hand, to overcome the information overload problem some researchers are focusing on intelligent e-mail systems for organizational coordination and communications, such as knowledge-based mail system (KMS). A KMS can assist users in message processing by filtering the e-mail messages automatically. Furthermore, it can automatically prioritize and categorize incoming messages, suggest potential recipients of messages, and perform a variety of other tasks such as routing, alerting, responding, and delaying mail messages. A KMS uses intelligence derived from knowledge about the organization, end users, or even a combination of resources.

We reviewed a sample of 14 KMSs reported in the literature to present a framework to evaluate the underlying communication theory used by these KMSs. Because KMSs deal with the general problem of minimizing human information overload through better communications in organizations, we are anchoring our analysis to OC theory, which deals with the study of human information processing and the development of shared meaning through gatekeeping activities within a context of formal organizations.

## 2. Organizational communication (OC)

According to Krone et al. [16], the multi-disciplinary characteristics of human communication research makes it hard for researchers to articulate the theoretical frameworks underlying their work. Moreover, they suggest that scholars have conflicting perceptions of the essence of communications and generally fail to explain the underlying perspectives and assumptions of their research.

To avoid the problem of conflicting perceptions, Krone et al. [16] have categorized communication research into four major perspectives: (1) mechanistic, (2) psychological, (3) interpretive-symbolic, and (4) systems-interaction. Each perspective models the process of communication from a relatively distinct point of view and makes its own special contribution to the understanding of human communication in organizations.

1. The mechanistic perspective suggests that clarity of the communication channel (medium) can be improved by reducing noise, breakdowns, and other communication barriers. Similarly, fidelity (accuracy) of messages can be improved by providing organizational gatekeepers to filter messages and exercise control over information flow to all subsequent recipients. A KMS anchored in the mechanistic perspective can play the role of an organizational gatekeeper by filtering messages and coordinating their distribution. It can use organizational structural knowledge and human resource databases to assist e-mail users in identifying their message recipients through the content of the message.

2. The psychological perspective suggests that an individual's attitude, cognition, and perceptions or "conceptual filters" play an important role in organizational communication. An individual can use a variety of conceptual filters, depending on the informational requirements and stimulus from the environment. A KMS anchored in the psychological perspective can play the role of an individual gatekeeper by assisting users in filtering their personal in-box messages. It can use the knowledge, captured in the form of rules or preferences, to sort, prioritize, or take some action on behalf of the user.

3. The interpretive-symbolic perspective suggests that role-taking and shared meaning play an important role in organizational communication. Role-taking consists of forming a mutual understanding through empathic bonding or social interaction with others. It also leads to the creation of shared meanings for common events and actions." [16]. A KMS anchored in this perspective can play the role of a coordination mechanism by sharing information filtering knowledge among the organizational, the group, and the individual user levels. It can segregate knowledge jurisdictions to determine what knowledge should be used to filter messages. In addition, the KMS should encourage role-taking and shared meaning through flexible and dynamic communication and coordination structures.

4. Finally, the systems-interaction perspective suggests communication is dependent upon external behaviors and patterned sequential behavior; that is, the grouping of sequences of communicative behavior. This perspective conflicts with the psychological or mechanistic perspectives because it assumes that sequential patterns are more important than individual cognition, message fidelity, or channel clarity. A KMS anchored in this perspective can play the role of a facilitator, by allowing users to distribute messages on paths that are determined dynamically, based on the reaction of message recipients. Thus, the reaction of a message recipient decides the message's subsequent recipient and this perspective facilitates the integration of sender and recipient knowledge sources.

## 3. KMSs from the four OC perspectives

In this section, we review a sample of 14 KMSs. Based on the description of the system, we have categorized them in one or more of the OC perspectives. The sample was gathered by convenience, because we know of no comprehensive list of KMSs. In addition, when the knowledge source was obscure, we looked at the role played by the KMS in filtering information to make our judgement.

1. COKES: The Carleton Office Knowledge Engineering System [15] consists of a network of cooperative knowledge-based office assistants that perform complex office tasks, such as preparing a report or budget in conjunction with an e-mail system. The complex tasks are performed by multiple knowledge-based assistants using organizational structure, procedure, and employee profiles. These assistants work cooperatively through active messages, such as requesting compilation of monthly report from another assistant. Because COKES uses a common body of organizational knowledge to serve as a general message gatekeeper, it is classified as using the mechanistic perspective.

2. COORDINATOR: The Coordinator [10] is a collaborative work system that provides a facility for generating, transmitting, storing, retrieving, and displaying messages in a conversation format which consists of mechanisms that allow easy action/reaction and which store the sequential patterns of conversations. The Coordinator is based on language/action theory which assumes that human beings are linguistic beings and that their behaviors or actions are regulated externally in the form of requests and promises from others. Thus, the locus of communication in the Coordinator is patterned sequential behavior; i.e., it concentrates on the categories, forms, and sequential patterns of message behavior and this classifies the system as using a systemsinteraction perspective.

3. EDMAS: The Eden Mail System [1] is structured as a collection of objects, such as MailMessages, MailBoxes, and MailLists that provide the user with an automatic facility to suppress duplicate messages, maintain automatic distribution list, enhance security by limiting access to messages depending on authority level, etc. This list of functions suggests that EDMAS acts as an organization level gatekeeper and regulating the flow of message through the intelligent objects to all subsequent recipients. Thus, EDMAS is classified as using the mechanistic perspective.

4. EXECOMS: The EXpert Electronic Communication System was developed to model electronic mail management at a academic institution [12,23]. Whereas most of the KMSs listed here focus on incoming messages, this system concentrates on outgoing messages. It is used in conjunction with any of the other KMSs. The EXECOMS knowledge base represents relationships by a organizational profile template and rules governing policy constraints. The key characteristic of this system is that the organizational rules governing information flow are centralized. Because the major focus is on channel clarity through organizational level gatekeepers for message coordination, EXECOMS system is classified as using the mechanistic perspective.

5. I-LENS: Information Lens was developed to facilitate the receipt and dissemination of mail messages [20]. This system allows users to register their personal profile of rules so that I-LENS can sort, filter, and prioritize messages as well as locate useful messages that the user may not otherwise have received. The knowledge source used for this system is conceptual filters or human cognitive preferences in the form of rules to sort and categorize messages and therefore the I-LENS system is classified as using the psychological perspective.

6. IMAIL: The Intelligent Mail Filter system [4] uses the concept of user profiles that are matched with the keywords extracted from the bulletin board (a form of e-mail) messages to compute a total score for each message. The user profiles consisted of keywords and their synonyms, relations between keywords, and numeric weights for each keyword. The knowledge source includes user defined profiles which provide cognitive preferences for filtering messages; thus the system is using the psychological perspective.

7. IMS: The Intelligent Message System adds information filtering intelligence to messages by attaching scripts consisting of a series of questions such as, "Can you suggest any additions to the recipient list?" to the user whose answer triggers a processing action [14]. As sender-specified programs, scripts can execute in the receiver's environment, query the receiver, reroute the message, or report back to the sender. The knowledge source of the system is the use of scripts that concentrate on external behaviors (recipient actions) and patterned sequential behaviors of e-mail users. Therefore, IMS is classified as using the systems-interaction perspective.

8. ISCREEN: The ISCREEN system [25] uses a message filtering (or screening) approach similar to I-LENS, but it focuses on increasing the accuracy of the personal knowledge through a better verification scheme, such as consistency checks and conflict resolution. The knowledge source used for this system, like I-Lens, is conceptual filters or human cognitive preferences in the form of rules to sort and categorize messages, and it therefore is classified as using the psychological perspective.

9. KMMS: The Knowledge-based Message Management System [6] uses a linguistic message filter and a expert system to process mail messages automatically. The linguistic filter plays the role of organizational gatekeeper by making use of message (channel) frequency, length, relevance (message fidelity) as determined from message and user profile, and network traffic to classify messages into J for junk messages, G for general processing mail, and I for messages requiring immediate action. These categories are used to coordinate the flow of messages to subsequent users. Therefore, the linguistic component is classified as using the mechanistic perspective. The second component, an expert system consisting of personal rules to triggers actions on in-box messages, such as database retrievals, office and filing activities or message categorization actions. These include express\_in,"express\_out," and "call\_express" for message prioritization action. This component of the KMMS is classified as using the psychological perspective, as it uses conceptual filters of individual users.

10. MAFIA: The MAil Filter Agent [18] filters information at two levels. At the first, messages are classified organizationally using the conceptual structure definition methodology for pattern-directed storage, retrieval, and distribution of information. Because this component deals with the issue of message fidelity and gatekeeping (filtering) which occurs at the organizational level, it is classified as using the mechanistic perspective. At the second level, message filtering is performed through user-defined rules or conceptual filters based on user cognition and social relationships with other communicating partners. The second level is therefore classified as using the psychological perspective.

11. MAIL-MAN: A knowledge-based MAIL assistant for MANagers [24] integrates e-mail with office applications to provide comprehensive communication support in a variety of managerial tasks, such as environmental scanning, incoming message management, message dissemination management, document routing, computer supported cooperative work, activity planning, project coordination, task management, alerting, and meeting scheduling. The system derives its knowledge from two sources: individual cognitive preferences and procedures to filter incoming messages (that means that MAIL-MAN is using the psychological perspective) and organizational structure, communication policies and procedures, and employee profiles to identify message recipients and select subsequent message recipients (by focussing on the clarity of the communication channel through organization level gate keeping, MAIL-MAN is also classified as a mechanistic perspective).

12. O-LENS: Object Lens provides intelligent work agents as objects that can be used by individuals for information sharing and coordination [17]. Its intelligent agents can trigger information filtering actions; such as searching a database for information or reminding the user of deadlines. A collection of such intelligent agents forms a pattern of coordinated behaviors whereby individual users can either specify their own rules to trigger actions or users can inherit rules from objects shared with other users. Thus, the knowledge source is a set of objects triggering sorting and filtering actions that can be shared among users through message passing or file sharing. This causes responses based on role-taking and shared meaning derived symbolically through consensual interpretation of shared objects and therefore O-LENS is classified as using the interpretive-symbolic perspective.

13. MMS: The Message Management System [21] integrates e-mail with a database management system to route messages intelligently in an organization. A centralized communication base serves as a repository of all message types and instances of MMS. In addition, it stores profiles of every e-mail user in a role network that represents the relationship between the user and the role(s) he or she plays in the organization. This communication base, managed by an administrator, acts as a organization level gatekeeper and reduces channel noise by removing unwanted messages and other communication barriers, such as finding recipient address by coordinating the flow of message through the routing function to all subsequent recipients. Therefore, the MMS is classified as using the mechanistic perspective.

14. TEIES: The Tailorable Electronic Information Exchange System [28] allows each user on a network to tailor the communication system to his or her needs. A set of intelligent objects (or software functions such as, Directory, Members, Groups, etc.) are provided by TEIES directly to the user, who can control all communication activities such as choice of conference or triggering of automatic message actions. Because, the knowledge source used for this system is conceptual filters or human cognitive preferences in the form of objects to sort and categorize messages, TEIES is classified as using the psychological perspective.

Overall, the incorporation of knowledge-based technology in e-mail offers opportunities in all four OC perspectives. Figure 1 presents the OC perspectives and the respective KMS project names as an example of our framework.

## 4. Discussion

An analysis of the cases, as shown in Table 1, reveals that the majority have taken either mechanistic or psychological perspectives in providing intelligence for organizational coordination and communication. The first encourages message filtering at the organization level using a centralized coordination approach. It ignores the individual and group level communication needs. The second stands at the other extreme, focusing solely on individual communication needs. Using one of the perspectives alone can create unbalanced systems and hence some, such as MAIL-MAN, MAFIA and KMMS, have combined the two. However, even combining them causes filtering to occur either at the top-most or bottom-most level.

Furthermore, combining the perspectives does not solve the usage problems because their underlying assumption is that only organizational or individual characteristics determine the communication process. Both overlook the fact that communication processes can also shape organization characteristics $[8]$ . Therefore, the systems using these two perspectives are not flexible and dynamic in adjusting to constant change in the organizational structure. Such systems are technologically feasible today with the use of object-oriented design approaches, neural networks, and natural language processing.

Further analysis of the location of filtering process is shown in Table 2, which reveals that message filtering is almost equally split between the organizational and personal (individual) level. Organizational level filtering allows coordination of message distribution at the source or sender level, which may be more desirable than coordinating messages at the end or receiver level. In a distributed computing environment, it is economical to process messages at the host level rather than at each computer node. The latter causes redundant processing, which is undesirable. Furthermore, organizational level filtering can reduce electronic junk more efficiently by constraining the proliferation of message distribution lists.

## 5. Conclusion

The linkage of KMSs to OC theory provides a useful and holistic framework to examine the shortcomings of current KMS and for orienting future developmental efforts to support human communication more effectively. Most of the existing KMS take either a mechanistic or psychological perspective to reduce information overload. This is inadequate, because it causes filtering to occur only at the top and bottom levels of the organization. Furthermore, both perspectives encourage modelling of communication systems based on organizational or individual characteristics. This leads to static (snapshot) systems development, with most systems unable to learn or evolve with the dynamic communication structures of the organization. Future KMS should use the systemsinteraction and interpretive-symbolic perspectives because both encourage patterns of coordinated behavior to create, maintain, and dissolve flexible organization structures. For example, Tapestry [11], a collaborative filtering system, filters a message based on the number of responses received. Tapestry assumes the messages receiving multiple responses from eager users are likely to be useful (or interesting) to casual users of the system. Systems such as Tapestry have the capability of allowing users to create, maintain, and dissolve electronic organizations that transcend intra- and inter-organizational communication barriers.

The purpose/benefits relationship is, however, still obscure. For example, within each perspective and organization, what is the estimated improvement in productivity or quality? What is the estimated cost of maintaining the knowledge-base? Organizations should pursue the benefits by focusing on ease of development and maintenance of a KMS.

It is also necessary to pursue reasons why more systems are built in the mechanistic and psychological perspectives than on others. Are these reasons due to ease of implementation? What e-mail usage problems can be solved with the use of other perspectives? Does the mechanistic perspective lead to more centralized systems? or the psychological perspective to more decentralized systems? Could the interpretive-symbolic and systems-interaction perspectives lead to more distributed systems?

In our opinion, a greater emphasis on the interpretive-symbolic and systems-interaction perspectives in intelligent information filtering systems and combining it with multi-media message dissemination capabilities, e-mail technology can overcome the usage problems and become a useful communication tool of the future.
