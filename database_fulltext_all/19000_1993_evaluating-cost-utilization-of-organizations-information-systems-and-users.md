# Evaluating cost/utilization of organizations' information systems and users

Israel Borovits

Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv, Israel

Ran Giladi

Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer-Sheva, Israel

The cost/utilization evaluation model of Borovits and Ein-Dor [6] is extended to include the users of the information system, thus enabling all overall organizational viewpoint of cost/utilization. The utilization evaluation process is segmented vertically and horizontally: three levels of utilization evaluation are defined vertically – the computer system, the information system, and the organization – and three major components of the information system are recognized horizontally – hardware, software, and users. The users are further subdivided into “peopleware” and endusers. Adopting this segmentation allows the use of numerical indices and graphical representations that make it easy to identify insufficient use of resources, bottlenecks, and suboptimized resource sharing from an overall organizational viewpoint.

Keywords: Performance evaluation; Cost/utilization; System balance; End users; Peopleware; Organizational information systems.

## 1. Introduction

Traditionally, information systems (IS) were judged first by their hardware components, and only after this by their software components. In the 1960s, IS were portrayed as hardware systems; however by the 1970s the software component had become important, both in terms of cost and success factors, and in the 1980s a clear dominance of the software component emerged [3]. There is, however, another important component of IS that has not yet received the attention it deserves – the users. Only in recent years have researchers begun to examine the IS from the viewpoint of its users, and to define user characteristics.

It is possible to estimate the value of the information system as a function of its users [1]. If the system does not meet the user's needs, it may not be used; however, if there is no alternative, or if the manager is sufficiently persuasive, this is an imperative. The extent of use is indicative of the system (e.g., [12], [7] and [14]).

Taking a broad view of information systems, it is possible to divide the information system users into two groups. The first consists of the end-users:

those users for whom the information system was first devised. These users generally belong to the organization served by the information system but are not part of the IS department. They may just manipulate information or they may do some programming for themselves. The second group consists of the programmers. They are part of the IS department and their product is designated for utilization by the end-users. They include the programmers of applications, such as databases, finance systems, DSS, etc. We also include in this group the system engineers, among whom the DBA, system programmers, communications programmers, etc. may be counted. They constitute part of the IS department group and take care of the integral software and hardware systems of the computer, handle maintenance, allocation, etc. This second group will be called “peopleware” here; in a functional organization they represent the IS department, while the another group consists of the other departments and divisions of the organization. With certain variations, this basic division of users is accepted by the majority of researchers. Ein-Dor and Jones [9], for example, define the second group as broken into two components – a work component (operations) in the computer center, and a software development component, not necessarily in the computer center. Division into six categories of users are also to be found [13] but these can also be reclassified into the same two groups.

The purpose of this article is to present a study of involving the inclusion of users of the IS in the evaluation of its performance.

## 2. Evaluation of computer system performance

Vast sums of money and other resources are invested in computer system acquisition, in IS organization, and in improving the efficiency of organizations by means of information technology. It is imperative, therefore, to avoid waste of these resources due to problems of compatibility and effectiveness, efficiency and optimality, and differences between manufacturers. Hence, the questions that inevitably arise are, for example: What is the maximal limiting capability of a particular system? What is its utilization? What is the actual system performance?

The evaluation of performance, resource utilization, and cost of both computer and information systems in an organization can be considered on several levels, as defined by Borovits [4] (with some modifications):

a. the organizational level;

b. the computer center level (the IS);

c. the computer system level;

d. the job level.

While the job level is of interest to the user who forms a view of the system, this is not necessarily of interest to the organization. From the point of view of the user, job effectiveness involves proper performance, and efficiency – the range of options and information that the user can obtain from the IS plus the performance speed, frequency, etc. It is possible to generalize and say that the job level can be examined with tools used in research on information value evaluation [2]. But it is important to distinguish between evaluation of the information itself and evaluation of the IS – its performance and utilization.

Performance of computer systems can be evaluated by qualitative and quantitative measures. The three classes of quantitative performance indices (productivity, responsiveness and utilization) are mutually correlated by performance models $[10]$ , $[11]$ . The qualitative measures, and the productivity and responsiveness quantitative performance indices are not treated here directly. They are reflected, though, in the usage of the information system as a whole, and they can be disregarded in the context of the users and the IS usage in the organization.

Utilization of computer systems can be evaluated by measuring the various components by means of software or designated hardware tools. Many models have been developed to describe the connection between system utilization and efficiency, among them the KIVIAT diagrams and cost/utilization analysis. Such models may be conveniently used for determining the utilization of each of the three levels (the organization, the IS, and the computer itself) and they can include the effects of IS components; i.e., hardware, software, peopleware, and end-users.

## 3. The cost / utilization model

Borovits and Ein-Dor developed a cost/utilization model for evaluating the basic level, that is, the computer system level [6]. This may be conveniently used for presenting system efficiency in terms of cost and component utilization, and for identifying system bottlenecks and determining their economic implications, as well as for locating overuse or underuse throughout the system, balance in system use, etc. The model involves computer performance as a combined independent function of hardware components, their cost and utilization. This model has been extended to handle software elements in a similar fashion, by weighting the software components in accordance with their planned proportion of the total system software and their use [5]. It is possible, moreover, to extend this model to include the peopleware components, in accordance with their cost and numbers as a proportion of the two totals. The justification for this extension on the basis of cost is the fact that the peopleware component characteristically comprises 30–50% of information system budgets, as shown in annual statistics and research reports over the years [8].

The first model, which describes cost/utilization of computer hardware components, is based on two indices - balance and utilization. The utilization index is simply a weighted average of the utilization of each hardware component by cost:

$$
F = \sum_ {i} P _ {i} U _ {i},
$$

where $P_{i}$ is the cost of the ith hardware component, and $U_{i}$ is the use/utilization of this hardware component.

The balance index measures the weighted variance of the hardware component usage by cost:

$$
B = 2 \left[ \sum_ {i} \left(F - U _ {i}\right) ^ {2} P _ {i} \right] ^ {1 / 2},
$$

where F is the utilization index, and $P_{i}$ and $U_{i}$ were the same as those defined for the utilization index. Thus as F increases (it ranges from 0 to 100%), system utilization is greater and more efficient. Similarly, as B decreases (it ranges from 0 to 1), the system becomes more balanced.

An advantage of this model is the manner in which it may be represented graphically. If, for example, terminals represent 50% of total hardware cost, memory represents 33%, CPU 13% and the disk 4% of total hardware cost, then it is possible to represent the utilization of each item (use divided by capacity), along the relative cost "axis", in order to weight the hardware component utilization, as depicted in Figure 1.

Here, the memory is 60% utilized, the CPU 40%, the disk 75%, and the terminals 50%; thus F = 53% and B = 0.157. Those measures indicate an average Cost/Utilization of 53%, and a nearly balanced system; i.e. one with no irregular use of the system in the sense that a significant component is overutilized (or underutilized).

This model serves as a basis for the construction of the plan/utilization model for software, wherein the software component cost is replaced by its planned proportion of the total system, or its planned use. This model is also based on two indices – a balance index and a utilization index, though they are calculated in a different manner. The utilization index is a weighted average of the utilization of each software component, according to its proportion of total software components in the planned system:

$$
S = \sum_ {j} \left(Q _ {j} V _ {j}\right) \frac {1}{\Sigma_ {j} Q _ {j} ^ {2}},
$$

where $Q_{j}$ is the planned share of the jth software component in the total software system, and $V_{j}$ is the actual share of that software component in the system (or its utilization).

When there is no use of the software, S = 0 (as all the $V_{j}$ terms are zero). When S = 1, software use is as planned, and when S > 1, software use is greater than planned and, in practice, involves use that exceeds the hardware capacity.

The balance index measures the variance of system software components use from the planned use, weighted by planned use. It is defined as:

$$
C = 2 \left[ \sum_ {j} \left(V _ {j} - Q _ {j}\right) ^ {2} Q _ {j} \right] ^ {1 / 2}.
$$

The balance index varies between 0 for a completely balanced system (usage equals planned use), and 1 for a system in which imbalance is at a maximum. In a completely balanced system, $V_{j} = Q_{j}$ , and then S = 1 and C = 0.

This model contains the implicit assumption that there is a connection between computer hardware cost and software usage (and planned use). A 100% usage of software on computer hardware means there is an optimum use of the computer hardware and therefore for a given cost, as software utilization decreases so does hardware utilization. Hence, the software capacity is constrained by the hardware capacity.

These models share the fact that performance (i.e., actual use versus planned use or capacity) is measured for each system component (hardware or software). In the graphic representation this is the y-axis. However, in weighting the components (the x-axis in the graphic representation), a problem arises. Weighting must be in accordance with the importance, usefulness, worth, or value of the system's component to the organization. But these characteristics are difficult to measure, since they are not objective. It is difficult to compare value of the CPU to that of the DBMS component, or the programmer, or even to the disk. The cost/utilization model weights the hardware components according to their relative costs (which is the actual share of the component in the system), while the part/utilization model weights the software components according to their relative importance (the planned share of the component in the system). Among other possibilities, weighting may be carried out in terms of the value of the output (the valued share of the component in the system).

These two models deal with performance evaluation at one level only – that of the computer system. In the next stage, they are combined, and two performance indices are formulated for the entire system – the system utilization and the system balance index. This, then, constitutes an attempt to evaluate the IS in a limited way without considering peopleware. The system utilization index is a weighted average of the two utilization components (hardware and software), each multiplied by the weight of the hardware cost and software planning components required by the hardware and software. As may be seen from the computation of the averages and variances, the implicit assumption is that these components are independent of one another.

A modification must be made to include users in a manner that will be consistent with the basic hardware model. Thus, the components have to be uniformly weighted with respect to their cost. Two other factors provide support for this approach. The first is the cost attribute, which has totally changed; today the components of software, peopleware, and hardware costs are similar, and the trend seems to be towards dominance of the peopleware costs. The second is the gap that has developed between hardware capacity and software capability. This stems from the fact that inherent in the plan/utilization model, is the implicit assumption that the software is constrained by the hardware on which it is run. In practice, during the past decade we have witnessed the creation of a situation in which hardware power has become virtually unlimited, while the achievements in the area of software technology have lagged far behind. The proposed model is thus a calculation of the use of each of the components and their weight according to cost. In determining software use, it is possible to examine actual software performance in comparison with planned performance, in a definitive way, and hot compared to its proportion of the total software. For example, the use of the DBMS program will be measured by the number of transactions performed on it versus the number of transactions it was required to perform at the time of its acquisition. In addition, as in the original part/utilization model, hardware capacity affects software use, though the reverse may also be true.

## 4. A model for evaluating IS performance

The basis of this model is that of Borovits and Ein-Dor [6], which measures use or output divided by capacity or planned use for each component, weighted by its actual share in the system as a whole. The model includes the hardware (as in the original model), the software, and the peopleware components according to their cost. If the capacity of one part is known, for example the storage volume of the disks, the utilization is simply the ratio of use to capacity. Thus objectives are determined for each component, according to the purpose for which it was acquired or rented – for example, number of transactions, queries, or reports, volume of communications traffic, or quantity of projects or work hours; the utilization is the ratio of the output to the planned value. For the purpose of evaluating the performance of an IS containing hardware, software, and peopleware, the utilization for each of the three separately is:

$$
F _ {j} = \sum_ {i} P _ {i} U _ {i},
$$

where j is 1, 2 and 3, for the hardware, software, and peopleware, respectively; $P_{i}$ is the cost of the ith hardware, software, or peopleware component, depending on which F is being calculated, and $U_{i}$ is the use/utilization of that component (for hardware – use/capacity, for software and peopleware – output/planned value or output/objective). The weighted average of F (the sum of the $F_{j}$ products of the hardware, software, and peopleware and the relative cost of each of the three factors) is calculated, in order to compute the utilization index of the information system as a whole.

The balance index measures the weighted variance of the utilization of the hardware, software, and peopleware components according to their costs:

$$
B _ {j} = 2 \left[ \sum_ {i} \left(F _ {j} - U _ {i}\right) ^ {2} P _ {i} \right] ^ {1 / 2},
$$

where j is 1, 2 and 3, for hardware, software, and peopleware, respectively, $F_{j}$ is the respective utilization index, and $P_{i}$ and $U_{i}$ are as defined previously for the utilization index. In order to compute the balance index of the information system, a further calculation of the balance factor between the hardware, software, and peopleware, is made:

$$
B _ {4} = 2 \left[ \sum_ {j} \left(F - F _ {j}\right) ^ {2} P _ {j} \right] ^ {1 / 2},
$$

where $P_{j}$ is the cost portion of the hardware, software, or peopleware in the cost of the information system as a whole. B, the balance factor of the information system, is obtained from the average of $B_{1}$ through $B_{4}$ , in order to express the imbalances between hardware, software and peopleware, even when each of them is balanced. In accordance with the model, as F increase (F now can be greater than 100%, if the software or the peopleware components utilize more than their planned usage), so does system utilization and efficiency. Likewise, as B decreases the system balance increases.

It is now possible to delineate graphical representations for hardware, software and peopleware. In the software representation, salary-package represents 33% of total software costs and it is fully utilized, the DBMS (Data Base Management System) represents 14% and is 40% utilized, communications software are 5% of all software costs and 120% utilized, and the CIM package (Computer Integrated Manufacturing) is 48% of total software costs and 80% utilized (Figure 2).

## 5. A functional model for evaluating IS at the organizational level

In none of the models to this point has the effect of the user on computer or information system performance been considered, although it is clear that users have a most decided effect on IS performance. Their impact is felt very distinctly first and foremost in the performance of the various software components and then in the hardware components and peopleware. Thus the level of expertise of an end user will determine the performance of the various compilers (and the remaining software tools), while the type of user will affect TPS as compared to DSS program performance.

Several characteristics can be attributed to users and they may be used to examine the effects of users on the IS. Characteristics include user expertise (professionalism, education), user type (development, decision-making, traffic recording), user motivation (satisfaction from using the information system, level of complaints, persistence time, session length), profitability of the IS to the user, etc. A model that attempts to describe the effects of the organization on the IS by determining user characteristics is somewhat troublesome. First, it is hard to quantify how a particular user characteristic influences IS performance (for example, expertise affects performance, but it is not so clear by how much). Second, determination of various user characteristics is difficult for the organization as a whole; e.g., how is level of expertise of an organization determined?

The model for evaluating performance at the IS level has two dimensions - relative cost to the organization (the $x$ -axis), and utilization of the component, (i.e., its use or output divided by its capacity or planned use - the $y$ -axis); for the entire organization - a third dimension is added, that of the organizational departments, divisions, or functions ( $z$ -axis). As for the IS resources, the organization functions are weighted by their importance with respect to their relative annual budget, profitability, or output. The implicit assumption is that each organizational function has a particular and homogeneous type of user; this obviating the need to identify the users, quantify them, or examine the effects of their characteristics. Thus, instead of discussing utilization of the resource for the entire organization, utilization of the resource in the particular function is shown. It appears graphically in Figure 5.

Utilization and balance indices can now be defined for each organizational function separately, using the weighted products of the utilization and the relative cost of the resource to compute utilization, and the weighted variances to compute the balance. In this manner, it is possible to identify organizational functions that are wasteful or have bottlenecks and to allot resources within the organization. In addition, organization-wide utilization and balance indices can be defined, by weighting the utilization by the relative cost of the information system resource and by the relative share of the function in the organization:

$$
F = \sum_ {i} \sum_ {j} P _ {i} S _ {j} U _ {i j},

## 6. Discussion

The scheme allows the IS managers to determine the performance of IS components across organization, and to assess the relative importance of IS projects and efforts. This helps the IS managers in allotting and reallocating resources in the correct organization preferences.

The computed indices afford the IS manager some insight into the usage of the IS in the organization. The static usage might well reflect the overall utilization of the IS resources in the organization, indicating the need to extend them (as $F$ approaches 100%), or the need to educate the organization's users to take advantage of the IS resources (when $F$ diminishes). It might also imply irregularity (hyper or hypo utilization) in the use of IS resources by principal functions of the organization (as B tends to 1).

In the case of a hyper utilized IS resource, say the memory or DBMS package, the IS manager should add or allot such resources, so that their total utilization (actual vs planned output or capacity) will drop to the organization average utilization. In an organization, this might be as simple as reallocating resources, taking them from the hypo utilizing function, and balancing the organization.

The main difficulty with the models lies in the connection between the value of the information and the weighting factors that were employed. Choosing relative cost or relative budget as the weighting factors is one way to determine relative importance, and the values obtained, from both the resource and organizational functions, must be incorporated in this model as preferred weighting factors.

## 7. Conclusion

This article reports a study that has implications for practitioners and researchers. Extension of the Cost/Utilization model makes several important contributions; among them is the possibility of evaluating hardware, software, and peopleware performance separately as well as the overall information system performance on a common basis. The model enables evaluation of IS performance from an organization-wide perspective, and provides tools for comparing different organizational functions while avoiding under-optimization of resources in any given organizational function. The model also enables precise identification of bottlenecks or waste giving expression to economic implications and the importance of rectifying them. Finally, the model makes use of visual tools for perceiving the state of the system and provides analytical indices for purposes of comparison and examination of trends.
