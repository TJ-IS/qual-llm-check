# Knowledge workers' use of support software in Saudi Arabia \*

Mawdudur Rahman

Suffolk University, Boston, MA, USA

Abdullah Abdul-Gader

King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, Dhahran, Saudi Arabia

The study reports results from a survey, in Saudi Arabia, of the use of applications software by knowledge workers. Results indicate that user productivity is positively related to satisfaction and negatively to alienation. Knowledge workers in Saudi Arabia generally use low-end applications software. Expert Systems and 4 GL's are rarely used in a micro-environment. Informal knowledge sources are more significantly correlated with higher proficiency and productivity of knowledge workers than are the formal sources. Computer periodicals are an important information source for successful users. Younger knowledge workers are more productive than the older ones. Sharing with others has a negative effect on productivity. Implications of the use of applications software by knowledge workers in developing countries are discussed.

Keywords: Knowledge worker; Knowledge workers' support software (KWSS); Applications software; Satisfaction; Alienation; Productivity; Saudi Arabia; End-user

The degree of their influence on organizational decision making and performance depends on the nature of their responsibilities. Nevertheless, the quality of decisions made, depends on their ability to process and use information.

The influence of computer technology on knowledge worker's information is indisputable [Drucker, 1988; Galbraith, 1973; Ives and Learmonth, 1984, Cash et al., 1988]. Information manipulation, interpretation, communication, and use constitute major tasks of knowledge workers. Adequate computer skill is indispensable in performing these tasks efficiently and effectively.

Scholars have emphasized the importance of KW's computer efficiency in developing countries like Saudi Arabia. (Bogod, 1979; Tsai and Wei, 1987). As developing countries are expanding industrially and commercially, the volume of domestic consumption, export production and imports is growing at a very high rate. This new phenomenon has created and expanded the demand on the supply and use of information (Abdul Ghani and al-Sakran, 1988). This study provides empirical evidence of the use of the computer in business and industry in Saudi Arabia. The study focuses on variables relating to knowledge, source, environment, roles, and proficiency of the user, and the intensity of use. It also investigates relationships of user productivity with computer alienation and satisfaction.

## Methodology

## Study variables

The of impact on user productivity and user satisfaction is usually said to be as an indicator of the success of computer utilization in business and industry. Research in computer utilization includes many variables to study the nature and extent of computer utilization. Variables selected in this study are primarily derived from studies done by Igbaria (1992), and Abdul-Gader (1990a).

## User productivity

It is assumed that computer applications influence user efficiency and improve knowledge workers' productivity. However, it may affect productivity of different users in different ways. Depending on orientation and background, some users may achieve higher productivity than others. Productivity may also be related to user satisfaction and knowledge level. The facets of user productivity included in this study are: (1) time saving, (2) output volume, and (c) output quality.

To indicate which software, used by a respondent, contributes to his/her productivity, each of the facets was scored on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, with 5 being highest and 1 the least.

## User satisfaction

Some researchers have suggested that user satisfaction with computer applications is an indicator of computer systems success (Cheney, et al., 1986; Montazemi, 1988). Generally, user satisfaction enhances employee morale and reduces turnover and absenteeism. Moreover, a higher user satisfaction in computer applications is essential for a continuous growth in the degree of use and ability in handling complexity. Computer user satisfaction was measured by a 12 item Likert type 5-point scale, as developed by Doll and Torkzadeh (1988) and now used in the international environment.

## Computer use environment

The positive influence of the computer environment on the level of use, user satisfaction, and efficiency is well documented in research studies (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Yaverbaum, 1938). Factors like accessibility, coworkers' expectations, and hardware sharing are considered important in this regard. The environment variables were measured by self-reported single item questions.

## User roles

The level of use and user productivity are related to the roles of the KW's. They may be a simple user or a designer and evaluator of information system. In the business and industry environment, an organization's capacity to process information and handle complexity depends on the user role(s) performed. User roles included in this study, as proposed by Davis (1987) are:

User: Supplier of data or recipient of outputs from predefined applications.

User-developer: Formulator of retrievals, developing information processing routines, and writing processing commands using application software. Large application developer: Active member of a project team for large application development.

Information requirement specifier: Specifier of information requirements in end-user application development.

Data base designer and user: Designer and user of large data bases.

Data and information evaluator: Evaluator of the quality and appropriateness of data.

Information System controller and auditor: Auditor and controller of data processing.

The respondents were asked to select one or more of the above to specify their computer user roles.

## Knowledge workers' support software

Applications software (ASW) used by the KW's are classified as knowledge workers' support software (KWSS). Those included here are: word processing, spreadsheet, statistical package, graphics, modelling software, electronic mail, data base management, audit, desktop publishing, and Arabic software. Lee (1986) in his study of micro computer usage included word processing, spreadsheet, database, graphics, other packaged programs, and own programming as support software.

## Knowledge source

Usually, KW's have access to different media to acquire skills in KWSS. In this study we included: College degree, on the job learning, formal organizational training, and self-training to identify which of the knowledge sources are more effective in enhancing users proficiency and productivity. Users were asked to identify one or more knowledge source they had used for each of the KWSS.

## Information source

The users were asked to rank order seven information sources about computer use, according to their level of importance. These sources are: coworkers, friends, data processing staff, vendors, Computer periodicals, systems manuals, and radio/T.V.. The first six were used by Lee in his survey.

## User proficiency

User proficiency in applications software varies among the users and between the use of different ASW's. User proficiency influences his/her level of use and productivity. It is not necessary for a knowledge worker to possess the same level of skills in all applications software. Though the desired level of skill and the nature of software depend on the job performed, it is also related to knowledge worker's personal orientation towards the applications. As in the knowledge source, the KW's were asked to indicate their level of knowledge in each KWSS by assigning a number from 1 to 4 (1 = None, 2 = Introductory, 3 = Intermediate, and 4 = Advanced).

## Computer alienation

People who have negative attitude towards computer are likely to be low level users. It is important to find out the knowledge workers' attitude towards computer and the affects it has on user productivity. Traditionally, social as well as work alienation have been linked to technology artifacts (Lystad, 1972; Meltzer and Rosow, 1978; Seeman, 1983). It is only recently that an alienation construct about computers has been suggested. Computer alienation is defined as the socially and psychologically induced subjective state of separation from computers. To measure computer alienation, a 22 item psychometrically tested scale has been used (Abdul-Gader 1990b; Minch and Ray 1986, 1987).

## User background information

User background data on age, experience, educational levels, company size, and monthly salary were collected to provide descriptive statistics.

## Sample and procedure

Self-reported data on variables relating to environment, roles, information source, proficiency, knowledge source, and user productivity were collected through single item questions. Alienation and satisfaction data were collected through Likert-type scales previously constructed and validated in other studies. The questionnaire was pretested among a group of 20 part-time M.B.A. students, all of whom were working in business and/or industry. The questionnaire was distributed to managers and professionals in selected companies; these people were users of microcomputers. The questionnaires were distributed through contact persons to KW's who agreed to participate in the study. Most of these were M.B.A. students, but working full time.

A total of 200 questionnaires were distributed and 171 useable responses were received. Of these, 11 non-users responses were excluded from subsequent analyses. Out of 160 respondents 93.3% have college or university degrees. Average monthly salary for the respondents is above SR.7,000 and 84% have a salary greater than SR.5,000, while 17% have more than SR.15,000. A wide range of industries, in size and business, was represented in the sample. However, most (53%) respondents were from oil or oil related industries, and of the remaining, 11% were from banking, 20% from chemical and manufacturing. The functional areas of the respondents included accounting, engineering, marketing, general management, systems, training, production, planning, and purchasing. More than 80% were from engineering, general management, and systems. Table 1 shows descriptive statistics on respondents' demographic profiles.

## Summary and conclusion

The study reports empirical results from a survey of computer utilization in Saudi Arabia. It shows that younger knowledge workers use computers more than the older ones. KW's mainly perform simple user or user developer roles. They primarily use low-end application software for routine work. The most commonly used software include word processing and spreadsheet; DSS are least used. Few users have advanced proficiency in software. Users reported that their most important knowledge sources are self-training and on the job learning, formal training having less impact. Sharing with others adversely affects productivity. It has been observed that, computer periodicals as an information source has significant impact on productivity, skills, and use intensity. Age has negative effect on productivity, while education and use experience have positive effects. User satisfaction is positively related to productivity and computer alienation is negatively related.

Findings can be used to address the issue of overall improvement of knowledge workers' efficiency in developing countries like Saudi Arabia. Special attention should be given to the quality and numbers of in-company formal training. Companies should encourage the use of computer for decision making and train employees in 4GL, SQL, analytical tools, graphics etc. It seems, that younger managers are quick to adapt to the new technology. People with higher education have a better chance of success in computer use and may be preferred for recruitment in positions where this use is essential.

Business and industries in developing countries are competing globally. To meet the challenges from competition, managers must make quick and complex decisions. Timely information is essential to support them. Results from this study may be useful in developing managers' computer skills for business decision making in a competitive environment.
