# Lessons from the 80s The tale of one MIS department

William L. Cats-Baril and Ronald L. Thompson

University of Vermont, Burlington, VT, USA

The MIS department of a mutual insurance company survived rapid and dramatic changes during the 1980s and early 1990s. The external environment of this company went from stable to very turbulent, with many aggressive new competitors, many new products, and plummeting profits. On the MIS side, changes included a new hardware platform and operating system, a new database representation, changes in leadership, a rapid expansion in the use of personal computers and other forms of end-user computing, new software development tools, continuous pressure for new applications, and a shift to stricter fiscal responsibility. After much pain, the MIS department not only survived, but emerged stronger, leaner and a full partner in the strategy making process of the company. This article is a case study that summarizes the issues, challenges and traps of the MIS world in the 80s. It also presents ten lessons to help steer a course through the next decade.

Keywords: Case study; MIS operations; Insurance industry; Strategic alignment

## Introduction

The case presented here describes the evolution of a typical mid-sized information services department during the eighties. It is a story of how an astute manager negotiated what can arguably be described as the most challenging years in the history of managing information systems. The story describes the MIS environment of a mutual insurance company where everything was changing at once. On the one hand, the business went through phases of great profitability, great growth, and great losses. ## Part I: 1981–1989

## The industry

Until 1980, the life insurance industry was relatively stable and growth for many companies was steady, gradual, and well managed. The primary products offered were life, health property/casualty insurance, and annuities. Although some companies specialized in one area or another, many major companies offered all four. The industry was generally perceived as conservative and stodgy, and business was transacted pretty much the same way it had been for years.

In the early 1980's the financial services environment changed rapidly. It was a period of high inflation and high interest rates. The regulatory environment changed. Companies that had traditionally offered investment products began to enter the insurance business. The mid-1980's also saw a rapid increase in new insurance products offering customers a much wider selection of options. The external environment for an insurance company suddenly became very turbulent.

## The company

Headquartered in northern New England since its founding in the mid-1800's, TDIC $^{1}$ ranks among the 25 largest mutual life insurance companies in the United States. The company serves about 250,000 customers throughout the country. Two major competitors which TDIC focuses on are roughly 5 times larger than TDIC.

Although TDIC's primary business is life insurance, it also offers additional products through subsidiaries. One company offers a variety of financial products (including annuities and pension plans), while a second distributes mutual funds. As a mutual life insurance company, TDIC is owned by its policyholders. Earnings are required to meet contractual obligations to customers, finance future growth, and provide opportunities for employees and members of the sales force. Life insurance sales grew from \$1.5 Billion in 1981, to a peak in 1987 of over \$4.4 Billion, and then dropped to \$2.9 Billion in 1991.

## The growth years

During the period of 1983 to 1987, TDIC experienced extremely rapid growth, with sales growing 30% per year. The number of products being introduced increased substantially as TDIC tried to keep pace with the competition. The staff grew from 800 to 1450 during the same period, and work began on a new building that was completed in 1988. The company projections envisioned years of continued expansion.

Part of this growth was fueled by the development of a Universal Life product that was introduced in response to competitors' offerings. The Universal life product separated premiums into term (life insurance) and investment portions, giving policyholders the potential to benefit from the extremely high interest rates of the late 1970's and early 1980's. At its peak of popularity, the Universal Life product comprised about $30\%$ of TDIC's total policy portfolio.

For TDIC, this rapid growth in sales and staff did not result in increased profits. Adding to the normal profit lag $^{2}$ for new contracts was the high cost of administration associated with the Universal Life product. Estimates indicated that the transaction costs for Universal Life were about 7 times those for other products because of the extensive calculations involved. TDIC lost money on the Universal Life product, as did most of the insurance industry. Such factors caused TDIC to show losses between 1985 and 1987 (about \$40 Million in 1987). These were the first losses experienced by the company in its 140-year history.

Senior executives of TDIC were slow to institute change. The Chief Executive Officer (CEO) announced late in 1985 that he would be retiring. The CEO wanted to leave on a positive note, and was reluctant to institute cost-cutting measures that could negatively impact the company's reputation and image. Those interested in succeeding him were also hesitant to advocate drastic measures. The downward slide continued.

## Information systems at TDIC

TDIC entered the world of electronic data processing during the 1960's, with first-generation, solid-state technology. They purchased a Univac computer that used vacuum tubes and punched cards for data input. They later skipped to the third generation when they purchased an RCA computer that was modeled after the newly introduced and very popular IBM 360 mainframe. The decision to go with RCA was based primarily on cost. At that time most of the program development was done inhouse by TDIC staff, using COBOL and assembly language programming. The applications were developed from scratch and were designed specifically for the needs of the company. When Univac purchased RCA in the early 1970's, TDIC found themselves dealing with Univac once again.

By the late 1970's, most insurance companies were busy automating back-office functions, and TDIC was no exception. The computing environment had changed significantly. Software development companies sprang up across the country; these companies sold standard applications. Moreover, programmer turnover was high, and it became inefficient to develop all the necessary systems internally. Most of the development in the insurance industry was for IBM computers, given its $90\%$ share of the insurance industry market. The implication for TDIC was that when they wanted to purchase software, they had to convert it to the Univac system.

Another limiting factor of the Univac environment was the operating system (OS), which was, of course, different from that on the IBM mainframes. With Univac providing limited support, TDIC had to do much work to implement OS changes. For example, during the late 1970's the company implemented on-line transaction systems; these required substantial enhancements to the OS.

The introduction of personal computers, and more specifically the IBM PC, provided more evidence of having made the wrong vendor choice; indeed, Univac had little to offer, and hence TDIC was unable to take advantage of opportunities presented by PC platforms. One example of the adverse effect that lack of microcomputers could have on business is in the presentation of “illustrations” (customized numerical examples of specific products) to potential clients. With the mainframe environment, sales agents would work through the initial parameters of a policy with the client, and then send the material to the Home Office for processing. The “illustrations” would then be run in a batch mode and returned to the agent. The turnaround time was normally 10 days, but it was not unusual for two weeks to pass before the client could see the financial implications of the policy. Competitors of TDIC, who were using personal computers with resident software, were able to provide feedback to customers immediately in some cases and usually within 2 days. Not surprisingly, agents for TDIC complained.

## The move to IBM

By 1982 the Vice President of Information Systems was under pressure to institute changes. He was reluctant to switch from Univac to IBM, because the Univac systems continued to be significantly less expensive. He also realized, however, that by staying with the Univac system his IS group was unable to be as responsive to internal clients.

Univac helped solve the Vice President's dilemma by announcing a new corporate marketing strategy stating that the financial industry (including insurance) was no longer one of their priorities. Just as important, Univac announced they would not continue to enhance their version of the IBM 360 architecture.

To respond to these announcements, TDIC hired a consulting firm, Data Architects of Boston, to examine its computer operations. They recommended preparing a Request For Proposals (RFP) to upgrade TDIC's hardware, since even staying with Univac would now require a major conversion effort. The RFP went to a few computer suppliers, including Univac and IBM. Although the costs of the IBM hardware were not the lowest, conversion costs were lower (it would actually be easier to convert to IBM than to a new Univac system, since they would be staying with the 360 architecture). Because of the ability and ease of acquiring standard insurance industry software, coupled with the hardware conversion costs, the decision was made to change to IBM. After the conversion, TDIC purchased packaged software for standard applications, such as Accounting and Payroll, freeing up the development staff to concentrate on new applications specific to TDIC.

From 1983 to 1986, the company spent between \$18 and \$20 Million on the conversion, and the IS staff doubled to 240 people. During the same period the IS budget increased over 400%. Data Architects helped with the conversion effort; the consultants looked after the management of most of the projects, and TDIC hired programmers with specific expertise (including CICS) since they did not have IBM expertise in-house. The transition period was actually quite smooth, considering its magnitude. The conversion was finished ahead of schedule and under budget.

## The introduction of personal computers

The decision to go with IBM mainframes also facilitated the acquisition of PCs. Agents had been demanding PC-based illustrations and IS was under pressure to deliver. As the current Senior Vice-President of IS stated, “when the big producers (agents) talk, we listen.” The company decided to promote PCs in the work place, and offered them for personal use to all exempt Home Office employees. The price was about 1/3 of the original cost, and TDIC would finance the purchase over 3 years. About 80% of the employees took advantage of the program.

The program was coordinated and managed by the IS department, which set up a series of policies vis-a-vis choices of hardware and software and created an Information Center (IC) to help new users. The IC was staffed with 12 specialists who provided guidance, training and support for the hardware and software that the company had chosen. A similar PC program was offered to the field staff; about 50% of the 1,000 agents took advantage of the offer. The discount to field staff was based on their volume of business, thus many of the newer agents did not qualify.

The IS department also set up a second Information Center (Field PC Support Group) to help the agents. The Field Support Group was to develop PC-based software for the agents (including programs for “illustrations”) and to provide support to the agents using the standard software packages (such as word processing).

Although there was no chargeback system in place for the use of mainframe systems or for application development, the business units were responsible for PC acquisition and applications in their budget. The IS department offered free advice concerning PC use, but relied on users to follow their standards and guidelines. The introduction of PCs was considered a success.

## Application development

TDIC decided in 1985 to move from a flat file environment to a database environment. IBM's

DB2 database management system (DBMS) was evaluated, but the performance of the system in terms of machine efficiency was determined to be inadequate. Cullinet's relational DBMS was chosen. Converting from a flat file environment to a DBMS was no easy task, but it was accomplished without major catastrophe.

The database conversion had major implications for application development. The systems specialists were not only dealing with a new hardware architecture and operating system, they now had to embrace database data representation. After the move, application development and support to the business units was supplied by creating multiple functional databases. This led to data redundancies, multiple data definitions, increased storage requirements, and increased maintenance requirements. Considerable time was spent by the systems specialists in testing, modifying and adjusting systems to meet changing client specifications.

The rapid change in the business environment between 1983 and 1987 had a direct influence on the IS operations. With the introduction of the Universal Life product and the subsequent proliferation of new products, pressure was placed on IS to develop new applications to support those products.

The new product process originated with the sales force, who monitored competitors and provided feedback concerning new products to the actuaries. They responded by developing similar products and creating new product concepts of their own; they then provided specifications to the IS department for support programs. There was almost no interaction between IS and the actuaries other than IS reacting to their demands. The power of actuaries within the organization was high (most of the executives at that time, including the Chief Operating Officer, were actuaries) and most of their wishes were fulfilled on-demand with little discussion.

Not only did the IS department have to face the pressures of simultaneously having to implement hardware and DBMS conversions, but they had to respond at the same time to a demand for new applications that was greater than ever before in both the industry and TDIC. The company was trying to put out 6 to 8 new products or major revisions each year. With the insurance industry becoming increasingly competitive, the instruction from the CEO to the IS group was to "do it faster". Systems developers were working with new hardware and new software, but because of the business pressures were not given adequate training and had to learn on the job. The proliferation of new products also put a strain on the sales agents, as it became very difficult to keep up with the changes.

Understandably, productivity went down. An enormous backlog developed, corners started to be cut and some product development projects ended up with what came to be known as “time bombs”. Since many insurance products had some features that didn’t have to be implemented until the first anniversary of the policy, the IS developers would get the application working well enough to handle the initial sign-up and then move on to the next project in the priority list. Without very careful monitoring, the first anniversary of a product could come up with some features of the application still missing; hence a “time bomb” would go off creating a frenzy in the IS development group.

User satisfaction was low. Among other problems, there was no single contact person for a specific department. If a user had any questions or concerns about a specific project or an existing system for his/her department, the user would have to track down someone within the IS department who would accept responsibility and provide answers. As one person put it, “The users had the impression that they would throw it (a development project) over the wall, and they would never hear about it again”.

Setting priorities for project development was another issue. The Automation Planning Committee (APC) was established; it was composed of the president and the senior vice presidents of the company. Although the stated objectives of the APC related to high-level systems planning, more often than not the discussions concentrated on detailed reviews of problems with on-going projects. Very little planning was actually done in the meetings and projects were prioritized on the basis of operational urgency rather than on a coherent long range plan.

## The crisis

In early 1987, after two straight years of losses and projections for a third year of consecutive loss, the pressure was on the senior executives. When it became necessary to evaluate profitability, the company had a great deal of difficulty in determining which products were making money. Also, the business units had not been accountable for their individual bottom lines. In the past, given the continued growth in sales and profits, there had been little difficulty in getting budgets approved, regardless of how rapidly they increased. Now every department in the organization came under scrutiny, with a completely new focus on cutting costs and insisting on fiscal responsibility. For the first time the IS Department found themselves trying to justify costs.

In March of 1987, the Vice President of Information Systems died of a heart attack on the job. He was replaced by Steve Jackson, who had joined the company in 1970 as a programmer. By 1982 he had worked his way up to Director of Applications Programming, was becoming more involved in Department-level decision making, and had supported the move to IBM. Although Jackson had little experience with the commercial side of the business, his technical training and background was solid. The promotion to Vice President was sudden and unexpected. He responded, while taking charge, by making a conscious effort to increase his knowledge of the rest of the business.

Jackson took over management of the IS department just as the company was beginning to implement cost-cutting measures. The IS group was still under pressure to continue rapid development of new applications and its budget continued to grow even though the business itself was no longer expanding. For example, the NO-MAD fourth generation development language was acquired, and additional staff hired to work with it. One data center was filled and a second one was added in 1988 with the completion of the new building. A second IBM mainframe was purchased. A formal back-up and disaster recovery plan was put in place.

The organizational cost-cutting and consolidation efforts began in 1987 and gathered momentum through 1988 and into 1989. The CFO viewed the IS department as an expense to be contained and saw the continually expanding IS budget as a prime target for budget cuts. The CFO wanted to know what TDIC was getting for its IS expenditures, and that it was making the most of the technology that was already in place.

Steve Jackson raised the issue of a chargeback system to rationalize demand of IS services, but the CFO did not support the idea. The CFO's experience with IS chargeback at his prior company (before joining TDIC) had convinced him that the costs involved with gathering and reporting the necessary information for an effective chargeback system exceeded the potential benefits. Furthermore, the CFO agreed with TDIC's policy of keeping users from going outside for IS services. Therefore, in his view, even if users received detailed information about their IS charges they would have no greater control over them.

## The meeting

Early in 1989 a group of external auditors were examining TDIC as part of a regular audit. They indicated some concern that the company was overstaffed for the volume of business. Knowing of the CFO's view of IS, they made a sales pitch to study the IS department. They offered to focus on efficiency, getting applications out faster with less expense. They claimed to have industry data and efficiency measures that would enable them to evaluate IS and provide recommendations for improvement.

The CFO convinced the CEO that it would be advisable to listen to the auditors and perform an IS audit. A meeting was scheduled to give a subsidiary of the auditing firm an opportunity to make a formal presentation showing how they had helped other organizations and how they proposed to help TDIC. Steve Jackson was invited to the meeting, but he was not told of the participants or the agenda in advance. He was surprised and somewhat angered by the presentation; it was the first indication he had received that the other executives were dissatisfied with IS performance. In his view, the Department had managed a difficult series of conversions, had introduced end-user computing, and supported most of the stated business needs successfully. A very frank discussion ensued.

## Part II: 1989–1991

The April, 1989 presentation by the consulting firm was treated quite seriously by the CEO. He was convinced that TDIC needed help. However, he respected Steve Jackson and wanted to make sure that Steve was satisfied with the consulting firm that was chosen to perform the IS review. The CEO called Steve into his office for the first time to discuss the perceived need for IS change at TDIC. Steve saw this audit as an opportunity to prove how good his department really was. The CEO and Steve agreed that the IS review should address three issues:

1. Is the IS department doing the right things?

2. Is it doing them efficiently and effectively?

## 3. Are there further areas where IT could be used productively within TDIC?

They discussed several possibilities, including having a single consulting company complete an overall analysis of TDIC in addition to the IS audit, or having two different companies conduct one audit each. Steve felt strongly that the IS audit should be done by a company with a great deal of expertise in IS. Negotiations and refinements of the proposals continued until late summer. In mid-September of 1989, the senior executive team decided to go with two separate companies: A well-established consulting firm (SPCF $^{3}$ ) specializing in strategic planning was chosen to review the overall company; and an experienced and well-known information services auditing company (ISAC $^{4}$ ) was selected to review the IS department. Jackson was very pleased. In the course of responding to the RFP, ISAC had made it clear that a review of the IS department had to be done in conjunction with the development of an overall business strategy for TDIC. One of the consultants put it succinctly: “It is impossible to determine whether IS is doing the right thing if nobody knows what the company is trying to achieve”.

The review began in October 1989, and ran through the early part of 1991. Going into the review, the senior executive team was thinking of making some costcutting adjustments and then resume growth. It did not take long, however, for SPCF to change the view of the executives; sales were not going to rebound to the previous levels anytime soon. They recommended downsizing the company by 30%. The suggestion for downsizing was for all functional areas, including the IS department. ISAC agreed with this recommendation.

In March of 1990 the company instituted a voluntary leave and early retirement program. Those that decided to leave within two months received an additional bonus as an incentive. The campaign was a success, bringing the total number of Home Office employees to less than 1,000 from 1,450 in 1987. The IS department met their target of about 25% reduction in staff through voluntary leave. In July of 1990 the company underwent a re-organization to accommodate the reduced staff levels.

SPCF developed a 5-year corporate strategy that included refocusing the business and changes in the product line. TDIC decided to focus on a specific market niche, consisting of professionals and owners of small businesses. A new corporate planning process was put in place, with IS having a major role. To insure that the IS department would be developing projects in line with the corporate strategy, a series of changes were made. First, a new project review process was implemented. Any proposed project requiring IS resources had to show a benefit in at least one of five areas: market share, user (client), customer (policyholder), decision support, and unit cost.

Second, the Automation Planning Committee (APC) changed its focus and now met bi-monthly to set plans and guidelines. A Project Review Committee (PRC) was established to perform many of the functions that APC had reluctantly been involved with. The PRC consisted of vice presidents, who met on a monthly basis to react to problems with on-going projects.

Third, to address the issue of efficiency in the IS department, ISAC completed a post-implementation review of 8 projects. Based on their benchmarks, they found that TDIC was spending too much time on “back-end” functions, such as maintenance. Their analysis of TDIC’s systems development life cycle concluded that too much time was being spent on testing and modifying, and not enough on requirements determination. To redress the unbalance, a new approach was implemented emphasizing greater interaction with the user or field agent to better understand the business case for a specific project.

Fourth, ISAC also found that the approach of building systems by functional area had encouraged the development of redundant databases and stovepipe systems, and that more emphasis should be spent on data and information resource management. An Information Resource Manager was hired to develop an enterprise data model.

Fifth, although the data center operations received very high marks from ISAC, they recommended combining the two existing data centers into one. TDIC implemented this recommendation, and did observe some reduction in overall cost. Unfortunately, moving to a single facility complicated the issue of backup and disaster recovery, and hence they were forced to sign a contract with a third party to supply backup facilities.

Sixth, with respect to the database environment, ISAC recommended switching to IBM's DB2 database management package. Although performance had previously been a major problem, the package had been modified and now this issue was not a concern. ISAC suggested that DB2 was becoming a standard in the industry, and moving to it would provide substantial benefits. This was implemented in late 1991.

Seventh, another key suggestion was that the IS department needed to build closer bonds with their users. The position of business analyst was created, and analysts were assigned to specific user departments. The role of the analyst was to provide a single contact point for the users and to reduce past frustration of not being able to locate the appropriate person within the IS department responsible for their project.

Eighth, in 1990 one of the two Information Centers, the one supporting internal users, was eliminated. Some of its previous responsibilities were given to the business analysts, who also helped determine if a proposed project should be developed/acquired for PC systems or for the mainframe. The Information Center that supported agents was left in place; in late 1991 it was still handling 400 to 500 phone calls per week, and some staff were traveling to assist agents.

Finally, ISAC determined that the IS staff training was inadequate. The training level was 2 or 3 days per person per year, while ISAC's benchmark was 10 days. Jackson agreed to budget more resources for staff training.

One year after these changes were implemented, a few problems were still being experienced. For example, the fourth-generation language that had been installed was being used—heavily by non-IS staff. A NOMAD users group was formed, which met once per month to share insights and problems. The users developed more expertise with the package than the IS department. Jackson was somewhat concerned that standards were not being followed for user developed applications, that documentation was poor, and that some applications were very inefficient in terms of machine processing.

All in all, however, Jackson believed that the IS department was finally beginning to assume a strong and appropriate position within the organization. He felt that ISAC had vindicated the way the Department had managed its operations. The IS Department was now a partner in strategic planning, and the infrastructure was in place to support future business needs.

## Part III: Concluding comments and lessons

Was the IT audit necessary? Consider, for a moment, some of the challenges which were occurring simultaneously. On the business side, there were many aggressive new competitors, many new products, and plummeting profits. On the MIS side, they experienced (1) a change in the hardware platform and operating system; (2) a transition from flat files to database data representation; (3) changes in leadership (IS and CEO); (4) a rapid expansion in the use of PCs and end user computing; (5) the use of new software development tools; (6) continuous pressures for new applications, and (7) a shift to tighter fiscal responsibility. Should the VP of IS have been concerned about strategy? The easy answer is obviously “yes”. However, during a period of high environmental turbulence, putting the infrastructure in place to meet changing needs can be a significant challenge in itself.

What are the lessons that can be extracted from this case?

Lesson 1: The IS function can not be aligned with the strategy of the organization unless the organization has a strategy.... But beware: The IS function will be held accountable anyway.

Prior to 1989, TDIC had a vague mission statement but no set strategy. Trying to align the IS department with a non-existent strategy is an exercise in futility. Researchers in the 1980s proposed that IT managers should contribute to the strategy making process in the firm since information technology is a critical element in that strategy [5,7]. But at TDIC, Jackson did not have access to the inner circle of executives involved in the process of strategy making. Typical of those years, he did not have a voice in the process. Today, some authors [1] suggest that the role of the senior IT executive is to focus on ownership and sharing of knowledge to improve the firm's strategic initiatives and contribute to them, and not focus on the ownership of a large organization and technical facilities structure as his prime mission. Jackson made the latter his prime mission because of his training and background but also because of the circumstances. He was eventually accused of not being aligned with the business strategy. But what choice did he have? What we can conclude is that without a clear company strategy, managing the IT organization is a risky proposition.

## Lesson 2: The realities of managing the day-to-day operations will preclude consideration of strategic alignment issues.

Rapid change in the environment (internal and/or external) will lead to a lag in adjusting to the change. Management better stay on top of it or face the possibility of being so overwhelmed by the necessity to catch up to the environment that strategic thinking becomes impossible. When Jackson took over as Vice President of IS, there were serious operational IT challenges facing the company. The operational challenges, due to lack of planning, were so intense that there was no time to worry about a strategic framework. In other words, when the ship is taking water in the middle of the sea, the priority is to keep the ship afloat not ask whether it is pointing towards the right destination. Besides, Jackson did not have the business background, or the organizational power and access to push for strategic alignment, even if he had wanted to. Should TDIC have looked outside for a VP of MIS who had greater business orientation and experience? Possibly, but top level management looked at the MIS function from an operational point of view, not a strategic one, and Jackson who had a strong operational background matched the perceived needs.

## Lesson 3: The role of IT must be communicated to senior management, or the IS department will be viewed solely as an expense to be controlled.

The somewhat skeptical view held by the CFO at TDIC is not atypical [6]. In the 80s, there were still many top level executives that saw IT as primarily, if not exclusively, support for back-room operations. It took persuasion on the part of the external consultants to get senior management to see the potential contribution of IS. Keen [3] has predicted that in the 1990s, managers in Finance and Accounting will come to see information technology as capital, not overhead but it will require a major educational effort on the part of the IT managers. Furthermore, Jarvenpaa and Ives [2] have found that CEOs participate in IT activities rather infrequently and that “CEOs in large firms are most likely to participate in IT when the organization has a problem, and the CEO perceives IT as a way to reorient or change the organization”. Therefore, communicating the role of IT to senior management in times of no crisis will require a constant and sustained effort on the part of the IS manager.

## Lesson 4: If the IS budget grows disproportionately as a percentage of sales, be prepared to receive a lot of attention and to justify your expenditures.

There is a constant management pressure to ensure that the IT investment is justifiable and consistent with organizational realities. Excessive growth of the IS budget as a percentage of sales is seen by management as an indicator of problems. This point of view may not be right but it certainly is common [2]. Management will expect that any growth in budget be off-set by productivity increases, particularly if the growth is greater than the rest of the company. Therefore, the MIS manager needs to develop a set of performance indicators to monitor, evaluate, and demonstrate the impact and contribution of IS to the firm. In many instances, these performance indicators should not be “traditional” accounting measures but strategic business measures (e.g., satisfied customers; reduced cycle times; increased flexibility; etc.). At TDIC, Jackson did not have the data to prove how IT was affecting the business and the evaluation of the IS department was done only on cost.

Lesson 5: Choosing hardware and software on short-term criteria rather than on considerations of longer-term strategy can be costly.

Early equipment decisions taken by TDIC were made on the basis of visible hardware costs only and without having a formal process. For example, the purchase of hardware from RCA instead of IBM was never framed in any type of long-term strategy. The decision impacted negatively on programmer productivity, user satisfaction, and support for business needs. MIS departments need to implement formal and comprehensive administrative policies and processes, and an active watching of vendors to cope with challenges caused by technological change [4]. In the early 1980s, TDIC's MIS department had none of the above in place and paid a high price for it.

## Lesson 6: L.nkages must be created and maintained between IS and the users.

The MIS manager must ensure that the organizational linkages that promote an on-going dialogue between the MIS Department and its critical internal customers (e.g., business units, agents, actuaries) are implemented and working. Having good relations with internal customers is crucial for the IT function, particularly in situations of conflict with top-level management. Lederer and Mendelow [4] have identified a strong public relations campaign with users as an effective coping mechanism for IS managers. They suggest that such a campaign makes users more understanding and less demanding, resets their expectations to a more realistic level, and “creates an atmosphere of understanding and confidence in the IS department”. At TDIC, the dissatisfaction with IS at the user level was addressed with the creation of the business systems analysts positions, and with the revised role for the Automation Planning Committee. Had Jackson made these changes earlier, he could have counted on the users possibly interceding in his favor and reducing the negative image that the IT function had with top-level management in general and with the CFO in particular. In the 1990s, the success of the IT function is increasingly dependent on the building of strong partnerships with users [1].

Lesson 7: Taking short cuts with requirements determination (including cost/benefit analysis) may ease short term pressures but will lead to long term problems.

The attempt to satisfy all user requests for new application development at TDIC only prolonged the period of hectic activity. The department used system design approaches to cope with the constant resetting of priorities (as Lederer and Mendelow found the departments in their study doing) but it backfired. Standing up to the demands of the actuaries earlier may have brought the issue of a lack of strategic direction to a head sooner. An alternative approach would have been to demand more extensive up-front analysis, forcing prioritization of projects and possibly an examination of corporate strategy. The feasibility of this alternative approach depends on the attitude of the CEO vis-a-vis the role of the MIS function. If the function is seen as a service support unit, it will have a hard time being heard.

Lesson 8: Assuming the IT function is not being outsourced, investments in training need to be made to avoid a degradation of capabilities and to take advantage of new technologies.

The small allocation of time for training IS specialists at TDIC, coupled with the constantly changing architecture and environment, led to a situation where the developers were spending far too much time trying to learn on the job. This situation led to the IT function being unable to take advantage of emerging technologies and programmers putting greater emphasis on maintenance rather than on development. As the executives in Moynihan's study [6] made clear, the consequences of not having high quality in-house IS skills leads to “an over-reliance on external resources, with consequent loss of control, prolonged development time scales, large development and maintenance backlogs, and an inability to implement more highly integrated systems”.

Lesson 9: A demand-pull environment provides excellent opportunities for managing technological diffusion.

At TDIC, the demand for personal computers by sales agents allowed the IS group to foster diffusion throughout the organization. The success of this technological diffusion was due in part to the aggressive pricing of the technology, the sustained and high quality support provided by two differently focused Information Centers, and by the rapid development of high visibility applications. The successful application of personal computers also facilitated the subsequent introduction of other end-user computing alternatives.

Lesson 10: An external audit is sometimes the only way for the IT function to establish its role as a strategic presence within the company.

Though Jackson's initial reaction to the meeting with auditors was one of disbelief and anger, the meeting was a great opportunity to address issues that had either been swept “under the rug” or been presented to the CEO by the not-always objective CFO. As mentioned above, the involvement of the CEO in IT is more likely if there is a crisis situation [2]. Asking for a formal review by an external auditor could be a way to involve senior management to address IT issues. Selecting the appropriate auditor, however, is key.

The alignment of the IT function with the organizational strategy has been a major theme of academic discourse in recent years. However, strategic alignment is possible only if the firm has a set strategy, if the firm involves the MIS manager in strategic issues, and if the appropriate infrastructure exists. While strategic alignment is clearly a key goal for any MIS department, many senior IS managers have found themselves in positions where short term survival necessarily takes precedence over long term goal setting. The case presented here shows that strategic and operational issues are closely intertwined, and that strategic alignment is a joint responsibility of general and IT management.
