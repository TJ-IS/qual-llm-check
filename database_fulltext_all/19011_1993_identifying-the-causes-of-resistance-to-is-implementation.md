# Identifying the causes of resistance to IS implementation

# A change theory perspective

Ravindra Krovi

Memphis State University, Memphis, TN, USA

The major deterrents to successful IS (Information System) implementation include both technical and organizational issues. However, it is widely recognized that the more serious dimension of this problem is essentially behavioral in nature. This is because the introduction of any IS causes change in the organization; i.e. to individuals, responsibilities, socio-political structure, etc. The purpose of this research effort is to investigate the relationship between organizational change and IS implementation. Towards this end, organizational change theories serve as the basis for assessing the causes of resistance to IS implementation.

Keywords: Implementation process; Change theories; Levels of change; Organizational change; Resistance; Planned change; Incremental change; Radical change; Implementation strategies.

## 1. Introduction

With the advent of low cost, high performance computing and significant advances in technologies such as communications, organizations are increasingly using IS at all levels of the management hierarchy. Faced with an increasingly competitive market, businesses have to rely on innovative uses of IS just to remain solvent if not to forge ahead. The intent here, is not to discuss IS for achieving efficiency or competitiveness, but rather to assess the consequences of introducing such systems in the organization. IS have attained a level of complexity that can transcend departmental boundaries, allow the communication of geographically dispersed individuals, change roles and responsibilities and even shift the power structure. Technologies and concepts such as communications, ISDN (Integrated Services Digital Networks), distributed databases, etc. have made such complicated IS applications a reality. Leavitt and Whisler [18] have stated that information technology would alter the shape of organizations and the nature of jobs; information systems have indeed had an impact on the organization [24]. Besides technical proficiency, the success of these systems depends largely on how well they are received in the organization. Indeed, the introduction of the IS can cause changes in various facets of the organization: its business, its processes, its culture, and possibly even its mission. If such changes are considered negative by organizational members, then there is likely to be resistance. This can have ramifications both on the success of the particular IS and also on the ability of the organization to deal with IS related change. Hence, as IS professionals, it is important to study and understand the behavioral problems in the process of implementing IS. The problems include organizational issues such as the dependence of the organization on the implemented system, its integration with other organizational systems and generally its impact on the organizational structure [29]. We assume that successful management of the implementation process should be guided by organizational change theories. Both the behavioral and cognitive aspects play a pivotal role in assessing organizational change. Bartunek and Moch [3] discuss three levels of organizational change; first, second and third order. While first order change is more concerned with the improvement of an existing situation, second order change attempts to replace existing conditions. Third order change places more emphasis on the training of organizational members so that they can accordingly adapt the IS to their needs.

The purpose of our research is to investigate the relationship between levels of organizational change and IS implementation. More specifically, to determine the impact of desired levels of change on the IS implementation.

Prior studies relate change theory to organizational dynamics. It is argued that by understanding the relationship between organizational change and IS implementation, system analysts and users (or the group involved in the implementation effort) can target and initiate specific changes by using appropriate strategies.

## 2. Organizational change

What is change? It means different things to different people:

"When change finally does occur, it is often like an earthquake" [22]. "To many people it is threatening. It conjures up visions of a revolutionary, a dissatisfied idealist, a troublemaker, a malcontent" [5].

The traditional assumption is that organizational change is evolutionary and diffuses gradually through the organization. It can be either reactive or proactive; i.e. because of external pressure or an internal desire to change. Goodstein and Burke (1991) provide examples of factors that can cause organizations to change: decrease in market share, new inventions, new regulations, depressed economy etc. Based on this, they define organizational change as the “movement from a present state to a future state”. This requires assessment of the initial state, determination of the future state, and then designing ways to attain the desired state. Also “planned change” [19] is purposefully intended by organizational members.

## 2.1. Theories of Change

A fundamental taxonomy of change theories consists of process theories and theories on the possible levels of change. The former deal with how change can be attained and the latter with what can be achieved.

## 2.1.1. Process Theories

Two seminal models related to the IS implementation process were proposed by Kurt Lewin [20] and Kolb and Frohman [17]. Lewin suggested a three stage process of organizational change: Unfreezing, Moving, and Refreezing. Unfreezing means creating the need for change. Moving is choosing a particular course of action and implementing it. Refreezing entails bringing back the organization to stability. The Kolb and Frohman model considers the interaction between the 'client' and the 'consultant' and treats the implementation process as consisting of seven stages. Ginzberg [10] has compared the two models by matching the corresponding stages of implementation. Other researchers have drawn parallels between the theories and the stages of implementation ([27], [11]):

Unfreezing Definition stage
Moving Physical Design stage
Refreezing Implementation stage

## 2.1.2. Levels of Organizational Change

Researchers from various fields make a distinction between two types of change. Levy has summarized this in his work on second order changes. Although, most of the definitions complement each other, there are differences.

1. Evolutionary versus revolutionary changes (15): Evolutionary change: "The modest adjustments necessary for maintaining growth under the same overall pattern of management". Revolutionary change: "The serious upheavals and abandonment of past management practices involving finding a new set of organizational practices...".

2. First order versus Second order change (25): First Order Change: “... involves a variation that occurs within a given system which itself remains unchanged”. Second Order Change: “... involves a variation whose occurrence changes the system itself”.

3. Alpha and Gamma change (13): Alpha Change: “... involves a variation in the level of some existential state”. Gamma Change: “involves a redefinition or reconceptualization”.

Other definitions are similar: single loop learning and double loop learning [2], equilibrium systems and far from equilibrium systems [12], incremental change and transformational change [16] etc.

## 3. Information systems and change

As can be seen, the two types of change identified by researchers represent two extreme ends of a spectrum; i.e. one end deals with incremental change, the other implies a ‘radical’ change. Clearly, there is room for a third level that represents a compromise.

Only two research efforts have attempted to define a third level. Bartunek and Moch [3] stated: "third order change requires the consultant to play more of a teaching role, training the client system to distinguish among schemata and develop and implement alternatives".

What are the implications of this? First and second order changes indicate the consequences of using the system. However, third order change refers more to the process of attaining a particular level. The third order change does not fit into the spectrum between first and second order change; i.e. from incremental to extremely large ones.

Another model that defines a third level was proposed by [13]: In their words, Beta change “involves a variation in the level of some existential state, complicated by the fact that some intervals of the measurement continuum associated with a constant conceptual domain have been recalibrated.” The instrument used to measure the change has itself undergone change; for example, a transition from group decision making throughout the organization to decisions made at the top. This may be construed as a success if we consider control as a criterion. On the other hand, it is a failure if the degree of participation is important. The IS field is replete with constructs which are not well defined and are subject to interpretation. For example, the introduction of a distributed system may result in flexibility but loss of control for the IS division. Unfortunately, most IS interventions may fall under this category because of a general lack of consensus about the use of constructs.

Hence, we need a separate model that not only incorporates a definition of first and second order changes but also another level somewhere in between. This new level of change will be termed as Middle Order Change.

These three levels of change can be compared through their potential effect on five factors: critical success factors, organizational processes (the way the business is run), the number of individuals affected (including employees, customers, suppliers, etc.), the impact of the change on the external environment (industry, competitors, rules and regulations), and forces that might drive this change; see Table 1.

First order change is typically incremental; for example, a system designed to monitor data entry, targeted at data entry operators (a few individuals), might create a minor change in organizational processes; it is intended to improve the effectiveness of evaluation. However, this system does not modify any critical success factor nor does it have direct impact on the environment. Second order change involves major change in any or a combination of the factors. This could occur as a reaction to external factors, such as major reduction of market share. For example, Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account system combined information on a customer's accounts into one statement; this revolutionized the processes used to conduct the business. This increased the customer base. Similarly, Federal Express developed a system to track customer parcels and letters. The introduction of ATM's defined new success factors for banks and forever changed operations in the banking industry. The force driving such changes is often strategic, affecting many people.

Middle order change represents a compromise; the magnitude of change is greater than first order change, yet it neither affects the critical success factors nor is strategic in nature. As an example, American Transtech improved its customer service in the telemarketing industry by using ISDN to reduce the setup time for customer calls. Digital Equipment Corporation's decision to use XCON, an expert system, was driven by marketing considerations, affected some employees but not changing the business. ## 4. Mapping potential causes of resistance to levels of change

First order systems generally affect a few individuals with a minimal impact on the external environment and do not affect the organization's critical success factors. This might result in a lack of commitment from top management. Also, it might result in users perceiving job security threat. Some such systems are initiated by ad hoc requests and may be met with resistance by IS personnel. Hence, the implementation of first order systems can result in resistance: see Figures 1a, 1b, 1c.

Middle order systems are characterized by efficiency and operational concerns of the organization. In cases where communications are used, they may cross departmental boundaries and seem to pose a threat to the existing power structure. For example, the introduction of electronic mail messaging systems may affect interpersonal communication. In the case of a transition from a manual to an automated system, it may be perceived as a threat to job security. Also, since such systems are capable of altering the organizational processes, they can increase the job complexity resulting in newer methods of job evaluation. Finally, there might be a lack of consensus about the need for the system. These relations are represented in Figure 2.

Second order systems are carriers of sweeping change. The critical factors may be changed leading to power asymmetry between departments. Since the driving forces are often strategic, there is increased pressure on the IS department to develop a comprehensive system (timely, low cost, and meets expectations). This might result in a sudden acute shortage of IS resources. These factors might arouse resistance from IS personnel (see Figure 3).

Means of overcoming this resistance can be remapped to the various stages of implementation. By anticipating potential causes of resistance to certain systems, the IS team can design ways and means of overcoming resistance and the 'planned change' will be successful. The following are proposed methods.

Pre-implementation strategies:

\- Align goals of IS and organization [9].

\- Ensure that the need for change exists [4].

\- Keep senior IS managers as part of top management, but Make IS independent of other functional areas [23].

\- Define and organizational standards/policies –to coerce users [21].

## 5. Conclusions

Successful management of the IS implementation process should be guided by organizational change theories. The kind of change induced by IS can assume various forms. At one end of the spectrum, change is typically incremental. At the other end, change may be radical. This paper has illustrated the impact of desired levels of change on organizational attributes such as critical success factors, organizational processes, external environment etc.. The source and form of resistance to implementation depends on the kind of impact each level of change may induce. Depending on the type of change desired, information systems managers can identify potential causes of resistance and accordingly plan the implementation process. Admittedly, this does not represent a panacea to cure all the ills of implementation. Future research should focus on identifying certain classes of systems that induce certain levels of change. This is well characterized by Woodman [26]: "Information technology has become so crucial for effective organizational functioning that it needs careful attention and consideration in organizational change interventions. richer notions of information technology need to be woven into the theory and practice of organizational change".
