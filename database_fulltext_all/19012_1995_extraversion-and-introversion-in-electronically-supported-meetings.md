# Extraversion and introversion in electronically-supported meetings

Richard E. Yellen, MaryAnne Winniford, Clive C. Sanford \*

Business Computer Information Systems Department, College of Business Administration, University of North Texas, Denton, TX 76203-3677, USA

## Abstract

Laboratory experiments with Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) have studied their effects on group and individual outcomes, such as equality of participation, time to decision, quality of decision, consensus, and satisfaction. Research models for GDSSs include individual member characteristics as independent variables in the group process and their outcomes. How individuals feel about group meetings will influence their perception about satisfaction and participation. This study examines how individual personality characteristics influence outcomes in an electronically-supported meeting. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator was used to create mixed groups of introverts and extraverts. These groups then used a traditional face-to-face meeting and a GDSS to discuss ethical scenarios. All participants contributed more original solutions in the GDSS environment than the face-to-face context but provided more comments in the traditional environment. Overall, the extraverts had more comments in both environments.

Keywords: Group decision support systems; Collaborative work; Electronic meetings; Group process; Decision support; Group problem solving; Interaction process; Human information processing; Personality types; Extraversion and introversion

## 1. Introduction

Electronic meeting systems have been moving out of the research laboratory and into corporate America. In a survey of 135 randomly chosen Data Processing Management Association members, 45 responded that they were currently using some kind of electronic meeting support [3]. Several case studies have been done on corporations using electronic support ([6], [7], [13], and [21]).

The popular press has brought descriptions of the technology and its benefits to the public (e.g. [34] and [36]). This maturing interest in the technological benefits of electronic meeting systems points to a continuing need for consideration of its effects.

Electronic meeting systems have been examined from four perspectives: technological assessment, organizational studies, technical capabilities, and social psychological studies [35]. Social psychological studies examine the impact on group process as well as group and individual outcomes. These help us to determine where and why to use electronic meeting systems to support group interaction. Previous laboratory experiments have studied equality of participation, time to decision, quality of decision, and consensus and satisfaction ([15], [16], and [44]).

One area that has received less scrutiny is the effect of individual characteristics on the group process and outcomes of electronically-supported meetings. How individuals feel about computers, the type of task, and group discussion will influence their perceptions about ease of use, satisfaction and participation, as well as affect their actual participation. We examine how one individual characteristic, extraversion/introversion, impacts individual satisfaction and participation.

Prior research on the impact of a GDSS on group and individual participation and the quality of decision-making has been extensive. We begin with a review of several studies which focused on how individuals respond to the process and effectiveness of such systems.

## 2. Review of electronic meeting support systems

Electronic meeting support systems use computers to aid in group communication and decision making. They vary in their technical configuration. A GDSS is typically a group of people located in one room with each person using a computer for electronic assistance in the group meeting. A Level 1 system provides communication support such as electronic idea generation, as well as decision support, such as software for voting and ranking. When decision modelling or mathematical techniques are added to the system, the GDSS becomes an enhanced, or Level 2 system. A comprehensive review of group decision support systems and other types of electronic meeting support can be found in ([8] and [20]).

Research frameworks in MIS and in GDSS have included individual characteristics as a variable that can impact outcomes ([23] and [45]). Individual characteristics include such variables as need for achievement and cognitive style ([4] and [42]). The usefulness of cognitive style – the particular problem-solving process used by an individual – has been the focus of a lively debate ([17] and [31]). However, there is general agreement that individual differences will impact both group process and group and individual outcomes.

When individuals work as a group, the process by which they interact will affect the group outcomes. Process losses cause the group to perform below potential, while process gains should help the group to outperform its individual members. Huber [18] operationalized group effectiveness with the expression:

Group Effectiveness

= Potential Effectiveness - Process Losses + Process Gains

Process loss may occur if individuals are unable to communicate their ideas. Frequently, group discussions become a forum for a few individuals who seek to force the group into agreement with their positions. Applegate et al. [1] hypothesize that process gains may come from the ability of an electronic “conversation” to carry several “speakers” at one time. This prevents one person from dominating the air time available to the group and stimulates group participation.

Fear of judgment or communication inhibition can also discourage individuals from contributing to the group. A GDSS allows individuals to be anonymous. Anonymity provides a low-threat channel for contribution to participants who are lacking in skills or are inhibited ([36] and [43]).

Many studies have found that the use of computer-based communication leads to greater participation and less domination of the group by an individual ([29] and [41]). There seems to be a more democratic environment in an electronically supported discussion. However, we should also focus on the individual characteristics of who is participating. We would not want to encourage participation of those who have nothing useful to say while inhibiting those who are most knowledgeable or the best thinkers. While we have increased the equality of participation through the use of anonymity and broader communication bandwidth, we seek to identify the personality type of people who may be more or less encouraged to participate in an electronic discussion.

Another form of process loss may occur if the group does not implement the decision due to dissatisfaction with a decision or a decision process. The benefits of using a GDSS are inconsistent; many researchers found an increase in satisfaction (e.g. [19]), while some report a decrease (e.g. [10]). Therefore, satisfaction with an electronic meeting system may be a function of personality type. Some may prefer the automated detachment of a computerized conversation while others might find it cold and cheerless. We are interested in examining how a GDSS impacts individual satisfaction. Do some types of individuals experience process gains from the experience, while others experience process losses?

## 3. Review of personality types

All events are viewed through the filter of one's personality and experience. How a group interacts together or its action using a GDSS depends, in part, on the individuals involved. In particular, we believe that a person's facility with spoken language may influence participation in and satisfaction with a GDSS.

There are several ways to participate in a meeting. Some people may try to dominate it while others may avoid participation. In a review of leadership, Bass et al., [2] cited several sources listing high verbal output as a characteristic of leaders. This has been supported in the literature of electronic meeting systems [33]. Good leadership can improve group processes, however, poor leadership can harm them. A GDSS can also promote more equality in participation, although the value of “democratic” discussions has been questioned [12]. Whether we truly get better group outcome when there is more equality of participation depends on the individual characteristics of the participants and how they use and perceive the system.

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) has been used to measure four dimensions of individual characteristics, or personality. Cognitive style can be indicated by combining three of these dimensions (Sensing/Intuition, Thinking/Feeling and Judging/Perception). The fourth dimension, Extraversion/Introversion (E/I), is of interest to us here. “Extraversion relates to preferences for action, impulsiveness, talkativeness and gregariousness, and introverts tend to work alone, dislike distraction, and think before acting” [37].

Since all four MBTI scales are independent, ignoring the others does not affect the outcome. The MBTI essentially asks questions about how a subject reacts in certain situations. It requires the scorer to count the number of answers that indicate a tendency towards extraversion or introversion. The two scores are compared and used to arrive at a personality type on the E/I scale. A subject's score may indicate that he/she is an extravert (that is, the number of answers indicating a tendency toward extraversion was very large), an introvert, or the scores may be inconclusive.

As with any personality measure, the construct validity of the MBTI has been questioned. In particular, the question has been raised whether the E/I scale measures inclination to verbal output and the outer world of materialism or the more Jungian concept of focus on the inner world of concepts. However, a person may talk a lot about ideas or may quietly contemplate the natural world.

Studies using the MBTI have confirmed that subjects measured as extraverts do speak more often with longer comments and introverts pause longer between comments $[32]$ . Extraverts have also been found to be more talkative in groups, and are perceived as more talkative $[40]$ .

While some question the MBTI's value as a valid, useful, or reliable indicator of personality types ([5] and [24]), there is a long standing and substantial support for the test ([14], [22], [26], [28], and [30]). Additionally, some organizations are using the MBTI as part of their efforts to improve the people handling skills of its managers [27]. Practitioners apparently value the MBTI as a valuable tool in determining personality type.

## 4. Hypotheses

In this study, we examine the individual characteristic of Extraversion/Introversion and its effect on individual participation and satisfaction in a GDSS versus a non-GDSS meeting. Extraverts prefer verbal interaction, and therefore like to talk and communicate. It is proposed that introverts probably prefer the written channel, which allows them to focus more on ideas and concepts without having to participate in a face-to-face discussion.

The utility of a GDSS is enhanced if it encourages more contributions from introverts while maintaining the same level of contribution from extraverts. If, on the other hand, a GDSS improves one group of participant communication at the expense of the other, no overall benefit should be expected. Neither would benefits accrue if one group's level of contribution is dampened.

A GDSS offers different socio psychological arguments for efficiency. The speed and flexibility might increase subjective feelings of control and focus attention on the task, but since exchanged text is the only channel, aural and visual cues are absent. Differences in satisfaction with a GDSS may also be partially explained by differences in verbal inclination. Those who enjoy conversation and discussion may express low satisfaction with a system designed to replace or supplant verbal interaction. Therefore, the first hypothesis is:

H1: Introverts will be more satisfied with the GDSS environment than extraverts.

Introverts might also feel more inclined to contribute to the discussion in a GDSS over a non-GDSS environment. The inhibition of the flow of social feedback, feelings, and social meanings, and the perception of participants of a greater physical distance among themselves should create an environment more conducive to participation by introverts. Participants communicating via the computer would therefore have a sense of more equal participation than in a non-GDSS environment. Thus, the perceived “fairness” or non-threatening environment might lead to a higher confidence in the final outcome or perceived quality of the solution. The second and third hypotheses are then:

H2: Introverts will feel less inhibited to contribute in the discussion in a GDSS versus a non-GDSS environment.

H3: Introverts will perceive that the GDSS environment is more conducive to generating a quality solution.

The anonymity provided by a GDSS should allow participants to express ideas and opinions more freely. Therefore, extraverts and introverts should collectively generate more original solutions in the GDSS environment. We propose, however, that the absence of the technological constraint of typing and reading text will lead to more comments in the non-GDSS compared with the GDSS environment. With more time and effort being devoted to general comments about the proposed solutions in the non-GDSS environment, the window of opportunity for proposing original solutions is decreased. Therefore, a GDSS promotes more new idea generation, and should lead to the generation of more original solutions than a non-GDSS environment. The proposed magnitude and difference in the relative impact of idea generation and comments generated by extraverts and introverts in both environments leads to the fourth and fifth hypotheses:

H4: Extraverts and introverts will generate more original solutions with a GDSS.

H5: Extraverts and introverts will generate fewer comments in a GDSS.

## 5. Research design

The experimental design is a $2 \times 2$ repeated measure with the independent variables of Extraverts/Introverts and non-GDSS/GDSS. Each group participated in both a non-GDSS (traditional face to face group meeting) and a GDSS. This design employed 36 subjects per cell; thus a total of 72 subjects were used.

The subjects were students enrolled in an Introduction to Computers class at a large public university in the USA. The class, usually populated by Freshmen or Sophomores, is required for all College of Business majors. At the beginning of the semester the researchers offered several sections of the class an opportunity to learn about their personality type, to earn extra class credit, and to use group decision support software. Since this study examines personality attributes that are independent of decision making skills or business background, we believe that undergraduates can serve as surrogates for real world workers.

Additionally, the subjects indicated, in an exit survey, that a primary motivation for participating in this study was the desire to use GDSS software. Taking a 126 question personality test and participating in a two hour laboratory experiment is too long a process to make the few extra credit points offered by the classes worth the effort. We therefore suggest that the subjects were as involved and motivated as real world workers, regardless of whether the involvement and motivation is due to job requirements or a chance to experiment with new software. One hundred thirty-five students expressed an interest in participating in the experiment. After taking the MBTI the students were placed in a pool of potential subjects.

Seventy-two of the subjects with the highest scores on either end of the E/I scale were selected to participate in the study. Choosing subjects from the extremes of the scale allowed us to have more control over the independent variable: extraversion versus introversion. Selected demographic characteristics of the subjects in the experiment are presented in Table 1.

Since nearly all participant contributions provide input into the decision making process, we decided to categorize the type of contribution made by each subject as “original solutions” and “comments.” Placing each comment into a category and then analyzing the number and type of contributions and comments in each category would duplicate work of previous studies (e.g. [43]). Rather, our investigation expands upon these by non-judgmentally recording contributions and then determining whether the GDSS environment is more conducive to introverted personality types making contributions to group discussion when compared to the non-GDSS environment. The determination of the environment most receptive to contributions to the decision making process was assessed by counting the number of statements each subject made.

Our scheme coded both proposed, or original solutions and comments addressing these solutions. An example of a proposed solution is “Reduce the inventory level.” A comment on this solution would be “This would prevent the timely filling of orders”, or “Do we really need to consider inventory levels?” Supportive critical comments or arguments and questions about the solutions were generically categorized as comments. Comments about the computer system and group process or comments off the topic were not included in the count.

The experiment, which was repeated 12 times, was conducted over a three month period. The percent of introverts and extraverts who participated in each experiment was varied so that the groups resembled a natural demarcation of work groups. The size of the groups was limited to a maximum of eight, which is small enough for successful functioning of a non-GDSS group [38] while large enough to allow the technology to be more of an aid than a hindrance to the group process.

A template for the group decision making session was available on networked personal computers. The template was developed with the VisionQuest $^{®}$ GDSS software, and implements the Brainwriting, Rating, and Voting tools used to guide the subjects through a group decision making session. A Level 1 GDSS, VisionQuest automatically records all work performed in support of a GDSS session.

After signing a consent form and listening to a brief training session, which included an oral presentation, a written guide, and some hands on assistance, the subjects were presented with a scenario from a classical text book in Business Ethics [9]. The scenario (Case 9: “The Foreman in a Bind”) describes a situation in which a person must make a difficult ethical decision. It is a Judgment-type task in the McGrath task topology [25] for which there is no “correct” answer. The task, therefore, encourages discussion. The group members used the GDSS software to generate suggested solutions to the dilemma. All the group members could comment upon any of these. Thus, each suggestion was followed by a stream of comments that resembled an oral discussion. Each subject’s contribution was completely anonymous to the others. This part of the experiment took thirty minutes.

Next, the subjects used the GDSS software to remove suggestions that, based upon the subjects' comments to the suggestions, had a low likelihood of solving the problem. The suggestions that survived the culling process were then evaluated by the subjects for their practicality or reasonableness. As before, the subjects used the previous comments as the basis for removing impractical suggestions. The subjects then used the GDSS to vote on the suggestions that survived. Since we are interested in the discussion process, and not the results, the voting is not considered here.

Upon completion of the GDSS session, the group moved to a conference-like room set up to encourage group face-to-face discussion. The room had chairs set in a U-shaped arrangement. There was a chalk board at the top of the “U”.

A second similar scenario, Case 10 (“I Never Make Big Mistakes”) in Eckel’s book, was then presented. The subjects proceeded with essentially the same steps. This time, however, the subjects used the non-GDSS method to arrive at a solution, which, by group consensus, was most likely to solve the problem. The subjects first generated suggestions and made comments on them. After thirty minutes, the subjects voted to eliminate those suggestions that they considered to have a low likelihood of solving the problem. Finally, the subjects voted upon the “best” course of action.

The researchers provided the non-GDSS session with as much guidance in both discussions. This involved a strictly defined agenda. One researcher wrote the group's suggestions on the chalk board but took no part in the discussion. As with the GDSS session, the problem solution is immaterial.

To ensure that the order of scenarios did not influence the results (GDSS versus non-GDSS), the order of exposure was varied. The sessions for the first four groups were conducted as follows:

Group 1: Case 9 with GDSS first; Case 10 with non-GDSS second

Group 2: Case 10 with GDSS first; Case 9 with non-GDSS second

Group 3: Case 9 with non-GDSS first; Case 10 with GDSS second

Group 4: Case 10 with non-GDSS first; Case 9 with GDSS second

Since the experiment was conducted twelve times, the above schedule was repeated three times. Neither the case nor the order of exposure contaminated the experimental results (F = 12.34, p = 0.039). After participating in both sessions, the subjects completed a post-experimental questionnaire, were debriefed, and released.

VisionQuest merges and saves all the subjects' comments in a text file. Working from McGrath's definition of group interaction process, we measured the process using the comments that the subjects made through the GDSS. After each session, the text file was content-coded, parsing out the original solutions from comments about the solutions. Redundant solutions were stripped from the list. All comments were used to measure the total number of parsed units of speech (total number of comments). There were typically few, if any, comments about the system, about the group process, off the topic, or of uncodable text. The non-GDSS sessions were videotaped and content-coded with the same scheme.

## 6. Results and discussion

Overall, the questionnaire results concerning the experimental task indicate that the participants had previously been involved in group discussions concerning unstructured problems (Table 2). As expected, the extraverts are more comfortable about giving their opinions in this type of discussion than introverts (F = 7.49, P = 0.01). At the same time, the introverts were more comfortable using computers than the extraverts (F = 7.67, P = 0.05). All participants liked the GDSS software and felt that it was easy to use.

Hypothesis 1: Table 3 shows the opinions of the participants on the computerized group process. Overall, the subjects found the software to have a high utility and to be efficient and interesting. In general, they also found the computerized group process to be fair, with the extraverts rating the software more fair than the introverts (F = 6.23, P = 0.02). Thus, the data supports the converse of hypothesis 1. The participants' comments during the debriefing session indicated that the environment was non-threatening, and allowed enough latitude for full expression of thoughts and feelings. The extraverts did not feel that the GDSS environment threatened their style of expression, and recognized that the feeling of anonymity allowed for their uninhibited expression of thoughts and opinions.

Hypothesis 2: Introverts felt that the GDSS environment provided the best forum to contribute to the group discussion (In Table 4, F = 6.9, P = 0.04). Even though extraverts were more satisfied with the computerized environment, the introverts felt that this was more receptive to their contribution to the group discussion. These conclusions suggest that while introverts are more uncomfortable in group discussions than extraverts, the computerized environment provides more of an opportunity for equal participation than a non-GDSS environment. All the subjects indicated that the GDSS environment allowed for equal contribution by all participants and hence provided the best platform for discussion.

Hypothesis 4: The results of the tabulation of original solutions and comments generated during the non-GDSS and GDSS sessions are listed in Table 5. Both extraverts and introverts had more original solutions during the GDSS sessions than during the non-GDSS sessions (F = 20.0, P = 0.002), but the overall difference in the number of original solutions was insignificant, and there was no interactive effect. This supports the notion that individuals are more likely to focus on new ideas and solutions in a GDSS environment, and spend less time embellishing and formulating well-structured arguments in support of their ideas.

Hypothesis 5: There is support for the assertion that more comments are generated in a non-GDSS session (F = 21.3, P = 0.0001). Extraverts were also more inclined to offer more verbal support for their solutions (F = 11.8, P = 0.0008). The non-GDSS environment appears to be less solution- or goal-oriented, and the extraverts were more reflective on proposed solutions.

## 7. Conclusion

This study used a laboratory experiment to investigate the effects of a non-GDSS and GDSS environment on individual responses from those who possess the characteristic of being an introvert or an extravert. The experimental results support three of the hypotheses. Although both extraverts and introverts felt less inhibited in the GDSS environment, the introverts felt best able to contribute with a GDSS. Also, all of the participants generated more original solutions with the GDSS and more comments in the non-GDSS environment. The extraverts were more inclined to elucidate and embellish on their solutions in the non-GDSS environment. When in a face-to-face meeting, the extraverts were more reflective and focused on general comments, which included problem definition and clarification.

All of the participants expressed more satisfaction with the GDSS environment, and the extraverts rated its overall “fairness” higher than the introverts. They also recognized that more potential solutions were generated with the GDSS, and more comments and discussion occurred with the non-GDSS. There was no consensus on which environment produced the “best” decision.

This study supports our general proposition that not only does a GDSS influence group process, but also the personality characteristic of introversion/extraversion plays a significant role in affecting the level of member participation. Face-to-face meetings provide a forum for the exchange of gestures and body expressions; this gives the extraverts more flexibility to expand upon and argue their proposed solutions. While the quantity of original solutions does increase in the GDSS setting, the relative number remains about the same. Therefore, while all participants preferred the GDSS, this seemed to have no effect on any perceived improvement in quality.

If the quantity of proposed solutions is important, the GDSS appears to provide the best forum to accomplish this. It provides a mode that supports idea generation, with fewer comments. On the other hand, if the organization prefers more comments and embellishments on the proposed solutions, the traditional face-to-face meeting might be better.

The generalizability of the results to other situations could be limited to applications of a Type 1 GDSS. Further investigation into the effects of personality types where there is decision support based on multi-criteria, multi-objective, or systems dynamics based techniques (Type 2 GDSS) would help us better understand their nature, contingencies, and effects. Further research which considers all of the Myers-Briggs personality types and a facilitator for the non-GDSS groups in a more structured, goal oriented situation would help extend the generalizability of the conclusions.

The primary point of this study, however, is that individuals have different personality characteristics and therefore respond differently to group problem solving settings. Many studies have focused more on the macro concept of how a group responds but a more micro analysis that includes personality characteristics may yield more conclusive results. Just as individuals with certain personality profiles gravitate to particular professions, they will also exhibit preferences and quantifiable reactions to different meeting environments.
