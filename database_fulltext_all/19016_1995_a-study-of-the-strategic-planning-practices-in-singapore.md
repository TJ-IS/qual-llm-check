# A study of the strategic planning practices in Singapore

F. Pavri \*, J. Ang

Department of Decision Sciences, Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent Singapore 0511 Singapore

## Abstract

Though much has been written on strategic IS planning, little empirical research has been done. This study examined the practice of strategic IS planning in Singapore, and is based on work undertaken in the United States in the 1970s. Data were collected using a questionnaire which was sent to the highest ranking IS executives (identified by name and title) of 320 firms picked at random from the Singapore Phone Book. Seventy usable responses were received. Forty-nine percent (or 34 out of 70) of the respondents indicated that their firms undertook some form of strategic IS planning. Of these, 29 percent stated that their firms did not model their IS strategic plans on their corporate plans. This is indeed surprising, given a literature that stresses the value of having such a linkage.

Keywords: Strategic IS planning; Corporate objectives; Planning practices and methodologies

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) $^{1}$ play an important role in determining the way an organization does business. There has been a significant shift of emphasis in their use from simple data processing to strategic planning. IS can also provide new forms of customer service, new distribution channels, rearrange industry boundaries, redesign business processes, and enable companies to capture global economies of scale [4,5,7,11,18,22,32]. For example, McKesson Corporation, one of the largest distributors of pharmaceuticals, introduced a sophisticated order entry (called Economost) in its dealings with independent drugstores and pharmacies [8]. Merrill Lynch's CMA Financial Product provided three appealing services to investors: cash withdrawal by check or Visa credit card, credit through a standard margin account, and automatic investments of cash, dividends and other available funds in high-yielding money market accounts [43].

This logical progression from purely quantitative, operational applications to systems designed for qualitative, strategic purposes reflects the increased sophistication of IS and a growing reliance on these systems by middle and upper management $[23,24]$ . This greatly increases the importance of IS strategic planning, because firms can be at a serious strategic disadvantage if they are unable to control their operations firmly and manage them in a co-ordinated manner at home or globally.

The essence of Schumpeter's [36] hypothesis is that profitability is needed to support organizational innovation and that innovation is the core of effective competition. This is especially true in high wage countries, such as Singapore: competitiveness of firms depends critically on their ability to use IS to keep up in innovative products and processes. The recent World Competitive Report 1990 ranked Singapore first in the effective use of information technology [6]. Given the important role of IS, we are interested in what Singapore firms are doing with respect to IS strategic planning.

Our two objectives here are: (1) to compare the macro-organizational features (e.g., firm size, IS budget, nature of industry) of those Singapore firms that undertook IS strategic planning and those that did not, and (2) to describe existing IS planning practices in Singapore. We are not examining any theory of IS strategic planning because there is none; however we do have normative frameworks $[1,25,28,27]$ based on pragmatism and an historical perspective of what has and has not worked in the past. McLean and Soden's $[28]$ framework was used in this study because it is the most comprehensive for IS planning.

This study is especially important to Singapore companies. Through the efforts of the government's National Computer Board it has launched an ambitious national IT plan, (IT2000), which provides for various initiatives to make Singapore an ‘intelligent island’ in the next 15 years. There is now subtle pressure on companies to become strategic, given the governmental plans. This study provides insights on how some companies develop and implement their plans with their associated problems and opportunities.

## 2. Literature review on IS strategic planning

IS planning has been perceived as a very important activity. American IS professionals ranked IS strategic planning as the most important issue $[2,3,12,20]$ . Watson $[41]$ , using a three-round Delphi method, polled Australian IS executives on the key issues of IS. Again, IS strategic planning was a most important issue. Herbert and Hartog [21] expanded their study a year later with a sample size ten times larger. Aligning IS with business goals (ranked second in their earlier study) was the most important issue out of a list of 21. IS planning, which was ranked second earlier, slipped to sixth. There could, however, be a confusion in terminology. IS planning could conceivably subsume aligning IS with business goals. Their earlier study was conducted with IS executives in the St. Louis area, while their later study was conducted with responses from 600 IS executives drawn from Fortune 1000 companies. Companies that make it to the Fortune 1000 are usually large conglomerates, which tend to treat IS as a synergistic tool to co-ordinate the objectives of the enterprise.

An IS which does not serve corporate strategies should be a source of managerial concern and frustration [10]. Misalignment of IS and corporate strategy could have detrimental effect on organizational performance. Warner's [40] example of a manufacturing firm spending millions to automate a warehouse only to find it has lost its investment when, a year later, the firm adopted just-in-time inventory management. "What is appropriate strategy today may become inappropriate tomorrow, and technical advances provide a continuing source of change in information technology" [15]. Having a strategic information system vision is, therefore, important because it provides firms a competitive advantage [42].

It is, therefore, important for IS executives to obtain a clear statement of commitment from senior corporate managers who should be convinced that competitive advantage can accrue by means of a new (or enhanced) IS, and share their objectives with IS executives [26]. This sharing is essential for the practice of IS strategic planning.

## 3. Methodology

Although several important studies on the impact of IS planning efforts on organizations have been undertaken [27,33], little work has been done on the process of IS strategic planning. McLean and Soden [28] performed the first attempt to study the various facets of IS strategic planning practices. However, their sample was limited to the 20 top IS executives attending a conference on IS planning practices. They used two survey questionnaires. The first survey sought to determine “the objectives, development process and end products” of IS planning efforts. The follow-up sought to gather important data (e.g., degree of participation in the IS planning process by top management, IS managers and users) that were overlooked during the initial phase. Some 15 years later, Conrath et al [9] surveyed the IS strategic planning practices of 138 Canadian companies using a mailed questionnaire that contained most of the items in McLean and Soden’s follow-up questionnaires.

Some 40 items that pertained in one form or another to “potential pitfalls” in the planning process were dropped because some could also be interpreted as referring to the corporate planning process. ## 3.3. Analysis of the companies

Thirty-four out of the 70 participants (i.e., 48%) had some form of IS strategic planning process. The other 36 did not have any. Table 2 shows the results of the chi-square tests $^{2}$ between macro-organizational variables, such as firm size and industry type, and IS strategic planning.

## Firm size / IS sub-unit

Harris and Katz [19] Doll and Torkzadeh [13] used annual sales revenue as a measure of firm size; while Guimaraes and Gupta [17], and Raymond [34] used number of employees. We used both annual sales revenue and total number of employees. The results show that the number of firms that carried out IS strategic planning increase directly with annual sales revenue and total number of employees. The relationships between the proportion of firms that conducted IS strategic planning and annual sales revenue and total number of employees are significant (chi-square = 12.257, df = 3 and p < 0.007 in the case of annual sales revenue, and chi-square = 9.315, df = 3 and p < 0.025 in the case of total number of employees). In Conrath et al [9], the relationship was not significant when size was measured by revenue, though it was significant with respect to total number of employees.

To compete effectively, large firms have to co-ordinate their activities in order to secure a competitive advantage $[31]$ . Large firms are, therefore, more likely to undertake IS strategic planning in order to deliver strategic effort, whether in manufacturing, services, distribution, or sales; e.g., firms cited by Rockart $[35]$ as practising IS strategic planning are very large indeed.

The relationships between IS strategic planning and the pair “total IS budget and number of IS employees” are highly significant (chi-square = 12.753, df = 2 and p < 0.002 in the case of total IS budget, and chi-square = 6.913, df = 1 and p < 0.009 in the case of number of IS employees). The findings suggest that a large IS sub-unit has more resources and, therefore, is in a better position to undertake IS strategic planning. Recent statistical studies show that international differences in the levels of firms’ investments in technology explain differences in productivity and world market shares, not only with firms competing across national boundaries, but also with within national boundaries [35]. One should add that investment in information technology is only the first step; to yield tangible economic benefits, the investment must be carefully managed [19].

## Industry type

No relationship exists between industry type and the practice of strategic planning for IS. Our finding does not seem to support McFarlan et al's suggestion that IS strategic planning is more important for the finance and insurance industries for manufacturing.

## IS strategic plan / corporate plan

A firm's corporate plan should guide the IS strategic planning process [25,28,39,41]. Of the 34 that conducted strategic IS planning, 24 (71% compared to Conrath et al's 64%) modelled these plans upon the corporate plans. This is encouraging since it points to a reasonably high level of awareness of the need to link the IS strategic plan with the corporate plan [5,33]. The high proportion of firms that developed their IS strategic plans using corporate plans as a framework could also be explained, at least in part, by the lead taken by the Singapore Government in the IS strategic planning process [6,38].

Top management, IS management, and users reported high satisfaction levels (54, 64 and 42) with the IS strategic planning process. No low satisfaction responses were reported. ## Contents of the IS strategic plan

The questionnaire listed 14 items that might well be included in an IS strategic plan. The participants were asked whether each item was listed in their plan, and if not whether or not it should be. Table 5 lists the 14 items in the order of the frequency with which they were included

Results suggest that there is a broad consensus amongst IS executives regarding the contents of and IS strategic plan. The 6 items most frequently included are:

– Projection of possible future IS environment;

\- Hardware plan (i.e., computers to purchase over the planning horizon);

\- The statement of objectives for the IS sub-unit;

\- Systems development plan (i.e., the systems to be developed, their priorities);

\- Recommended implementation plan; and

\- Personnel plan (i.e., the staffing of the IS unit). The three items that survey participants generally excluded from the IS strategic plans are:

– Summary of strengths and weaknesses of the IS staff. Maybe, participants did not want to include this in a widely circulated document;

\- Definition and evaluation of alternate strategy. Including this implies a lack of rigor in the planning process;

\- Projection of possible industry environment. There is likely to be difficulty in gathering information on this. Environmental changes (e.g., consumer preferences, business processes, competitors' shift of strategy, available technologies) are difficult to monitor [16]. This has led to research on intelligent environmental monitoring systems. One such architecture is “Knowledge Cache,” a collection of semiautonomous agents called “Apprentices” [14]. The state of the art in such systems development is still in its embryonic stage.

## Planning objectives / their perceived attainment

Respondents were asked to rank the importance and degree of attainment of 18 planning objectives/purposes. For each, the respondents were asked to rank its importance on a 5 point scale (1 for least important) and the degree of attainment, also using a 5 point scale (1 for poor relative to expectation). The average values for each objective's importance and degree of attainment are shown in Table 6.

The top two objectives are:

\- Increasing top management support; and

\- Identifying new applications.

They are consistent with the high ranking of “improving communication with top management” and “forecasting resource requirements more accurately.” These are intricately tied to “improving communication with top management” and “improving resource requirements forecasting and allocation”.

Two exceptions are noted. First, 82% of our respondents rated “identifying new applications”

as having above-average importance (i.e., scoring it with a 4 or 5 on the 5 point scale). This is comparable to Conrath et al's 90 percent, but is in sharp contrast with McLean and Soden's 45 percent. The business environment is certainly more volatile than that of the mid 1970s. Environmental changes entail increased coordination of a firm's activities for it to remain competitive. This, in turn, makes demands on IS executives to identify, develop and deliver new applications.

Second, 59% of our respondents rated “increasing the visibility of IS in the company” as having above-average importance. This contrasts with McLean and Soden’s 25 percent. Conrath et al reported that a significantly greater proportion but gave no figures. To exploit information technology for strategic gains, top management perceives IS functions as a component of the corporate strategy.

Our perceived attainment results are at substantial variance with Conrath et al's. Our average values range from 3.03 (“increasing ROI of new applications” and “decreasing cost of computer operations”) to 3.84 (“increasing top management support”). Those reported by Conrath et al hovered around 3.00 (at expectation), the high being 3.17 (“improving morale and sense of purpose") and the low 2.72 ("increasing ROI of new applications"). For every objective, their average value of importance exceeded that of the perceived attainment (i.e., underachievement was reported for every objective), probably because a majority of their participants rated 3 on the 5 point scale. It is difficult to make any clear comparison between our results and McLean and Soden's, because they were reported in narrative form.

In general, the degree of attainment of the objectives was highly correlated with the degree of importance seen by the IS executives, except for the relatively high attainment recorded by “improving short-term decision making,” “securing budget increases,” “increasing ROI of new applications,” and “canceling marginal projects” The Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient (rs) between the two columns of average values is 0.995. The likelihood that this would arise by chance is very low (p < 0.0001). One certainly hopes that greater stress on importance of the more important objectives of the IS plan would result in higher degree of attainment. One factor that may bias the results is the reluctance of our respondents to admit that they underachieved objectives having high priority (this would reflect negatively on their ability).

## MIS steering committee / IS strategic plan

Seventy-nine percent of those firms that undertook IS planning had an MIS steering committee. This percentage is much higher than the 56 percent reported by Doll and Torkzadeh [13]. This is encouraging; it suggests increased sophistication of IS planning practices in Singapore. This is an effective way of getting top management involvement in the practice of IS planning. Other reasons given include: improving communications with top management, ensuring that systems developed are intended to support corporate objectives, acting as a mechanism for resource allocation for IS projects, and developing a set of criteria for prioritizing projects for development.

## 4. Discussion and implications

Though much has been written on the importance of IS strategic planning practices, little empirical research has been performed. The first study on IS strategic planning efforts per se was conducted by McLean and Soden, and the second by Conrath et al. Ours is probably the latest. Doll and Torkzadeh's is not a study on IS strategic planning per se; rather, it examined specific relationships between firm size and the use of MIS steering committees, and four components of IS planning (i.e., having a written overall plan, developing mutual agreement with top management on development priorities, securing long-term commitment by top management to fund IS projects, and separating maintenance and development costs in IS projects).

A high proportion of firms that took part in our study are very large by Singapore standards. 55% of our sample had annual revenue over S\$100 million; 49% had more than 500 employees. While the proportion of Singapore firms (49% that conducted IS strategic planning is comparable to that reported in Conrath et al, we expected a higher percentage because of the Singapore Government's policy of improving productivity through effective utilization of information technology. The policy should act as an impetus to firms to conduct IS strategic planning practices. It is possible that some IS executives who completed the questionnaire were not aware that their firms had some form of IS planning in process; we call this “organizational ignorance.”

Juxtaposed with the findings of Conrath et al and McLean and Soden, ours suggest that the level of sophistication of Singapore IS executives is comparable to that of their Canadian and American counterparts. Though the McLean and Soden study was done some 15 years ago, we have reasons to believe that today's American IS executives do not differ much from their Canadian counterparts, given the economic links between the two countries and the physical and psychic propinquity between the two groups of executives. The Singapore Government's policy of developing a set of national initiatives to sustain and enhance competitive advantage in international trade has spawned a generation of highly skilled IS professionals.

Our sample, though relatively large is still not large enough. Future studies should involve larger samples, and address other such as:

(1) Are there firm-specific factors that deter a firm from undertaking IS strategic planning?

(2) Why do some firms not model their IS plans after their corporate plans? Are there advantages to separating the two?
