## Abstract

Each of the overwhelming number of information systems analysis and design tools and methods has relative strengths and weaknesses that make it more or less appropriate for a particular design. Therefore, procedures for conducting comparative feature analysis are clearly needed. The primary focus of this paper is to demonstrate that speech act theoretic concepts are useful for analyzing differences between various systems analysis tools and methods. Using this theory, we analyze two separate systems analysis methods. We then compare and contrast the results of our analysis with findings obtained by other researchers. We conclude by providing tentative guidelines for matching systems analysis tools and methods with the design task.

## 1. Introduction

An increasing number of published accounts demonstrate innovative approaches to information system (IS) research based on ontology [42], semiotics [25,39], symbolism [19], narratology [30], discourse analysis [1], and communicative action [22]. We intend to demonstrate that speech act theory provides unique insight into the IS developmental process and is useful for investigating IS developmental (ISD) tools and methods.

Communication, often in the form of spoken discourse, is essential to determining information requirements. Gane et al. [14] state that structured methods have been particularly effective in improving communication between the analyst and the user. Ross [33] argues the structured analysis and design technique (SADT) is an actual language for expressing structure and communicating ideas. We contend it is a natural progression of ideas to investigate tools and methods of systems analysis and design using speech act theoretic concepts.

Our approach relates best to what Turner [40] refers to as the “sensitizing analytical scheme.” Its objective is to stimulate the formation of research propositions to be tested experimentally.

We propose that IS analysis tools and methods differ with respect to the communicative interaction they support and, furthermore, that these differences can be investigated using speech act theory. These in turn, enable the analyst to match observed speech act patterns with the tool or method of systems analysis best suited for the task in hand. We argue that describing the analyst-user pattern can lead to prescribing a tool or method of systems analysis.

## 2. Previous research

The contribution of communication to successfully developing IS is receiving increasing attention $[6,26,29]$ . The research results reflect two orientations: (1) identifying features of the design environment and investigating how they influence communication patterns, and (2) evaluating and analyzing the communicative interaction between the analyst and the user and investigating how the design environment can be structured to facilitate this process. Focusing on the design environment implies considering factors that affect ISD success, such as the personal characteristics and human limitations of analysts and users, the ISD methods used, and the kind of system being developed. Alternatively, focusing on language and communication suggests a primary interest in evaluating and analyzing the communicative interaction between the analyst and the user and the process is, perhaps, best described as meta-language.

The major objective of the two approaches is to contribute to fundamental or to strategic understanding. Fundamental understanding implies that the research increases theoretic insights into ISD activities and analyst-user interaction, that are independent of the specific tools and methods. Strategic understanding implies using the research results to establish linkages between design environmental factors or communication patterns and the appropriate ISD tools and methods.

To illustrate (upper left quadrant, Table 1), Guinan [17] first identifies features of the design environment in the form of high and low rated analysts. Next, she evaluates and analyzes the communicative interaction between analysts and users. Her primary objective is to develop a fundamental understanding of the role of communication in making analysts successful and to provide guidelines for training analysts. Alternatively, Vitalari [41] uses protocol analysis to investigate differences in the knowledge bases of high and low rated analysts. The primary objective of his research is to develop a fundamental understanding and to provide guidelines for analyst training.

The primary focus in the lower left quadrant is on language and communication. Hirschheim et al. [19], for example, use symbolism to analyze analyst and user discourses. A symbol generally denotes a word or phrase that signifies an object, which in turn signifies a different object. Thus a metaphor is a symbol because it signifies objects that are different from the object used in the metaphor [24]. An analyst, for example, might tell the user to think of a database software package as a large file cabinet. Thus the analyst equates two different objects that share similar functions. The interpretation of symbols is culturally dependent, because the user has to be familiar with a file cabinet before grasping the intentions of the analyst.

Hirschheim et al. [18] use the metaphor to interpret many analyst and user discourses. For example, some systems analysts see ISD as a struggle where user resistance has to be overcome.

They use such military metaphors as “clearing the path,” “winning the battle,” and “forcing the issue.” By analyzing analyst/user discourse using symbolism, the authors show that the actual practice of ISD does not progress according to the basic assumptions of economic rationality which is the hallmark of traditional ISD methods. Instead, they argue that ISD projects need to be interpreted as social actions and, thus, the research contributes to a fundamental understanding of ISD.

Jepson et al. [23] provide an alternative method for analyzing analyst/user discourse. They demonstrate the use of diaries in which analysts record their discourses with the users. The analyst can then introspectively reflect on possible causes of his or her and the user's behavior. The outcome of this process contributes to a fundamental understanding about analyst/user interaction. A result may be the analyst modifying individual interaction with the user in response to perceived shortcomings.

A different approach is taken by Lyytinen et al. [27], who reflect on the role of language in structuring social action. This knowledge is then applied to develop a better understanding of analyst/user behavior during the analysis, design, and implementation of office information systems.

## 3. Speech act theory and the communicative process

Speech act theory (AST) originated with Austin [3] and was further elaborated by Searle [37,38]. That language does something is demonstrated by the sentence “I promise you that I will complete the job by tomorrow.” By uttering this sentence the speaker performs three concurrent acts: he (1) says something meaningful, (2) does something in saying the sentence, and (3) affects the listener by saying the sentence. First, the speaker performs a locutionary act; the articulation of sounds that evoke and connect the concepts represented by the words. Second, the speaker performs an illocutionary act; making a promise. Third, the speaker performs a perlocutionary act; has an effect on the listener. By making the promise (illocution) the speaker tries to convince the listener the promise will be honored. In short, the speaker’s statement affects (perlocution) the listener’s feelings, thoughts, or actions.

Table 2 shows the relationships among these three acts and the requirements for a successful speech act. One is the correctness of the utterance with respect to syntactic rules. The desired illocutionary effect occurs when the listener grasps the meaning. The utterance obtains meaning for the speaker and the listener when it is analyzed on the basis of a shared social context. Therefore, syntactic correctness by itself is not sufficient for understanding; there also has to be agreement with prevailing social norms.

Illocutionary force plays an important role in the overall success of the speech act. This concept expresses the degree of fit between the speech act and the social environment in which it is used. For example, when the speaker intends the listener to act in a certain way he or she can try to effect this by ordering or by politely requesting the listener to do so. However, of these two the polite request clearly is more appropriate when the speaker is in a less powerful position. Thus, illocutionary force arises out of the degree of fit between the words and the conditions in the environment in which they are used.

Speech act theory is concerned with the pragmatic use of language.

Speech acts are performed for (1) making assertions about facts and states of nature, (2) requesting someone to do something, (3) making promises and commitments, (4) bringing about changes, and (5) expressing feelings and emotions. Accordingly, Searle presents an exhaustive speech act classification that matches these objectives with (1) assertives, (2) directives, (3) commissives, (4) declaratives, and (5) expressives.

These concepts can be illustrated by analyzing several of the cryptic messages that are part of the exchange between a bank customer who withdraws cash from an account and an automatic teller machine (ATM). Because the purpose of this interaction is uniquely goal oriented, this example accentuates the performative aspects.

Table 3 illustrates the components of a speech act. The assertive denotes speech acts that inform the listener of facts or states of nature. For example, “Your Transaction Is Complete” describes the condition of the customer’s ATM transaction. Its specific function is informing.

The directive denotes speech acts that request the listener to do something. Hence, “Please Take Your Card” is requesting. The speech act’s purpose is to motivate the listener to withdraw the card.

The commissive denotes speech acts that commit the speaker to accomplishing specified acts. "Our CDs Pay 8.25% Interest" has the specific function of promising and commits the speaker to making good on a promise. The overall purpose is to persuade the customer to invest monies.

The declarative is used to effect change. The speaker brings about a match between the world and the words by the act of vocalizing the utterance. The successful completion represented by “Your Card Canceled After 3 Invalid PIN Entries” hinges on the speaker’s authority to make such decisions. It also follows that the card is not returned.

The expressive implies a certain psychologic state by the speaker or by the transmitter of the message. The utterance “We Apologize For The Inconvenience” has the specific function of apologizing by expressing concern for the customer’s possible feelings of irritation. The implicit goal is to make the inconvenience more acceptable.

The orientation toward a goal suggests specific validity criteria for assessing the efficacy of the speech act. A successful assertive act implies propositional truth, a successful directive act indicates appropriateness to the situation, a successful commissive act implies sincerity and the ability of the speaker to deliver what is promised, a successful declarative act requires the speaker possess the authority to effect the action, and a successful expressive act necessitates the speaker be sincere.

## 4. Speech acts and analyst-user communication

How can speech act theory be useful to ISD? Although effective communication is necessary throughout the design process, we restrict our discussion to analyst-user communication in the context of information requirement analysis.

Table 4 matches speech act occurrences with three commonly occurring systems analysis situations $^{1}$ . Table SA-USE is not exhaustive. Speech act behavior could differ somewhat from these generic patterns without interfering with the essence of our argument. Case I illustrates a user who might not be able to see what tasks can be automated. The user therefore lacks a clear grasp of information requirements and, in fact, might be altogether unable to see how an IS can help him.

Case II depicts speech act usage when the user is familiar with information technology and information requirements. Directives and assertives feature heavily in the analyst/user dialogue. The analyst employs directives in the form of questions to obtain the information requirements as well as for user action. For example, the analyst can ask the user to produce certain documents. The user may respond with directives for additional information-to clarify any ambiguities. When the user knows and is willing to disclose the information requirements he or she will give definitive answers. Thus the user responds with assertives. The analyst may use commissives for promising the user particular system features. This is sometimes necessary to encourage cooperation.

Case III occurs when the IS is requested by the user, who is also knowledgeable about systems analysis, requirements analysis, and information technology. The user takes a commanding role: specifying system features and setting deadlines for the analyst. The user's communicative process features a heavy use of assertives and directives toward action. The analyst is limited to employing directives and commissives. The directives, in the form of questions, are needed to obtain design information and system requirements. The commissives are used when the analyst agrees to accomplish what the user directs him or her to do.

These cases can be used as templates of the prevailing patterns of speech act usage. For example, when the user employs directives exclusively for obtaining information, a Type I case is indicated: the communicative pattern suggests that the user is unable to disclose the needed information.

## 5. Studying systems analysis methods with speech act theory

Language enables the formulation and expression of ideas through a differential interaction. Thus, what is expressible and the manner of expression are determined by the nature of the signs that comprise a language $[5,36]$ . Methods for information analysis also function as a system of signs and, therefore, are similarly constrained. Thus a method for systems analysis and design places limits on the type and scope of design solutions that the analyst can conceptualize. It also suggests that a method can be unsuited for a particular application environment and that its inappropriate use may lead to system failure.

## 5.1. Expressing processing logic using structured english and decision table

Consider a user who shares a decision-making process with the analyst in the following way: If the plane is over half full and tickets cost \$350 or more per seat, we serve free cocktails, unless it is a domestic flight. We charge for cocktails on all domestic flights where cocktails are served, i.e., those flights that are more than half filled.

Structured English and decision tables are separate, perhaps simple, languages. One can argue that the analyst would detect the missing data regardless of the language used to express the problem. However, analysts are fallible and situations are often more complex than this example.

We do not claim that decision tables are always preferred over structured English. In fact, decision tables cannot describe a processing logic that involves looping. Furthermore, if a processing logic is formally documented, for example, as office procedures that have been tested for many years, then the deficiency of structured English (as just described) is less serious.

The analyst-user communication patterns for the decision-making example are analyzed in terms of Searle's speech act categories (Table 6). In the case of structured English, the user employs assertives to describe the processing logic to the analyst and to respond to the analyst's questions. In cases of ambiguity concerning the decision-making process the analyst employs directives to obtain additional information from the user. Because structured English does not readily support verifying the decision-making logic, using alternative speech acts is less likely.

The decision table, however, generates additional user-analyst communication. The user describes the processing logic to the analyst (assertives). The analyst clarifies uncertainties (directives). The user replies to the analyst's questions (assertives). The analyst documents the processing logic using the decision table. The analyst may then find additional ambiguities, contradictions, inconsistencies, and uncovered rules, going back to the user to resolve them (directives-information). The user might respond with further questions if he or she does not understand the analyst's requests (directives). The user might need some time to come up with answers because the problem has never occurred before. In this case, the analyst requests the user to work out a solution (directives-action), and the user promises the analyst to provide an answer at a later date (commissives).

To illustrate the appropriateness of a particular method, consider two users working in an environment where work procedures are well established. However, one user is very experienced at describing the work and the other is not. The communicative behaviors of the analyst and the experienced user and the analyst and the inexperienced user correspond to types III and I, respectively, with the user-analyst communication patterns of structured English and decision table very similar. In fact, structured English is recommended for the experienced user, because it is less likely this user will omit something important, and the expressive power of structured English will help the analyst to describe constructs such as loops. However, in the case of the inexperienced user a decision table is preferred: it gives rise to a speech act pattern more conducive to gathering information and to checking completeness. Thus the preponderance of user and analyst directive speech acts is decisive for evaluating the appropriateness of a method.

Consider now a different situation where the analyst initially uses a language tool based on the incorrect assumption that the user fits into the Type III (i.e., experienced) category. This results in a mismatch between the language tool and the task environment. The analyst can detect this mismatch by monitoring closely the type and mix of speech acts employed during the analyst-user communicative process. For example, the analyst may consider switching to decision tables for describing processing logic when the user resorts frequently to directives to obtain additional information from the analyst. The decision table focuses the analyst's attention on the causes of the user's uncertainty, leading to their successful resolution.

It is not always possible to select or switch tools such as structured English and decision tables during systems development: some organizations have standardized the choice of tools and methods in order to gain consistency. It is also possible that a mismatch was discovered at a stage where switching tools was impractical (e.g., switching to a different tool will duplicate effort). Nevertheless, knowing the appropriateness of a tool will help the analyst to avoid its potential shortcoming For example, knowing that the user belongs to Type I (i.e., inexperienced) category when using structured English suggests the need to check for possible missing information.

## 5.2. Linking systems analysis methods with design environments

Because of greatly diverging properties, the Jackson method of systems analysis and design (JSD) and structured analysis and design technique (SADT) are appropriate vehicles for illustrating the claim that analyzing speech act performance is useful in uncovering deficiencies in the communicative process. Drawing on the results of comparative studies [9] we find theoretic support for the proposition that speech act patterns can be linked to systems analysis methods.

## Communication pattern of the JSD method

JSD develops formal systems specifications in a series of distinct phases [7,8,21]:

1. Modeling: identifying real world entities and their structural relationships, and organizing them into a preliminary model of the IS

2. Networking: connecting the model in the previous step with external events (i.e., input and output of the model)

3. Implementing: translating the specifications to be run in a computer environment

Aspects of analyst-user communication for JSD are shown in Table 7. In the modelling stage, the user describes the application to the systems analyst (i.e., assertives). The systems analyst then identifies events and entities in the user's description and questions the user about missing information (i.e., directives). The user responds to the analyst's directives with assertives. JSD's role at this stage is restricted to analyzing and recording the user's description in terms of events and entities. Because this process is similar in character to structured English, we expect assertives and directives to dominate analyst-user communication.

During the networking phase the analyst defines the user's information requirements as events and entities and forms connections between them. Although this activity proceeds with little input from the user, occasional inconsistencies and missing information require user involvement. In this instance, assertive and directive statements are used in the same way as during the modeling phase. Finally, during the implementation phase, the systems analyst will perform all of the translations without communicating with the user.

Table 8 shows that the user employs many assertive statements whereas the analyst uses many directive statements. Both JSD and structured English give rise to user communicative patterns dominated by assertives.

## Communication pattern of the SADT method

SADT [28,34,35] divides the systems life cycle into three phases similar to that of JSD. To enhance communication and to improve description, a rich and compact graphic symbol is employed in SADT. Activity and data, at each phase, are modelled by a box with four arrows that represent input, control, mechanism, and output (Figure 2). The box mechanism is the basis for expressing 1) system functions and relationships, and 2) consumption of resources by processes.

The user employs commissives to promise the analyst to investigate their implications on the information system. For this reason, SADT is more likely than JSD to result in richer analyst-user communicative behavior. The analyst-user communication pattern for the SADT method is summarized in the second row of Table 8.

SADT is preferred when the user is familiar with the information requirements but the application is not user-requested (Type II, Table 4). ## Comparing the results with existing beliefs

Combining the comparison work done by others (e.g., [12]), areas of strength of the two systems analysis and design methods are shown in Table 10. SADT outperforms JSD in supporting all facets of systems analysis. JSD, however, outranks SADT in system design and implementation. This also indicates some important differences between the two methods. SADT is hierarchical, JSD is not. JSD is basically process driven in its approach to system modelling, whereas SADT can accommodate both activity and data based views.

JSD and SADT are suited for applications with different characteristics. JSD is best when systems specifications are reasonably well understood and the user is able to communicate them clearly and unambiguously. An additional benefit of using JSD in this context is its strength in systems design and implementation. However, when the ambiguity of information requirements is accompanied by conflicting demands from different users SADT is a better choice.

Although the main result in the table (i.e., matching methods with design environments) is not new, we claim we have proposed a useful approach to compare different systems analysis methods, and the promise of this approach is demonstrated. Furthermore, we believe this approach can provide additional insight into the different methods that existing approaches cannot provide.

## 6. Conclusion

Our objective has been to create what Turner refers to as “analytic sensitizing schemata” for formulating propositions that can be subjected to experimental testing. We propose that observing, analyzing, and classifying speech act patterns provides important insights into the analyst-user relationship and the design environment. These insights help the analyst to choose a course of action, method of user information requirements assessment, or to evaluate the efficacy of systems analysis methods in a particular design environment.

The connection centers on the fact that the effectiveness and efficiency of these two activities are language dependent. Our focus has been on speech act behavior in the context of information systems analysis and design. However, identifying and applying speech act patterns does not have to be limited to this environment. For example, it can be applied equally well for knowledge acquisition in the case of expert systems design.
