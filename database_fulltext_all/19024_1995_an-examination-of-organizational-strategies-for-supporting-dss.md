# Research

# An examination of organizational strategies for supporting DSS

Stephen R. Hawk $^{a,*}$ , Martin L. Bariff $^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Parkside, 900 Wood Rd., Box 2000 Kenosha, Wisconsin 53141-2000 USA $^{b}$ Stuart School of Business, Illinois Institute of Technology, 10 West 31st Street Chicago, Illinois 60616 USA

## Abstract

Decision support systems (DSS) are widely used by managers to provide problem solving assistance. Despite their widespread use, little is known about how organizations support DSS users. This paper discusses support practices that are thought to affect DSS groups' ability to provide support services, and reports on a field study that sought to identify the support characteristics of a sample of groups. This study also investigated differences among groups that were placed in different organizational locations. Managers of twenty-three DSS groups were interviewed to provide information on group organizational location, staff-to-user ratio, staff backgrounds, formalization, and other support characteristics. The results suggest that DSS groups generally follow some of the recommendations found in the literature with respect to their management while disregarding others. The support characteristics also tend to vary when comparing groups across organizational locations, suggesting that certain strengths and limitations may be associated with organizational support strategies.

Keywords: Decision support systems (DSS); DSS groups; Managing DSS; End-user computing; Information center

## 1. Introduction

The DSS area has been the focus of considerable research for many years [3]. Since the introduction of the DSS concept in the early 1970s, they have become commonplace in many organizations. Despite the attention given by academia and industry, relatively little is known about how organizations support DSS development and use. Early research addressing organizational support for DSS suggests that the using departments generally played a leading role in the development and management of DSS, while the MIS department's role tended to be limited to providing hardware or technical support [13,26]. More recent research has focused on the information center as a means of supporting end-user computing [5,18,27,30]. Since most end user applications are decision support in nature [25], such literature provides insight into a strategy used by many organizations to support DSS. To understand how organizations support DSS, however, we need to examine other strategies for delivering support services. Evidence for this view is provided by recent surveys which show that a variety of strategies besides information centers are in common use [10,29].

Our study was undertaken to provide some insight into the nature of different organizational strategies for supporting DSS development and use. By taking an organizational perspective, the emphasis is on the organizational entities that are responsible for supporting DSS. The term DSS group refers to this entity; it plans, supports, and controls DSS activities as an important element of its mission. Although information centers have been extensively examined, research on other DSS support strategies is relatively limited.

A number of other organizational strategies exist for providing DSS support. These include [28]:

1. Traditional applications development group

2. Operations research or management science group

3. Planning department or staff analysis group within a functional department

4. A formally chartered DSS group

5. A DSS group within end-user services

Specialized staff groups frequently evolve within planning or functional area departments. The formally chartered DSS group is a standalone unit, within the MIS department, that concentrates on supporting DSS. A DSS group located within an end-user services group is similar to this, except that it is located in a group that houses other end-user support groups, such as an information center. DSS groups can accomplish their support mission by providing end-user computing support services such as training, consulting, hardware/software evaluation, and access to corporate data $[6,9]$ . Many DSS groups also provide other services, such as DSS development, maintenance, and operation.

This paper presents five DSS support group characteristics that have been proposed as important to the success of DSS groups in accomplishing their support mission. Functional areas of their users are also included in this examination in order to provide information about who uses the services. These characteristics will be used as a basis for investigating the support practices of a sample of DSS groups that are situated in different organizational locations. The purpose of this examination is two-fold. First, such examination can provide a better understanding of the state of DSS practice, including, where possible, a comparison of current practice with that which has been recommended. Second, an analysis of similarities and differences of groups in different locations may shed light on the potential strengths and limitations of the DSS support strategies employed in organizations.

## 2. Factors involved in providing DSS support services

Despite limited empirical work on this topic, the DSS literature suggests a number of factors that could be varied to improve the performance of support groups. The intent in summarizing normative statements concerning the management of each factor is not to propose guidelines appropriate for all organizations under all circumstances, but rather to bring the various suggestions into focus. Our work presents five factors related to the management and operation of DSS support groups. Two of these (staff backgrounds and staff-to-user ratio) concern decisions surrounding group staffing. Two others (services provided and use of an evolutionary development methodology) relate to the nature of a group's services. The fifth, formalization, indicates the extent to which the methods for managing and operating the groups are guided by formal written policies. As noted earlier, functional areas of users served by the groups was included as a sixth characteristic.

## 2.1. Staff-to-user ratio

As this ratio becomes smaller, it will be less likely that users can receive much individualized attention: too many users are served by each staff member. Additionally, a low staff-to-user ratio would probably result in decreased responsiveness to users' needs. This could be very important, since many decision support needs are ad hoc in nature with short turn-around requirements [14]. Several studies have shown that an information center staff member typically supports about 100 users [19,22]. Although no measure exists for comparing the adequacy of a group's staff-to-user ratio, it should probably be higher than this to lessen the strains on staff resources [28].

## 2.2. Staff backgrounds

Two different factors have been suggested as being important to the success of a DSS group. Staff members with a conventional data processing background would provide the in-depth technical, systems development expertise that would allow for development of complex or large-scale DSS that are beyond the capabilities of most end users $[11]$ . Such staffs would also be an asset in providing end-computing support services since many of users' needs are technical in nature. DSS groups also need staff with a good understanding of the users' area of application. By hiring staff with backgrounds similar to those of the users, groups would tend to be more oriented to the needs, and should be able to fully understand the users' DSS requirements $[15]$ . DSS groups should therefore attempt to develop a staff with a mix of technical and business backgrounds.

## 2.3. Services provided

Groups can support DSS by providing end-user computing support services, such as training, consultation, and technical support. The mix of services can be an important influence on the nature of an organization's use of DSS. The sort of applications developed by end users would tend to be ad hoc and limited in scope [24]. Exclusive reliance on end user DSS development would therefore tend to limit the development of larger scale, departmental or organization-wide DSS, or DSS whose development requires time and technical capabilities that are beyond the typical user. If an organization's DSS needs go beyond such small-scale systems, it should include DSS building as one of its services.

## 2.4. Use of an evolutionary development methodology

Adoption of an evolutionary, iterative approach has been widely accepted as the appropriate method for developing DSS $[7,20]$ . The use of an evolutionary methodology tends to result in the delivery of higher quality DSS within a shorter time frame than the traditional life cycle approach [1,17] and should therefore be the methodology of choice for DSS groups.

## 2.5. Formalization

DSS groups may adopt policies that govern how various DSS support activities are performed. The degree of a group's formalization is dependent upon the number of DSS activities governed by such policies. By establishing codes of good practice related to the planning, delivery and evaluation of DSS services, formalization should help to increase the quality and consistency of DSS services and may ensure orderly and productive use of DSS support resources [4,8]. Neal and Radnor's [21] study tends to confirm the recommendations found in the prescriptive literature: they found that formalization of management science groups was positively associated with group effectiveness, suggesting a similar pattern for other types of DSS support groups.

## 2.6. Functional area of users

The percentage of users located in the different functional areas was measured in order to provide a profile of the groups' users. We expected that groups located in functional area or planning departments would concentrate their services on users within their “home” departments, while groups reporting within MIS were expected to support users from different functional areas more evenly.

## 3. Research method

Fifty-seven Midwest organizations were contacted by the researchers to provide information about the support practices of DSS groups. Twenty-three of the organizations agreed to participate in a cross-sectional field study, a response rate of 40.4%. The potential existence of DSS groups in organizations was suggested by the following resources: a directory of top computer executives: listings of recent DSS conference attendees: a customer list provided by a DSS software vendor, and: the authors' personal contacts.

Phone calls made on the basis of these leads eventually led to a conversation with the manager of a DSS group, or the fact that no DSS group existed in the organization.

We attempted to include groups that represented a variety of organizational DSS support strategies. We defined DSS in our initial discussions with firms, using criteria described by Hogue and Watson [12]: groups were included in the sample if an important element of their mission was to provide services related to the development and operation of DSS for users. Of the fifty-seven organizations, 29 did not meet our selection criteria: i.e., organizations were excluded if they had no DSS group or if they provided very limited DSS services, such as formal classroom training, data downloading, or hardware installation. Four of these organizations had recently dissolved their DSS group, while three others were in the process of establishing one. Five out of the 28 groups that met the selection criteria declined involvement in the study, citing policy, workload, or confidentiality concerns. Thus, the response rate for groups that met our selection criteria was 82.1%. Summary information on the industries of the sampled organizations is shown in Table 1. The average number of employees was 20,300, with an average annual sales of \$5.2 billion.

During an in-person structured interview, DSS group managers provided background information about their organizations, their groups' organizational placement, policies, development methodology, staff backgrounds, services, and the locations of users. Since data were collected in an interview setting, we were able to ensure that questions and answers were understood. This data collection approach also allowed respondents to provide comments and qualitative explanations that were useful in interpreting the results.

## 3.1. Measures

Some of the measures (such as group location and year of formation) are fairly straight-forward and need little explanation. The following describes the method for measuring staff backgrounds, staff size, staff-to-user ratio, services offered, development methodology, formalization, and functional areas of users.

Staff background, Staff size, and Staff-to-user ratio: DSS managers were asked to identify each staff member's most recent occupation prior to being hired into the group using the categories listed in Table 3. The totals of all staff members for all backgrounds were summed to indicate the staff size. The staff background measure indicates the percentage of a group's staff from a particular background. This was determined by dividing the number of staff with a given background by the total staff in the group. DSS managers were asked to estimate the number of users their group had personally contacted during the previous year while providing DSS services; this indicated the number of users. Staff-to-user ratio was determined by dividing staff size by the number of users.

Services provided by the DSS groups: In examining the services provided by DSS groups, we were interested in the relative effort spent on providing various types of services. This approach allows for a comparison of the type of support provided by groups in different locations. DSS group managers were provided with a list of services and were asked to determine the percentage of their group's effort that was used in providing each service. The number of categories needed to be relatively small in order to make this a manageable task. The services included were:

Training - both classroom and individual instruction

Technical support - database maintenance, data downloading, network support, hardware/software troubleshooting, and hardware repair.

Conceptual DSS design – helping users formulate a DSS solution at the conceptual level, e.g. what data are needed, where will they come from, what sort of retrieval and analysis routines are needed. This could occur while assisting users in defining the needs of an application that will eventually be implemented by users or the DSS staff.

DSS building - implementing the DSS design, including data base creation, development of methods for maintaining data, programming retrieval/analysis routines, and constructing interfaces between different software tools.

Selecting appropriate software - helping the users decide which software tool is appropriate for their problem

DSS Operation - running the DSS applications.

Use of an Evolutionary Development Methodology: DSS managers were asked to estimate the proportion of the DSS developed by their group using an evolutionary approach versus that developed using a sequential life cycle approach. A score of 0% indicates complete reliance on a sequential methodology while 100% indicates that all were developed using an evolutionary approach.

Formalization: DSS group managers were asked to state whether a formal, written policy was in place to prescribe how DSS management and service delivery issues should be handled. Policy issues covered by the items in this measure were suggested by prior discussions of DSS formalization. An index of formalization was constructed by summing the number of activities governed by formal, written policies. Although the specific policy issues are somewhat different, this procedure is similar to that used by Neal and Radnor in their study of management science groups. The issues included in this measure are shown in the appendix.

Functional area of users: DSS managers were asked to estimate the percentage of users served by their groups that were in each of the following organizational locations: marketing/sales, accounting/finance, production/operations, human resources, information systems management, corporate planning and other.

In discussing group similarities and differences, we concentrate on comparing the characteristics of information centers, formally chartered groups, and groups reporting outside of the MIS department (there were seven sampled groups in each of these locations, but only two in an end-user services department). To determine whether a characteristic differed across groups, Dunn's multiple comparison procedure was used [16]. This test is appropriate when performing two or more planned comparisons between group means. In the case of the present results, we performed three comparisons for each characteristic; (1) information centers vs. formally chartered DSS groups, (2) information centers vs. functional area groups, and (3) formally chartered DSS groups vs. functional area groups. In addition to presenting the means for information centers, formally chartered groups, and functional area groups, the means for end user services groups and each of the specific functional area locations are shown in the tables.

The results suggest that groups in the various locations differed in terms of staff size, number of users, and staff-to-user ratios. Information centers tended to have more staff members than formally chartered groups (p < 0.05) or functional area groups (p < 0.05). Information centers also served more users than either of these (p < 0.01). Finally, information centers had a smaller staff-to-user ratio than either formally chartered (p < 0.10) or functional area groups (p < 0.10).

As shown in Table 3, the most common background was traditional data processing systems development. Only three of the 23 groups in the sample (one in MIS, and two outside) had no staff with a traditional data processing background. The next most common staffing approach was to hire people who had work experience in the functional areas served by the group (e.g. finance, marketing, operations). In the case of groups located in functional area departments, this often occurred as a natural outgrowth of the groups' evolution. Prior to the establishment of these groups, a core of managers became their departments' experts in using DSS software. They continued in this role when their groups were formally established. In other cases, a deliberate effort was made to hire staff with backgrounds matching those of their users.

The findings indicate that formally chartered groups devoted a smaller proportion of their time to technical support services than either information centers (p < 0.01) or functional area groups (p < 0.10). Information centers, on the other hand, spent a smaller proportion of their time on DSS building than either formally chartered groups (p < 0.05) or functional area groups (p < 0.05). Table 5 shows the group means for use of an evolutionary development methodology and number of formal policies. As can be seen, the DSS groups tended to be managed in a fairly informal manner. The mean number of formal policies for the sample was 2.7; only three groups had more than 5. Although DSS managers tended to be unconcerned by the relative informality, some expressed concern about having no method developed for handling DSS group evaluation, DSS planning, and prioritization of user requests. The relative informality of DSS group management found in this study has been observed previously in the context of information centers. No significant differences in formalization were found among the groups.

The average proportion of DSS developed in an evolutionary manner was 70%. Only one group used a sequential, life cycle approach for all DSS development. Five groups developed all DSS in an evolutionary manner. Thus, groups tend to follow recommendations that DSS should be developed using an iterative approach. There were no significant differences in the groups' use of an iterative approach.

The user percentages shown in Table 6 indicate that the marketing/sales and accounting/finance areas had the largest percentage of users. Included in the other category were users in engineering, R&D, and senior executives. Except for some differences in categories, these results are similar to those of Watson et al.: i.e., users in marketing/sales and finance/accounting tend to be the major consumers of DSS services, while those in human resources and data processing tend to be minor users. In our sample, corporate planning departments were significant users of DSS services. No discernable pattern emerges in the number of users found in different locations when the groups reporting within MIS are examined. We did not conduct significance tests when comparing the distribution of users across group locations, since our main expectation for these results was that functional area groups would tend to focus on users within the same department and other groups' users would be more evenly distributed across different areas. Most users served by the groups located in finance/accounting, however, were found outside this department. All groups located in non-MIS departments supported users outside their own department.

The results suggest some potential limitations of using an information center as a DSS support group. Since information centers supported a relatively large number of users per staff member, DSS users would be less likely to receive the same level of service provided by the other groups. This was reflected in the relatively low level of effort spent on DSS building by information centers. A second difference relates to staff backgrounds. The low proportion of staff with functional area backgrounds in information centers suggests that such staff would be less likely to understand the functional areas being supported than the other groups.

Although functional area groups had a lower proportion of staff with a conventional systems development background than formally chartered groups, they seemed to have greater technical capabilities than one might expect. A potential problem with providing support through functional area groups may be the lack of technical expertise on the part of their staffs. Five of the 7 groups in functional area departments, however, had at least one staff member with conventional data processing systems development background. This suggests that these groups may have been able to compensate for the supposed technical weakness of other staff members by hiring people with a complementary set of skills. It also did not appear that the staff with non-data processing backgrounds in these groups were as technically weak as one might presume. DSS support work was the sole occupation of most of the staff in these groups. This provided them the opportunity to develop the necessary technical skills. With increasingly easy to use fourth generation tools, the time required to become technically proficient is fairly short. Also, the staff with functional-area backgrounds usually had a fairly good set of technical skills prior to their becoming DSS staff members. Many had informally provided computer support to their co-workers before being hired or transferred into a group.

Support groups located in the MIS department tended to serve users who were dispersed throughout the organizations more than groups in the functional areas. However, the functional groups did support a number of users outside their departments. This was especially so for groups in the accounting/finance area. This suggests that functional area groups saw their mission as one that extended beyond their departments' boundaries.

## 5. Conclusions

Conventional systems development continues to be the predominant background of DSS staff. Despite recommendations to the contrary, people with functional area backgrounds tend to account for a relatively small proportion of DSS staff. Lack of staff with backgrounds similar to those of their users will likely result in a limited ability to understanding users' DSS needs. According to Keen [15], this will tend to result in support being limited to aid users understand how to use the DSS tools. Such staffs will be less capable of taking a more active role, such as suggesting how the tools could be used to solve the users' problems.

The staff-to-user ratio for information centers in the our sample is similar to what has been reported in prior research. The staff-to-user ratios for formally chartered and functional area groups, however, are much higher. This suggests that organizations that have developed these groups have recognized the need to increase staff for better support.

Although most groups were involved in developing DSS for users, there seemed to be a widespread opinion among the group managers that their groups do too many things that users should be able to do on their own. This suggests that a challenge facing many DSS managers is the division of DSS development responsibilities between DSS staff and end users. Although the goal of some managers was to get users to do more application development themselves, they recognized the continued need to do some DSS development for users. To leverage the staff resources, many groups apparently need to develop better user understanding and willingness to do as much as they can, leaving the more technically challenging or time consuming tasks to the DSS staff.

Few differences may exist between DSS groups in functional area departments and formally chartered DSS groups. Both:

\* had a relatively high staff-to-user ratio.

\* had a relatively high proportion of staff with functional area backgrounds.

\* placed greater emphasis on building DSS for users than information centers.

These factors suggest that formally chartered and functional area DSS groups tend to have a more appropriate mix of staff for supporting DSS and are better able to concentrate on providing a full set of DSS support services than information centers. As a result, organizations may wish to consider the creation of DSS groups in these locations if small scale, ad hoc DSS are not an adequate solution to the decision making needs of their managers. Although the responsibilities of some information centers have evolved over the years to include some systems development, their fundamental purpose continues to be the support of applications development by end users.
