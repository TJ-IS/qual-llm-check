# Attitudes of end-users towards information technology in manufacturing and service industries

Oya Culpan

School of Business Administration, The Pennsylvania State, University at Harrisburg, Middletown, PA 17057, USA

## Abstract

This paper proposes a conceptual framework for end-user attitudes towards information technology and defines how end-users initially develop interest in and learn about computers. Additionally, it reports the results of an empirical inquiry in two industries, based on the conceptual model. The findings suggest that end users in the manufacturing industry manifest attitudes and intentions towards computer use that differ from those of users in the service industry. The research suggests the need for consideration of industry-specific characteristics in information systems research and applications. Finally, the paper discusses managerial implications of the findings and recommends that managers integrate end-user and computer technology.

Keywords: End user attitudes; Computer use and decision making; End users and promotion; End users and information technology; End users in manufacturing sector; End users in service sector; Purpose of end user computer usage; End users and motivation

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of information technology (IT) in the last two decades has affected business organizations profoundly. One major impact has been on users of the technology. Many employees had to learn how to work with this emerging technology. IT has offered advantages such as increased speed, greater productivity, and convenience of operation, but end-users had to adjust to change. Learning about and then working with IT required employees to acquire new skills and knowledge to modify old attitudes. Hence, end-users have become the focus of various research studies. Many researchers $[7,14,18,21]$ have studied the attitudes and motivation of end users and attempted to identify issues pertaining to them;

they also offered suggestions aimed at achieving useful integration of end users with IT.

Although various researchers defined end-users in a variety of different ways [19], we identify them as common users who interact with ready-made software through a limited set of proceduralized written instructions. Thus the persons surveyed here are representative of non-programmer or non-systems personnel working in industry in south-central Pennsylvania.

The objectives of this paper are to provide a conceptual framework for discussion of end-user attitudes towards IT, to define how end-users initially develop interest in and learn about the computers that perform selected applications, and to identify end-user attitudes in the manufacturing and service industries. In addition, the paper examines the purpose of end-user computer usage in these different kinds of industries, and provides suggestions to managers on integrating IT for end users.

## 2. Previous studies

It is important to know how end-users in industry develop an interest in computer technology and acquire knowledgeability about computers and the skills needed to carry out assigned tasks. From this perspective, the preparation and training of end users has received much attention $[3,14,20]$ . Because end-users must learn a variety of new skills and because they must adjust to a new work environment, management must provide them with substantial support. Therefore, some researchers emphasize managerial and staff support for end-users $[11,13]$ . Overall, the most important factor involved in successfully introducing IT is that of providing appropriate training for employees.

There is a significant relationship between end users' attitudes and their degree of command over the use of an information system. End-users must react favorably to a system to ensure that it will be used widely and effectively. This is why IT literature is replete with studies of the attitudes of end-users. Some researchers have tested for evidence of user attitudes via such popular management models as “the expectancy model of motivation” and “the Job Diagnostic Survey”(JDS) [8]. Yaverbaum's [23] investigation of the relationship between work design and user attitudes utilized the JDS developed by Hackman and Oldham [9]. The researcher's study [23] showed that clerical end-users felt that their work setting was more stimulating than the traditional one without computerization; managers and professionals, on the other hand, did not perceive their jobs to be more stimulating than previously. As a result, experts recommended establishing support centers for end-users and education programs for the managers and professionals. The virtue of Yaverbaum's study is its integration of task and organization factors with user factors in explaining user satisfaction and motivation.

Some other scholars examined stress and anxiety as demonstrated by end-users stationed for work at a micro computer terminal [22]. For example, Igbaria and Parasumaran [12] stated the following:

...education is negatively related to computer anxiety and external locus of control and math anxiety contributes to increased computer anxiety. Managers' age, education, external locus of control, and the feeling-thinking dimension of cognitive style are related to attitudes toward microcomputers. However, math anxiety and external locus of control affect microcomputer user attitudes only indirectly which is the strongest predictor of negative attitudes toward microcomputers.

Despite wide coverage of user attitudes, no study has focused on them in relationship to industries that are naturally different from each other. Do end-users, for example in different industries show similar attitudes? Do industry-specific conditions involve different IT applications? Do these conditions reflect an end-user's emotional reactions? For example, while Computer Aided Design and Computer Aided Manufacturing are common applications in the manufacturing industry, spread-sheet and database applications are popular in the service industry. Most computer professionals believe that the assimilation application of some categories of technology require many cognitive tools and special behavior by the end-user. Hence, application of production and knowledge-based technology may have different results. For example, by grouping knowledge-based technologies by two kinds of criteria – task variability and problem analyzability, Perrow [17] devised a classification for four different types of technology: routine; engineering; craft; and nonroutine. Each category of technology, as listed, has its distinct concrete and functional characteristics and hierarchy of methods. As a result, people using different kinds of technologies might exhibit different attitudes towards their work. In other words, the core dimensions of a job, (namely task variety, task identity, task significance, feedback received while performing a task, and task autonomy) vary from one situation to another. Therefore, industry-specific conditions will inevitably contribute to perceptual differences among the end users of IT.

If attitudes towards IT vary among end-users across industries, then what factors contribute to their perceptual difference? To date, this important question has not yet been addressed. Therefore, this research offers a conceptual model for identifying the factors and explaining the relationship between end-user attitudes and use of IT.

## 3. The conceptual model

Figure 1 shows a conceptual framework for examining end-user attitudes towards IT. The manufacturing and service industry are good examples of different industry-specific technologies: e.g. manufacturing firms have historically employed production technology, whereas service organizations used knowledge-based technology. The implication is that employing any kind of technology affects the initial interest of end-users and their motivation for learning about micro computers, and also that each technology requires special learning skills for a user. Additionally, support from management plays an important role in enhancing end-user interest and rate of learning.

Management's supportive role can be played out best by either providing the trainees the necessary hardware and software or by guaranteeing formal instruction for trainees. Such support contributes to the development of initial interest, the overcoming of obstacles to learning, and, of course, the learning process itself. The literature recognizes the importance of effectively introducing trainees to IT [4]. Especially positive reinforcement for end-users augments their greater use of computers. Therefore, training increases the productivity of end-users in their respective areas [2].

To the degree that these critical factors interact positively between and among themselves, they influence the formation of positive attitudes among potential end users. Indeed, the attitude formed by an end-user towards IT is associated by that person with his or her past experience. Developing a positive attitude towards IT does lead to increased usage, presumably because the effective and cognitive components of the end-user attitude are linked significantly with initial interest, learning to use IT, and surmounting obstacles to learning.

IT is used for the three reasons:

1. decision making: IT is considered an effective tool for decision-making.

2. control: IT offers control over the state-of-operations, and provides comparisons of actual with projected performance.

3. speed: IT provides a speedy way for handling a variety of operations [5].

The kind of IT employed within an industry usually determines its purpose or function.

Based on a review of the literature and on the conceptual model, we developed three hypotheses:

H1: Initial interest of end-users, learning computer use, and obstacles to learning in the manufacturing industry have significant differences from those in the service industry.

H2: There are significant differences between the attitudes of end-users towards IT in the manufacturing industry and the service industry.

H3: The purpose of computer use in the manufacturing industry is significantly different from that in the service industry.

## 4. Research method

A sample of 25 manufacturing firms and 25 service organizations in south central Pennsylvania were randomly selected from Pennsylvania Business Directories. Most of the manufacturing firms included machine and electronics manufacturers. Service organizations included: banking/insurance, utility, health, and education. The number of employees in these organizations ranged from 20 to 2500. Of the 50 businesses surveyed, 10 were businesses (up to 100 employees), 27 were businesses of (101–1000 employees), and 13 were corporations (over 1000 employees). Annual sales of these firms ranged from \$200 thousand to \$25 million. Twenty companies had several kinds of computer systems including mainframe, minicomputers, and microcomputers; 13 had only mainframes and microcomputers; 11 had only minicomputers and microcomputers, and six had only microcomputers.

Of the 239 respondents, 44% were male and 56% were female. Most of these were between 20 and 39 years old and had a college education. Respondents held a variety of jobs: secretarial/clerical (32%), specialists (34%), lower level managers (20%), middle level managers (11%), and top level managers (4%).

## 4.1.Procedures

Data were collected through questionnaires that were mailed to the 50 sample companies. Of the 500 questionnaires mailed, 239 valid responses were received; i.e. a 47% return rate. The questionnaire contained 41 items. Reliability tests of measurements yielded 0.92 for questions regarding computer use at work and 0.89 for questions concerning user attitudes.

A telephone contact was made with the companies surveyed prior to addressing the questionnaire. During this initial contact, the researcher requested assistance asking people randomly forward the questionnaires to end-users. Eight of the companies initially contacted were replaced with substitutes because they either lacked computers in the workplace or they were not interested in participating. Questionnaires were mailed to each company with a letter of explanation requesting that they be completed by regular users, not programmers or systems analysts. Throughout the project the investigators also contacted the companies by telephone for the purpose of providing them with further explanation and any follow up information. A stamped return envelope was attached to each questionnaire.

## 4.2. Measurements

Thirteen of the questions posed focused on each of the randomly selected businesses characteristics: eight queried the individual respondent's education and work background and their demographic characteristics; seven identified the end-users' computer-hardware, software, and computer usage, and twelve questioned each respondent's attitudes towards computers and computer usage. This last group of questions used a Likert-type scale with answers ranging from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree). See the Appendix for a copy of the survey.

It focuses on three specific areas of concern: learning about computers, user attitudes, and the purpose of usage. As a learner, the user was asked to identify the reasons that he or she developed an interest in computers. The questions helped to identify user attitudes for using computers, and increase of productivity in the work place.

## 5. Findings

The answers to the question: “how do respondents become interested in using computers?” showed that 58% required them for their job; 27% were personally interested in computers; 5% were stimulated by seeing others using computers effectively at their work place; 2% were influenced by seeing others using computers effectively outside their work place; and 7% said that a combination of these factors affected their decision.

When asked how they learned to use computers, most of them (80%) had received in-house training. Interestingly, a notable percentage of respondents (13%) indicated that they taught themselves, while (7%) said that their colleagues helped them.

Respondents reported that the major obstacles to learning were: reluctance to invest time initially (29%); lack of availability of hardware (23%); lack of instruction (19%); and initial fear of computers (18%).

A multivariate discriminant analysis of the means of end user initial interest, learning techniques, and major obstacles to learning in the manufacturing industry and the service industry showed a significant difference between industries. (Table 1). The degree of significance with p < 0.07 is considered high enough to accept this difference as valid. Kinds of initial interest in computers, the means to gain knowledgeability about computers, and the kinds of obstacles to learning are not similar among end users in the manufacturing and service industries. Nevertheless, the most discriminating variable, i.e., the one with the highest loading, is initial interest in learning about computers. This is more stimulating in the service industry (64%) than the manufacturing industry (48%).

In general, respondents strongly agreed that the use of computers was a job requirement (62%), a convenience (60%), a solution to their job problems (40%), a pleasure to work with (51%), supported by management (51%), and helpful for increasing their productivity (49%). Most of the respondents agreed that computer use increased their competence in performing their job (66%) but disagreed that usage generated work-related stress(54%). The use of computers, however, did not result in promotion. On the other hand, a discriminant analysis of attitudinal variables in the two industries being considered showed that end-users differ significantly in manufacturing and service organizations for all three principal factors: job requirement, convenience, and solution to problems (Table 2).

An individual analysis of three discriminatory variables demonstrates that employees in the service industry tend to believe that the use of computers are the result of their job and that such usage is convenient and the best solution to work-related problems. This finding implies that service industry employees tend to use computers more than manufacturing industry employees.

Most of the respondents reported that, although they do not use computers for decision-making, they do for controlling and speeding up data processing. This kind of usage probably stems from the fact that most of the end users are secretaries/clerks and types of specialists but not management. In fact, the impact of decision making using computer techniques on human resource management is very clear [1]. These authors emphasized that increasing computer use accompanied changes in HR system structure, strategy, personnel, and culture.

Other inter-industry multivariate analyses indicated that decision making as well as controlling and expediting data processing contributes significantly to the differences in the purpose of use in the two industries (See Group Centroids in Table 3). While the decision making and controlling variables do contribute to this discrimination, the speed variable does not.

Individual examination of the two discriminating variables shows that decision-making and controlling are the underlying reasons for use of computers in the manufacturing industry. In other words, service organizations do not use computers for decision-making and controlling purposes as much as do manufacturing organizations. Probably service organizations rely heavily on computers for data processing.

Overall, end-users in both industries demonstrate differences in their origins of initial interest in computers. They also hold different attitudes about computer use and the purpose for computer use. In brief these two industries manifest dissimilar characteristics in all three dimensions.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

In studying employee interest in computers, end user attitudes and their purposes for computer use, researchers should account for industry-specific characteristics. Each industry has its own special applications; in turn, end users of these applications display attitudes or behavior that are different. Thus manufacturing organizations using computer program applications such as CAD or CAM require end users who have special educational backgrounds or previous professional expertise suitable for working with such programs. On the other hand, the service industry specializes in database management applications and therefore demands end-users with different skills and knowledge. Probably specific sectors within each industry need their own specialized computer program applications depending on the type of technology used. Hence, research on end-users should be industry-specific.

Integrating IT within jobs compels employees to learn about and use computers. In-house programs seem to be the most effective means for training in both industries. Obviously, management should support individual training efforts. But management also should prepare and encourage co-workers to tutor others for two reasons: (1) colleagues are easily available; and (2) they may be less threatening than instructors hired from outside the organization.

Moreover, integrating IT with job situations usually stimulates employees to gain appropriate technical knowledge, ability, and skills, but management should create an environment into which end-users and technology are easily integrated. Management, especially in the service industry, should try to show employees both why and how computers are convenient for solving work-related problems.

Presently, end users see little or no the connection between computer usage and the possibility of promotion. Nevertheless, the need for promotion and compensation of professional performance of end-users is gradually felt at managerial levels $[16]$ . Management should further recognize end-user efforts to improve work operations via the use of computer applications. Managers should consider linking performance to rewards, as suggested by an expectancy model of motivation $[24]$ .

The situation for end-users varies throughout the organizational hierarchy from secretarial/clerical employees to top managers. Concomitantly, intention to use computers varies for each group of end-users throughout the hierarchy. While top managers probably use computers for decision-making, clerical workers use them for efficient information processing. As manifested by the findings of this research, a variation of purpose also exists between the manufacturing and service industry. Manufacturing firms use computers more for decision-making than do service organizations. This means that the potential exists for service organizations to use computers for decision-making. Indeed Broderick and Bour-dreau [1] confirmed this evolution. Furthermore, the organizations should strive to take advantage of advanced IT in strategic planning [6].

In summary, end-users in the manufacturing industry manifest attitudes and intentions towards computer use which differ from those in the service industry. No matter how sophisticated and how capable the technology, its effective implementation depends upon people having a positive attitude towards it.

Oya Culpan is an assistant professor of management at Penn State University, Harrisburg. She has a Ph.D. in Sociology. Her research and teaching interests are in the field of international business, women in management, business ethics, organizational behavior and entrepreneurship. She has given workshops on women entrepreneurs abroad and conducted panel discussion at Penn State University. Her most recent publications

include “Women in Banking: Comparative Perspective on the Integration Myth” International Journal of Manpower Vol 13, Number 1, 33–41, 1992; “Determinants of CEO Pay in Service Industry Firms” Group and Organization Management Vol 17, Number 2, 1992. She is presently involved in a comparative study of Women Expatriates. She was nominated for James Jordan Best Teacher Award in 1986–1987 and also for the International Women’s Day Coalition Award in 1990 in Harrisburg. She is a member of the Eastern Academy of Management, Society for Human Resource Management and Academy of International Business.
