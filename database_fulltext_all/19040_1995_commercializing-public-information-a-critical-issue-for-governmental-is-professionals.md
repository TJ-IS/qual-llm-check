# Commercializing public information: A critical issue for governmental IS professionals

Fritz H. Grupe

Department of Accounting and Computer Information Systems, University of Nevada, Reno, USA

## Abstract

Public data is viewed as an increasingly valuable commodity by business. Access to this data is affected by many federal and state legislative acts, most notably the Freedom of Information, Privacy Act, and Open Access laws. Some governmental agencies recognize that their data is a saleable commodity and are seeking to charge access fees that exceed actual copying costs. It is important for public information systems managers to recognize their role in dealing with the most salient issues arising from the commercialization of public data, including their ability to charge fees for their data that are in excess of copying costs to recover reasonable costs related to obtaining and formatting the information and the degree to which they must respond to specialized formatting and access requests.

Keywords: Information systems policy; Commercialization of public data; Freedom of information; Data privacy; Governmental data; Government information systems; Information technology

## 1. Introduction

Policies on public access to government data must be reconsidered because this data is a valuable commodity that is increasingly accessed and resold for profit by companies. For example, in one state, a private company proposed becoming the centralized data repository and disseminator of data for eleven major state agencies and functional areas; the public data would have been sold through a private time sharing system [1]. State motor vehicle departments regularly sell names and addresses and address changes to direct marketers. One study estimated that approximately 60 percent of the counties in the U.S. sell some of their data [11]. Some companies are collecting data bases from multiple public sources and reselling access to it. Commonly, requests for information request that public agencies at all levels produce unique maps, tailored mailing lists, or uniquely formatted reports for companies seeking economic assistance. Other examples of requests for formatted data originate with court-held data bases focus on landlord and tenant records, renter credit histories, lists of frequently cited traffic offenders and high-risk drivers, and criminal history. Further evidence of the importance of government data to the private sector is shown by the expansion of the Information Industry Association (IIA), a U.S. organization, to a membership of over 650 companies with a for-profit interest in government data. The number of public data bases is growing rapidly. A 1989 study [17] identified two dozen federal agencies operating electronic data acquisition and release programs.

A variety of policy issues are being discussed and decided without real thought. The questions that must be answered include: Is the commercialization of public data a form of subsidy for the information industry or a logical, desirable extension of open records laws? How should public agencies at all governmental levels respond when data is requested by a commercial third party? Issues need to be addressed and IS professionals need to be involved.

## 2. The basis for open access

Democracy requires open government. The U.S. Freedom of Information Act of 1966 (FOIA) and its subsequent revisions implement the principles of open records access implied by the Constitution and the Bill of Rights. With certain exclusions (e.g., security data) this legislation guarantees citizens the right of full disclosure of information held by the government and establishes low-fee procedures by which citizens and the press can sometimes force the disclosure of data held by the government. Virtually all state and local governments have enacted similar legislation to enable citizens to exercise this important right. Even though open record policies are implemented ineffectively at times, FOIA holds that individuals and corporations ought to be able to access most of the government information held about them, as well as to access data on which public policy decisions are based.

Access, however, is controlled by the technologies available. Open records laws were adopted before the electronic revolution which transformed government's ability to create, store, manipulate, and integrate data. Electronic data systems are essential for making huge amounts of data manageable. Public agencies employing these systems may become efficient and effective in carrying out crime prevention and detection, census taking, tax assessment and collection, social services, licensing, regulation, etc. These systems do, however, open many loopholes that allow abuse of the information. Administrators hiding behind technology may institute arcane access procedures and develop data base structures that make public access difficult. Unaware of the limited case law that exists, many federal and state agencies have access practices in conflict with FOIA, and charge fees that are disincentives for public access. Although FOIA has a 28-year history, there is still little agreement over when and what information can be released, especially when it is going to be used commercially. Although the principle of openness remains, electronic technologies pose numerous problems that confuse a seemingly clear objective. A recent survey found that only 9 states have specific open records provisions that deal with electronic records.

Many factors facilitate access to public data by third parties. These include:

\- Open records legislation.

\- Political philosophies that encourage data availability. Recent administrations preferred to have data published by the private sector (i.e., privatization) when possible, as, for instance, that provided by the U.S. Department of Transportation and the Interstate Commerce Commission.

\- Massive government investment. The cost of developing a major geographic IS (GIS) can be in the tens of millions of dollars. Electronic access to this data enables a corporation to avoid significant costs of their own.

\- Increasingly transferable data. Public agencies and private sector companies have large numbers of similar, often highly compatible systems. Huge quantities of data can be transferred rapidly.

\- User-friendly, widely known software. When data can be manipulated by spreadsheets, data bases, or fourth-generation languages, interorganizational data exchanges are easily made.

\- Consortia of public and private organizations. Some GIS have been developed under cooperative arrangements involving private companies, such as power utilities, airports, and telephone companies, as well as public agencies (e.g., planning departments and tax assessors offices) [10].

Unfortunately, protections offered by the Privacy Act for governmental use are often circumvented and ignored, especially by commercial data users [16]. This is common. Government data on private individuals is extensive. For example, a Wisconsin survey identified 73 computer data bases containing personally-identifiable information and reported that one city alone had created 26 local data bases [29]. When data that is of a nonpublic nature leaves an agency, the Privacy Act may be violated if people cannot determine what records have been disseminated about them. The records obtained for one purpose are often used for another without the permission of persons about whom the data is collected even though this may be illegal in some states. Third party users holding public data bases may not install effective review and correction procedures or assure data accuracy and correct usage.

Many states are attempting to amend their open records laws to include electronic records. Public sector IS professionals have a special role to play in the discussion of public access issues. Legislators do not always know the technologies involved, nor are they always aware of how electronic data can be used and misused. There are excellent arguments for greater access forwarded aggressively by the IIA, the American Bar Association, and various public media interest groups. Even when legislators are well informed, it is often easier for legislators to side with the advocates of greater access (often the press) without regard for costs and problems of implementing greater access than it is to fight them.

## 3. Issues and problem areas

People seldom have a voice when data is transferred to the private sector. The press and commercial interests claim that all governmentally-held data is theirs for the asking. To an extent, they are right. Governmental agencies at both federal and state levels must assume that their data is public and that requests for it must be honoured unless specific legislation justifies exemption. Numerous exemptions exist. Recent studies in Nevada and Maine [7] each identified over 200 pieces of legislation including FOIA and the Privacy Acts that exempt many types of data from disclosure. Nonetheless, new questions have emerged as data has been commercialized. Policies to settle these questions are needed and must involve many parties. IS professionals have a role to play in finding legislatively acceptable answers to the important issues.

What constitutes a public record? Some administrators believe, usually in error, that data does not have to be released if it is not easily conceived of as a record. Traditionally, a “record” was a paper document. “Record” is increasingly taken to include data appearing in card files, tape files, data files, data bases, computer screens, programs, electronic mail, voice mail, word processing documents, image data bases, or in other electronic media regardless of its physical form or characteristics.

Some “records” may exist only temporarily as the result of an online query. Electronic records are different from more conventional forms of records that existed at the passage of FOIA. Commercial users and most analysts hold that, regardless of its format, public data can potentially can be disclosed $[5]$ . Federal and state open record laws have moved toward defining “records” by function, i.e., records result from the documentation of “government activity or from the ‘informational value’ of the data” $[2]$ . Others introduce complications: some question whether a request for data that is not easily available needs a response. Providing answers to new queries may produce information that was not previously “known” or used in any public policy decision. Although there is a question whether nonexempt-electronic data bases are “records” and not simply pools of data elements, there is growing belief that all data they contain are potential candidates for release $[24]$ .

There is legal support for the position that neither federal nor state agencies are required to engage in special programming in response to a request. A study of open access requirements suggested that Ohio agencies do not have to respond to a request that creates new records, generates new analyses, or provides records in the format required by the requester [6]. Some requests for data can be handled routinely with low priced, “standardized products.” Maps that have already been created by a GIS can be photocopied or redrawn. Data bases can be interrogated with a query language. Often, computer systems reduce the retrieval costs rather than increase them. On the other hand, some requests may require specialized programming, excessive computer time, or specialized knowledge to interpret the data. Sometimes, these services can be charged at a higher rate. This raises the question of what constitutes special programming, since multiline queries or short executable files could be described as programming.

Does “access” only mean access to data? Data access alone may not satisfy a commercial third party. If data is available, the third party may request publicly-produced programs to manipulate the data because data is useless without the software that created it. This raises the question of whether the software itself is a record. At least some analysts attribute an obligation on federal and state agencies to provide publicly-developed software as well as data. The courts have established that copyrighted, proprietary software is not part of the public record and, thus, need not be provided, but they have issued mixed decisions on whether agency-developed data base indexes or retrieval software are protected from disclosure. A U.S. Justice Department study director stated that FOIA could be interpreted as requiring the production of made-to-order software to fit commercial demands $[27]$ .

Conceivably, private sector requests for assistance can go beyond software and data release. If the requester has difficulty running the program, can they obtain support and assistance installing and running the program? In interpreting the results? Can they demand that data collection procedures be modified to enable them to make more effective use of the data? Many agencies find that they do not have the resources with which to keep up with data requests that do involve special requirements and may find special programming requests to be impractical.

Is public information being made available for private gain at public expense? Data dissemination is costly. How access is paid for is an open and pressing question. The effort to produce data products may involve substantial analyst, programmer, and other professional services, as well as costs for computer time, plotter usage, paper, and other consumables. At times, the commercial value of the data is sufficiently large that businesses seeking this data will not only pay for the data but will pay for the creation of automated systems where there were none before [14]. The expense incurred for creating value-added services, unless funded by a third party, reduces the money available for the on-going, mission-oriented services that a state or county agency such as tax collection or child welfare provides. This position is disputed by Ralph Nader's Taxpayer Assets Project.

An important aspect of governmental record keeping is the maintenance of archival records. The maintenance of electronic records with enduring legal and research value is often ignored or inadequately addressed $[13]$ . Just as untranscribed proceedings on dictaphone belts are no longer usable, so changes in personnel, hardware, and software can make it impossible to retrieve public data. To the extent that electronic records are privatized, there is a real question as to whether maintenance can be assured if the costs for data migration and dissemination are not justified by the profits generated.

Who decides the format for requested records? To reduce their own expenses, requesters seek data in a format that is most useful to them. There are many forms and formats that may be requested. Must a request be honoured as it was made, or should the requester be forced to use the data in the form provided by a federal or state agency? The IIA argues against allowing any public agencies to dictate data format because format greatly affects the data's utility to the private sector [8]. The provision of “value-added” services appears to be beyond the expectations of FOIA, but it is unclear whether public agencies can control the format. Some courts at both the federal and state levels have allowed the public agency to format the data as they see fit. Other courts have permitted, but did not require, a public agency to respond to a request if the requester was prepared to pay for value-added services [24]. Thus, if responsiveness had a substantial impact on productivity, an agency could refuse to provide value-added services.

What form of access is required? Just as data can be requested in a specific format, third party users find specific access technologies (tape, diskette, dialup line, etc.) more to their advantage than others. Just as a choice must be made in the selection of format, so too must a decision be made to determine the type of access provided. Agencies can provide access by:

\- publishing data in bulk using hard copy, disk files, and so on,

-- offering an on-site, terminal or reference room for access and printing,

\- publishing data through a private corporation,

\- publishing data through a specially-organized, governmentally-operated agency.

Can a corporation require dialup links to access data directly? Some federal and state agencies offer dialup lines for interested parties. In Nevada, the state legislature allows access to its data processing centre through a system that tracks the text of legislative bills and their status, budget reports, and revenue reports. It also provides bulletin board information and E-mail services. Subscribers with plans to redistribute the data pay a monthly fee three times higher than normal. Corporations and individuals have dialup access to the SEC securities filings, a Department of Commerce bulletin board, and National Weather Service reports and forecasts. Many IS professionals opposed to dialup access argue that every external account is an insecure gateway for unauthorized users.

How much should government charge for data? A major question to be examined is whether the government can or should charge market value for data. Although federal agencies are generally prohibited from charging, some is sold through the National Technology Information Service. This question is more important to state and local governments. Private sector users maintain that a charge levied against users of public information is a form of double taxation. It may be that a company pays taxes or fees in the same political district in which an agency wishes to charge for the use of its data. However, data may be requested by a corporation or individual outside of the taxing jurisdiction. Should these requests be treated differently?

At times a role reversal occurs. When free data is provided to a nonpublic entity for use in profit making activities, commercial sources of comparable data (or potential developers of comparable data) complain that their taxes are supporting competitive operations. These data providers seek to restrict government competition by requiring the public agency to charge higher than market prices.

Another important question is related to value-added services. If the public provides support to a private company, even under a cost-for-service arrangement, does the private company receive an unfair advantage over its competitors? Does any public agency that produces value-added services become a partner in a proprietary venture? Some federal and state agencies limit their distribution of data to a specific company and not to all requesters. Is this fair? Should not all requesters receive the same data at the same cost? Should there be a procedure that permits equal opportunity for data usage?

Decisions about who should publish data and at what cost present a controversial issue. Recent federal administrations have taken the position that the private sector should be given primacy in the publishing of data whenever possible. This may limit access to only those data bases that are profitable. One can ask whether the private sector will continue to maintain data that no longer holds commercial value. Satellite data that once was free but is now published by a private firm can move out of the financial reach of many users.

It is not difficult to imagine a public agency warping its priorities in order to increase revenues. It might release data that is and ought to be exempt from disclosure. It also might collect data not because it needs it but because it can sell it or because it is pressured to collect it and make it available to profit-making firms. To the extent that revenues generated are returned to general revenues, the selling agency has no incentive to sell data.

Similarly, most state laws specify that no charges are to be made for the examination or review of a public record. Consequently, free public data can be used for a commercial organization's own purposes or it may be resold, perhaps after reformatting, indexing, matching, sorting, etc.

As a rule, if no legal basis for exemption is present, federal, state, and local agencies are required to provide a copy of a record at the actual cost of duplication. While “actual cost” is usually undefined, it is expected to be nominal. Typically, the data is provided “as is” and, except for providing some user-friendly front ends, the IS department often does not do the work of querying and extracting useful information.

A little discussed policy question is whether public data supplied to commercial source, constitutes a subsidy to a private corporation that can pay for the benefits that it receives. The IIA argues aggressively for free private sector access to government data. It cites as legislative principles the concepts that pricing should be nondiscriminatory, that data should not be copyrighted or otherwise limited in its use, and that the costs for data should be limited to the marginal cost of reproducing it. The IIA argues that restrictions such as copyrights are unconstitutional and that data access is a right and not a privilege. Once public data is acquired by the private sector, the third parties “should be free to use it, sell it, or otherwise disseminate it without special fees.”

Increasingly, public agencies at state, county, and local levels are trying to recover the actual costs of developing the data requested. When they do, the full, fixed costs (software, hardware, personnel, facilities, research, and supplies) of setting up a data base, of entering the data, and of copying the requested data is passed on to the requester. It is important to establish a defensible, uniform fee structure to avoid favouring one buyer over others. Data products may be sold by the department that acquired it, may be centralized through an MIS department, or may be sold through a separate entity created for this purpose.

The rational for selling data and data access is obvious; it provides a potentially great source of income if the demand exists and if data is sold at market value and not simply at the cost of reproduction. Revenues can be used to enhance the data base beyond what is possible with only public funding. Further, if existing data can be put to use by someone, society benefits. Although some agencies that have moved in this direction have secured substantial levels of revenue, most have not. A state department of motor vehicles can realize in excess of \$10 million for its lists and a City of San Diego data sales unit has obtained \$3 million in one year [3]. High revenue levels are not common, however. It can be argued that data that is advertised and packaged for resale gets wider exposure and utilization and that regional planning is improved.

The sale of data may not be a clear and obvious thing to do. Many agencies do not have the authority to charge a cost-recovery fee because fees limit public accessibility for noncommercial users. Some state and local agencies have required special legislation to obtain this authority. Higher costs for data access could divide users into classes of information “haves” and “have nots” [23]. Assuming that a market for the data exists, any agency planning to sell data must adopt an entrepreneurial approach that requires active promotional efforts, a staff that act as sales people, and extra costs for promotion and distribution. It must also be committed to supplying its customers with the data they are marketing on an on-going basis. The products need to be updated regularly and must be available in appropriate electromagnetic and paper formats. These tasks may not be feasible for any agency that is not market driven. Collection of data usage fees may present a problem if users do not pay their bill. It is easier for a business to sue for debts than it is for the government to do so.

Simply creating data does not give the collecting agency exclusive ownership of the data. To protect their data, the state agencies collecting fees must either copyright or license their databases. While federal agencies are prohibited from copyrighting their data, states are not necessarily so restricted. Colorado copyrights all of its data bases and Washington and many other states copyright their revised codes. Florida is reported to have had a significant trade in the selling of data and software. Hawaii INC, a public access network, charges for access to five databases of information while making many other databases available without charge.

A variation on this theme is the charging of an additional fee for access if the requester is planning to resell the data. Such fees are authorized in several states, but the enforcement of prohibitions against commercial utilization of the data is difficult whether the data base is copyrighted or not [4]. The question of whether any agency's data can be protected as its sole property is a difficult one. The courts appear to be categorizing data as property, but counter cases prevent this from becoming a reliable basis for preventing a third party from redistributing government data [21].

Some jurisdictions have formed independent agencies to handle data sales: what one author describes as a joint powers authority $[19]$ . Multiple state departments provide data and data products to be marketed by this entity. The Vermont Centre for Geographic Information, Inc. is a joint venture of the State of Vermont and the University of Vermont that seeks, in addition to coordinating the strategic use of geographic information systems in the state, to sell those products (i.e., maps, documents, walk-in and dial-up access) that cannot be provided by the private sector $[28]$ . The San Diego Association of Governments and the Atlanta Regional Commission formed their own nonprofit organizations to conduct market research, accumulate public data, and sell products to the private sector. Private organizations may also act as public data clearinghouses.

In some circumstances, data access is controlled by contract. In Nevada, for example, a private firm, using government data, charges for dialup access to data bases as diverse as UCC filings, state corporation filings, driver license look up, vehicle registration look up, state public works procurement system, court dockets, and building permit data bases. The Los Angeles Urban Research Division and Santa Cruz County are examining and implementing contracts with private organizations to publish their data.

An interesting question arises when records are produced with a mixture of federal and state funds. Suppose, for instance, that a state department of transportation develops data with funds from both sources. Federal law prevents copyright but state law permits it. Which laws and regulations cover their access? This question is, as yet, unanswered.

To what extent must the public be informed of, and give consent for, the use of governmentally-held data by commercial users? The Privacy Act restricts the secondary use of data (i.e., the transfer of data to a third party) in several ways. In addition to having to make subjects aware of data held by a federal and state agency, these agencies are only authorized under the Privacy Act to use the data for purposes for which the subject has specifically consented. Consent of the subject is required to permit disclosure to a third party. It may be impractical to obtaining consent for all secondary uses of data [25]. If all benign uses of data required consent, subjects would be inundated with requests for permission. For instance, the U.S. Postal Service's selling of address corrections to direct marketeers saves the Service millions of dollars by avoiding misaddressed mail. Excessive requests for consent could lead to citizens ignoring the requests. On the other hand, the sale of governmental data may make citizens hesitate to provide data essential to good government. It may also lead to law suits for the inappropriate use of data collected for a legitimate purpose but applied to an inappropriate one.

Under what conditions can a request for data be denied? Many custodians of public data believe that they can just say “No” to a request for data. Normally, the burden of proof in justifying a denial, however, falls on that custodian. As a rule, custodians of public data must respond positively to most requests. Depending on the jurisdiction involved, failure to do so can lead to financial, criminal, and/or civil penalties applied to the administrator as well as to the agency. Custodians withholding requested, nonexempt data may be liable even though they act in good faith. However, there are valid reasons for denying a request (see Fig. 2). There is no statutory basis for the oft heard conclusion that a custodian must weigh the public’s right to know against the individual’s rights to privacy.

As a rule, access laws do not obligate a citizen to cite a reason for requesting government data. Moreover, a review of public records access decisions in Ohio suggests that this state's agencies may not even be able to require that the requester fill out a form before access is permitted. There is no question surrounding an individual's request for data about one, or a small subset, of all records. There is a question as to whether the intent of FOIA gives anyone the right to manipulate and copy entire data bases.

It is possible to exempt public data from disclosure as a matter of public policy. At least two state legislatures-Iowa and Oregon-enacted legislation that enables those state and local agencies developing geographic information systems to charge full cost recovery fees. These acts remove geographic information system data bases from the definition of public records. One state exempted its Department of Wildlife from distributing software despite press requests for it. The programs involved a big game (sheep and elk) tagging lottery.

Many federal agencies with high volumes of FOIA requests or with requests that require specialized programming do not respond within the 10 days required by FOIA even when acting in good faith. The Department of Justice, for example, had over 10,000 requests in one year. Requests involving specialized programming may need longer response times even when an agency desires to respond.

Does the release of public data extend the government's liabilities? Given that persons in search of redress always look for “deep pockets,” the governmental unit that sells or gives out data may open itself to liability. Suppose a state agency, perhaps against its wishes, releases a data base that contains inaccurate, misleading, or incomplete data. Or suppose an ill-formed query produces an incorrect response. The public agency involved may be taking on a liability exposure they would have avoided if they had the ability to prevent access. Is there safety in simply refusing to release data? Probably not, since some companies might sue for the release of data anyway. This has not been tested in court, but most administrators of data selling operations feel that they are protected as long as they identify their data as a “best effort” at providing data that is the official governmental record at a specific point in time.

More litigation will eventually define whether defective software or an inaccurate data base should be treated like a defective machine, in which case the liability is greater, or like a book, in which case liability is limited. The courts, wishing to encourage the dissemination of information, have generally held that there is “no implied warranty for information in books” partly because authors do not generally profess to be experts or acting as professionals because they are not merchants of information and, therefore, are not liable [22]. Publishers and booksellers are not liable because it is impossible for them to determine the accuracy of everything they publish. On the other hand, if data is treated like a machine, the creators of the data bear greater liability. The use of public data bases is parallel to the use of publicly-created maps in a book. When a plane crash was attributed to an error in a map, both the government and the publisher paid damages.

Drawing on court records, some private sector organizations are attempting to set up their own criminal history data bases to be sold for corporate background checking. State laws generally prohibit the release of criminal history data from public repositories. Judicial and private companies may not be subject to the same restrictions. Neither is it clear who is responsible for seeing that privacy laws are followed by the third party, or who should insure that third-party users update their information on court actions (including case dismissal and case expungement).

An unexamined area involves the licensing of proprietary software. Almost all governmental agencies receive preferred pricing because of their governmental, not-for-profit status. Does an agency receiving this price reduction and offering time-sharing services or providing products for sale to commercial vendors, jeopardize the terms and conditions of software licenses and pricing discounts?

## 4. Addressing the issues and problems

Many state and local agencies have moved from questioning whether data should be commercialized to assessing how the transfer should be structured. A framework for managing access is adapted here from that recommended in a different context by the Wisconsin Legislative Council Staff [20]. However, policies about open access can change quickly. Procedures should be reviewed regularly with legal counsel. New laws, court decisions, attorney general opinions, and political philosophies can change a public agency's obligations quickly. IS professionals need to be involved in the creation of a framework for their states.

Adopt access policies. It is generally accepted that FOIA requests must be promptly addressed. Facilities and assistance for record access must be provided during normal working hours. Providing data to an external party should not deter the agency from accomplishing its primary functions. It is important to determine what data must be archived and to provide retention and disposition schedules where necessary. Budget requests should include explanations that funds are needed to migrate archival records onto new media.

Identify the custodian of public records. One person or group should be identified as being responsible for handling all requests for records. This custodian, should be familiar with pertinent local, state, and federal legislation governing access and privacy and should be aware of exemptions from open access. Given the electronic nature of much data, the custodian should also understand the technologies.

Formally establish the procedures by which requests for service can be submitted. A standard set of procedures should be installed to process requests fairly and economically. Set up a system for tracking the number and types of requests. Requesters should be assured of a reasonable response time and reasonable and justifiable charges which are based on the entity's public records laws [9]. One writer [5] advocates a structure that provides for: (1) a means for public access without relinquishing control of the actual data or allowing duplication of it; (2) exclusion of information for which cost-recovery is sought from the definition of a public record; (3) modification of the open records law to allow licensing of data bases under approved terms and conditions, which include restrictions on duplication and resale.

Records that have not been classified as such can be exempted from disclosure on a temporary or permanent basis. The custodian should determine what procedures are required to classify records as exempt and institute procedures before a request is made. Minnesota suggested that an application for exemption establish that the data in question is not already exempted, that similar data has been treated as exempt by other, comparable agencies and by the public, and that there is a compelling need to treat the data as confidential because its dissemination would adversely affect the public interest, make a program unworkable, or in the case of data on an individual, adversely affect that individual's health, safety, or reputation [12].

Separating “exempt” from “nonexempt” data is difficult in many computerized systems. But this is rarely an adequate excuse for failing to provide requested data. Separating the two requires preplanning and data base restructuring. A good practice was recently proposed in Connecticut [19]; it would require all state agencies obtaining new information systems to demonstrate that they have considered, at the lowest cost possible, the rights of the public to access the nonexempt portions of the data base when the agency analyzed new system requirements, designed new systems, or acquired hardware and software.

Respond as promptly as possible to a request. Responses should be given quickly. If a response cannot be given immediately, it should be provided in less than three days. The response may provide access with or without modification to the request, with a delay (typically of less than ten days), or to deny access on a valid basis. A negative response may be given in kind. Oral requests can be denied orally although a request for a written denial should be honoured. A written denial should be given to a written request. Fax and telephone requests cannot be disqualified.

Several state courts have indicated that the requester's identity and motive are immaterial as a basis for denying a request for information. Thus, a commercial firm cannot be barred from data access simply because it plans to realize income from the use of public data. Requests must be specific. Ill defined requests need not be answered to but requests for specific groups of records should be addressed. Questions may be asked to further define what data is being sought.

Any agency providing access must provide more than physical access to the records. They must also provide a means of copying or printing the records and, if the user must access a computerized data base, users must be supported in extracting the records they seek.

One need not release material that is not a record. Currently, a record is understood to be any material that contains nonexempt information. Materials that need not be released include (a) personal property; (b) drafts, notes, preliminary computations, and other work in progress; (c) material that is protected by statute; (d) material that is limited by copyright, patent, or bequest; and (e) published materials that are already available for sale or at a public library. Firm guidelines defining “public record” are missing, however.

Provide for an appeal process to handle requests for reconsideration of a denial of access. A denial of a request often produces a challenge. It is beneficial to have an internal review procedure for reviewing access denials: some applicants may pursue litigation immediately. There are independent, intermediate board in some states; they review any denial of a request for records before a person may take the issue to court.

Adopt standard costs for services. There are many alternatives by which data, reports, maps, and other information resources can be made available. Charges should be authorized by statute and consistent with the agency's goals and objectives, as well as with the level of demand for services.

Specify the level of service the agency can provide. No public agency can honour all requests while effectively addressing its critical missions. To control its resources, policies may be needed that state what response time can be expected for standard and nonstandard requests alike. A process for obtaining an exemption from usual practice may be needed.

Consider limiting the availability of resources by producing a standard product list. In many instances, users request the same reports, data files, programs, and so on, again and again. These items can be created in advance and sold as requested. This reduces frequent, one-at-a-time requests and production costs.

Think about tomorrow. Although challenges to governmental release of data have been few, many observers predict increasing conflicts. It is incumbent on public IS professionals and on the custodians of government data to track the issues closely. Some further recommendations are:

(1) An agency that has not yet installed procedures should establish them. Review them periodically with legal counsel so they conform to the applicable federal, state, and local laws.

(2) Inventory the data bases agencies have; be sure that they have the statutory authority to create them and that persons whose information is in the data base know how the data is to be used.

(3) Move toward software systems that separate nonexempt from exempt information and that limit access to individually-identifiable data while opening access to aggregate data.

(4) Anticipate the emergence of a philosophy that the consumer, not the government owns the data.

(5) IS professionals should be proactive in their agency and with their legislators in shaping legislation that enables government to continue to be effective and efficient while it complies with FOIA requirements.

(6) If a public IS administrator wishes to charge a fee for data in excess of actual copying costs, seek the passage of legislation to authorize this.

## 5. Conclusion

As profit seeking companies have discovered the value of publicly-owned data, they have become more aggressive in seeking access to it through the use of FOIA and other avenues. In response, many public agencies have attempted to recover development costs in addition to the actual costs of reproducing a record. This clash of interests raises interesting and important issues that must be resolved in statute or in practice.

Because information systems professionals in most instances control these repositories of data, they should play a leadership role in defining and solving these issues.
