# Experiences and expectations with CASE technology – an example from Slovenia

Erna Rupnik-Miklič $^{a}$ , Jože Zupančič $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ Governmental Office for Statistics, 61000 Ljubljana, Vožarski pot 12 Slovenia $^{b}$ University of Maribor, School for Organizational Sciences, 64000 Kranj, Prešernova 11 Slovenia

## Abstract

Adoption and current use, perceived benefits, problems, and expectations in the application of information system development methods and tools, specifically CASE technologies, are presented in the settings of Slovenia. Current practices and trends in the use of CASE technology were surveyed; major differences in the IS development approaches in organizations that currently use or do not use CASE were identified. The findings are believed to be indicative of some other new Central and Eastern European democracies that are at a comparable stage in developing their IS technology markets. Although most IS managers in Slovenia are aware of CASE technology or have considered its use, organizations with a comparatively high level of IS maturity – ones with some corporate IS development standards in place – actually use it. More than limited funding for investments in advanced information technology, the lack of related methodological and management knowledge and skills seems to be the major reason why system developers do not adopt it or, with limited use, slowly adopt CASE.

Keywords: Systems development; CASE; Methodology; Adoption; Benefits; Expectations; Empirical study; Slovenia; Software tools; Implementation

## 1. Introduction

Computer-Aided Software Engineering (CASE) provides an automated engineering discipline for software development, maintenance and, project management. It consists of a set of computer-based development tools to automate certain portions of information system (IS) development methodologies. Thus, CASE tools work within a methodology. The key concept of CASE is employment of the computer as a tool to build models that describe business enterprise and corporate planning and that document systems development from planning through the implementation of the complete system [8]. CASE methodologies are combinations of structured system development methodologies and CASE technology [12].

CASE tools that automate the analysis and design techniques of data flow diagramming, logical data structuring, and entity life modelling differ considerably from tools that automate code generation by use of structure charts and reuse of existing code modules. These differences have resulted in development of various categories of tools such as upper-, middle-, and lower-CASE.

However, the latest generation is integrating more of the system development life cycle (SDLC) into single tools or tool sets. With this trend, the full potential is now beginning to be realized $[19]$ . Currently, the majority of CASE tools assist software development by performing mundane data consistency, completeness, and conformance checks.

Advocates of CASE claim many benefits, such as higher productivity, higher quality systems, more maintainable systems and shorter system development time $[3]$ . CASE has been promoted as one of the solutions that will counter the problems of poor software quality and inadequate documentation. On the other hand, user experience has been extremely mixed, ranging from a high level of success to a series of costly failures $[18]$ . Due to learning effects, productivity usually declines for some time. There is no way to estimate the length of that learning period which ranges from six months to two years $[10]$ . The implementation of CASE technology must be regarded and treated as a substantial change affecting the organization. It must therefore be properly planned and managed $[25]$ .

Very few organizations have the mechanisms in place to measure software development productivity or even the true costs of implementing CASE tools. The vendor's price of a CASE tool is only a small portion of its the true adoption cost. It includes significant investments in people and time as well as other costs [9]. Industry cost estimates in the USA run from US \$25,000 to \$100,000 per developer. This includes education and training, creation of a development environment, integration of new tools in the existing environment, tool customization, etc. Quantifiable benefits include improved staff productivity, reduced maintenance requirements, mainframe and machine resources, while unquantifiable benefits range from increased user confidence and improved IS responsiveness to the ability to hire and retain higher qualified staff. However, if current productivity has not been measured, there is no baseline for comparison. Studies have recently been published that attempt to measure the effects of CASE on system development by examining percentage changes in number of function points between phases of the system development life cycle [7].

In spite of the relatively slow implementation of, and reported resistance to the use of CASE in many companies, most authors, however, expect the use of CASE tools to lead to increased staff productivity and generation of valuable quality software. Some believe that automation of system development will prove as successful as that of computer-aided design and manufacturing.

Some investigations (e.g. [16,21]) have looked at differences between various brands of CASE tools and on the tool selection processes; others have studied the use of CASE and its adaption to organizations [17]. Experiences and expectations of CASE in Denmark and Finland [1], UK, Netherlands and Australia [20] have recently been described. Several case studies have also been published that report successful (e.g. [2,26]) and unsuccessful (e.g. [16]) implementations.

## 2. Framework and goals

According to Moeller [13], German unification and the opening of Eastern Europe is one of the three major factors that will determine the development of the IT market in the 1990s. Kempfer [11] predicts that the fastest growth of value-added services will be in Eastern Europe, which is expected to expand at an annual growth of about $20\%$ during the next five years. Dyson [6] identified two groups of Central and Eastern European countries; she grouped Slovenia with Hungary, the Czech Republic, and Poland as the four leading countries and found that the level of IS activities and industry in other countries was less promising.

In Slovenia, the use of IT has developed rapidly. Computer use in all sizes of organizations is becoming widespread. A comparison of major issues in information systems management in Slovenia and in the USA [4] revealed a large number of management-related issues in Slovene organizations, as opposed to a greater number of external issues in the USA. While educational, legal, and professional services are abundant in the USA and Western Europe, they are still scarce or inefficient in Slovenia, although improving rapidly. The same can be said for communication facilities, product supply, and external databases, etc. Among system development related issues, the use of an integrated IS development methodology and the application of development tools were rated as the most important. This matches with results in some Western countries [23], Greece [5] and Poland [24].

This article presents the results of a survey carried out in companies in January 1993. Its aims of were:

(1) To collect and analyze information on adoption and current use, perceived benefits, problems and expectations in application of IS development methods and tools – in particularly CASE technology;

(2) To survey current practices and trends in using CASE technology;

(3) To identify major differences in the approach to IS development between organizations that currently use or do not use CASE.

## 3. Survey design

The Governmental Office for Statistics provided a list of 300 Slovene industrial, commerce, and service organizations with more than 300 employees. Because major political and economic changes initiated in the last three years have caused dynamic restructuring of businesses, many organizations had been forced to disintegrate, downsize, or restructure. Although outdated, the list used was the best available at that time.

In designing the survey questionnaire a pilot survey with few respondents was used to improve the final questionnaire, which was divided into two parts: the first for all respondents and the second for CASE users only. Where possible it included multiple choice questions with space to collect additional information. The questionnaire is given in the Appendix.

## 4. Respondent profile

The survey used questionnaires sent to 300 unidentified executives (managers) of IS Departments. Usable responses were obtained from 102 firms – a 34% a response rate. Among them, 22 (21.6%) actually used CASE, according to the following (translated) definition in the questionnaire, “CASE users are organizations where at least one real-world development project with a minimum size of 6 man-months has been completed using a CASE tool”. Three of these 22 organizations completed such a project and temporarily abandoned CASE. The portion of CASE respondents cannot be considered representative for the whole population, as reasons for non-reply were not investigated. Contacts with a limited number of non-respondents and similar studies showed that the majority of non-replies may be because they did not use CASE. A recent investigation, though not focused on CASE [22] found that of 120 Slovene organizations surveyed, 12% have purchased a CASE tool. Twelve organizations returned the questionnaire with a remark that they have outsourced the IS function.

Respondents who supplied data came from a wide variety of business backgrounds and sizes. The average number of employees was 1080. Only 30 (23 non-CASE respondents) employed more than 1000 people; the largest had 6200 employees. The vast majority were medium or large sized. No major differences in the total number of employees between CASE and non-CASE respondents were found; IS Departments from CASE respondents were generally larger than non-CASE, as shown in Fig. 1.

The formal education level of the IS Department staffs was slightly higher in CASE than non-CASE respondents: 56% of staff members hold at least an associate degree compared to 45% in non-CASE organizations. Some difference in the organizational position of the top IS executive or manager was found: his/her position was one level below CEO in 57% of CASE and 42% of non-CASE respondents. Most of the CASE respondents (71%) used project team organization and employed it for all larger development projects, compared to less than half (43%) of non-CASE respondents. Only one organization replied that it felt no need for project team organization. In the questionnaire, project team organization was defined as work in project teams which included professionals from differing fields of work experience who were at least temporarily released from their regularly assigned duties.

## 5. Development approaches and methodologies

Virtually all investigations of major issues in IS management identified strategic IS planning as one of the most important issues, one closely related to general corporate strategy planning. Findings of a recent study [4] indicate that, due to Slovenia's volatile economic condition and its lack of a national long term strategy, businesses often do no general strategic business planning. We therefore collected data about the status of strategic IS planning, which is presented in Fig. 3.

As expected, considerably more strategic IS planning was done in organizations with CASE than without. Out of 10 (non-CASE) organizations who had no plans or guidelines for strategic IS development, 9 had been developing them. Apparently IS managers are concerned about strategic IS planning but frequently fail to conceive and execute well defined plans for strategic IS development. Traditional / classical approach: The developer follows no clearly defined procedure: products of application development phases are not well documented. Primary tools are narrative description, flowcharts, and input/output layouts.

System Development Life Cycle (SDLC) approach: The development project is divided into activities, such as initial investigation, feasibility study, maintenance etc., to accomplish the overall work in the project.

Structured approach: The main idea is to decompose system complexity and to describe systems in terms of data flow diagrams, structure charts, data dictionaries, process descriptions etc.

Automated approach: The tools, often based on structured techniques (application generators, data dictionary packages, CASE tools), are used to support the work of developers. At least one system development project using CASE has been completed.

Prototyping approach: An initial model of the desired application is developed early in the SDLC and reviewed by the end user to define his/her need more completely and accurately.

Information Centre (End-User Computing) approach: Support personnel assist end-users in developing their own applications and in adoption of application packages to satisfy their unmet information needs. The main technologies supporting this concept are 4GLs and personal computers.

## 6. CASE implementation

Only 8 organizations out of 22 respondents have been using the tool for more than one year, with 2 for more than 3 years. The average number of IS professionals using CASE was 2.4; the highest number was 6 in 2 organizations, while CASE was used by only one developer in 6. The most popular tool was POSE with 11 installations, followed by ORACLE\* CASE and Excelerator with 4 installations.

A small portion of CASE users (9%) developed an explicit plan to implement CASE technology before its introduction, 57% developed written guidelines, but 32% had no written plan. Most of CASE users (64%) provided training for their staff both in using the particular CASE tool and in underlying methodology, while 18% had training only in methodology, and 9% had training only in the use of a particular tool. Average training time was 4.4 days for using the tool and 6.9 days for methodology. External consulting support was used by 32% of respondents, 41% did not use consulting services but expressed the need for consulting support, while 18% thought no adequate support was available in Slovenia.

Fig. 5 presents reasons why 80 non-CASE respondents did not implement CASE. Respondents were asked to identify the main reason for not adopting CASE.

The most evident difference between respondents in Slovenia and in UK is the broad lack of knowledge regarding CASE is the most frequent reason given by Slovene respondents, while no respondent from the UK expressed that reason. When the results of this study were presented to a group of IS professionals from a number of Slovene organizations, some thought that one reason for this discrepancy was lack of self-confidence among IS executives. Previously, hardly any management education had been available, and IS as well as and other managers were less prepared to manage organizational change. Also, lack of knowledge may be due to scarce or inefficient educational, consulting, and professional services in Slovenia compared to those in Western Europe and the USA.

## 7. Perceived benefits, problems and solutions

A list of benefits from CASE that are most frequently claimed by CASE vendors was given in the questionnaire. Respondents estimated the degree of realization of these claims on a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 indicated fully unrealized and 5 fully realized. Table 2 presents the distribution and average values of ratings.

The extent to which the apparent benefits of CASE tools were met varies widely. Average ratings are the highest for a disciplined and uniform methodological approach to system development and for more orderly and transparent system development procedures. Due to the short period of working with CASE technology (most organizations have been using CASE less than one year.), we can expect major benefits to become evident in the future. On the other hand, exaggerated claims of vendors promoting of their products may have resulted in users having unrealistic expectations.

## 8. Future evolution of CASE

Opinions of CASE respondents about the future use and role of CASE technology in their organizations were collected. Most (64%) plan to standardize the use of CASE for system development in the next 2-3 years, and two (9%) respondents plan to use CASE only for large projects. Findings are comparable to those published in [1] where the study showed that most users in Denmark and Finland (64%) will increase the use of tools. In Slovenia, 22% of CASE respondents currently plan no projects using CASE; they do not plan to abandon CASE, they intend to change their approach. Only one respondent planned to switch to another CASE tool. Table 3 shows respondents' perceived future for CASE.

Both CASE users and non-users in Slovenia are very optimistic about the future of CASE technology. Most of them expect many professionals to accept CASE as the preferred method of system development or they even expect it to alter the way software is developed and maintained.

## 9. Conclusions

Although only a minority of organizations in Slovenia currently use CASE, most of the IS managers are aware of CASE technology and have considered its use or evaluated its benefits and problems. Results of our investigation suggest that organizations employing CASE are characterized, relatively to their peer organizations, by a higher level IS maturity, where the diffusion of the use of system development methodologies, structured techniques, strategic planing and project management are significant, and where some corporate development standards are already in place. A relatively low number of IS developers in a company use CASE tools. None of the sampled organizations adopted CASE as the standard way to develop and maintain software. Although available funds for investments are limited, thereby hindereng wider implementation of advanced information technology, lack of related methodological and management knowledge and skills seems to be the major reason for the slow adoption and limited use of CASE by system developers. In general, IS professionals in Slovenia are very optimistic regarding the future of CASE.
