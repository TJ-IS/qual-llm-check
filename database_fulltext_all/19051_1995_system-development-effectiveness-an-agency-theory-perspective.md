# System development effectiveness: An agency theory perspective

Adam S. Huarng

Indiana University - Purdue University at Fort Wayne, Fort Wayne, IN 46805 USA

## Abstract

Agency theory can be used to explain the effectiveness of system development methods. As the cost of computing decreases and end user computing technology becomes feasible, end-user departments are developing their own application systems. Agency theory suggests that end-users will desire greater autonomy over their computing in order to avoid the agency costs that arise in traditional MIS development. This study provides empirical support for the proposition that user development allows end-user departments to minimize the agency costs resulting from the work of the MIS department. Data were collected from 162 users of 59 different applications in 30 organizations. The results indicate that user development is more effective than MIS development. Among user-developed application systems, departmental applications are more effective than corporate applications.

Keywords: System development; User-developed application; System effectiveness; System utilization; Agency theory

## 1. Introduction

User-developed applications (UDA) are receiving considerable attention in the MIS field. The benefits of user development have been discussed in depth by Rivard and Huff [19], etc. The factors identified as important for user satisfaction include: (1) higher user productivity, (2) better user satisfaction, (3) direct control of the system, (4) ease of implementation, and (5) enhanced decision-making effectiveness. However, some problems and risks associated with the use of UDA have been identified by researchers [1,2], which include: (1) use of faulty analysis technique, (2) errors in data retrieval and analysis, (3) lack of documentation, (4) lack of training, and (5) lack of comprehensibility.

Given the growing use of and problems associated with UDA, assessment of the effectiveness of UDA has received much attention by researchers. Cheney, Mann and Amoroso [7] studied the impact of organizational factors on the success of UDA. Amoroso and Cheney [3] proposed a causal model of UDA success. Rivard and Huff [20] identified several factors that are important for its success. Several similar studies also suggested that certain user and organizational variables may have impacts on the effectiveness of UDA [5,13,14,24]. However, all of the studies are based on the premise that UDA consists only of personal applications (the user's own products).

McLean [17] suggested three types of computer applications: personal, departmental, and corporate. The first are designed to provide information for an individual. Departmental applications are designed to provide the reports, analyses, etc. The last are generally large systems designed to serve internal and external requirements for the organization. In their study, Sumner and Klepper [23] found that less than five percent of the UDA are “personal.” Most applications are used by single departments or among many departments. Those applications are developed by functional area specialists and are used to support multiple users. Rockart and Flannery [22] also found that less than a third of the end-user applications are personal, while more than half of them provide complex analysis such as financial, engineering, operations research, simulation, and optimization. Those applications are relevant to entire departments and are developed by functional support personnel.

Based on this discussion, there is evidence that personal applications were overemphasized in previous studies. Much less research has been directed at departmental or corporate applications. In particular, little attention has been given to the difference in effectiveness between user development and traditional MIS departmental development. The advantages and drawbacks of user vs. traditional development are based on conceptual discussions; very little empirical research exists. The research question addressed is:

From the non-developer users' viewpoint, is there a difference in system effectiveness between user- and MIS-developed applications?

## 2. Theoretical perspective

One of the major problems in prior user-developed applications research is the lack of theoretical basis $[6,9]$ . Robey and Zmud $[21]$ criticize the end user computing (EUC) literature; they state that

"The importance of EUC is reflected in the literature, where issues of benefits, costs, expansion, and control are extensively debated. Unfortunately, the debates are rarely backed by strong empirical research. Where research has been conducted, it has mainly been descriptive and not grounded in specific theories of organizational behaviour. Since the issue is an organizational issue, the failure to draw upon existing theory within the organizational sciences is disappointing."

The reference discipline of agency theory has provided the theoretical grounding for this research.

## 2.1. Agency theory

Agency theory examines the relationships between two units, one of which is “acting for” the other. An agency relationship exists when one unit, the “principal,” engages another, the “agent,” to perform some service that involves delegating some decision making authority to the agent [4]. Agents may be engaged for many reasons, including situations where the principal is incapable of performing the service and where the agent is engaged for symbolic reasons [18].

The major issue in agency relationships is ensuring that the agent acts in the interests of the principal $[10]$ . Often, the goals of the principal and the agent are based on their own self interests and are rarely in perfect alignment. According to Gurbaxani and Kemerer $[11,12]$ , this occurs because (1) their goals are inconsistent with one another (goal incongruence) and (2) the principal cannot perfectly monitor the actions and the information of the agent (information asymmetries). Control structures are thus instituted to align principal and agent interests. Robey and Zmud state “An agency cost arises from the separation of task execution and task control, one mechanism for reducing these costs involves re-locating task execution and task control.”

## 2.2. System development effectiveness from an agency perspective

In system development effectiveness research, an end-user can be considered the principal and the system developer as the agent. There are two resulting principal-agent relationships. In one, the agent is the functional support personnel. In the other, the agent is the MIS department (see Fig. 1).

When computing was first introduced, most end-users were not familiar with the technology. Application systems were developed almost exclusively by professionals in the MIS department. In this environment, a user department relied on IS professionals. These agents were typically in one centralized department for economics of scale and specialization. However, this agent relationship introduced costs through goal incongruence and information asymmetries [11].

The primary goal incongruence in the traditional environment is that the MIS department is acting on behalf of the organization, it provides application systems that meet the needs of the entire organization, not just an individual user department. The primary information asymmetries in the traditional environment is that knowledge of the value of a given application system is possessed by the end-user, while information about execution of the task is possessed by the MIS department. Thus, corporate managers face the problem of constructing a control system that will maximize the net value of information services to the firm while taking into account the existence of these information asymmetries.

Because the costs of computing continued to decrease, and EUC technology became an option, end-user departments started to implement their own application systems. User development offers an option to minimize the agency costs resulting from the self-interested behaviour of the MIS department.

Based on the agency model, there exists an essential tension between user development and MIS development. Agency theory suggests that end-users will desire greater autonomy in order to avoid the agency costs that arise in the traditional development solution.

## 3.1. System development effectiveness

The performance variable for this model is system effectiveness. This refers to the effectiveness of a specific application system. Several surrogate measures have been proposed in the literature. System utilization is generally recognized as one of the important indicators of success. Trice and Treacy [26] reviewed the past ten years of research literature and concluded that “utilization measures are also of great practical significance in a computing environment that is increasingly driven by voluntary users." In this study, system utilization is selected as the surrogate measure for system effectiveness.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1. User development will be more effective than MIS development.

Specific support for this hypothesis comes from a number of studies in the user-developed applications literature and the literature on agency theory $[16]$ . The basic premise is that users will have a more positive attitude toward application systems that are developed by functional support personnel. It is expected that in the user development environment, goal incongruence and information asymmetries between developer and user can be minimized.

Hypothesis 2. For departmental applications, user development approach is more effective than an MIS development approach. For corporate applications, MIS development is more effective than user development. That is, the interaction of systems development method and application scope will be significant.

The application systems of interest in this study are departmental and corporate applications. When used by one department only, it is a departmental application. Otherwise, it is a corporate application. It is expected that user development will be more effective than MIS development when the application is used by one department. When the application is used by more, MIS development is expected to be more effective.

## 4.2. Research variables and operational measures

## Dependent variable

The effectiveness of different system development approaches is defined as the degree to which an application achieves its goal from an end user perspective. Here, systems utilization was selected as the surrogate measure.

Systems utilization. Based on previous MIS research $[15,25]$ , intensity and frequency of system use were included here. Intensity of use was measured by the respondent selecting one of 6 time categories of daily use from almost never to more than 3 hours per day. Frequency of use was measured in 6 categories from less than once per month to several times a day. An index of the system utilization was computed by averaging the two component scores.

## Independent variables

System development method. The method was measured by responses to the question “Who developed this application?” They were either MIS or user developed.

Application scope. Scope of application systems refers to the number of departments that use the application. It was measured by asking the question: How many departments have personnel who run this application? Applications were departmental applications (used by one) or corporate otherwise.

## Controlled variables

System characteristics. This refers to the nature of the application system. To ensure comparability, corporate MIS managers were asked to provide applications that meet the following criteria in the initial contact.

(1) be in use for at least 6 months;

(2) serve more than 5 users;

(3) support but do not replace decision making;

(4) have interactive processing;

(5) are flexible and adaptable;

(6) have use and control determined by the user.

(6) have use and control determined by the user.
Departmental status. In order to ensure cross-organizational comparisons, users in the MIS department or information centre were excluded from this study. The helps ensure that all respondents have similar access to the organizational information and respond from an equivalent bias.

## 4.3. Research design and data collection

The research design was a cross-sectional survey in which a structured questionnaire was used to gather data from a sample of organizations. The faculty at the author's institution initially reviewed the instrument for ambiguities and face validity. A pretest was performed in order to discover unforeseen problems in the construction and administration of the questionnaire. One local company served as the pretest study site. Seven questionnaires were administered there.

Ninety-five organizations selected from the Book of Lists published by the Memphis Business Journal were sent a brief letter describing the research project. Corporate MIS directors were targeted as the initial contact person. In most cases, subsequent contacts were made at the same level. Thirty organizations which indicated their willingness to participate in the study were contacted by telephone to explain the data collection process of the study in further detail. Questionnaires were sent to and distributed by MIS directors or functional department managers (on referral by MIS directors). Respondents sealed their questionnaires in envelopes that were pre-addressed to the author.

For the purposes of this study, an end user was defined as a person who directly interacted with an application system and used the outputs to make decisions in their work [8]. Since the application system was the focus of the field survey, each one was considered as a separate case for data analysis purpose and three users completed the survey questionnaire. Instructions asked the user to write the name of the application system. Thus, the responses are application specific.

## 6. Conclusion and implication

This study was undertaken to address the nature of system development. The agency theory provides a meaningful way of explaining differences between user- and MIS-developed applica-

The results show that there are differences between user- and MIS-developed applications. User development approaches might be an effective solution to avoid agency costs in developing application systems. It is, however, quite possible that the scope of the application may affect the degree of system effectiveness. When the application is used by more than one department, the MIS development approach is more effective. The agency theory suggests that the MIS department will act for the organization and provide applications that meet all its needs.

Functional area specialists often develop applications for other departments, and this is certain to affect the extent of user acceptance. The more understanding that managers have of the system development approach, the more precisely they can control their allocation of resources.
