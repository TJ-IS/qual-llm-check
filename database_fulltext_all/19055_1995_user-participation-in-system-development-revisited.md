# Techniques

# User participation in system development revisited

Angèle L.M. Cavaye

School of Systems Engineering, Policy Analysis and Management, Delft University of Technology, Delft, 2600 GA Netherlands

## Abstract

There is an extensive literature on user participation in system development. Individual papers discuss separate components of the participation issue. Papers study individual factors associated with participation, investigate the process of participation and discuss the link between participation and system success. In spite of the many studies in this area, findings are not consistent or cumulative. One reason for this may be that participation is a nebulous term that is difficult to define clearly: it is a concept with many dimensions. This paper highlights a number of attributes of participation, presents a framework which is used to describe and synthesise existing research findings, and includes an analysis of empirical literature in an attempt to explain inconsistency in findings.

Keywords: User participation; User involvement; System development; System success

## 1. Introduction

A significant body of information systems (IS) research is concerned with the involvement and participation of users in the system development process. Many different factors impacting on and affecting participation have been identified. The process of participation has also been discussed in detail. Participation is a necessary component of participative design methods; some form of it is considered desirable in most traditional system development methods. Participation is often encouraged in order to ensure that user requirements are met, to gain user commitment, and to avoid user resistance.

Participation is not purely an IS topic, but also is the subject of research in many other disciplines. IS research on user participation draws on literature in other areas like organisational behaviour, human relations, and psychology. The current paper focuses on research findings in the IS context only.

Much of the IS studies on participation investigate its effect on system success. For many years it was considered axiomatic that user participation has a significant positive influence on the eventual success of the system. However, empirical studies are not able to show consistently that there is a causal relationship between user participation and system success; research provides mixed results. It has been known for some time that there are information systems development projects where users participate but which are not successful; there are also projects which are successful but where users do not participate $[18,13]$ . It seems that participation is neither sufficient nor necessary to guarantee system success. The link between user participation and system success is considerably more complicated than the direct, bivariate relationship traditionally assumed.

The existing body of user participation literature is somewhat fragmented and presents inconsistent results. Existing findings are by no means useless, but in order to see the value of individual contributions, the literature needs to be properly organised and synthesised. In addition, an analysis of the findings can indicate possible reasons for inconsistency in results and can suggest potential avenues for further research.

## 2. The concept of user participation during system development

Although user participation is a concept that has been discussed for about three decades, it is a vague concept which is interpreted and applied in many different ways. It is therefore prudent to define terms and explain the concept as clearly as possible. This then provides the frame of reference for the rest of the paper.

## 2.1. Defining terms

The construct ‘user participation in system development’ contains a number of ambiguous terms. Firstly, the term user needs defining since different types of users can be identified [34]. There is senior management that may use a system’s output and that is ultimately responsible for an organisation’s investments and profitability. There is middle management that manages and monitors the work affected by the system. Thirdly, there are the employees who carry out the work and who would interact with the system on a day-to-day basis. During system development, participation from different types of users may be required at different stages of development: senior management may have to agree to development; middle management and other employees may contribute during problem definition, identification of requirements, design, and testing.

The next ambiguous term is user participation. Barki and Hartwick $[5,6]$ highlighted the fact that the terms user participation and user involvement have often been used interchangeably, but that the terms do not have the same meaning and that the two should be clearly distinguished. They define user participation as “a set of operations and activities performed by users” during system development and reserve the term user involvement for a “subjective psychological state” which influences user perceptions of the system and thus affects system success.

Next, there is the term system development. Sometimes development refers simply to designing and building of systems; at other times development refers to the whole process of developing a system with designing and building as just two stages within that. In the present context system development refers to the complete process. Individual authors identify different numbers of development stages and name them differently, but all describe essentially the same process: information system development from initial problem statement through system analysis to system design, building, and implementation.

## 2.2. Describing participation

Having defined individual terms, one still has no clear picture of “participation”. Describing user participation in terms of activities engaged in by users does not provide a complete and accurate picture, because there are many different ways in which users can partake in system development. Participation is not a clear-cut, homogeneous concept: in practice it takes many forms and can occur at many levels.

Several dimensions (or attributes) of user participation have been identified in the literature. These attributes are summarised in Table 1 and are briefly described below.

Type of participation refers to the proportion of users that participate in development [43,44]. It is conceivable that all intended users take part in development of a system; it is more likely that representatives of users are involved during development.

Degree of participation recognises that users may have different levels of responsibility during participation [29]. It is possible for users to participate in development in advisory capacity; users may be given sigh-off responsibilities at various stages; users may be deeply involved in development as part of the design team; users may also be given full responsibility for development of the system.

Content of participation refers to the fact that users may be involved in different aspects of system design [24]. It is usual that participation involves users in activities which enhance technical design of a system; users may also be involved in social design of the system and may consider the social and human impact of the new system.

Extent of participation acknowledges that participation can vary in scope during different phases of the development process [22,41]. Participation is particularly appropriate in the early stages of development which relate to problem definition and identification of requirements. Users are not likely to be involved in the physical design and coding stages. Participation is again appropriate during testing and installation of the newly built system.

Formality of participation [6]. Participation of users during system development may be formally organised using formal groups and teams, and holding discussions in official meetings. Participation may also take place through informal relationships, discussions and tasks.

Influence of participation addresses the effect of participation on the development effort $[43,29]$ . Users may participation but this may be a matter of form only: user suggestions or input may be completely ignore. Alternatively, user input and help may be taken very seriously and may effectively determine the direction and outcome of a development process.

## 3. A descriptive model of user participation

Reported research presents a fragmented picture of the need for and value of user participation in system development. Research efforts focus either on one factor or on a set of related factors influencing user participation or discuss the dynamics of the process. Both factor and process research provide valid insights but neither, on its own, can explain a concept completely. Findings from factor and process models complement each other and, when presented to gether, are able to describe a concept more fully than either can on its own.

## 4. Contingencies for user participation in system development

In an effort to explain mixed results in the early user participation literature, some researchers have adopted a contingency approach. The research literature identifies a number of factors that make user participation particularly appropriate or which facilitate it. These contingencies relate to the organisation, the project environment, and the user.

## 4.1. Organisational variables

Time for development. Involving users in the analysis, design and implementation of IS may cause delays in the process. By involving more people, with different viewpoints, it becomes more difficult (and hence will be more time-consuming) to reach consensus. Thus, if there is a time constraint on development, there is likely to be no or little user participation.

Both Hirschheim [25] and Tait and Vessey found that time constraints lead to a substantial decrease in the participation of users in the development process and that this may preclude participation. Users and designers agree that applications developed in a traditional manner are more likely to be completed on time [36].

Financial resources available. User participation increases the demand for financial resources as more people are assigned to the project and as it takes more time to implement the system [35].

When there are insufficient resources, the degree of user participation in system development may be decreased to meet the budget. When there is no constraint on resources and when top management is financially committed to system development user participation in development is facilitated [16].

Top management commitment. Top management commitment to system development is a prerequisite for the provision of funding and adequate manpower resources [25]. Commitment need not involve top management personally during development; its translates into support for IS and development projects [30]. Support from top management encourages and enables the participation of users in the development process.

## 4.2. Project-related factors

Degree of task-structure. One reason for involving users in the development process is to ensure that their requirements are captured and that the completed system reflects their needs. If needs are known, there can be less participation during development.

Schonberger [54] proposed that structured tasks at the operational control level require no or minimal participation whilst unstructured, strategic planning tasks need considerable participation. Later researchers consistently find that systems that are highly structured and well-defined do not require user participation to enhance quality or to improve the technical content of the system.

Project complexity. Complex projects may require user participation [10]. Managers tend to involve users in the development process of projects that are technically complex [56], when the tasks to be automated involve a high degree of task interdependence [37], and in cross-functional projects [10]. Size can also be considered an indicator of project complexity. User participation is common in the development of large systems, but it is often considered impractical for small systems [25].

Initiator of the project. User participation in development is particularly appropriate if the need for the system originates from non-user sources. Involving users from an early stage will enhance user commitment.

Alter [3] found that systems that are not initiated by users tend to incur higher resistance and are more likely to fail. However, in his sample all systems not initiated by users but involving high user participation during development turned out to be successful. It appears therefore that user participation may compensate for lack of user initiation. These findings were confirmed by Kim and Lee [32].

Technology available. The availability of easy-to-use software to help design an application limits the need for designer involvement and enhances user participation. In the extreme case, this can lead to independent user development of applications.

The use of design tools requires skills that users do not normally possess, whilst application generators are easier to use. Thus, projects for which application generators are available enable high user participation in development, while projects that rely on the use of tools involve less user participation [37].

Expected change brought about by the system. New systems may bring about changes to jobs, interpersonal relationships, and organisational structure [4]. If changes are unexpected, the new system may encounter resistance. User participation will help gain general support for the development effort, will make users feel more committed to the system, and thus may avoid resistance [38]. Development projects that are likely to lead to significant change, will benefit from user participation, even if the task to be automated is relatively structured and straight-forward.

## 4.3. Factors related to the user

Willingness to participate. It may be desirable for users to participate in a project but there is no guarantee that they want to. The primary motivation is that participation is more likely to lead to a system that is designed according to user needs and wants [35]. User willingness to participate increases if the proposed system and its output is perceived to be important by the user. Non-participation of users, even though given the opportunity to participate, may indicate user resistance to the system developed.

Ability to participate. Users may not immediately be capable of contributing to system development in a meaningful way, unless they understand the technology, the tasks involved, and the environment within the system will operate [4]. Designers often feel users do not have the knowledge to contribute to the development process [46,57]. However, effective communication between system specialists can overcome the knowledge gap: it can allow for learning, enhance user knowledge, and enable meaningful user participation [1,11].

Users may not be able to participate because they are geographically removed from the project or because their own jobs take priority [10].

User characteristics and attitudes. Cognitive differences between users and specialists may deter user participation [58]. However, when users are interested in the system, they are likely to want to participate regardless of potential conflict with system designers [56].

## 5. The process of user participation in system development

There is no guarantee that user participation is effective in contributing to the final outcome of the project. It is a process that takes place over time and involves interaction between users and developers. Users contribute to ensure that the system will conform to their needs; system specialists contribute to the process to ensure that good use is made of technological opportunities.

Effectiveness of user participation is affected by the user-analyst relationship. The different backgrounds and perspectives of users and specialists potentially lead to conflict during user-designer interaction $[52,53]$ . The degree and effectiveness of participation depends on the relative ability of users and designers to exert influence, their relative power positions and the ability and willingness of each party to communicate $[40]$ .

User participation can only be effective if users can exert influence during the development process. Users may participate, but their input may be ignored, thus limiting their degree of participation. Conversely, users may be able to force the system specialists to accept their input, thus enhancing their degree of participation [29].

The extent to which users are able to exert influence is related to their power position. In many cases actual or perceived power implicitly lies with system specialists [40]. Descriptive studies show that designers are aware of and use their position – at times to the detriment of the final outcome of the project [23,45].

Effective communication can ensure mutual understanding and thus can affect agreement between user and analyst. Effective communication can be influenced by the relative position in the organisation of the two parties, by the extent to which either party's involvement is mandatory, and by the semantic gap between the users and specialists [14].

Because system development is a process, the user-specialist relationship is not likely to remain stable over time: the relative ability to exert influence changes as time progresses [39]. Newman and Robey show that user-analyst relationships indicate the trajectory of a project; they suggest that critical encounters may change the nature of relative user-specialist influence and thus may change the trajectory of a project.

The development of systems involving user-specialist interaction is a process characterised by likely conflict and by fluctuations in relative influence of the parties. Because of the dynamic nature of the process, there is no guarantee that participation is effective. The outcome of the development venture is thus affected by the quality and nature of user-specialist interaction.

## 6. Variables moderating the participation-success link

Users may have participated and the process may have been effective, but the outcome need not necessarily be successful. Recent empirical studies have investigated the effect of intervening variables on the relationship between participation and system success: there mechanisms are user cognitive and motivational factors.

Perceived control. Baronas and Louis [8] propose that the process of implementing a new system represents a threat to users' sense of control over their work. By giving the users a sense of control during implementation, they are able to accept the new system and will rate it in a more positive way. They conclude from their study that user participation during development may have less impact on subsequent satisfaction with the new system than efforts that enhance or restore perceived control.

Desired level of participation. A user's desired level of participation as compared to his/her actual level of participation may affect user satisfaction. Most participation research assumes that are involved to the degree that they wish. However, this is not necessarily so, and it may have a detrimental effect. Testing discrepancy theory Doll and Torkzadeh found that users who are substantially more or less involved than they desire, are less satisfied with the outcome of system development. King and Lee [33] conclude that the actual amount of participation in IS development may well contribute less towards satisfaction than ensuring that users' desires for participation are closely matched with actual participation.

Perceived importance and relevance. Barki and Hartwick [5] introduced the notion of user involvement as a need-based psychological state. User attitude is generated by the extent to which the system is both “important and personally relevant” to the user.

## 7. The user participation-success relationship

The standard model of user participation shows a direct causal relationship between user involvement and system success. However, studies have reported mixed results. Olson and Ives [48] reviewed 22 empirical studies carried out between 1959 and 1981. They show that there are positive, negative and inconclusive findings. Only 8 studies (36% of the sample) supported a positive participation-success link.

Updating the review, Table 2 identifies 19 empirical studies in this area between 1982 and 1992 and shows the conclusion drawn concerning the participation-success relationship. Only 7 of these studies (37%) found a positive relationship. Comparing the two review results, it is clear that, in spite of 10 more years of research, the 1982–1992 findings are still just as inconclusive as those of earlier studies.

A different approach for comparing research results was taken by Pettingell et al. [49] They report on a meta-analytical study of findings: they combine research findings and statistically test the strength of the relationship between user participation and system success. The study indicates that there is a positive and significant participation-success link. In spite of conflicting individual research findings the relationship cannot be dismissed as unimportant or irrelevant.

The literature regularly proclaims the importance of and need for user participation in development, but empirical research continues to provide inconsistent results. The remainder of this paper attempts to explain this inconsistency.

## 8. Analysis of existing empirical research on user participation

This section highlights the limitations and weaknesses of current research findings. They concern the limitation of widespread use of quantitative research methods; incomplete and inconsistent operationalization of variables; absence of a repeated use of the same research instrument; a lack of attention to important contingencies like the type of system studied and the development method employed. Each of these is discussed in turn.

## 8.1. Selection of research approaches

Table 3 shows the incidence of research strategies employed in empirical studies on user participation.

Surveys are the normal research strategy amongst the studies investigated. The use of surveys enables the collection of a large amount of structured data to test hypotheses, but it is not appropriate for exploring context and uncovering new relationships. Divergence of findings among existing survey-based studies, suggests there may be a need to more clearly identify contextual factors which impact on user participation before testing relationships with survey methods.

Increased use of case research and qualitative methods can enable researchers to capture the 'rich picture' by studying the concept of participation in its particular context. Use of quantitative research to study participation is not new. Much of the literature on the participation process is based on case research (e.g. [46,52]). Also, the European literature on socio-technical design [41] and on the Scandinavian design approach (e.g.

[19]) provides examples of participation studies based on action research, with the authors having been actively involved in the participation projects they describe. Such studies provide a full and rich description of the setting, the context, and the participants themselves. Qualitative work of this kind may lead to deeper understanding of the participation relationships, the process, and the intervening mechanisms.

## 8.2. Operationalization of participation and success constructs

Mixed results in existing quantitative studies may be the result of inconsistent operationalization of constructs. This was the conclusion of Ives and Olson in their review of research between 1959 and 1981. Table 4 lists empirical studies on user participation since 1981 and shows the measurements employed for user participation and for system success.

Participation is consistently measured by user activities during various phases of system development. Most researchers also attempt to measure the actual influence of the user during the development process. However, earlier in this paper it was shown that participation can be described in terms of a number of dimensions (Table 1): type, degree, content, scope, formality, influence, and perhaps still more. By only measuring scope (user activities during system development phases) and sometimes measuring degree and influence of participation, only a partial picture of participation is presented. The inconsistency and incompleteness of operationalization of the participation construct may well account for inconsistent results.

System success is ideally measured in economic terms by return on investment: a system is successful if its return compares favourably with alternative investment opportunities. However, economic justification and evaluation is difficult as intangible costs and especially benefits of IS are, firstly, hard to identify and, secondly, difficult to express in financial terms. Instead of evaluating system success in economic terms, the IS community relies on surrogates [9].

User satisfaction is the most widely used surrogate for system success in the empirical user participation literature at large. Satisfied users do not necessarily indicate that a system is successful [42]: it is possible to have unsuccessful systems with satisfied users and, similarly, to have an effective system with unsatisfied users. However, user satisfaction is accepted as the main indicator of success, particularly when system use is mandatory.

System use is an appropriate measure of system success when system use is discretionary or voluntary $[15]$ . Other success measures employed in empirical studies on user participation include progressive use of information technology $[30]$ , and resolution of conflict $[52]$ .

## 8.3. Use of research instruments

The diversity in operationalization has, by default, meant that researchers have used many different research instruments to measure the constructs. Use of a mixture of instruments prevents research findings from being cumulative.

Actual instruments to measure participation are different for nearly every study. Only two used the same instrument: King and Lee used the instrument developed by Doll and Torkzadeh. Recent work by Barki and Hartwick [6,7] presents a research instrument which is the most comprehensive one to date for measuring participation. It addresses three dimensions of participation: overall responsibility, user-analyst relationship, and actual hands-one activity. This instrument could be adopted by other researchers and may thus provide a solid base for accumulating further evidence on participation.

The success construct is usually operationalized in terms of user satisfaction, though actual instruments used to measure the construct vary. Further research should be aimed at clarifying the construct and, particularly, at building of validated research instruments [29]. Only then can studies be replicated and a cumulative body of findings be produced.

## 8.4. User participation and types of system

Most studies discuss user participation in the context of a traditional data processing environment: data is collected on user participation in the development of transaction processing and management information systems. Very few studies concentrate on other types of systems.

Previous studies have called for further research into user participation in the development of particular types of system and in a variety of environments (e.g. [17]). Different environments create different contexts for user participation. Studies focusing on a specific type of system as the unit of analysis allow us to build prescriptive models for particular applications. Examples of specific environments include End User Development and inter-organisational systems.

Development by end users. End User Development (EUD) is the development of computer applications by non-computer people for their own business needs. EUD became possible due to falling costs in computer technology and an increasing number of easy-to-use development tools. System development need no longer be confined to specialists. Instead, the user can take control of the development effort and develop applications with little or no assistance from system specialists. EUD can thus be considered an example of ultimate user involvement [12].

User participation in the EUD environment is different from that in the traditional environment. In the EUD environment, users lead and steer the development project or independently develop their applications. This means that problems concerning determination of information requirements are reduced or eliminated $[51]$ . It also means that concerns about the user-analyst relationship are less relevant.

Development of inter-organisational systems. Inter-organisational systems (IOS) extend beyond the boundaries of one organisation; they occur due to advances in computer and communication technologies, globalization of businesses, and an awareness of the strategic importance of inter-organisational links.

User participation in the development of IOS differs from that in systems that are only internal to the organisation: in IOS, intended users are outside the organisation developing the system. This may have implications on their ability and willingness to participate. Also, the development of an IOS is likely to have a strategic impact and, for reasons of confidentiality, the sponsoring organisation may want to limit or avoid participation by users external to the organisation [50].

## 8.5. Participation and type of development method

In existing quantitative research on participation, little attention is paid to the link between development method and participation. In some development methods participation is an integral part of the method: high involvement of users is sought and great importance is placed on their input. In other development methods participation takes a much less central role and is only sought if necessary or unavoidable. Two diametrically opposed underlying philosophies in this respect are the functionalist and the neo-humanist paradigm $[26,27]$ .

Functionalism. Many system development methodologies were and are based on functionalist assumptions aiming at efficiency and effectiveness. Participation in the functionalist tradition means that involvement of users is sought in order to build functionally correct and efficient systems. Participation, then, is considered to be a means to an end: it can help provide better information on requirements, overcomes resistance, and validates design options. The objective is to create a 'better' system that is more likely to be used by potential users. If there is no apparent need to involve users (for instance, when the task to be automated is structured or when use of the system is going to be mandatory), participation may not be sought or may not be taken very seriously.

Neo-humanism. In this world view participation is accorded a central role. Rather than placing the system at the centre of the development effort, people and the potential users of the system are considered the most important. Examples of methods in this tradition include ETHICS [27] and Scandinavian approaches [19]. Participation is seen as a way of improving understanding between users and system builders; participation is also encouraged to enable users to have input in changes to their own work environment. It is the objective of the development effort to create a superior workplace for the people working there; the end result of participation should be a human being with increased understanding, an expanded workplace, and en enriched working life.

## 9. Summary

This paper provides an overview of current findings related to user participation in system development. A framework was presented and user participation findings were discussed within its context. The findings concerning the relationship between user participation and system success were discussed.

In addition, an analysis of findings was presented. Possible reasons for the inconsistent results were explored by highlighting limitations and weaknesses of existing empirical research. It was suggested that limitations may be overcome by

\- increased use of non-experimental and non-quantitative research approaches (as common in European literature on participation);

\- full and consistent operationalization of constructs;

\- development and use of validated instruments;

\- examining the user participation construct for specific types of systems (for instance, in the context of inter-organisational systems);

– viewing participation in the context of development method employed.
