# Measurement

# A blueprint for theory-building in MIS

Ashok Subramanian

University of Missouri-St. Louis, St. Louis, MO, USA

Sree Nilakanta

Iowa State University, Ames, IA, USA

This paper provides a methodology for validating measures used in MIS research. In the absence of any referent disciplines upon which to base the development of a measure, the appropriate method would be to refine the instrument through a series of validation checks. We describe a procedure that accomplishes this function. The methodology consists of combining three statistical methods, namely, principal components analysis or exploratory factor analysis, multitrait-multimethod analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis. The use of these three conforms to the notion of triangulation in research methods. The user information satisfaction measure is employed to illustrate the application of our methodology. We feel that such cumulative testing gives information system researchers a valuable tool for theory-building.

Keywords: MIS research; User information satisfaction measure; Triangulation; Principal components analysis; Exploratory factor analysis; Multitrait-multimethod; Confirmatory factor analysis.

## Introduction

Progress in theory development depends on a cumulative approach to theory-building [17]. In this respect, the importance of measurement rigor in testing various theories cannot be overemphasized. The development and adoption of standard and universal constructs and measures greatly facilitates progress in theory development [13]. Behavioral research in MIS is replete with measures of all kinds and with varying claims about their validity.

Information is the life blood of organizations. Its flow and accessibility to the vital organs are essential to the vitality and long-term survival of the firm. Information systems researchers, cognizant of the significance of information to organizations, have been concerned with the successful and effective implementation of information systems to attain these goals. In this respect, measuring the effectiveness of information systems technology has been a long-standing research endeavor.

This paper provides a methodology for validating measures used in MIS research. Researchers have espoused the need for rigor and precision in measurement but no one has given the researcher a practical, clear, and focused direction of how this can be achieved $[4,9,19,26]$ . Our approach does not presume either an empiricist's or a theorist's model of theory development to be employed in designing and using measures. We believe, however, that only measures subjected to this verification methodology are capable of substantiating any theory. The methodology consists of combining three statistical methods, namely, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), multitrait-multimethod (MMA), and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The use of these three methods conform to the notion of triangulation in research methods. The user information satisfaction (UIS) measure is employed to illustrate the application of our methodology. In information systems research, the most widely used construct for over two decades has clearly been user satisfaction.

## The UIS measure

UIS is defined as the extent to which users perceive that the information system available to them meets their information requirements. The UIS concept has its foundations in the works of Cyert and March [6], who suggest that an information system that meets the needs of its users will reinforce their satisfaction with the system.

The UIS construct has been operationalized and measured in many different ways. Other researchers have attempted to measure information system effectiveness through different constructs, such as characteristics of the information, namely, accuracy, relevancy, and perceived value, etc. [22,28]. Many early studies utilized single-item measures and have drawn considerable criticism because of their inherent unreliability problems. Subsequent studies, however, have utilized multiple-item measures, which typically are of two types. The first type focuses on the information system product; i.e., the content of the information systems (e.g., report accuracy, report timeliness, and response time). The second type, in addition to focusing on the information product, focuses on service issues, such as training and documentation. Thus, the latter type of measures provides an indicator of the overall quality of information service provided by the information systems function. Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [12] developed such an instrument, which has been generally accepted by the information systems research community. Their UIS measure is based on the instrument developed by Bailey and Pearson [2]. To enhance its usage in the field, they developed a shorter version by eliminating certain items and scales from the original. This shorter version seems to be quite popular in information systems research [3].

## Commonalties in UIS measures

## Item construction and validation

In several instances, item construction, determination, and validation of the constructs have followed a common path. Items to be included in a questionnaire were often determined by searching the literature on user satisfaction and/or by asking managers and users of the system to enumerate the aspects of the system that they considered important determinants of their satisfaction. Insofar as the constructs are valid and reliable, they yielded information about overall satisfaction with information systems products and services. Arguments continue to rage, however, over the theoretical basis for the user satisfaction construct. (For an assessment of the theoretical basis for the user satisfaction construct, see Kim [16] and Melone [20].)

These arguments result from a fundamental disagreement concerning how theory evolves and hence how measures evolve. For example, theorists believe that all measures must be grounded in referent disciplines, whereas empiricists place a premium on the experiences or observation of the subjects or the researcher [8]. It is not our intent to settle this dispute, but we advocate the use of our methodology equally to those measures that have evolved from both the theoretical and empirical routes.

## Identification of dimensions

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) formed the basis for identifying dimensions as well as serving as a criterion for retaining or deleting items. For example, if an item loaded equally on two or more dimensions, that item was ordinarily eliminated from consideration. Theorists would retain all items, because the items were chosen on the basis of theory. Empiricists, on the other hand, seem far too willing to throw out these items, because they would confound the factor structure.

## Shortfalls of prior methods

Validation of the UIS measure

During the early days of information systems research, instruments that measured perceptions such as UIS were not carefully validated. Later, however, more rigorous attempts at instrument validation were made. The instruments were tested for evidence of acceptable psychometric properties of reliability and validity. For clarity, we have provided definitions of the various psychometrics used in validation.

Reliability. Synonyms are dependability, stability, consistency, predictability, and accuracy. The reliability index of an instrument indicates a measure of the accuracy or “error freeness” of the instrument. There are basically two types of reliabilities.

Test-Retest Reliability. An index of the stability of the scores obtained from an instrument over multiple administrations of the instrument to the same subjects.

Internal Consistency. The degree of homogeneity or cohesiveness between items that purport to measure the same construct.

Reliability tests in information systems research have mainly been tests of internal consistency of UIS instruments. The most commonly used index of internal consistency is the Cronbach Alpha. Generally accepted UIS instruments have yielded acceptable levels of internal consistency.

Validity. There are various types for which an instrument may be tested. Some of the most common ones are:

Content Validity. Refers to the representativeness or sampling adequacy of the content and attempts to show the extent to which the measure is representative of the content or the universe of the property being measured. UIS instruments such as the Bailey and Pearson [2] questionnaire, the Jenkins and Ricketts [14] measure, and the Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [12] long and short form questionnaires have been developed after extensive surveys of users, with the intention of including all issues that users considered important contributors to UIS. Thus, these instruments display an adequate degree of content validity, because the items in the instrument represent an adequate sample of the universe of content of UIS.

Predictive Validity. Pertains to the ability of the instrument to predict outcomes. The focus is not on “what” the instrument measures but on “how well” the instrument can predict or correlate with other outcomes. In information systems research, predictive validity of an instrument is often evaluated by correlating scores obtained from the instrument with another measure of user satisfaction. For example, responses to the items on various components of UIS can be correlated to an item that measured overall satisfaction with the information system. A high degree of correlation is assumed to be indicative of evidence of predictive validity.

Construct Validity. Pertains to the extent to which the instrument measures the theoretical constructs it purports to measure. In other words, it indicates how well the theoretical constructs have been operationalized via the instrument.

Each of these types of validity criteria assumes varying degrees of importance, depending on the purpose of the study. Thus, predictive validity becomes an important issue if the instrument is to be used as a tool for prediction, e.g., predicting the success of system implementation. Construct validity, on the other hand, assumes a great deal of importance if the goal of the research is to understand the nature of abstract theoretical constructs. Thus, a test of the construct validity of a UIS instrument would help explicate the underlying dimensions or properties of the UIS constructs and facilitate the development of theoretical knowledge about them and how they relate to other constructs of interest. Since UIS is a generally accepted measure of information systems effectiveness, it is important to understand the underlying general dimensions of the UIS construct. This understanding would enable researchers to infer the effectiveness of various forms of information systems based on perceptions of users along these general dimensions.

## Tests of construct validity in IS research

UIS instruments in information systems research have been tested for evidence of construct validity using two main techniques: exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and Multitrait-Multi-method analysis (MMA). In EFA, responses to the items on the instruments are subjected to EFA to yield the underlying dimensions. Although this technique is useful for assessing the dimensionality of abstract constructs such as UIS, the construct validity of an instrument cannot be conclusively established on the basis of this technique alone [11].

In IS research, principal components analysis (PCA) has been used most often to assess the dimensionality of constructs [e.g., 10]. PCA is a variance-maximizing procedure for which the components are extracted from the sample data in such a way as to ensure that the first component explains the maximum variance in the data. The second component extracted then explains the maximum residual variance not explained by the first component. This process may continue until all the variance has been accounted for. Theoretically, one will have to extract as many components as there are variables, because the total variance attributable to each variable comprises variance that is shared with other variables as well as the unique variance of the variable [23].

To assess dimensionality, however, common factor analysis, often referred to as EFA, must be employed. It is based on the extraction of factors that represent the covariance shared by a set of variables. In essence, EFA represents a measure of commonality among the set of variables. A factor, therefore, is portraying the underlying dimension measured by the variables. Even though PCA and EFA are substantively different methods, they usually yield similar results when the number of factors extracted is small (usually tests such as SCREE tests are used to retain a small number of factors that explain a significantly large amount of variance). Hence, the use of PCA instead of EFA, though incorrect, may be acceptable as long as the number of factors is small.

However, EFA has a number of limitations, are often attributable to the assumptions of the factor model. The following assumptions are often made:

1. All common factors are correlated or all common factors are uncorrelated. This means that the structure of the construct in terms of the factors extracted cannot be specified a priori.

2. All observed variables are equally likely to be affected by all common factors. This implies that it is not possible to state a priori which variables measure a particular dimension. One may, in fact, not even know how many dimensions are truly residing in the data.

3. Unique factors (those not shared in common by the variables) are not correlated with one another, implying that common factors account for all the shared covariance among the variables. The unexplained covariance is attributed to the unique covariance of the variable.

Note that it is well known that measures of constructs often have methods variance; i.e., the shared covariance among the variables and traits is not attributable to some property they share in common but that it also includes variance in the data attributable to the common method used to measure the construct. If method variance does exist, the shared covariance is not completely explained by the common factors. In other words, the unique factors are not really unique or uncorrelated because they contain variance shared in common by the variables.

It is clear that the assumptions of the EFA model are not substantively motivated. Moreover, arbitrary assumptions must be imposed on the model to estimate its parameters. The inability of the EFA model to incorporate substantively meaningful constraints while imposing substantively meaningless constraints has earned it the label of a GIGO (garbage in garbage out) model $[1]$ .

In addition to the noted limitations, there are two other criticisms of the EFA model.

1. Constraints must be imposed on the factor analytic model to estimate the factor structure of the construct. These, however, are not substantively meaningful or justifiable because they have no theoretical basis. Therefore, EFA has been accused, in the past, of being purely data-driven and the factors that emerge may be artifacts of characteristics of the sample.

2. The criteria used to eliminate or retain factors in the final solution are rather arbitrary and so there is a tendency to either “overfactor” or “underfactor” a construct.

Despite these problems, EFA is useful as a first step for assessing the nature of abstract constructs in the absence of any guiding theory. The construct validity of instruments, however, cannot be established unless the factor structure suggested by the EFA technique can be verified further.

The MMA method of assessing construct validity involves assessing convergent and discriminant validity of common traits measured by more than one method. Thus, the traits are clearly based on some extant theory and the methods used to assess the convergent or discriminant validity among the traits must be maximally different [5]. The shortcomings of this approach are attributable to the following two reasons:

1. The MMA technique is misused in UIS research and has been inappropriately applied in some studies. In a study conducted by Larcker and Lessig [18], individual instrument items were treated as different methods. Thus, each "method" was effectively a single-item measure. Also, measures of the same item across different settings were treated as measures of different traits. Clearly, the MMA method was misused in this study. In another study, conducted by Doll and Torkzadeh [7], single items once again were treated as traits and each (determined from an EFA conducted prior to the MMA analysis) was treated as a different method. Again, the MMA method was misused.

2. The MMA technique has many shortcomings [21].

a) The criteria suggested by Campbell and Fiske for assessing convergent and discriminant validity are only rules of thumb and may be inconsistently applied.

b) Tests of significance of differences in correlations are not possible.

c) Differences in the reliabilities of the different methods are not accounted for in this approach.

The misuse of the MMA approach in UIS research and the shortcomings of the technique make the results of studies inconclusive. In the studies mentioned, single items have been treated as methods or traits and the reliability of a single-item measure is highly suspect. In addition, since items from the same instrument are treated as different methods, substantial variance may have contaminated the results.

## A cumulative approach to instrument validation

We suggest an alternative approach to information systems instrument validation. It is very clear that any single technique has strengths as well as limitations. An approach that utilizes a combination of several techniques, however, can offset the weaknesses of one with the strengths of another, thus leading to more conclusive results. We suggest an approach that utilizes three techniques: EFA, MMA, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The process of validation occurs over three phases, with a different technique of analysis applied in each. Each succeeding phase builds upon the results of the previous one by refining the analysis. This phase-wise, cumulative approach will yield stronger results and lend a high degree of credibility to claims of construct validity of the instrument.

In the case for which no useful or accepted prior theory exists, EFA provides a starting point for understanding the dimensions of the underlying constructs. However, the factor structure should be further verified by subjecting the measures to stronger and rigorous procedures. Instruments whose items are firmly rooted in referent disciplines need not be subjected to EFA. One may proceed directly to stronger CFA techniques to validate the factor structure.

The limitations of the EFA model have been resolved with the CFA model. Here, the constraints imposed on the model are substantively motivated [15]. These constraints determine (1) which common factors are correlated, (2) which observed variables are affected by which of the common factors, (3) which pairs of unique factors are affected by a unique factor, and (4) which pairs of unique factors are correlated. Statistical tests, such as the Chi-square test, goodness of fit index, and root mean square of the residuals, can be performed to determine if the sample data are consistent with the imposed constraints (i.e., to test if the data confirms the substantively generated model).

At this point, it may be worthwhile to examine some of the major differences between MMA and CFA. Some of the limitations associated with MMA are:

1. There are no statistical tests to determine if the differences in correlations (when checking for evidence of discriminant validity) are statistically significant.

2. Discriminant validity may not be achieved, because the instruments used to measure common traits have different reliabilities. For instance, if one of the instruments has a low reliability, the correlation in the validity diagonal may be low compared with the correlations in monomethod heterotrait block. This can lead to incorrect conclusions.

3. As the number of traits and methods used to measure those traits increases, the probability of some of the Campbell and Fiske criteria being violated also increases. There is no clear rule for determining how many must be satisfied before an instrument has construct validity.

4. There is no way of identifying the impact of methods variance or shared methods variance. For example, one may suspect the presence of methods variance, because the correlations in the monomethod heterotrait block are higher than the correlations in the validity diagonal. But it is not possible to determine if the correlation between traits is attributable to the fact that they are truly correlated or to the use of a similar method.

5. Finally, it is not possible to compare alternative models of the underlying structure of the construct. In other words, the data may or may not validate the model and, furthermore, different models cannot be compared to assess their relative validity.

## CFA surmounts all of these problems:

1. Statistical tests may be performed on the parameter estimates (factor loadings and factor correlations) to check if they are significantly different from zero.

2. Total variance can be explicitly partitioned into variance caused by traits (indicated by the variance explained by the common factors representing traits), variance caused by methods (indicated by the variance explained by the factor representing the method), and variance caused by unique factors (indicated by the residuals).

3. It is possible to compare two models by comparing their goodness of fit measures and statistically testing the difference in the goodness of fit measures.

For our cumulative approach, the total sample of responses is divided in a random fashion into three subsamples. One is utilized in each phase of the analysis. This method will allow, for instance, the results of the analysis on one subsample in phase 1 to be verified and refined using another subsample in phase 2. Verification of results using such “hold-out” samples ensures that the effect sizes are attributable to the properties of the construct being measured and not to any characteristic(s) unique to the sample. In addition, responses are collected using more than one method, such that each method is maximally different from each other. For example, one may use questionnaires, interviews, objective data on system use, and other nonreactive measures. Since the articulation of the user satisfaction construct, a number of questionnaires (instruments) have been developed by researchers. Each of these is believed to be significantly different from the others because they purport to measure satisfaction with respect to the differing aspects of different systems. Therefore, one may conceivably use these instruments as maximally different methods.

## Phase 1

An EFA is performed on the first subsample. The usual criteria for retaining the factors (Kaiser's rule or the Scree test) may be used. It is advisable to retain as many factors as possible at this stage: to overfactor a construct rather than to underfactor it, because the factors that are not stable will eventually disappear from the solution after repeated subsequent analyses with hold-out samples. In the absence of a guiding theory, this phase provides a starting point from which the dimensionality of the construct may be further explored.

## Phase 2

The factor solution of the previous phase is then subjected to a MMA. Each of the factors determined in the previous phase is treated as a trait, and those measured in common by different methods are tested for convergent and discriminant validity on the basis of the Campbell and Fiske criteria. This phase provides a tentative measure of the construct validity of the instrument. If the results suggest evidence of construct, the analysis proceeds to the next phase.

## Phase 3

CFA is next performed on the third subsample. The factor structure suggested by the analyses of the previous phases is tested and verified. The CFA has a number of refinements over the EFA or MMA methods, as follows:

(a) CFA provides estimates of parameters. Further, the constraints imposed on the factor analytic model in the CFA are substantively meaningful, because they are based on the knowledge and insights gained from the previous analyses.

(b) It is possible to perform statistical tests of “goodness of fit” of the factor structure. This provides an indicator of the true structure of the construct. No such tests are possible with the EFA or MMA methods.

(c) The approach adjusts the results to accommodate the differential reliabilities of the instruments. The MMA analysis with unreliable instruments may provide misleading results.

(d) The technique allows researchers to partition the variance into parts caused by construct, method, and error. This helps provide insight as to why convergent and discriminant validity has or has not been attained. Thus, it may be used as a diagnostic and hypothesis testing tool.

## Phase 4

This phase need not be conducted if the results of the previous phases are satisfactory. Here, a number of different factor structures of the construct are evaluated by “nesting” one model within another. We begin with a relatively unconstrained factor analytic model (e.g., that suggested by the analysis in phase 3). Additional constraints are then imposed on this factor pattern, leading to a more restricted factor model.

The nesting process can continue imposing additional constraints on each previously restricted model. Each of these nested models is tested for statistical goodness of fit. The most parsimonious model (one with the maximum number of constraints) with an acceptable goodness of fit index is then selected as the model of the true structure of the construct being examined. These tests of nested models, however, should be performed using additional hold-out samples. Thus, if the process is carried out, the total sample should be divided into more than three subsamples.

Failure to satisfy the conditions or tests indicated in the phases suggests that the instrument is problematic. The designer must go back to developing the items with more attention to a theoretical basis. This implies that if an empirical approach to theory building is undertaken, several iterations may be needed to develop a useful instrument. In the absence of a reliable one, each application will yield nongeneralizable results.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we have argued for the use of a methodology built on the cumulative tradition to validate measures used in information systems research. We have shown how the methodology may be employed in the case of UIS measure, which has received a great deal of attention in IS research. In the absence of any referent disciplines upon which to base the development of a measure, the appropriate method would be to refine the instrument through a series of validation checks. We describe a procedure that makes use of three commonly used methods: exploratory factor analysis, multitrait multimethod analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis. Taken separately, these methods have limitations that will curtail their utility but taken together in a lock-step fashion, they overcome most if not all problems. An instrument validated in this fashion will be more stable and reliable.

Psychometrically sound instruments are essential for the validation of any theory because only through observation of the phenomena can one test theories. Instruments help make observations easy. We believe that our methodology of cumulative testing gives IS researchers a valuable tool for theory building.
