# The test-retest reliability of user involvement instruments

Gholamreza Torkzadeh

The University of Texas at El Paso, El Paso, TX, USA

William J. Doll

The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH, USA

It is almost an axiom of the MIS literature that user involvement is a necessary condition for successful development of information systems. Despite this acceptance, Ives and Olson have observed that, hampered by conflicting findings and weak measures, over two decades of research has not convincingly demonstrated the benefits of user involvement. They contend that there is a need to develop reliable and valid measures of user involvement and system success. This paper reports on and extends a program to develop standard user involvement instruments that can measure end-user developed applications as well as others using more traditional methodology. The short-range and long-range stability of instruments that measure perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence is assessed via the test-retest method. The results suggest that these instruments are internally consistent, stable, and can be used with confidence in user involvement research without concern about a reactivity effect.

Keywords: Test-retest reliability; User involvement.

One would expect a relationship that is so widely accepted would have strong theoretical and empirical support. MIS researchers have shown significant interest in the role of user involvement in the success of information systems (Robey & Farrow, 1982; Franz & Robey, 1986; Swanson, 1974; Olson & Ives, 1981). However, Ives and Olson (1984) observe that research results do not convincingly demonstrate the benefits of user involvement. Determining when and how much, under what conditions, or even if, user involvement is appropriate are questions that have received inadequate research attention. Ives and Olson contend that user involvement research is hampered by weak measures of involvement and satisfaction. Citing non-significant findings and conflicting research results, they call for a rigorous program of instrument development to create standard measures with demonstrated reliability and validity. More recently, Straub (1989) supports the contention that instrument validation has been inadequately and inappropriately addressed in MIS research and that this has hampered substantive research efforts.

Researchers in the information systems field have shown considerable interest in developing standard measures of important dependent variables, such as general user satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Ives, Olson & Baroudi, 1983) or end-user satisfaction with a specific application (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988). Using 27 respondents, Galletta and Lederer (1988) conducted a test-retest for Ives, Olson and Baroudi's general user satisfaction instrument. Based on the significant difference observed in mean scores at two points in time, they concluded that the instrument previously accepted as reliable is, in fact, unreliable. Possible explanations of this instability are: inherent instability of the phenomenon itself; a reactivity effect, suggesting the need for prior exposure; and shifting frames of reference between test (i.e., the first administration of the instrument) and retest (i.e., the second administration of the instrument following a time period), a problem inherent in the nature of the general user satisfaction construct. Such concerns have prompted researchers to assess the test-retest reliability of the end-user computing satisfaction instrument (Torkzadeh & Doll, 1991). Although the end-user satisfaction instrument was found to be stable, these studies point out that stability can not be assumed. Stability is assessed by the test-retest method; the stability measure is equal to the correlation between scores on the same test obtained at two points in time.

The development of standard measures of important independent variables such as user involvement in systems design has received less attention. The importance of user involvement, the weak measures used in this research domain, and the non-significant or conflicting research findings in this area suggest that there is a need for a valid and reliable instrument. A standardized measure of user involvement would enhance research efforts by facilitating comparisons between studies. It would also help in assessing the relative impact of variables, identifying contingency relationships and validating measures of related constructs. Carmines and Zeller (1979) point out that, before an instrument can be used with confidence by researchers, careful documentation and reporting of the instrument development process is necessary, including a description of the methods used to assess validity and reliability. Measures of reliability include internal consistency and stability. Nunnally (1978) maintains that if a test does not even correlate with itself when administered on two occasions, it is hopeless to seek other evidence of reliability and hopeless to employ the test in correlational studies.

## 2. A program to develop measures of user involvement constructs

The explicit objectives of the instrument development program were to develop user involvement instruments that:

(1) could be used for applications developed either by traditional methodologies or by end-users;

(2) focus on user participation in specific development activities;

(3) provide measures of user involvement in the development of a specific application (i.e., an application specific measure);

(4) provide measures of perceived involvement, as well as other related constructs such as the user's desire to be involved;

(5) were short, easy to use, and appropriate for both academic research and practice; and

(6) can be used with confidence and without prior exposure across a variety of application types (i.e., have adequate reliability and validity).

In the past, user involvement studies had assumed a traditional data processing context, where the involvement arrangements often imposed range restrictions (i.e., limit the extent of user participation in systems development activities). Such restrictions, as well as weak measures, have been considered a possible explanation of the many non-significant findings in the user involvement literature.

The traditional data processing context assumes a two party (user vs. analysts) relationship and a separation of responsibility for planning vs. executing computer mediated work. Opportunities for user involvement are necessarily restricted by this separation of “work planning” from “work doing”. In studies assuming a traditional context, user involvement is often conceptualized in terms of the relative influence of analysts vs. users (e.g., Edstrom, 1977) or as a dyadic communication relationship between user and specialist (DeBrabander & Edstrom, 1977; DeBrabander & Thiers, 1984). In this context, the systems staff exerts considerable influence in the development of new applications (e.g., planning how work is to be done on the computer). Working on behalf of management, the systems analyst is often primarily responsible for application development activities, such as project definition, system objectives, and logical design, as well as physical design and implementation. Users may participate or “share” to some extent in these planning activities, but they are primarily responsible for operating the system as designed. This splitting of managerial and operative work had its origin in the industrial revolution and the factory system. However, it is still frequently used in the development of many critical transaction processing systems where users have little autonomy.

Increasingly, end-users are performing activities that used to be done by systems analysts.

User friendly development tools (e.g., fourth generation languages) make it easier for users to develop their own applications (Rivard & Huff, 1988). When users develop their own applications, they do not have to translate and communicate their information needs to an analyst; thus, the problems inherent in determining information requirements are reduced or eliminated. Responsibility for both planning and executing computer mediated work rests with the end-users; this enhances opportunities for “learning by doing.” End-user development tools improve the directness of involvement arrangements, facilitate learning (i.e., continuous improvement), and create a greater variety of involvement situations. For example, in an end-user context, involvement can occur in at least three types of applications: those being developed by a systems analyst, usually a multi-user application; those being developed by another end-user such as functional support personnel; and those that the user is developing, perhaps a “personal” application. Measures of user involvement constructs (e.g., perceived involvement and desired involvement) must be appropriate for all three situations.

To provide a conceptual definition of user involvement that could be applicable in all three involvement situations, the participative decision making literature was reviewed (Lock & Schweiger, 1979; Lowin, 1968; Dachler & Wilpert, 1978; Walker, 1967; Cotton et al., 1988). Walker's conceptualization of participation seemed the most appropriate, with participation defined in terms of the extent to which workers take part in certain functions (i.e., activities) described as "managerial". These activities include planning, organizing, directing, and controlling task performance. Such activities are contrasted with "doing" or operative work. Thus managerial activities set the plan or blueprint by which work is to be done; they structure the work situation by determining the tasks that are to be carried out, the work methods and technologies to be utilized, the conditions under which work is performed, and the controls that are available to ensure performance.

Based upon this, user involvement was defined as the extent to which the user engages in these system development activities that determine how computer mediated work is to be performed. It is viewed solely in terms of the end-user rather than by being a construct of relative effort or influence vis a vis a systems analyst. Thus, it is applicable to all three involvement situations. Systems development includes analysis, design, and implementation activities. User involvement in these activities is described by Lucas's concept of “user controlled development” (1978).

## 2.1. Perceived involvement

The instrument development process, including the methods used to generate and exclude items, has been carefully documented and is reported elsewhere, along with evidence of construct and criterion related validity and reliability (i.e., internal consistency) (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1990). A factor analysis revealed three factors that were interpreted as involvement in systems analysis (project initiation and logical design), implementation, and administrative activities. An analysis of discriminant validity (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) revealed that the systems analysis factor was the only one whose items met the conditions for discriminant validity. Thus, we recommended an eight item perceived user involvement scale focusing on project initiation and system analysis activities. This eight item scale had a reliability (Cronbach's alpha) of 0.96. The eight item user involvement instrument was found to have adequate reliability and criterion-related validity across a variety of applications; it can be used for end-user developed applications as well as applications developed by more traditional methodologies. However, the test-retest stability of this eight item perceived user involvement instrument was not assessed.

## 2.2. Desired involvement

The instrument had been found to be useful in testing a discrepancy model of end-user computing involvement (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1989) that hypothesized a contingency relationship between desired involvement, perceived involvement, and end-user satisfaction. Under conditions of deprivation, equilibrium, and saturation, perceived user involvement measured via this eight item scale was found to have negative $r = -0.3$ , positive $r = +0.3$ , and non-significant correlations with end-user computing satisfaction. Desired involvement was measured using the same eight items that comprise the perceived user involvement scale (see Table 1). This highlighted the need for user involvement research that provides controls on the user's desire to get involved; thus, it suggested the need for a reliable and valid measure of desired involvement. Limited evidence of the validity and reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.93) of the eight item desired involvement scale was presented; however, no evidence of stability was given.

## 2.3. Involvement congruence

Building upon this discrepancy, Doll and Torkzadeh (1991) later suggested a congruence construct of user involvement (i.e., a measure of involvement “relative” to an individual’s desire to get involved) as an alternative way of modeling the discrepancy effect. The involvement congruence construct was found to have theoretical as well as empirical advantage over the use of component measures; it also has a research design advantage; it does not require dividing respondents by frame of reference. The involvement congruence construct has focused research attention on: both users’ desire for involvement and the contextual factors that influence this desire and the restrictive nature of typical involvement arrangements coupled with the question of how involvement arrangements can be structured so that desired involvement is the same as perceived involvement. Evidence of the reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.95) and validity of the involvement congruence construct was presented; however, no evidence of stability was provided. This program to develop measures of user involvement in systems development has resulted in three constructs (perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence) and corresponding instruments that, depending upon the research questions being explored, might be useful to researchers seeking to enhance our understanding of user involvement, its determinants, and the conditions necessary for its effectiveness. However, substantive progress in research on user involvement also depends upon the test-retest stability of these instruments.

## 3. Test-retest reliability of user involvement instruments

Our program to develop instruments was extended by examining the test-retest reliability of the instruments to measure perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence. In the effort to measure any particular attribute, numerous sources of error might be introduced by subtle factors that influence the behavior of subjects leading to variation in the measurements. People may respond differently to a set of indicators from one time to another simply because they are temporarily distracted or misunderstand the meaning of a query. However, several more systematic factors can contribute to lack of reliability in measurements over time. These factors are related to instability of the underlying theoretical construct, reactivity, when a person responding to the measures for the first time can be sensitized to the subject under investigation, and memory, people's ability to recall their first response at the second administration of the measure.

Memory factor seems to be a typical problem. If the time interval between measurements is relatively short, the subjects will remember their earlier responses and will appear more consistent than they actually are. In this case the correlation results are inflated. Thus, a single, short interval, test-retest correlation may not always permit an accurate assessment of reliability. To avoid this, Nunnally suggests an interval of at least two weeks; presumably then memory is unlikely to be a strong factor. Also, a two weeks lapse seems necessary to provide a reasonable test of stability for the underlying theoretical construct. Though time-consuming and administratively demanding, gathering data at three points in time rather than two may be justified, as it also avoids the reactivity problem by simply making the respondents familiar with the instrument.

The research design illustrated in Figure 1 was used to assess both short-range and long-range stability. In this design, the instrument to measure perceived involvement and desired involvement was administered at three points in time. Conceptually, we can distinguish between the true value, $X_{t}$ , and the obtained measurements $X_{i}$ . Here, we are making the customary assumption that true value is affected at different intervals by a random variable, $e_{i}$ , representing errors of measurement. In the short-range interval of two hours, correlation between scores for X1 and X2 is expected to be influenced by reactivity (deflating effect) and memory (inflating effect). In the long-range interval of two weeks, correlation between scores for X2 and X3 test-retest might be affected (deflated) by instability of the underlying theoretical construct.

A research design that assesses both short-range and long-rang stability has several advantages. It enables one to determine whether an apparent instability in an instrument is the result

of a reactivity effect or a continuing stability problem across multiple administrations of the instrument and whether an apparent stability in the short-range is the result of memory or an enduring characteristic of the instrument. This design may also help one distinguish between reactivity effect and instability in the underlying theoretical construct. If the results indicate that instrument is unstable in the short-range but stable in the long-range, then the results suggest a reactivity effect. If the results indicate that the instrument is stable in the short-range but unstable in the long-range, one's suspicion naturally focuses on the stability of the underlying construct.

To test the stability of the three 8-item involvement instruments, researchers used 40 subjects familiar with specific applications and administered instruments at three points in time. Respondents were M.B.A. students with considerable work experience. Instructions for the Likert-type questionnaire requested that respondents select an application they used frequently, write the name of their specific application on the questionnaire and, for each question, circle the response that best described their involvement in the development of the application. Respondents were also asked to enter their names, the purpose of the application, the type of application (decision support system or database), and the number of hours per week they interacted with the application.

The study used the items in the same sequence and with the same instructions for respondents. The scale and wording of items were exactly the same as used by us in our initial data gathering, including a single-item global perceived involvement item. In this study, a single-item global scale was added to measure users desired involvement. This item, “Overall, how much did you want to get involved in the development of this application?” used the same five-point scale.

Responses to actual and desired involvement were first gathered (X1). After a two hour interval, the instrument was administered for retest (X2). During the two hour intermission, the subjects received a lecture on a topic in MIS (not user involvement). Respondents had no prior knowledge that there would be a retest and were asked to repeat the process for the initial application. To match responses, respondent name, application name, application purpose, and application type were checked for consistency. Forty matching responses were obtained for decision support systems and database applications.

To test the instruments' stability in a longer duration, measures were administered two weeks later (X3) using the same group of respondents. To ensure responses to identical applications, name of respondent, name and type of application, and purpose of application were filled in by researchers prior to administration of the instrument. Again, respondents had no knowledge that there would be a retest. Only two members of the initial group were unable to attend this test. The remaining thirty eight responses were used in the analysis of long-range test-retest. During two week interval the respondents carried out their course requirements and carried out their regular jobs.

In this research design, stability is measured by the correlation between the scores on the same test obtained at two points in time. The short-range and long-range stability measures are equal to the correlations between X1 and X2 and between X2 and X3, respectively. If these correlation coefficients were significant, the instruments were considered stable. To assess internal consistency, we also calculated Cronbach's alpha for each instrument at three points in time. Even though test-retest correlations are high and significant, systemic factors may still cause differences in mean scores. Paired-samples tests were performed to assess the significance of differences between means. The sample size is adequate for using a paired t-test procedure (Downie & Starry, 1977). Where the differences between mean scores were not significant, the statistical power of the test (Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1989) was calculated to determine whether the lack of significance was due to poor power levels (e.g., too small a sample).

The power of a statistical test is the probability that it will yield statistically significant results. Cohen (1977) suggests that this represents a vital piece of information about a statistical test applied to research data. He prescribed a power value of 0.80; this is widely accepted as the norm. Thus there is an 80% probability of detecting an effect if it exists. Using the suggested method for the t-test on the means of dependent samples (i.e., paired values), this study employed widely accepted ‘effect size’ level of small (0.20) and medium (0.50) standard deviation between population means. The phrase ‘effect size’ reflects the degree to which the phenomenon is present in the population, or to which the null hypothesis is false. Large effect size level (0.80 standard deviation between population means) is considered superfluous. Setting the alpha level to 0.05, the mean power of the tests for small and medium effect size levels was determined by using Cohen’s power tables.

## 4. Results

On average, it took about ten minutes to administer each survey. Applications selected by respondents related to different business functions, such as inventory control, financial analysis, payroll, and cost accounting. Respondents' use of specific applications ranged from 1 to 30 hours per week with an average of 6 hours. Cronbach's alpha was highest for the eight-item perceived involvement instrument at 0.97, 0.98, 0.97 for X1, X2 and X3 respectively. Desired involvement had alpha coefficients of 0.93, 0.94, 0.94 for X1, X2 and X3 respectively. Involvement congruence had alpha coefficients of 0.94, 0.94, 0.95 for X1, X2 and X3 respectively.

## 4.1. Short-range stability

Test-retest statistics for the short-range (X1 and X2) stability of perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence are presented in Table 2. Short-range test-retest correlations for the eight individual items, the 8-item scale, and the single-item global scale for each construct were significant at p < 0.001. Results suggest correlations between test and retest scores of individual items ranging from 0.90 to 0.97 for perceived involvement items, from 0.64 to 0.89 for desired involvement items, and from 0.57 to 0.89 for involvement congruence items. The 8-item perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence scales had test-retest correlations of 0.99, 0.96, and 0.95, respectively. The single-item global measures had test-retest correlations of 0.96, 0.70, and 0.78 for perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence, respectively. These results support the stability of the perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence scales during the short-range interval with each of the 8-item scales being more reliable than its corresponding single-item global measure. However, correlations alone do not reveal whether systemic factors are operating to create differences in mean scores between test and retest.

Except for one involvement congruence item (C8), there are no significant (p < 0.05) differences between mean scores. These results indicate that all three instruments have short-range stability and can be used without concern about a reactivity effect.

These non-significant differences in mean scores do not appear to be due to poor power levels. Assuming medium-effect size, the individual items, 8-item scale, and single-item global measure had a power of the test 0.94 or higher for perceived or desired involvement, and 0.83 or higher for involvement congruence. Thus, at the medium-effect size, all items and scales exceed Cohen's prescribed power value of 0.80. These results compare favorably with the analyses of 57 MIS studies reported by Baroudi and Orlikowski who reported average power value of 0.53 for medium-effect size, while none of the tests reached a power value of 0.80.

At the small-effect size, there was greater variation (ranging from 0.27 to 0.99) in the power values for individual items and single-item global measures for the three involvement constructs. However, the power values for the 8-item scales are consistently high: 0.99 for perceived involvement; 0.99 for desired; and 0.96 for involvement congruence. Again these results compare favorably with an average power value for the small-effect size of 0.16 reported by Baroudi and Orlikowski.

## 4.2. Long-range stability

Test-retest statistics for the long-range (X2 and X3) stability of perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence are presented in Table 3. Long-range test-retest correlations for the eight individual items, the 8-item scale, and the single-item global scale for each construct were significant at p < 0.005. Results suggest correlations between test and retest scores of individual items ranging from 0.81 to 0.94 for perceived involvement items, from 0.67 to 0.85 for desired involvement items, and from 0.46 to 0.85 for involvement congruence items. The 8-item perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence scales had test-retest correlations of 0.97, 0.91, and 0.93, respectively. The single-item global measures had test-retest correlations of 0.93, 0.72, and 0.82 for perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence, respectively. These results support the stability of the perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence scales during the long-range interval with each of the 8-item scales being more reliable than its corresponding single-item global measure.

The last column of Table 3 presents the significance of the t-value for differences in mean scores between test and retest (long-range) for individual items, 8-item scale, and single-item global measure for perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence. There are no significant $p < 0.05$ differences between mean scores. These results indicate that all three instruments have long-range stability. It is unlikely that this long-range stability is due to a memory effect. Furthermore, these results imply that the underlying theoretical constructs are stable.

These non-significant differences in mean scores over the two-week period do not appear to be due to poor power levels. Assuming medium-effect size, the individual items, 8-item scale, and single item global measure had a power of the test exceeding Cohen's prescribed power value of 0.80. Again, at the small-effect size, there was greater variation (ranging from 0.22 to 0.94) in the power values for individual items and single-item global measures for the three involvement constructs. The power values for the 8-item scales are consistently high: 0.99 for perceived involvement; 0.83 for desired; and 0.90 for involvement congruence. Thus, assuming small-effect size, the probabilities that significant differences in mean scores for the recommended 8-item scales would be found if they existed were 99%, 83%, and 90% for perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence, respectively. Again, these results compare favorably with an average power value of 0.16 reported by Baroudi and Orlikowski for the small-effect size.

## 5. Conclusions

User involvement in systems development is an important theoretical construct in information systems research. It also has practical significance because of its presumed role in improving the quality of managerial decision making. This study examines short- and long-range stability of three 8-item instruments for measuring a user's perceived involvement, desired involvement, and involvement congruence for a specific application. The results suggest that these instruments are internally consistent, stable for both short-range and long-range intervals. Stability is an important issue in the use of any measurement method and it should be investigated when new measures are developed. If a reactivity effect is suspected, an instrument's stability is better examined when the same test is administered at three points in time. The results suggest that these involvement instruments do not elicit a substantial reactivity effect, and thus, can be used without prior exposure.
