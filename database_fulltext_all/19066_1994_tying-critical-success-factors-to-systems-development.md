# Tying critical success factors to systems development

C. Randall Byers

University of Idaho, Moscow, ID, USA

Debbie Blume

Hewlett-Packard, Boise, ID, USA

Critical Success Factors methodology has become an accepted top down methodology for information systems planning. This paper discusses how the CSF approach is tied to information systems development in one division of the Hewlett-Packard organization. A step by step approach which maps CSF into Critical Success Projects is described. These steps ensure congruence of information systems activities designed to support operational and strategic efforts with those factors managers have identified as critical. This approach has proven beneficial in generating high level user involvement and support. In addition, it has caused IS analysts to become business partners with the client business.

Keywords: Critical success factors; Critical success projects; Systems development; Organizational approach; Project selection; User involvement; IS/business partnership

## Introduction

Matching system development efforts to business needs continues to be one of many challenges facing today's IS manager. In most cases resources are constrained, IS personnel are spread across maintenance as well as new systems development activities, and hardware and software platforms are in a state of constant change. Business needs are equally dynamic as business conditions, practices and rules change. The combination of these factors leads to an environment in which it is imperative that current system activities be best matched to the business needs of the organization.

Niederman et al. [8, p. 475] in the article “Information Systems Management Issues for the 1990s” indicates the environmental changes in business “present demands on IS departments to provide timely, high-quality information and to support innovation in products, production techniques, and organizational designs.” One key finding of this SIM/MISRC sponsored study was that there exists continued concern for traditional issues such as information strategic planning and organizational alignment. Specifically [8, p. 479], Improving IS Strategic Planning ranked third, and Aligning the IS Organization With That of the

Earl [3, p. 1], in his recent article dealing with Strategic Information Systems Planning (SISP), draws similar conclusions. He states the literature recommends that SISP target

“- Aligning investment in IS with business goals
- Exploiting IT for competitive advantage

\- Directing efficient and effective management of IS resources

\- Developing technology policies and architectures."

He indicates the first two of these should be regarded as information systems strategies. Like others, he points to aligning IS with business needs as a primary benefit of SISP.

Robert Neiman [7, p. 6], in a similar vein, proposes one way for the systems function to increase its value is to design “...systems in terms of client business goals rather than in terms of technology goals.” He goes on to state, “IS professionals can make major gains by shaping their work to contribute directly to the achievement of client business goals first and then fitting whatever technology may be useful in terms of the business goals.” Furthermore, he ties IS performance to direct ties with the business partner [7, p. 7].

"The potential for producing value grows exponentially when systems groups team up with their clients and go directly for quality, productivity, and performance gains through collaborative effort."

Raghunathan et al. [9] found the most significant factor related to IS performance was relations with and support of the objectives of the parent organization. They [9, p. 161] state:

"The role of the IS manager is no longer just as the custodian of data with relatively little or no knowledge of business goals nor access to corporate plans. He/she has been charged with the responsibility for developing policies and strategies for information resources of the firm. This includes strategic planning, organizational learning, and organizational alignment. In discharging such responsibility, an IS manager must understand the inter-connections of information flow to the business."

Earl [3, p. 5] in his summary of SISP experiences in 27 companies proposes a taxonomy of five different SISP approaches. He suggests that rather than looking for specific SISP methodologies organizations should recognize that “successful SISP is more probable when organizations realize that method, process, and implementation are all necessary issue sets to be managed.” He describes this synergistic view as the SISP approach. From this view he develops five differing SISP approaches.

His study indicates that one approach – the Organizational Approach – is superior. A fundamental tenet of the Organizational Approach $[3, p. 10]$ is that “SISP is not a special or neat and tidy endeavor but is based on IS decisions being made through continuous integration between the IS function and the organization.” He states that this approach is not without method, but that methods are employed as needed to fit a particular purpose. The emphasis is on process, especially management understanding and involvement.

Earl [3, p. 17] presents two general lessons for practitioners.

“First, SISP requires a holistic or independent view. Methods may be necessary, but they could fail if the process factors receive no attention. It is also important to explicitly and positively incorporate implementation plans and decisions in the strategic planning cycle.

Second, successful SISP seems to require users and line managers working in partnership with the IS function. This may not only generate relevant application ideas, but it will tend to create ownership of both process and outcomes.”

Thus the alignment of systems development with business needs is established as one of the key factors in achieving advantage through IS. One component of systems planning is the selection of an appropriate approach to achieve the tie between business needs and IS development. One such approach is presented next.

## The CSF approach

In the late seventies, John Rockart [10] popularized the concept of identifying Critical Success Factors (CSF) as a means of focusing systems activities on those areas that return the highest benefit to the business client. Rockart [10, p. 85] indicated that CSF are defined as “...the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will ensure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where things must go right for the business to flourish. If results in these areas are not adequate, the organization's efforts for the period will be less than desired." He also pointed to the need for information on these CSF to reach the appropriate decision makers. The critical success factors are areas that "...should receive constant and careful attention from management. The current status of performance in each area should be continually measured, and that information should be made available." While Rockart primarily addressed his article to the information needs of executive management, he recognized that the concept would apply to other business managers as well [10, p. 88].

"There are significant benefits of taking the necessary time to think through – and to record – the critical success factors for each general manager in an organization."

Others have recognized the identification of critical success factors as an appropriate planning approach. Boynton and Zmud [1, p. 17] reiterate the point that the CSF methodology is a “...procedure that attempts to make explicit those few key areas that dictate managerial or organizational success.” They indicate CSF are useful in prioritizing potential information systems projects by identifying those information services that address critical organizational concerns. James Martin [6, p. 89] states CSF “...is a valuable managerial technique” which, when coupled with computers, “enables business strategy planning to be translated into information strategy planning.” He [6, p. 88] also indicates:

“An appealing feature of CSF analysis is that it can be done quickly with little manpower.... For this relatively low investment, results have been achieved which fundamentally improve the management processes and lead to far more effective information systems.”

E.W. Martin [5, p. 1] notes that the CSF methodology “...is equally applicable to any organization and to any managerial level within an organization in which multiple functions report to the manager.” His study, which focused on the needs of MIS/DP executives, found that one of the factors these managers indicated as critical to the success of most MIS/DP organizations was the importance of aligning MIS/DP priorities in a fashion that supports the priorities of the parent organization. Martin [5, p. 7] goes on to indicate:

“What may not be so obvious, however, is that support of the parent organization’s priorities involves more than the will or intention to do so. It also requires mechanisms for determining and expressing these objectives and priorities.”

Clearly, both academics and practitioners have accepted the top down CSF approach as an appropriate planning methodology. The CSF methodology is applied in various forms in a variety of organizations as a means of identifying focus areas. Such is the case in a division of Hewlett-Packard Corporation.

This particular division has world-wide responsibility for its product categories. Excellent product acceptance in recent years has led to significant increases in the managerial responsibilities of individuals in this area. The associated systems support group is faced with numerous service requests from the business clients. Typically, the ability of the Information Systems (IS) group to handle the volume of requests for new systems, as well as providing maintenance for production systems, is constrained both by resource and personnel availability. In order to provide best service to the client business, it is incumbent that the IS group at this division focus its activities on those projects mission critical to the division and to systems with the highest impact upon the organization.

## Systems development approach: an extension of the CSF methodology

As a means of delivering the best service to the business, the IS group employs a four-phase planning process that ties system development to critical success factors. The outcome of this process is the identification of systems activities which may best be described as critical success projects.

The four-phase approach follows Neiman's [7, p. 37] directive that, “Systems groups must reach out far more ambitiously and effectively to work with clients toward client goals as the primary focus with technology goals in support.” Emery [4, p. vii] indicates “Every organization must increasingly look to its information system as a critical component of its strategic assets.” He [4, p. viii] goes on to state:

“It is critical for an organization to focus the design of its information systems on strategic objectives, but it is a delusion to think that these objectives can be met through an independent SIS. A quest for such as SIS diverts attention from the very difficult, but far more rewarding, task of making continual improvements in the MIS in order to adapt to the changing strategic needs of the organization.”

Consistent with these comments, the four basic phases of this methodology are:

1. Information Gathering Phase

2. Information Summarization Phase

3. Information Systems Tactical Planning Phase

4. Information Systems Implementation Phase. A general overview of this process follows.

## Phase 1: information gathering

Information systems planning is an integral part of the normal business planning process. IS takes a proactive role during this phase and is primarily responsible for its initiation and coordination. The first step in this phase is a series of structured interviews. During these meetings objectives, critical success factors and information gaps for the various functional areas are identified.

The discussions first focus on isolating the views of the business managers as to their critical objectives. A variety of objectives are identified during this stage. For example, personnel may have as an objective the development of better selection and hiring processes or work force balancing. Objectives for finance might include understanding the implications of world-wide pricing policies or understanding cost structure to maintain profitability. Once the primary business objectives have been discussed, the next component of the interview focuses on identifying critical success factors. If these CSF do not occur, the business area can not meet its objectives. The identification of CSF is accomplished through a series of structured questions with these functional area managers. Questions asked during this phase include:

\- Critical success factors are key things that must go right for an organization to succeed. Which factors do you consider to be the most critical to accomplishing the goals of your unit?

\- On the basis of your knowledge of the current business systems here, how have they helped your competitive position?

These questions, along with others, are addressed to various managers by the IS manager. Figure 2 illustrates one of the above questions along with associated secondary prompts.

Various CSF emerge during this interview process. Marketing might have as a CSF the preparation of timely forecast data, while personnel may identify having the processes and systems in place to support personnel training activities as one of its CSF. Similarly, a CSF for finance might be the need for reliable, timely, accurate data.

During the interview process, questions which address hurdles to meeting objectives are also asked. These questions may find conditions which influence CSF in either a positive or negative fashion. One hurdle may be an “information gap.” Information gaps occur when the manager doesn’t have, yet wants and needs, certain information to support business processes and decision-making.

## Phase 2: information summarization

Each area (e.g., personnel) may have several managers involved representing different subfunctions. Managers typically identify several (two to eight CSF). Likewise, each may have identified a variety of hurdles and information gaps. The IS manager endeavors to sort these out. Here the importance of having the manager who understands the business operations is critical.

In order to summarize the comments gathered during the first phase in a clear and concise manner, a variety of formats are used. Venn diagrams such as presented in figure 1 are one media; matrices are another. One purpose of this summarization is to identify commonalities which exist. ## Phase 3: information systems tactical planning

Once the needs, obstacles and challenges are summarized, the IS manager prepares a listing of associated projects, the Critical Success Projects (CSP), that address these business issues. For example, the IS manager might propose a financial reporting project designed to enhance current financial reporting applications to meet the CSF enhance current applications to meet changing business needs. Another “project” might involve the assignment of an IS specialist to a functional area team “to go forth and find what the information needs are.”

Since this process is dynamic, projects that address some of these issues may already be in current development. The remaining projects are then prioritized. To do so, the IS manager constructs a project prioritization matrix similar to the one shown in Figure 5. Artificial data has been included in Figure 5 to illustrate the approach.

Projects are rated on their significance to the business clients in terms of achieving objectives and meeting critical success factors. The proposed projects are also rated on the difference between the current level of support for this endeavor and the comparative disadvantage to other systems, both internal and external to Hewlett-Packard. Here the input of the business partners and other senior managers is necessary to establish the relative significance and perceived comparative deficit.

## Phase 4: information systems implementation

Once the plan is accepted, a critical, ongoing managerial task is the need to maintain appropriate focus during the year. The IS manager becomes responsible for keeping the planning vision alive until the next formal planning cycle. This means maintaining close ties with the business partners.

Business needs change. As such, the IS manager must be able to respond to changing business priorities as business conditions dictate the need for such change. It, too, becomes the manager's role to ensure that the IS staff is part of the everyday life of the business client so, as the client's needs change, the IS specialist is in tune with these changes.

This is, in part, accomplished by periodic meetings between the IS solutions specialist and the business client. Quarterly progress meetings are typically held. The IS team members assigned to a specific project meet regularly with their business area counterparts. Since most projects are approached by teams involving members of the IS group and the business area, significant, ongoing interaction takes place between members of the groups in a team setting as systems are being built. The open-walled environment at this division promotes interaction as does the close proximity of IS staff to the client businesses. All these activities promote a better understanding by each group of the others needs.

## Evaluation of strengths and weaknesses of the CSP approach

The CSP approach serves as a vehicle for focusing systems development. It is critical to this process that projects receive sponsorship from the business area. “Signing on” signifies a commitment to work together to achieve the business objectives. The process of creating this synergy between the IS staff and the business client is an extremely important dimension of the IS manager’s task. The four-phase approach develops partnerships from the beginning; as such, it provides a vehicle facilitating sponsorship. It serves to focus activities during the formal planning cycle.

Earl [3, p. 17] indicates his belief that the Organizational Approach to systems development is more effective than other alternatives and that firms should consider its adoption. The corporate climate at this location matches well his [3, p. 11] description of salient features of the Organizational Approach:

1. Collective learning across the organization

2. Organization devices (e.g., teams) are used to tackle business problems

3. The IS function works in close partnership with the rest of the organization

4. Devolution of some IS capacity is common

5. SISP is part of the normal business planning

6. IS strategies often emerge from ongoing organizational activities.

The four-phase approach described here is one which fits his taxonomy.

Dixon and John [2, p. 247] in their article “Technology Issues Facing Corporate Management in the 1990s” assert “that the effective deployment of the well-integrated technologies in a corporation acts as the “enabler,” or catalyst, for innovative and more effective management of the various principle functions and departments of the corporation and the enterprise as a whole.” Similarly, Emery [4, p. viii] in his comments on Strategic Information Systems states:

“An adaptive MIS requires an infrastructure and implementation methodology that enables the organization to cope effectively with continual changes (in stark contrast to most existing systems built with an inflexible third-generation methodology). A corporate culture that accepts change stems from such hard-to-duplicate characteristics as the organization’s reward system, the ability of its coordination mechanisms to deal with fluid assignments, management’s view of its workers, and the worker’s feeling of shared participation in the fate of the enterprise.”

The nature of the corporate culture within this HP division is one which exhibits such traits. The open environment and regular area meetings promote this culture; shared participation and acceptance of change do exist. The IS group enjoys a high degree of creditability with its functional area customers who regularly seek the input of the IS specialist with whom they are involved. Priorities do change during the year. If close ties have been established and maintained between IS and the user-community, recognizing and addressing these changes is easier.

The process is causing the IS group to see that they are a business partner with the business client, not just code generators who are assigned to develop a specific program in isolation from the user client but, rather, systems specialists working in cooperation with the client developing systems critical to the business. One analyst in the IS group commented on the changing focus occurring within systems development. He stated, "In the past, users did the analysis and gave the project to IS to implement (code). Their attitude was “we have given you the project; it’s yours; see us when it’s done.” Now IS assumes a partner’s role in developing of systems. The users and the IS team jointly handle analysis. The goal is to make the project as important to the user as to the systems designer."

This study has limitations. No formal comparison of other approaches was undertaken. In an operational division such as this one, a controlled study evaluating multiple methodologies was not feasible. Using CSF as a starting point for the planning process certainly is justified by a number of studies and strong advocates.

Measuring success of IS endeavors remains evasive. Dixon and John [2, p. 250] indicate that “In a strategic approach to technology deployment, the impact on the company is macrofinancial and needs to be expressed in business profitability terms, not in “value of information” terms.” They go on to indicate:

"The macrofinancial impact of the use information technology on the company is still not well-understood – and therefore neither well-measured nor reported. Yet, the macrofinancial impact on the business is the most significant measure of the effectiveness of the deployment of technology."

While no attempt was made to identify specifically the macrofinancial impact of the four-phase process, it is worth noting that this particular division of HP enjoys strong financial health and is a significant contributor to overall corporate profitability.

## Conclusions

This four-phase process generates a high degree of user involvement at high levels of the client business in the identification and prioritization of projects. Such involvement is essential. Users must commit resources – both financial and personnel – to projects during this four-phase planning process. User involvement and commitment must not only be present in the initial planning stages but also ongoing throughout the development of the systems. Such commitment helps lead to ownership of the projects by the user group.

IS must understand the business functions in order to assist managers to make better decisions. Business managers must see information systems as a key resource, a critical success factor, for attaining competitive advantage. This planning process in this HP division is bridging the gap between the user and the IS specialist, building better communication between the client user and the IS team. This enables both to better see information as an asset of the business.

In summary, the four-phase planning process ties the IS staff more closely to the business itself, creating a needed partnership between IS and the functional areas. It allows IS to focus on those projects that are critical to the business, binding the IS development strategy to the objectives and critical success factors of the division.
