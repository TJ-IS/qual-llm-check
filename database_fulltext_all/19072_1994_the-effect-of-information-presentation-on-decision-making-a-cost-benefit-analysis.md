# The effect of information presentation on decision making: A cost–benefit analysis

Iris Vessey $^{1}$

Smeal College of Business Administration, Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA 16802, USA

## Abstract

Cost-benefit theory is presented as a cohesive way of organizing knowledge about information presentation. This theory suggests that decision makers change strategy so that they minimize the joint cost of effort and error in making a decision. Here we apply two aspects of the theory. First, the theory of cognitive fit applies to decision making on fairly simple tasks of information acquisition and simple evaluation. Certain strategies (in this case problem-solving “processes”) will dominate alternative ones when the problem representation matches the nature of the decision-making task. The processes are effectively controlled, resulting in maximum accuracy at minimum cost. Second, the more traditional view is applied to decision making on more complex tasks, where a number of appropriate strategies may be available, with trade-offs between error and effort. Both aspects of the theory are tested by evaluating results of published graphs versus tables studies. The theory presented successfully explains the results of the majority of these studies. Further, the paper examines the implications of the cost-benefit framework for the design of decision support systems (DSS) and the notion of DSS restrictiveness. Rather than addressing the role of strategy alone in investigating the effectiveness of different display formats, researchers in behavioral decision making are encouraged to also address the role of problem-solving processes.

Key words: Information presentation; Graph; Table; Decision making; Decision-making processes; Cognitive fit; Cost-benefit theory; Strategy shift; Performance constraints; Decision support systems; DSS restrictiveness

## 1. Introduction

Information presentation is becoming increasingly important to the IS community. Although there is uncertainty about whether systems should be designed to permit individual choice of display format or whether they should possess the intelligence to make such choices, numerous studies on the performance of graphs versus tables, in particular, have been conducted in a number of domains over an extended period of time. The studies have, however, been atheoretical in nature (see reviews by [20], [35]). Clearly, if the field is to progress, researches must seek to develop descriptive theories of the relationship between display design and decision-making performance.

Our research uses cost–benefit theory to organize knowledge about graph/table decision making. It was developed to explain contingent decision-making behavior in which decision makers change strategy to accommodate even minor variations in the task or the task environment. According to the theory, decision makers change strategy so as to minimize the joint cost of error and effort. Numerous factors influence the choice of strategy, among them the way in which information is presented.

Cost-benefit theory is a framework within which decision making can be examined rather than a theory that presents well-articulated relationships among variables. This research attempts to discover some of the relationships that apply to decision making using graphs and tables. In doing so, we distinguish between strategy and process. Both are dynamic descriptions of decision making. We use the term “strategy” to describe a general approach to decision making that involves a number of steps or sub-tasks: thus it most often describes a macro approach. We use the term “process” to refer to a specific approach or a micro operation that aids in accomplishing the task. Processes are used to address each of the sub-tasks that comprise the strategy. A major thesis is that behavioral decision making research has ignored the role processes play in the effectiveness of a particular display format (see, for example, [38]).

Specifically, this research applies cost–benefit theory to decision making using graphs and tables in two ways. First, the theory of cognitive fit, a special case of cost–benefit theory, describes decision making that primarily involves information acquisition and well-defined evaluation. Cognitive fit results when the processes required to act on the problem representation are similar to (i.e., match) those required to solve the problem. In this case, minimizing the joint cost of error and effort effectively minimizes both $[80]$ . Second, the more traditional view of cost–benefit theory involving strategy shift applies to decision making on more complex tasks where a number of appropriate strategies may be available. Such strategies result in trade-offs between error and effort, with no one strategy minimizing both.

## 2. Cost-benefit theory

Researchers in behavioral decision making have observed that decision makers change strategy in response to what appear to be quite minor changes in the task and its environment. According to cost–benefit theory, decision makers trade-off the effort required to make a decision vis-a-vis the accuracy of the outcome [2]. Cost–benefit theory has been applied extensively to choice tasks.

A number of factors may influence the error and effort required to make a decision and may therefore induce decision makers to change strategy. These factors are usually characterized as relating to “task” and/or “context”. According to Payne [51], task variables are those “associated with the general structural characteristics of the decision problem.” Those most often examined in choice tasks are: (1) response mode (i.e., whether the task requires judgment or choice); (2) task complexity (number of alternatives, number of dimensions, and time pressure); (3) problem representation; and (4) agenda effects. Task variables have been shown to influence effort. Context variables are those related to the actual values of the objects under consideration. The most common context variables investigated are: (1) similarity of the alternatives and the overall attractiveness of the alternatives; (2) the variance in probabilities; and (3) the presence or absence of alternatives that are worse than others on both error and effort [53]. Context variables influence accuracy [37].

Decision makers change strategy primarily in response to changes in task variables, and, therefore, to reduce effort. Numerous studies report strategy changes as the number of alternatives involved in choice decisions increases (see, for example, [52], [48]). More recently, Payne et al. [53] have demonstrated that strategy may change due to time pressure. Todd and Benbasat [68] in a study of decision aids for choice tasks, suggest that decision makers use aids only to reduce effort and not to improve accuracy. Note, however, that cognitively simpler strategies can be almost as effective as the optimal strategies in many decision environments. Russo and Dosher [60], for example, found that subjects in one experiment traded-off a slight loss of accuracy (20%) for a substantial increase in speed (approximately 7 times faster).

Studies reporting strategy changes due to context variables, and therefore a desire for accuracy, are much rarer; indeed, it appears that no one strategy is effective in all contexts. The relative accuracy of a strategy varies across contexts, although the relative effort does not; i.e., context effects influence accuracy but not effort. Similar levels of accuracy can, however, be achieved across different contexts, although the effort required will vary; i.e., context effects, and therefore accuracy considerations, also lead to strategy shift.

Problem representation is one of the task characteristics that influences strategy shift. Cost-benefit theory can therefore be used to examine the effect of graphs and tables on decision-making performance. Certain other task effects, notably task complexity and time pressure, also appear to be particularly relevant to decision making using graphs and tables and these factors are also examined.

## 3. Characteristics of graph/table decision making

Here, we view problem representation and decision making task as independent. There are two reasons. First, data and task can be presented independently. Second, solutions to the types of tasks examined here can be reached when the data are represented in either graphical or tabular format.

To apply cost-benefit theory to decision making using graphs and tables, we first determine the characteristics of both the problem representations and the tasks they support.

## 3.1. Characteristics of the problem representations

Let us assume we are considering graphs and tables derived from equivalent data, so that all information in one is inferable from the other, with a different type of information predominating in each. Our intuition quickly lets us see a meaningful distinction between graphs and tables similar to that characterized in the psychology literature as images and words (see [7], [49], [50]).

We use the terms “spatial” and “symbolic” to characterize the differences between graphs and tables. Graphs are spatial problem representations since they present spatially-related information. Graphs emphasize relationships in the data. They do not present discrete data values directly. Tables are symbolic problem representations since they present symbolic information. Tables emphasize discrete data values. They do not present data relationships directly.

Spatial representations facilitate viewing the information they contain at a glance without addressing the elements separately or analytically; i.e., the data in a graph are accessed using perceptual processes. Symbolic representations, on the other hand, facilitate extracting specific data values; i.e., the data in a table are accessed using analytical processes.

## 3.2. Characteristics of tasks

Although there is no comprehensive theory of tasks (see, for example, [11], [30], [87]), we can examine tasks used in graph/table decision making to determine the abstract characteristics that might be facilitated by each type of representation. According to Einhorn and Hogarth [26], decision making consists of three interrelated sub-tasks: information acquisition, evaluation, and feedback/learning. The first two sub-tasks are relevant to the analysis of graph/table decision making.

Information acquisition tasks are those whose solution is achieved directly via the information acquisition process. Several researchers have identified two basic types of information acquisition tasks used in graphs versus tables studies $[75]$ , $[76]$ , $[84]$ . One type of task, spatial, assesses the problem area as a whole rather than as discrete data values and therefore requires making associations or perceiving relationships in the data. The following example involves a comparison of trends and is therefore a spatial task:

“Between the years 1100 and 1438 whose earnings increased most rapidly, those of the wool, silk, or Calimala merchants?” [84]

Such tasks are best accomplished using perceptual processes.

The second type of task, symbolic, involves extracting discrete, and therefore precise, data values $[75]$ , $[76]$ , $[84]$ . The following example requires a specific amount as the response and is therefore a symbolic task:

"How much did the wool merchants earn in the year 1100?" [84]

Such tasks are best accomplished using analytical processes.

The strategy used in information acquisition tasks consists of just the strategy for information acquisition. That strategy may be accomplished via one of two processes depending on the nature of the task, spatial or symbolic. More complex tasks involve both information acquisition and evaluation sub-tasks. Here, the distinction between process and strategy, highlighted in the introduction, becomes critical. When a task is sufficiently complex, its solution can be achieved via a number of different sets of sub-tasks (i.e., strategies). Different strategies may have significant implications for the accuracy of the outcome and the effort involved.

More complex tasks may vary substantially in both the amount and type of evaluation. Regarding the type of evaluation, we can again identify:

(a) tasks that are basically spatial in nature; and (b) tasks that are basically symbolic in nature.

In general, more complex spatial tasks, for example, forecasting, involve perceptual evaluation. Since perceptual processes require less effort than analytical processes, the decision maker will have little incentive to use analytical evaluation processes to solve a spatial problem. In general, more complex symbolic tasks involve analytical evaluation. The decision maker may, at some point, be induced to use perceptual rather than analytical evaluation processes to reduce the effort involved.

More complex tasks vary substantially in the amount of evaluation required. We can identify:

(a) tasks involving simple, well-defined evaluation subtasks;

(b) complex tasks involving numerous, ill-defined evaluation sub-tasks.

Certain evaluation tasks can be analyzed in a similar manner to tasks that involve information acquisition alone. In tasks involving fairly simple evaluation, the evaluation sub-tasks will be either spatial or symbolic. For example, a task may require first the extraction of given data values, then, a series of calculations. The critical point, here, is that we can infer the nature of both the information acquisition and the evaluation sub-tasks to be completed.

The solution of complex tasks can be achieved using a variety of different strategies, and associated sub-tasks, with significant implications for the accuracy of the outcome and the effort expended; i.e., there is no well-defined set of subtasks ((b), above). Complex spatial tasks will normally be solved using perceptual processes since this will result in least effort.

Complex symbolic tasks have high cognitive or limiting demands and therefore place significant strain on the cognitive resources of the decision maker $[11]$ since they may require a substantial amount of analytical processing; e.g., they may require computations that are not difficult, but are tedious and time consuming, and therefore error-prone. A limiting task, is a special case of a complex symbolic task with high analytical demands; it may involve, for example, calculating trends from extremely complex functions. Such a task has further constraints placed on it from the viewpoint of its successful completion: it may not be possible to complete the task analytically without assistance. Johnson and Payne $[37]$ speculate on the existence of similarly complex decision-making tasks in discussing the process of expected utility maximization:

Such processes, however, could well require inordinate amounts of time, and, in practice, be impossible for the unaided decision maker. Processing constraints, therefore, may impose severe limitations upon the strategies and thereby provide a boundary for the feasible region in which accuracy-effort trade-offs could be made.

4. Application of cost-benefit principles to tasks involving information acquisition

The graph/table experiments conducted to date have mostly investigated simple tasks that involve information acquisition. The results of almost all of these experiments can be explained in terms of the theory of cognitive fit.

## 4.1. The theory of cognitive fit

Fig. 1 presents the model of decision making on which the theory of cognitive fit is based [80]. When the types of information emphasized in the decision-making elements (problem representation and task) match, the problem solver can use processes (and therefore formulate a mental representation) that also emphasizes the same type of information. Consequently, the processes the problem solver uses to both act on the problem representation and the task will match. The resultant, consistent mental representation will facilitate the decision-making process. Hence, cognitive fit leads to an effective (i.e., accurate or precise) and efficient (i.e., fast) problem solution.

Theoretical support for the relationships in the general problem-solving model comes from a variety of sources. The literature provides substantial support for problem solvers' use of processes that match the problem representation [8], [39], [59], [70], [71], [72]. There is also substantial evidence that problem solvers use different processes in different types of tasks [26], [65], [66], [73], [82]. Finally, there is evidence that matching the problem representation directly to the task has significant effects on problem-solving performance [9], [63], [88].

Clearly, then cognitive fit exists when spatial tasks are supported by spatial representations and when symbolic tasks are supported by symbolic representations. We state the following proposition.

Proposition 1: More effective and efficient decision making results when the problem representation matches the task to be accomplished.

Spatial tasks can be solved using symbolic representations, and vice versa. However, when the information in the problem representation and the task do not match, similar processes cannot be used both to act on the problem representation and to solve the problem, and the mental representation will have to be transformed. In terms of cost–benefit theory, the strategy in this instance will require more effort and will most likely be less accurate. Hence, there is no incentive to use tables to support spatial tasks and vice versa $^{2}$ .

We examine the robustness of the theory by considering whether strategy shift occurs under any circumstances when cognitive fit exists. Perhaps the most common occurrence in decision making not considered by the basic theory is the effect of performance requirements (i.e., task demands of either time or accuracy) that are not consistent with the task type and the problem representation that best supports it. In general, perceptual processes lead to fast responses, while analytical processes lead to accurate responses. Hence a time requirement is consistent with the perceptual processes used to support spatial problem-solving elements. With a requirement for accuracy, however, decision makers may be induced to switch from perceptual to analytical processes. If this were so, a performance effect for graphs over tables would be unlikely as processes would be uncontrolled. Similarly, decision makers using symbolic problem-solving elements may be induced to change to perceptual processes when a time requirement is imposed.

To assess the stability of the theory of cognitive fit, we need to determine whether graphs performed better than tables on spatial tasks with a requirement for accuracy and whether tables performed better than graphs on symbolic tasks with a requirement for time. First, accuracy with graphs was better than with tables on spatial tasks in four of five experiments, with equivalent performance on the fifth study. Second, Carter [12], [13] found that accuracy with tables was better than with graphs on symbolic tasks when time pressure was applied. These results suggest that decision makers are not induced to change strategy when cognitive fit exists.

Hence, there is substantial evidence to support the descriptive power and stability of the theory of cognitive fit for information acquisition tasks.

## 5. Application of cost-benefit principles to tasks involving evaluation

For tasks involving information acquisition, choosing a problem representation that matches the type of task being solved (i.e., cognitive fit) results in effective control of decision-making processes. Strategy shift may occur, however, in tasks requiring substantial evaluation. A number of different strategies might be used to solve such tasks and there is scope for applying perceptual or analytical processes in the sub-tasks. We use the basic notions developed in section 4, that cognitive fit results in (1) fast decisions when spatial tasks are supported by spatial representations, and (2) accurate decisions when symbolic tasks are supported by symbolic representations, and that strategy shift will occur when decision makers are induced to use analytical processes on spatial tasks and perceptual processes on symbolic tasks. Note, however, that the unit of analysis is the sub-task. Since there are large effort differences between perceptual and analytical process, time and accuracy of solution outcomes should be assessed simultaneously.

## 5.1. Tasks involving simple well-defined evaluation sub-tasks

Cognitive fit may occur in tasks involving well-defined evaluation. Two situations may arise: (1) the processes required to support information acquisition and evaluation are similar; i.e., both are perceptual or both analytical in nature, and therefore best supported by graphs and tables, respectively; or (2) the processes required to support information acquisition and evaluation do not match, in which case it is unlikely that use of either graphs or tables will result in performance advantages.

Lusk and Kersnick [45] investigated a problem that involved symbolic information acquisition and evaluation. Tables proved to be more accurate than graphs as would be expected according to the theory of cognitive fit. Benbasat and Dexter [3] used a task that required a spatial information acquisition strategy and a symbolic evaluation strategy. With a 15-minute time limit, subjects with graphs were faster than those with tables, as might be expected due to the use of perceptual processes; however, tables and graphs resulted in equivalent accuracy. This result, also, may be expected, since neither graphs nor tables support both information acquisition and evaluation subtasks.

## 5.2. Complex tasks involving numerous, ill-defined evaluation sub-tasks

When complex spatial tasks are supported by spatial representations, and no undue emphasis is placed on accuracy, there will be no incentive to change strategy and cognitive fit will exist. There are at least two general situations that may lead to strategy shift, however: (1) the consumption of cognitive resources on a fairly complex symbolic task, and (2) performance constraints placed on the decision-making exercise. Both are regarded as task variables and can be expected to lead to strategy shift largely through their influence on effort.

As the inherent complexity of a symbolic task increases, decision makers may prefer to use perceptual rather than analytical processes. The change in strategy may be due to either: (1) the high analytical demands of the task per se; or (2) the desire to expend less effort in decision making, i.e., conscious choice of a less effortful strategy [26], [60]. Switches in strategy to reduce effort have been observed in numerous experiments in behavioral decision making. In decision making using graphs or tables, therefore, the appropriate problem representation might not be a table, which supports analytical processes, but a graph, which supports the parsimonious perceptual processes. We state the following proposition.

Proposition 2: As the amount of analytical evaluation required increases, decision makers choose between analytical processes resulting in high effort and accuracy and perceptual processes resulting in lower effort and somewhat lower accuracy. (Note that the remaining propositions are stated in terms of processes.)

There may also exist the limiting case for which the use of perceptual processes alone is feasible. Again, perceptual processes are best supported with graphs.

Proposition 3: As the amount of analytical evaluation required increases, the problem can only be solved using perceptual processes.

Constraints placed on performance (time limit, accurate result) may also induce subjects to change strategy. To date, behavioral decision-making research has considered only time pressure in examining performance constraints.

Nonetheless, since a desire for accuracy is known to result in strategy change (albeit via context rather than task effects), it seems likely that a performance requirement of accuracy might also lead to strategy change.

The model of cognitive fit can therefore be extended to include the effect of performance requirements on the formulation of the mental representation and therefore on decision-making performance (see Fig. 2). As we have seen, a requirement for speed encourages the use of perceptual processes, while a requirement for accuracy encourages the use of analytical processes. Hence we state:

Proposition 4(a): A performance requirement of speed encourages the use of perceptual evaluation processes.

Proposition 4(b): A performance requirement of accuracy encourages the use of analytical evaluation processes.

A range of strategies will be possible depending on the sequence of sub-tasks chosen and the use of perceptual or analytical processes. It is likely, therefore, that no one strategy will be better on both error and effort than all others on more complex tasks. In this case the decision maker will choose among the strategies based on the preferred trade-off between speed and accuracy of performance and there are unlikely to be advantages in using either graphs or tables.

## 5.3. Empirical support for strategy shift

At present, we can identify only a few examples of strategy shift in the graphs versus tables literature. Table 2 analyzes the studies Jarvenpaa and Dickson report as involving higher-level decision activities. First, the theory of cognitive fit can explain the results of six experiments. Second, simulation experiments are used in six studies reporting a total of eight results (see section 6). Third, five experiments cannot be analyzed for a variety of reasons (see “Not analyzable”). This leaves just one experiment to examine for strategy shift (Dickson et al. [23]; Experiment 2). Literature search reveals two more experiments that can best be explained by the notion of strategy shift [22], [89] $^{4}$ .

DeSanctis and Jarvenpaa [22] provide an example of a forecasting task (spatial in nature) that is both complex and has performance constraints $^{5}$ . Subjects were required to forecast earnings per share for three years into the future using sixteen years' data on sales revenue, cost of sales, other expenses, net income, and earnings per share developed using Monte Carlo simulations having the same trend and random error terms. Subjects were told: "In order (to make a forecast of EPS), you first must estimate revenues, expenses, and net income...." $^{6}$ , i.e., they were informed of the sub-tasks (i.e., the strategies) they were to complete in arriving at their forecasts. Subjects were required to produce accurate forecasts and were permitted 15 minutes to complete the task for each of five firms. Subjects with graphs performed significantly better than those with tables on the last (fifth) trial.

Our analysis suggests several possible conflicts for the decision maker, foremost among them the requirement for accuracy on a non-deterministic (spatial) task and the requirement for accuracy with a 15-minute time limit when the task is quite complex (i.e., requires many operations on the data). Given such conflicts, it is likely that subjects' strategies will vary; some might be facilitated by tables; others by graphs. It is unlikely, therefore, that there will be substantive performance effects due to problem representation.

The authors present information on the strategies used. First, in pilot studies, with no time limit imposed, the authors report that:

subjects in the graphical condition took an average of 45 minutes, and as long as 70 minutes, to perform the task. The majority of time was spent on converting the bar chart data into precise numbers and then performing calculations on the numerical data. When subjects were provided with time to convert the graphs into tables, the graphical manipulation of the study was lost.

Subjects responded to the experimental demands for accuracy by using analytical rather than perceptual processes. Hence the natural match of graphs to the forecasting task was lost. This observation supports Proposition 4(b) (a performance requirement of accuracy encouraged the use of analytical processes). Subjects with tables would have required only slightly less time to complete the exercise since any time reduction would have resulted from the information acquisition phase of the experiment. Hence, the imposition of a 15-minute time limit on the experiment itself induced subjects with both graphs and tables to change the strategies they might otherwise have employed (based on the pilot study). We would expect them to use perceptual processes to a greater extent than analytical processes (Proposition 4(a): a performance requirement of speed encourages the use of perceptual processes).

Second, the authors report, based on retrospective protocols, that subjects used the following strategies in making their forecasts: (1) 23 of 48 subjects followed the strategy articulated in the task instructions, i.e., they projected all variables, then derived EPS; (2) 10 subjects projected EPS directly; (3) 8 subjects forecast net income and then derived EPS; (4) 7 subjects used “unidentifiable” strategies. These data support the notion that strategy was an uncontrolled variable in the experiment. Further, it appears likely that both perceptual and analytical processes were involved in most of the strategies. The result obtained in the experiment, i.e., neither graphs nor tables had a distinct advantage, is consistent with this analysis.

Despite the fact that the time restriction encouraged subjects to change from analytical to perceptual processes, subjects may have changed strategy of their own accord as a result of the inherently complex nature of the task itself (Proposition 2). This experiment highlights the need to examine strategy in investigating the performance of graphs and tables on complex decision-making tasks.

Both Dickson et al. [23] and Wright [89] provide examples of studies using limiting tasks, those that impose such high cognitive demands on the problem solver that the human information processor is unlikely to be able to complete the task analytically (Proposition 3).

The study by Dickson et al. (Experiment 2) required subjects to forecast demand for a range of products from product reports containing 30 months of historical demand data. The authors report that historical demand data for the products were generated using three increasingly complex sine functions, referred to as resinoid, resinforced, and vitrified. Subjects estimated demand for each of the three products for three periods using either graphs or tables. The dependent variable was demand accuracy, measured as agreement with values derived from the mathematical functions. There were no time constraints. Subjects with graphs outperformed those with tables for eight of the nine forecasts.

Subjects could not have conducted these tasks analytically, even if they had been given the formulas, due to the high levels of cognitive resources required. Subjects with tables would have needed to “visualize” the data provided and then extrapolate “visually” over the next three time periods. Hence they would essentially have had to use perceptual processes on data held in internal memory. It is likely that this process would have been more error-prone than using perceptual processes on spatial data directly. It is therefore unlikely that performance constraints had any effect on the choice of strategy: this experiment is biased towards the superior performance of graphs due to the high demand for cognitive resources (Proposition 3).

Wright's study provides another example of a limiting task. Subjects were required to estimate the correlation of a set of points presented as either scatterplots (spatial representation) or unordered data pairs (tables). Three data sets, each of 60 points, with correlations of 0.053, 0.643, and 0.847, were used in the experiment. Not surprisingly, graphs proved to be more accurate than tables for estimating correlation. This is again a limiting task (Proposition 3). Subjects with tables would have had to either estimate the correlations from the data points, plot the data points and then estimate, or calculate the correlations. Time was not assessed in the study.

We have identified in the literature on graphs and tables, therefore, tasks for which there is “an upward bound on processing capacity” [37].

## 5.4. Studying strategy and process

Strategy is central to cost–benefit theory. To understand the role of strategy in decision making using graphs and tables fully, researchers must therefore use methods that reveal strategies. It is clear, based on the foregoing analysis, that, in studying tasks that involve a substantial amount of evaluation, we first need to identify the sub-tasks that comprise a certain strategy. Then, we need to know the processes used to accomplish each of the sub-tasks; for example, whether they are analytical or perceptual in nature.

Process tracing is an approach that captures the dynamic aspects of problem solving and is therefore suitable for investigating strategy. There are basically three ways of obtaining process tracing data: monitoring eye movements, monitoring the acquisition of information, and recording concurrent verbal protocols (see $[54]$ for a comparison). Verbal protocols are generally preferred since they “provide a rich source of data at densities comparable to the densities of eye-movement records, and contain far more information per data point than the latter” $[27]$ (see, also, $[61]$ , $[64]$ ). Analyzing verbal protocol data is, however, extremely time consuming and subjective. Subjectivity is reduced by the development of formal coding schemes and assessment of coding reliability.

Researchers in the behavioral decision-making area, have adapted research on elementary information processes (see, for example, [14], [47], [57]) to examine strategies used on multiattribute choice tasks. Procedures are available to develop estimates of both the average time and accuracy of the strategies. The results of experimental studies can be compared with the simulation figures permitting inferences to be drawn about strategy (see, for example, [53]). The development of an automated system for data collection and analysis [36] has greatly facilitated this approach.

Like the behavioral decision making research that motivated it, the elementary information processing approach does not currently distinguish between processing information perceptually and processing analytically. Research into graphical methods of presentation provides some insights into assessing perceptual processes (see, for example, [15], [16], [17]), as does the artificial intelligence literature on vision (see, for example, [55], [74]). Essentially similar methods for assessing perceptual processes could be introduced into the models already developed for examining analytical processing.

## 6. Implications of cost-benefit theory for the design of decision support systems

An analysis of the effects of different modes of information presentation on decision-making performance has, perhaps, greatest significance for the design of decision support systems (DSS). The aim of a DSS is to support and extend the capabilities of the human decision maker. Hence the system does some of the processing, rather than the decision maker. Two situation may arise, one based on cognitive fit, the other on strategy shift.

## 6.1. Types of decision support systems

First, the DSS may embed the strategy and processes that the human decision maker might otherwise use. In this case, the decision maker will simply expend less effort for the same degree of accuracy. Such DSS are known as specific DSS [1]. Though powerful, they are not flexible in that they do not permit the decision maker to change strategy based on a desire to trade-off accuracy for effort, for example.

The theory of cognitive fit provides a way of examining the use of a DSS to reduce effort. The concept of cognitive fit readily encompasses the notion that a simulation tool or decision aid will best serve the decision maker when it also supports the processes required to solve the problem. An extension to the simple model of cognitive fit therefore offers a way to view the use of a decision aid (see Fig. 3). To apply cognitive fit to the use of a DSS, we need to identify the type of information emphasized in the task and choose a problem representation and a DSS that emphasize the same type of information. Vessey and Weber [82] and Sinha and Vessey [65] provide support for the notion that performance effects result when any tools or aids used match the task to be solved.

Fig. 3. Model of cognitive fit extended to include decision aid.

Second, a DSS may either permit, or more directly influence, the decision maker to change strategy. Hutchins [33] describes how different navigational support tools influence the use of particular computational strategies with the user adopting processes that match those facilitated by (i.e., inherent in) the support tools themselves. An example of a DSS that permits the decision maker to change strategy might take the form of a generalized system that supports a number of strategies [1]. Such aids are weak, though flexible. The major advantage of a generalized DSS is that it can be adapted to a number of different task environments. Decision makers may then choose a strategy to satisfy a desire to either conserve effort or maximize accuracy. Todd and Benbasat [68] developed a generalized DSS to support multiattribute choice tasks, such as choosing an apartment. Although it does not directly address decision making using graphs and tables, and therefore processes are not critical, it illustrates the notion of a generalized DSS that supports a variety of strategies (for example, additive-difference, additive-compensatory, conjunctive, elimination-by-aspects, majority of confirming dimensions). The authors report that decision makers use such DSS to reduce effort, but make no attempt to use them to improve accuracy.

## 6.2. Flexibility in decision support systems

Further examination reveals a conflict in that traditional DSS literature (for example, [67]) and the behavioral decision-making literature suggest decision makers be permitted to choose a strategy adapted to a particular task environment. However, recent thinking on flexibility in DSS suggests restricting the number of choices available to the decision maker (see, for example, [62], [90]).

Bettman and Zins [9] and Lindberg and Brehmer [42] suggest that decision makers do not always know what support materials best suit the task at hand. Vessey and Galletta [81] showed that 26.4 percent of decision makers did not choose an appropriate problem representation (graphical or tabular) to support problem solution. This study also shows that the effects of matching problem representation and task far exceed the effects of individual differences on decision-making performance (see, also, [84]). Further, several empirical studies suggest that restricted decision making may lead to better results than unrestricted decision making. For example, Laurel [40] shows that unrestricted user actions lead to poor performance. Dos Santos and Bariff [24] report that problem finding and problem prioritization are facilitated by structuring interfaces to provide a systematic approach to the problem.

Many ways of restricting decision maker choice are arbitrary. They are, therefore, normative or prescriptive approaches to improving decision-making outcomes. Although such approaches may well lead to improved performance, it may be more beneficial to incorporate into the DSS the mental model that facilitates task solution. If, indeed, reducing choices results in “better” decisions in practice, then cognitive fit offers a way of effectively restricting choices without the negative consequences that might arise from arbitrary restriction (i.e., cognitive fit is a descriptive rather than a prescriptive approach to facilitating decision making).

We have seen that the notion of cognitive fit applies to the support of quite simple symbolic tasks as well as all types of spatial tasks. In extending this notion to more complex decision-making environments in general, we find an analogy in the management literature, which has long investigated the notion of fit, known variously as contingency, congruency, coalignment, consistency, etc. (see, for example, [25], [77]). This approach is useful since the notion of fit spans micro to macro perspectives. Venkatraman [78], for example, presents the theoretical and statistical implications associated with each of six types of fit characterized on the basis of the degree of specificity of the fit relationship (micro to macro) and on the presence or absence of a criterion for fit. Some of the macro concepts of fit offer a way of viewing the design of more complex DSS that support task solution.

## 7. Discussion

Our research used cost-benefit theory to describe performance in graph/table decision making. We now discuss the findings and their implications from a theoretical, methodological, and practical perspective.

## 7.1. Discussion of findings

This paper examined two aspects of cost-benefit theory, cognitive fit and strategy shift. Although both are based on considerations of error and effort in decision making, they present divergent views of the role of the task. The concept of strategy shift arose from a desire to identify strategies that are invariant across tasks and the subsequent observation that decision makers change strategy with minor variations in the task and its environment. Cognitive fit seeks to identify specific task characteristics that can be supported by the problem representation and the task environment, thus effectively controlling decision-making processes. A further major distinction between strategy shift and cognitive fit is that the former minimizes the joint cost of effort and error, while the latter minimizes both effort and error. Hence cognitive fit results in the use of an optimal strategy (i.e., process) to solve the problem, while more complex decision making permits decision makers to use of a number of different strategies that differ on speed and accuracy.

Cognitive fit is clearly the more prevalent form of cost-benefit theory investigated to date: the theory successfully explains the results of the majority of graphs versus tables studies for which we can determine the nature of both the task and the problem representation. These are studies involving information acquisition and well-defined evaluation. Further, results show that there is little incentive for decision makers to change strategy (e.g., in response to either time or accuracy considerations) when matching problem representation to task permits them to solve the problem with maximum accuracy at minimum cost.

Our research suggests that the inherent complexity of the task induces decision makers to conserve effort by using perceptual rather than analytical processes. Decision makers are prepared to forego some accuracy for a substantial reduction in effort. They may also be induced to change strategy by task instructions that demand either speed or accuracy. Strategy shift is possible in these circumstances since a range of strategies is available.

## 7.2. Implications of findings

Researchers in behavioral decision making to date have pursued the application of cost/benefit theory in the form of strategy shift to graph/table decision making without recognizing the role of processes. When one is examining such different representations as graphs and tables, it is clear that different processes will be used. This argument is supported by this theoretical analysis and ex post validation, as well as hypothesis testing $[81]$ . If the field of information presentation is to progress, it is crucial that researchers acknowledge the role of processes in the use of display formats that emphasize different types of information.

For simple tasks, the theory of cognitive fit is supported by a great deal of empirical evidence. Further, this evidence suggests that the theory holds despite time and accuracy pressures. The stability of the theory remains untested ex ante, however. From a broader perspective, research is needed to develop taxonomies that reflect the types of information emphasized in different kinds of displays and in different kinds of problem-solving and decision-making tasks (see, also, [46]).

Since no studies have been conducted that seek to demonstrate strategy shift directly, this is a potentially fruitful area for research. The studies that do illustrate strategy shift are clearly biased in favor of graphs (and perceptual processing) due to the substantial amount of analytical processing that would otherwise be needed, i.e., the strategies used clearly seek to reduce effort. Our analysis suggests that task effects, such as the level of cognitive resources required for task solution, the desire to reduce the processing resources committed to a decision-making exercise, and the performance constraints placed on the decision-making process, result in strategy shift. However, there is some evidence that decision makers may be induced to change from perceptual to analytical processes in response to a desire to increase accuracy (see, for example, [22]; pilot study). Further, formal investigation of strategy shift in response to accuracy constraints, perhaps via context effects, would add to knowledge since this phenomenon has not yet been reported in the behavioral decision-making literature. The study by Wright [89], discussed in section 5.3, suggests a possible way of studying context effects. The effects on strategy of lack of familiarity with graphs and of practice/learning should also be investigated since graph/table studies reported to date may not illustrate strategy shift due to these factors.

This research touches on several methodological issues. First, reports of many graph/table experiments do not provide sufficient details about the task to permit meaningful analysis. Since task effects are clearly important, researchers (and journal editors) should ensure that are described experimental tasks in detail. Second, most of the research on graphs and tables to date has been based on the naive assumption that decision-making performance will be manifested in accuracy alone. Although the limitations of studies that address only accuracy have been acknowledged for some time, researchers have not yet recognized the need to assess time and accuracy jointly. This approach is necessary not simply to control for time-accuracy trade-off, but also to aid in identifying strategy shift. Third, we need to use research methods that investigate both the processes and the strategies decision makers use, rather than simply investigating the outcomes of decision making.

From the viewpoint of practitioners, this research suggests that we now have sufficient knowledge to help decision makers choose the correct problem representation to support a range of tasks: the one that matches the task. When a problem has a certain level of complexity, it may not be possible to solve it at all with analytical processes even though the task may be symbolic in nature and require an accurate response. In this case, the use of perceptual processes is best supported by graphs. When accuracy requirements induce decision makers to use analytical processes, task solution is best supported by tables.

Using the theory of cognitive fit, we can approach the problem of providing the appropriate problem representation to decision makers in two ways. First, we can train decision makers to choose the display that is appropriate to the type of task they wish to solve by means of either the education system or on-the-job training. Second, we can restrict systems so that they provide users with data in the form that supports the particular task at hand. Designers of packaged software can aid decision makers by limiting the problem representations available depending on the type of problem to be solved.

Limiting the availability of options goes against the current trend in software development. New packages and new releases contain increased numbers of display features thereby increasing flexibility rather than facilitating informed choice. For example, spreadsheets, perhaps the best known example of packaged software, now make many kinds of graphical formats, as well as tabular formats, available to decision makers. As we have seen, several studies suggest this type of flexibility may be harmful rather than helpful to the decision maker. Our improved knowledge of the decision-making process, therefore, permits us to make suggestions to the software developer as well as to the decision maker.
