# Applications

# A morphological approach to generating information technology product ideas

Jae Hyon Song \*

College of Business, St. Cloud State University, St. Cloud, Minnesota, USA

Carl R. Adams

Information and Decision Sciences Department, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA

## Abstract

The use of information technology (IT) as a component of new products and services is increasing, often in unexpected areas. Chief information officers (CIOs) recognize their information systems (IS) staffs are not used to thinking in product development terms and that their staffs are viewed as unfamiliar with their basic business operations. In order to take advantage of the apparent potential in this area, IS organizations need a conceptualization of opportunities afforded by IT and a process for generating possible products and services. This paper presents a model for such a conceptualization that includes three dimensions: product/service change possibilities based on IT, the distinguishing of product inherent versus product support functions in meeting customer needs, and common approaches to differentiate product/service. Using this model, a morphological process can help to generate alternative IT product possibilities.

Key words: Customization; Customer participation; IT function; IT product; Morphological approach; Product differentiation

## 1. Introduction

More and more products include computer based information technology (IT) components. The depth and breadth of recent IT applications that improve product performance and related customer services are remarkable. A successful new product creates excitement and growth by opening up a new market, setting a new standard, and providing an opportunity for corporate renewal and redirection. Generating a new product or a product differentiation is considered a key strategic use of IT [2,4,8,11,12,15,23].

The authors interviewed several CIOs of major corporations in retailing, manufacturing, and service industries to better understand the role that information systems (IS) groups currently play in new product and service development. Basically the executives were aware of the opportunities that IT afforded. However, the predominant role is for systems groups to be reactive rather than proactive in the product development area. While they are familiar with the technical capabilities, IS staffs are not used to generating new product or service ideas and are used to thinking only in terms of supporting existing products and services by automating manual processes. Also in interacting with other parts of the organization, IS groups lack credibility in terms of their understanding of the business. The difficulty of orchestrating and integrating technologists and functional mangers in marketing and production is a serious problem in product development $[1,13,18]$ . This lack of integration is especially critical in IT applications. Without a clear conceptual model that ties IT to product development, simultaneously coupling of IT to product development is difficult $[19]$ and technologists are typically reactive to the initiative of functional managers who are struggling to grasp the fast changing IT.

Given the apparent opportunities to utilize IT to create new (or improved) products, how can we facilitate the involvement of IS staff in the development process? One possibility is to use a morphological approach [5] that forces consideration of all possible combinations in terms of some principal dimensions. For a morphological approach, Zwicky [25] proposed five steps: (1) formulation of problem, (2) localization and characterization of all the parameters (dimensions) which might enter into the solution, (3) construction of a multi-dimensional matrix which contains all of the solutions, (4) analysis and evaluation of all of the solutions, and (5) selection of the best solution. Zwicky [26] claimed that the morphological approach is an unbiased way to derive all the solutions and gives us the maximum guarantee that no circumstance is overlooked. He showed many examples of successfully applied morphological approaches. Davis et al., [6] compared a morphological method to a creative thinking technique using idea checklists in generating product improvement ideas. Their experiment demonstrated that the morphological method sigsignificantly stimulated idea generation in both quantity and quality. These two studies indicate that a morphological approach is a useful way to generate IT product ideas. This paper discusses a morphological method by focusing on the idea generation steps that include (1) localization and characterization of the pertinent parameters (dimensions) of a new product/service, and (2) construction of a multi-dimensional matrix.

## 2. Characterization of pertinent dimensions

Product concepts provide a useful way to characterize new product opportunities. Product concepts mean (1) performance and technical functions, (2) package, configuration and components, (3) target customers, and/or (4) character, personality and image of the product [3]. For an IT product, performance and technical functions are related to IT. Package, configuration and components are related to the product function. Target customers as well as product character, personality and image are related to the direction of product differentiation. Thus, the dimensions that are useful in conceptualizing opportunities are: (1) product/service change possibilities using IT, (2) product functions including product inherent and product support functions in meeting customer needs, and (3) the typical approaches to differentiate products in response to IT development and market needs.

Using the three dimensions in a morphological approach that forces consideration of all alternatives has the potential to generate a richer number of product ideas than a method or perspective based on a single dimensions, such as the value chain [14] or the customer service life cycle [9]. By including information technology, product functions, and product strategies, the model clarifies the potential contributions of IT in the development of valued-added products and services. Focusing as it does on new product ideas, the proposed process pushes our thinking beyond the existing process-oriented product idea generation approaches such as, customer involvement, focus groups, or brainstorming. ## 3. Product differentiation

The product differentiation literature reports various ways to achieve positive product distinction, including: (1) product capacity based (horizontal) and quality based (vertical) differentiations [7], and (2) product enhancement, customization, and cost reduction [22]. The cost reduction depends on the use of customer effort or participation in the actual production or delivery of the product/service [17]. The model uses product enhancement, customization, and participation as the approaches to achieve product differentiation, because these are particularly affected by IT.

Product enhancement involves more capacity, or higher quality and includes enhancing the product inherent functions themselves and/or support services. Speed and accuracy provided by IT may be the basis for enhancement.

IT is particularly relevant to product customization where one or more information activities need to relate to individual needs. Customized producers assume that (1) no two customers and thus no two needs are exactly alike, (2) standardized goods or services have room for improvement, and (3) customers needs are varied, changing, ambiguous, and uncertain. IT can provide detailed information for individualized products, integrate standard products and services, and respond to customers' needs in real time.

Changes in the required amount of a customer's effort have economic impacts on the customer. As distinguished from product enhancement or customization, differentiation based on customer effort/involvement is referred to as participation differentiation. ## 4. Product functions to meet customer needs

A customer need is met by the product inherent functions/activities supported by acquisition, maintenance, repair, disposition, etc. The product inherent functions drive the utilization or consumption of the product. Support functions arise from the interactions between producing firms and customers or between the producing firms and the product. IT can be applied to the product support functions.

Applications have been addressed by Levitt [10]. To identify and take advantage of opportunities value chain activities can be examined systematically. Ives & Learmonth developed this concept further and addressed strategic opportunities in the company-customer relations using their resource life cycle, which proposes that a resource goes through a cycle of activities including requirements, acquisitions, ownership, and retirement.

New (or improved) product opportunities obtained through application of IT to improve product inherent functions/uses are considered in product engineering and largely ignored in the IS literature. Opportunities include: (1) phone display of the number just dialed; (2) automobile monitor systems for doors, lights, and engine functions; and (3) electronic monitoring devices connected to IS for recording magnetic strips of credit cards. Because product inherent functions are an important area of opportunity for IT, the model calls them out explicitly.

## 5. Product change based on IT

A third dimension of the model combines both a view of the IT capabilities that may be brought to bear on a situation and a characterization of the existing level of IT being applied.

## 5.1. IT capabilities

IT offers product improvement opportunities in terms of executing its four basic capabilities: conversion, storage, communication, and processing. In addition, IT facilitates the integration of the four basic capabilities in a way that traditional means such as the typewriter, book, telephone, and calculator may not $[16]$ .

The recent State of Maryland experiment allowing welfare recipients to collect benefits with ATMs (Automatic Teller Machines) is an example of replacing food stamps by electronic communication to lessen the stigma and inconvenience associated with using food stamps $[21]$ . Through processing, efficiency of information storage, communication, and effective presentation of information can be achieved.

In considering new product opportunities we must consider both the possible improvements of IT in executing individual product activities and the improvements that may be obtained through connecting the activities together in an integrated whole. Thus, we must characterize the current use of IT and the opportunities for improving either individual activities or integration of them to achieve improvement.

## 5.2. Phases of IT applications

IT has been considered to have three phases: automate, informate, and transformation $[24]$ . In the automate phase, one or more manual activities are replaced by IT; e.g., for a supermarket check-out systems. Relating automation to the product differentiation alternatives, we observe that automation usually enhances product capacity and quality with improved speed and accuracy, and thus supports customization and customer participation. Automation that requires changes in processes is included in either the informate or transformation phases, depending on the nature of the change.

The informate phase is based on new information presentation and user responses through human-machine interactions. The informate phase allows increased customer participation, product enhancement, and customization. In general, automated processes are hard or even impossible to observe, often resulting in mistakes and uncertainty. Control information is necessary to lessen the mistakes and uncertainty. By making the processes transparent, an informate phase opportunity is created. Information provided in real time and real images would allow a person to reduce uncertainty and is thus an opportunity for a new product. Programmable machines are examples.

In the transformation phase, the sequence of information activities is altered by adding, reducing, or branching. New patterns of activities can be generated. Each differing pattern represents a potential new product. This: (1) starts and ends with conversion activities, (2) through these, firms or customers interact with the product, and (3) processing activities support storage, communication, and conversion.

USA Today, a newspaper, transformed the traditional process of editing, central printing and distribution to a new process-editing, electronic distribution, local printing and local distribution.

There are two types of customization: (1) firms allow customers to tailor the products, and (2) firms customize the product by individualizing one or more activities. For the first type, firms provide information support so that customers can tailor the products to meet their needs; e.g., pay-for-view TV. This leads to both customization and customer participation.

For the second type, customer information is needed; e.g., in financial and medical services. The frequent flier programs of major air carriers have built in incentives for each customer to maintain an accurate individualized flight record. Customers' concerns for privacy and information control pose additional difficulties for firms in collecting customer information. In general, the desire to protect privacy contradicts the desire to have customized products.

Allowing customers to participate in one or more information activities could lower costs and improve customer flexibility. General ease of use in IT enhances customer participation. Customer participation in information services in terms of real-time interactions is necessary.

## 6. Product idea generation process

A morphological approach can help in generating new product ideas. The approach uses the structure of the model to force the consideration of all the logical possibilities. In Table 1, each column corresponds to a dimension in the proposed three dimensional model. Each combination created by running through one entry in each column suggests a possible product idea. There are eighteen $(3 \times 2 \times 3)$ logical possible cases. However, there are some infeasible ones. In the automate phase, replacing manual activities leads to enhancement of the current product in speed and accuracy. Automation without additional changes of the informate or transformation type seldom leads to customization or customer participation. Thus, in the automate phase, only product enhancement is feasible. This excludes four cases $(1 \times 2 \times 2)$ . Thus, there are only fourteen feasible cases.

In addition to the formalization of alternatives and opportunities, the morphological approach changes a simultaneous consideration to a sequential consideration, which simplifies the idea generation process and allows easy inclusion of additional dimensions.

The step by step procedure for examining all the possible combinations could start anywhere. Starting with an IT change is essentially a technology push. The opposite order might be seen as market pull i.e., start with approaches to product differentiation and then move to product functions and thus to the IT.

## 6.1. Idea generation at the automate phase

Starting with an existing product, identify manual activities in each product function that can be automated by IT. The application opportunities increase with the information intensity of product inherent and support functions.

Automation starts by converting activities into digital information. As an example, delivery of a newspaper could be replaced by conversion to an electronic newspaper or printing at the subscriber's site.

In summary, the new product idea generation process in the automate phase is to consider automating manual activities in each type of product functions by using IT capabilities.

## 6.2. Idea generation at the informate phase

When a process is automated, some information becomes available in real time, e.g., the balance in a checking account at the time of an ATM transaction. The real time information in turn can alter customer behavior and information needs. However, provision of status information is then needed to help the customer reduce the uncertainty associated with the automated process. When a process is automated we need to look for information that is currently available and that would be beneficial if it was made available to the customer. In addition, we should look for information that has been made invisible by an automation process. In some instances the form and content of information provided can be quite simple, e.g., the dial tone on a phone line reflecting its operational status or the notes of a touch tone phone reflecting the number “dialed”. Changing the form or presentation of the information is itself a way to develop different products.

In summary, the new product idea generation process in the informate phase is: (1) for each product function (inherent and support), consider presenting new information by adding an output conversion activity to the automated activities, and (2) for each output conversion activity, consider the approaches to differentiate by changing information formats and/or contents.

## 6.3. Idea generation at the transformation phase

At the transformation phase, activities in each product function (inherent and support) can be transformed to achieve one or more of the three differentiation approaches.

The first step in the transformation phase is to examine automated information activities in product functions to alter the consequences of information activities. This includes adding new activities, deleting old activities, and branching into two or more activities. In considering alteration possibilities, it is useful to think in three sub-steps. In the first, obvious reorganization possibilities should be considered such as (1) combining two or more information sequences (systems) together, (2) the elimination of obvious repetitions, and (3) expanding the information involved in the original activity. For example, an automated cash register can be a transformation if additional customer data is collected.

In the second sub-step, a set of possible transformations is created by (1) adding a processing activity to storage, to communication, or to output conversion, and (2) providing a conversion capability to the newly added processing activities.

In the third sub-step, consider all the possible simple logical changes in the sequence of information capabilities. This sub-step could force consideration of possible new transformations. Table 2 summarizes the possible changes in sequence between two information activities.

The second main step is to consider the reorganization of information activities motivated by a purpose in terms of a particular product differentiation approach. The arrangement of information activities can be changed by moving toward more enhancement, customization, or customer participation. Any such changes may also require additional information activities.

In summary, the new product idea generation process in the transformation phase involves: (1) considering each type of product function as a way to alter the sequence of information activities by adding new activities, deleting activities, and branching, and (2) for each altered sequence, considering transformation toward enhancement, more customization, or customer participation.

## 7. Summary

This paper develops a conceptual model that may guide the process of generating new product ideas utilizing IT. Further, it discusses examples of ways to generate a rich number of possible ways to transform products using IT. This model and the processes that it can sustain should be useful in assisting firms to consider product improvement.
