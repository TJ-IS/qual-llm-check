# A comparison of three computer cursor control devices: pen on horizontal tablet, mouse and keyboard

Richard Coll $^{a,*}$ , Khalid Zia $^{b}$ , Joan H. Coll $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Gwynedd-Mercy College, Gwynedd Valley, PA 19437, USA $^{b}$ ATT Bell Labs, Murray Hill, NJ 07974, USA $^{c}$ Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ 07079, USA

## Abstract

It might seem likely that an electronic pen manipulated on a desk top tablet would elicit better performance and greater user preference than other cursor control devices. This paper presents four experiments that examine this hypothesis by comparing speed, accuracy, and preference for a pen device with the two most widely used cursor control devices (mouse and keyboard) in a drawing (graphics) environment. In the context of these experiments pen use was never significantly faster than mouse, while both pen and mouse use were always significantly faster than the keyboard. In contrast, keyboard use was always more error free than the mouse, which in turn was always more error free than the pen. These error differences were significant in a majority of the cases. Subjects expressed preference for the mouse in general work (significant). For highly accurate work, subject preference was for the keyboard (significant).

Keywords: Cursor control devices; Electronic pen; Mouse; Keyboard; Ergonomics; Software psychology

## 1. Introduction

The most satisfactory cursor control device would be one that is an extension of the user's natural motions and is in harmony with the way the user thinks. The device would be effortless to operate from both the mental and physical perspective, so that the user would apply his/her whole awareness to the problem at hand; no division of the user's concentration would be necessary.

As an analogy, one does not have to think how to grasp a glass of water or climb a set of stairs. Nor is one tired by the act of talking.

Ergonomics is using what we know about the way people are to design tools, equipment, and systems which are safe, easy and pleasant to use and, within this framework, increase productivity (Thomas, 1982); ergonomics means designing to fit the user. But we are referring to qualities that go beyond ergonomics and software psychology – the application of ergonomics to issues relating to the human-computer interface (Shneiderman, 1980). We are talking about designs that would cause the computer and its peripherals to operate in a mode paralleling the flow of thought in the user's mind. What is envisioned is to cause “two minds to act as one”. We call this: Cognitive Match Interface Design, with the acronym COMIND (Coll and Coll, 1989; Coll and Wingertsman, 1990). No device currently can perform to these specifications, although some can be operated with little conscious effort.

Some of the devices listed, for example, the knee controller, are not easily available; others, such as the electronic glove, are currently experimental.

A down side aspect of pen-on-vertical-monitor-screen (and also touch screen systems) is that the hand must be held up against the screen over a long session; this causes arm and wrist fatigue, though a pistol grip can reduce it (Goodwin, 1975). There are a number of different designs of horizontal tablets (e.g., Ritchie and Turner, 1975) but the differences are transparent to the user.

If the gain is measured as the ratio of cursor movement to finger movement, it has been found (Arnaut and Greenstein, 1986) that touch tablets should have a gain in the area of 1 through.8 for small error and fast operations. These values are probably extensible to the pen on horizontal tablet combination; however, experimental work is in order. Touch systems have much to recommend them: they are natural to use and humans automatically point to things. Problems associated with such systems are lack of precision due to the broad surface of the finger tip and lack of tactile feedback when selecting (Gartner and Holzhausen, 1978). The electronic glove is a possible remedy to the precision problem, if the user does not mind working with it on his fingers.

Voice control (Haller et al., 1985) is effected by speaking screen coordinates; currently this method is exasperating to use.

Probably the first paper comparing cursor control devices was written by English et al., 1967 of the Stanford Research Institute. They compared five devices, including a mouse with wheels and a knee device, both developed by SRI for use in the study. The variables compared were speed of use, error count, and learning rate. The knee control and mouse appeared superior to the other devices. However, only 11 subjects were used and no statistical computations were performed.

Table 2 is convenient in summarizing existing work. However, we caution against the use of this table in drawing conclusions about the relative efficacy of the various devices for three reasons. First, many of the studies suffer from either testing only a small number of subjects and/or the fact that statistical testing was not performed on the results. Second, the tasks assigned from study to study vary widely and in some, are more compatible with certain devices than others. Third, in some specific application areas, certain pointing devices excel.

Regardless of culture and locale, humans have, from earliest times, developed a thumb and finger held pen/pencil-like device to be used in the off-vertical position for writing and precise drawing. Because of this and the resulting practice and experience of adults, it might be reasonable to expect that for computer activities of a writing/drawing nature a cursor control device of similar design would (1) outperform other cursor control devices and (2) be preferred by subjects. Yet few pen-on-horizontal-surface type devices are available. In contrast, mouse devices are readily available and the keyboard is ubiquitous.

Although there are many studies comparing and evaluating cursor control devices, very few of these involve the pen-on-horizontal-surface and those that do, have an inadequately small number of subjects. Several studies have investigated the light-pen-on-vertical-screen but this condition (as noted) is entirely different.

The purpose of this paper is to help to fill the gap. It compares a pen-on-horizontal-tablet against the two most widely used (available) alternatives: the mouse and the keyboard. Dependent variables are speed of use, accuracy, and user preference.

## 2. Method

Four experiments. Four experiments were performed, each utilizing the same 63 subjects. Tasks were designed to represent typical actions related to pointing, selecting, and drawing. In the experiments, the cursor was a red four pixel square on a medium blue background.

The subjects first performed the four experimental procedures on the same day, sequentially, one-on-one with the experimenter. The first three experiments of the sequence were repeated three times. Seven days later each subject repeated the first three experimental procedures.

The purpose of the repetition was to test for possible differences in retention and rate of learning among the three devices.

Subjects. Sixty three subjects were assigned to one of the three groups (pen-tablet, mouse-tablet or keyboard); i.e., twenty one subjects per group. The subjects were upper class undergraduate and graduate students at an urban university. Forty were male and twenty three were female. Subjects were assigned randomly, with the condition that an equal number of females must appear in each group. None of the subjects had any previous experience with the graphics system used in the study. Most had used keyboards for word processing, but none had ever used a pen, and only three had experience with a mouse. The latter group fortuitously were placed in the non-mouse conditions.

The experimenter compared the three groups for equality on variables of “number of computer graphics packages previously used” and “self reported typing ability”. A Kruskal–Wallace Ranked Data Analysis of Variance performed on these ordinal variables yielded no difference (p > 0.20 for each).

Apparatus. The ATT Frame Creation System (FCS) Series 500 was used because it is a graphics system which, conveniently, accommodates the three devices we wished to compare. It consists of a color graphics monitor, an alphanumeric keyboard, a pen, a mouse, and a horizontal tablet used in conjunction with the pen and the mouse.

The keyboard cursor control keys are located at the right side of the keyboard. The four keys are labelled UP, DOWN, LEFT, and RIGHT. The keys are typamatic; that is, when held down they function in auto-repeat mode. Diagonal movement is not provided. A HOME key in the center of the four is used to select or “freeze” a cursor image. The screen is divided into three regions: at the bottom is the function selection menu; at the top is the color and line-type selection menu; in between these two menu strips is a large drawing area. The cursor keys may be used in combination with the Control key located on the left side of the keyboard to switch between different regions of the screen (CONTROL-UP to move up from an area to the next higher; CONTROL-DOWN to move from an area to the next lower one).

A single tablet is used with the mouse and the pen. The mouse has a flat bottom surface which slides smoothly on the tablet. A button on the top of the mouse is used for clicking (selection). The pen is slightly larger than a regular ball point pen. Clicking (selection) is accomplished by pressing the pen against the tablet. Movement between menu areas and the drawing area is accomplished by moving pen or mouse so that the cursor crosses the border line. Tablet to screen gain is 1 to 0.8.

## 2.1. Description of the experiments

In the first experiment, each subject used the cursor control device to superimpose the red cursor on the white randomly positioned four-pixel (square) targets. The background was medium blue. When the subject was satisfied with a cursor placement, he/she froze its image by either a press of the pen, a press of the mouse button or a press of the HOME key. For simplicity any of these three actions will be referred to as “clicking” here. The user then moved on to the next target, etc., with 10 targets in all. The experimenter saved the completed screen image and then brought up a second screen of 10 randomly placed targets and finally a third similar screen. For each screen the subject began with the cursor positioned at the lower left corner of the drawing area. Time (by stopwatch) was started when the cursor left its initial position; time was stopped as soon as the subject froze the red cursor image on the tenth target of a screen. The three screens were saved by the experimenter for later error analysis; an error condition was deemed to exist whenever a cursor image did not completely cover a target.

In the second experiment, the subjects drew a line to connect (and cover) the vertices of each of various simple figures. Screen targets, again squares composed of four white pixels, were arranged to form points outlining a triangle (upper left quadrant), a rectangle (upper right quadrant), and a pentagon (lower right quadrant). For each repetition, a different shape of the types indicated appeared. The background was medium blue. Starting with the red cursor in the empty lower left quadrant, subjects were required to draw a red line from target to target and so create a perimeter. This involved the selection of the choice CLN (Continuous Line) from the bottom menu, then moving to one of the targets and clicking to freeze the start of the line. Each subsequent click froze the end of the next segment of the perimeter. The free end of the line was always on the cursor and as the cursor moved the line extended, the free end moving up or down, but always integral with the cursor. When the first target that had been clicked by the subject was touched a second time, the subject, having completed the figure, again selected the menu item CLN by clicking on it. A line segment followed the cursor from the last clicked target to the menu position. With the menu click, this extra line segment disappeared. The order of figure selection was at the discretion of the subject.

This procedure was repeated for three screens of three figures each. Screens were saved upon completion, for subsequent error analysis; an error was counted for each non-completely-covered target. Timing (by stopwatch) for each screen started when the cursor left the start position and ended with the final click on CLN.

In the third experiment, the subjects were required to superimpose three randomly placed yellow figures (rectangle, triangle, and circle) within red randomly placed exact outlines of their shape. (The background was still medium blue.) This involved more menu activity than Experiment 2, with the subjects moving back and forth from menu to figure, first to indicate selection of a figure and click on it and then to select MV and perform the move.

As in Experiments 1 and 2, the start position of the cursor was in the lower left corner of the drawing area. Timing began as the cursor left this position and ended with the click freezing the third figure in position. Order of figure selection was at the subject's discretion. Each subject proceeded through three screen repetitions, each with three randomly placed figures and corresponding outlines. Screens were saved by the experimenter for later error analysis; an error was deemed to exist whenever a figure was not totally fitted within its outline. In this experiment, at most, nine errors could occur for one subject (three figures times three screens).

In Experiment 4, each subject was asked to use the other two cursor control devices so that he/she could make a preference decision. The training task here was that of Experiment 1, overlaying the cursor on ten targets placed randomly on the screen. One screen was worked for each of the two (to the subject) new devices. Neither time nor error count was recorded.

## 2.2. Experimental design

The design of this study is between group within group repeated measures. The between group factor is Device (pen, mouse, and keyboard); the within group factor is Experiment (1, 2, and 3). The repeated measures factors are: (1) three repetitions of each task during each sitting and (2) two sittings seven days apart.

Data was analyzed using the SPSSPC + procedures described in Norusis (1986). The Scheffe range test was used to determine the locus of differences found by repeated measures ANOVA tests.

## 2.3. Procedure

Experiment 1. The experimenter, using a written protocol, explained the necessary cursor manipulations, leading the subject hands-on through the procedure. When the experimenter was satisfied that the subject understood and could perform the activities, he then required the subject to overlay the cursor on the ten targets of a practice screen. This screen was identical in form to the three screens the subject then used in the actual experimental procedure.

Experiment 2. The experimenter followed the same procedure: explanation and hands-on learning, followed by a practice session and completion of the three task screens of the experiment.

Experiment 3. The above process was repeated.

Experiment 4. Finally the experimenter explained the use of one of the other devices, leading the subject through its use, hands-on. When the experimenter was satisfied with the subject's competency, he then started the subject on the ten target test screen. The experimenter repeated this sequence for the other device. As noted, neither time nor error count was recorded, since the purpose was solely to provide the subject with the knowledge necessary to make a meaningful choice among the three devices.

Following this, the subject was given a one page nine item questionnaire to complete. The first five questions elicited background information. The last questions were:

6. Did you experience any problem with the experimentation or the devices used? If so, please explain.

7. Now that you have used the three types of cursor control devices, which one would you prefer to use and why? (A) Keyboard; (B) Pen; (C) Mouse. Reason(s).

8. Which device would you choose to use for very accurate cursor positioning? (A) Keyboard; (B) Pen; (C) Mouse.

9. Comments.

The answers to questions seven and eight were used to evaluate preference for general device use and when extreme accuracy was required.

Session 1, which ended with completion of the questionnaire, took about 45 minutes per subject.

In Session 2, which each subject performed on the eighth day following Session 1, the subject repeated the Experiments without the instructions in FCS use. That is, each subject performed a practice screen and then three test screens for each of the three Experiments.

## 3. Results

## Experiment 1 - overlay targets

Speed of use. As Table 3 shows, at all three repetitions of the task at Day 1 and at Day 8, mouse use was faster than use of the pen, which was, in turn, faster than use of the keyboard. A repeated measures ANOVA on Experiment 1 data found a significant difference $(F(2,60)=41.5, p<0.0001)$ among the three devices. The Scheffe showed the difference $(p<0.05)$ to be between keyboard and the other two devices (which were not statistically different from each other) {i.e.: mouse = pen < keyboard}.

Error count. As Table 4 shows, at all three repetitions of the task at Days 1 and 8, there were fewer errors for the keyboard than for the mouse, while the error count for the pen was always largest. A repeated measures ANOVA $F(2,60)=113.0,\ p<0.0001$ plus Scheffe found the three error counts to be different from one and other {keyboard < mouse < pen}. (Probability levels for the Scheffe are only reported at the 0.05 level by SPSSPC +.)

## Experiment 2 - draw outlines

Speed of use. As Table 5 shows, the mouse was faster than a pen in four out of the six trials. The keyboard was always slowest. As in the previous experiment, a repeated measures ANOVA $(F(2,60)=49.9, p<0.0001)$ plus Scheffe on the data found the mouse and pen speed to be significantly faster than the keyboard but not different than each other $\{mouse=pen<keyboard\}$ .

Error count. The keyboard produced the lowest error count in each of the six trials. See Table 6. A repeated measures ANOVA $(F(2,60)=49.8, p<0.0001)$ plus Scheffe found all devices significantly different from each other {keyboard < mouse < pen}.

## Experiment 3 - move figures

Speed of use. Table 7 shows that the mouse was always fastest, followed by the pen, followed by the keyboard. A repeated measures ANOVA $(F(2,60)=91.1, p<0.00001)$ plus Scheffe on data found the mouse and pen speed to be significantly faster than the keyboard but not different from each other {mouse = pen < keyboard}.

Error count. For this experiment, the maximum number of errors possible (all figures misaligned) was nine, which put a low ceiling on this measure. As Table 8 shows, across the six trials the keyboard again had the lowest overall error count. A repeated measures ANOVA $F(2,60)=4.5, p<0.02$ plus Scheffe found the keyboard error count to be significantly lower than the pen error count {keyboard < pen}. The differences between the mouse and the other devices were not significant.

## 3.3. Experiment 4

General device preference. Table 9 presents the count and percent of subjects selecting each device. Random selection would predict one third for each device. A Chi-square test found a significant difference for preference among the three devices (Chi-square(2) = 16.1, p < 0.0001). Using two binomial tests we compared theoretical to actual number preferring the mouse (combining keyboard and pen counts) and the pen (combining keyboard and mouse counts). These tests found 34 (54.0%) to be significantly greater (p < 0.001) than 1/3 and 8 (12.7%) to be significantly less (p < 0.001). Thus, for general work the mouse is significantly preferred over the keyboard and pen while the pen is significantly not preferred compared to the mouse and keyboard.

Device preference for conditions of high accuracy. Table 10 presents the count and percent of subjects preferring each device when high accuracy is required. Again, random selection would predict one third for each device. A Chi-square test found a significant difference among the three devices (Chi-square(2) = 50.7, p < 0.0001). Again, we used the binomial test on each of the devices. The preference for the keyboard was highly significant (p < 0.0001). Lack of preference for the mouse was significant (p < 0.05). Lack of preference for the pen was highly significant (p < 0.0001). Thus for work requiring high accuracy, most subjects would choose the keyboard, some few would choose the mouse, but most would avoid the pen.

## 4. General conclusions

For graphics and drawing applications with conditions similar to those described, the following conclusions can be formulated:

1. With respect to Speed of Use, the mouse is somewhat faster than the pen, and both of these are significantly faster to use than the keyboard.

2. With respect to Error Count, the keyboard produces significantly fewer errors than the mouse and both produce significantly fewer errors than the pen.

3. Under conditions of general use, subjects most prefer the mouse, least prefer the pen.

4. Under conditions of high accuracy, most subjects prefer the keyboard over the mouse. There is almost no preference for the pen.

5. Learning / retention rate is, over the long run, approximately the same for all three devices.

## 5. Discussion

The mouse is preferred for general work. The following factors probably contributed to this: it requires less effort in control and manipulation than the pen; it rests on the tablet, not in the hand, and the fingers do not need to do nearly as much work. As well as giving comfort, this stability makes clicking easier, because less effort is required to keep the device on target during clicking. The mouse is less tedious to use than the keyboard and requires less attention during travel.

The fewer errors resulting from the keyboard and its preference under conditions of high accuracy can be explained in terms of a “leverage” effect and a more static feedback loop. For pen and mouse, as the target is reached, hand and finger motions must become finer and finer, perhaps approaching the limit of human capability (Davis and Olson, 1985). This is not so for the keyboard, where the strength of the finger stroke bears no relation to the fineness of the cursor movement. The coarse stroke of the finger is leveraged into finer and finer motions of the cursor as the user goes from wide-step mode to typamatic movement to individual strokes and slower strokes. When the target is reached, the cursor remains statically positioned, while the other devices require precise attention and control until after the selection button has been pressed.

The keyboard preference of the subjects under a requirement for high accuracy seems to parallel the intricacy of device control. The pen requires more control manipulation (fingers, thumb, wrist, etc.) than does the mouse, than the keyboard (finger strokes only).

The early rate difference between performance on the keyboard and on the other two devices can, we believe, be attributed to the more involved actions required. That is, pointing maps directly onto cognitive schemas, while for keyboarding, cognitive pointing schemas must be translated into “stationary hand” finger strokes. This presumably causes manipulation with the keyboard to begin relatively more slowly. However, the human mind is flexible, and by the fourth trial all three curves run approximately parallel.

A system designed along COMIND principles might have all three devices available (as the Frame system does) so that the users may choose the mechanism that suits their need/personal preference. Unfortunately, there is always the utility versus time and money tradeoff.

Different devices excel under different conditions. In fact, the exact reverse of our results might be obtained in different circumstances, such as entering data into successive cells of a spreadsheet (where the keyboard could be superior to mouse and pen); the user would not even have to look at the screen.

Further work is necessary to catalog the various areas in which pointing is used and then to determine the most appropriate device(s) for each of these. As well, it has been suggested by MacKenzie, et al. (1991) that dragging is an action clearly different than pointing and this assertion deserves additional research.

Another worthwhile effort would be a re-evaluation of the knee control device. Its use leaves both hands available and frees the user from having to lift one hand from the keyboard to access the mouse or pen (Shneiderman, 1980).
