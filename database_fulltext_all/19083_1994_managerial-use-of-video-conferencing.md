# Case Study

# Managerial use of video conferencing

Christine T. Kydd, Diane L. Ferry

Department of Business Administration, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716, USA

## Abstract

The use of video conferencing systems (VCS) for meetings that transcend geographical dispersion in the workplace has increased significantly in recent years. But, where do video conferencing systems fit in relation to traditional communication media used in the workplace? Research suggests that VCS is somewhere between the telephone and face-to-face meetings in terms of media richness. Guidelines on how to use this technology more efficiently and effectively focus on the type of meeting, the emotional content of the situation, the number of sites and individuals included and preparation for the meeting itself. Appropriate use of VCS promises to provide great benefits in productivity.

Keywords: Video conferencing; Media richness; Telecommunications ; Communication; Equivocality; Information richness

## 1. Managerial use of video conferencing

Video conferencing systems (VCS) are one of many emerging group technologies that enable people to communicate and coordinate their work electronically. The use of video conferencing systems by firms has recently exploded. There are several factors. World events (e.g., the Persian Gulf War) and current budget cutbacks that many large firms are currently facing have resulted in the curtailing of travel by many managers. Thus, they have turned to the use of video conferencing for the purpose of holding meetings for which travel funds are no longer available.

Although the initial motivation may have been travel reduction [3], managers have now had time to consider the types of tasks for which VCS is most appropriate, and, just as important, the tasks for which it is not appropriate. While some discussion of VCSs appears in the literature ([1,8,12–14]), there are few studies of their actual use by managers or of the types of tasks for which such systems are most useful and effective. (See e.g., [2].)

The characteristics of traditional communication media such as face-to-face meetings, telephones and paper memoranda have been discussed in the literature in relation to the concept of media richness $[4]$ . This paper is concerned with the question: How does VCS compare with traditional communication media? Our society is quickly moving toward the increased use of electronic communication devices, because they are fast and can transcend the traditional boundaries of space and time. But few field studies have tried to determine how managers currently use VCS to communicate. By studying how managers actually use VCS, guidelines can be developed showing how others can improve their communication effectiveness using this technology.

Current information processing theory suggests that video conferencing should be a fairly “rich” communication medium, where rich means that the medium 1) allows for multiple cues through multiple channels of communication, 2) provides immediate feedback capabilities, 3) allows language variety, and 4) involves a high degree of personalization [5]. An example of a rich medium is a face-to-face meeting, while a formal paper memorandum is an example of a medium that is not rich. VCS appear to meet these criteria because users can both see and hear each other in real time, they can give and receive immediate feedback, and they can employ speech as well as non-language utterances to make their points. As a result, many users view video conferencing as a fairly personal way to communicate with others. On the other hand, individuals have been found to prefer face-to-face meetings over geographically dispersed meetings connected by technology [2]. Thus it is not clear how VCS are perceived and used.

It is important to consider the richness of a communication medium because if a very rich technology is used for a task that does not require that richness (a simple confirmation of meeting date and time), then precious resources are wasted. If, on the other hand, a medium that is not particularly rich is used for a highly equivocal task (such as determining the goals of a newly formed company), then the task may not be accomplished satisfactorily. It has been suggested that high rich media are necessary to resolve equivocal situations (multiple conflicting goals exist and a solution needs to be enacted from differing frames of reference), while media of low richness are sufficient for conditions of uncertainty (additional objective data is needed to answer predetermined questions).

Research has suggested that higher level managers and also higher performing managers are more media sensitive than others $[5,15]$ . They are able to communicate more efficiently and effectively because of their ability to select the appropriate communication medium for the task at hand. In fact, Trevino et al. $[15]$ refer to the “richness imperative”: the notion that equivocal situations demand use of rich communication media.

The purpose of this paper is to report on a study of the use of VCS by managers; it was carried out to determine what managers have learned about the effective use of VCS in recent years.

## 2. The study

Because of the exploratory nature of the research, an in-depth interview was deemed to be the most appropriate instrument for this study so that each manager could have maximum latitude in what information they wanted to share. An interview protocol was developed and is provided in the appendix. This process was used in all interviews, although some of the conversations wandered a bit more than others. Since it was decided that depth was more important than breadth, only fourteen middle or upper level managers were interviewed. Each participant had video conferencing capabilities available to use in the firm and had quite extensive experience in using VCS to communicate with others. All of the interviews were carried out in the manager's office.

Some of the sites of the firm with which these managers communicated had full motion video conferencing systems in place, while others had freeze-frame systems only. The headquarters location (at which the interviews took place) had a full motion system. Although meeting participants could send pictures of people or documents through the freeze-frame systems, there was usually much greater emphasis placed on sending documents. Thus, communication among individuals at the various sites basically dropped down to the audio channel. Even for the full motion system, there was a slight delay in the transmission of the picture so that facial expressions and other reactions were not seen immediately.

The interviews centered around the critical communication incidents (tasks or parts of tasks that require long distance communication with others) for which the managers had used video conferencing or other communication media. Specific information gathered for each critical communication incident included: whether the VCS used was a freeze-frame or a full-motion system, the number of people and sites that were involved in the communication, whether or not there was an existing relationship among the individuals involved in the meeting before this communication, why video conferencing was chosen for this particular incident, and how well it worked. Other information discussed was: ease of use of the system, preparation for using a VCS system, and effect of using video conferencing on general productivity.

Once the interviews were completed, a content analysis was carried out on the notes. For each manager, information was summarized for the name and type of meeting for which VCS had been used (e.g., whether the main purpose was fact finding, negotiating, information transfer, or open discussion), the type of technology used (e.g., freeze-frame or full motion system), what the participant believed was the most significant precondition for a successful VCS meeting/communication, optimal meeting length and number of participant sites/individuals, and other relevant comments regarding the use of video conferencing.

## 3. Discussion of results

The findings are discussed in terms of the three major areas that emerged as significant from the interviews:

1) the various types of meetings that individuals have held via video conferencing where VCS has worked satisfactorily;

2) what the managers believe are the major preconditions for a highly successful video conference; and

3) the optimal (or at least highly satisfactory) number of sites and individuals that should be involved in a video conference, as well as optimal (or highly satisfactory) length of a meeting held via VCS.

The most often mentioned meeting types were information sharing or exchange meetings and status checks. Almost all of the participants had organized and run meetings for these purposes. Very few managers held VCS meetings to discuss emotionally charged or controversial issues (only two mentioned these); in fact, several participants suggested that video conferencing was not appropriate for such meetings, or for meetings where critical decisions had to be made. Such reservations suggest that video conferencing is not as rich a medium as might be expected from its objective characteristics. Although a video conference looks similar to a face-to-face meeting along many characteristics, it does not seem to feel the same to those using it. Most of the items are more closely related to information sharing and exchange (needed to reduce uncertainty) rather than enacting a common frame of reference (necessary to resolve equivocality).

One reason for the perceived lack of richness may be the technology itself. When there is an unnatural delay in communication among individuals, it necessarily stilts the conversation. Thus, even when using a full motion system, managers are sensitive to the slight time delays in receiving reaction and feedback.

## 3.2. Preconditions for success

There were three major themes underlying the participants' suggestions about preconditions needed to hold a successful video conference. At least twelve of the fourteen managers interviewed mentioned the factor as being important for success.

The first was the importance of being acquainted with the other meeting participants before communicating with them via VCS. Most believed that if there was already a personal relationship, it was fairly easy to use an electronic link, either with freeze-frame or full motion video. However, if there was no pre-existing relationship, then it was difficult to meet and establish initial rapport electronically. It is interesting to note that, in general, the managers did not differentiate between freeze frame and full motion systems, except when participants were not previously acquainted with one another. In this case, the freeze frame system was considered to be inadequate, though the full motion system was considered acceptable.

The second important factor for a successful video conference was adequate preparation for the meeting. Every manager interviewed mentioned this as a critical factor for effective communication. High quality visual aids were also absolutely necessary when the focus of the meeting was exchange of information: the size of the photo or blueprint had to be large and highly detailed for distant participants to view the material easily. Most managers also agreed that effective meetings only occurred when the manager conducting the meeting was well prepared to discuss the material. They admitted that this should be a factor for any meeting and that its success hinges on the preparation of the participants, but this factor seemed more important when holding a video conference.

The third factor, the actual meeting process, is related to preparation although it is distinct from it. Again the interviewees suggested that a “tight” meeting process leads to a successful VCS meeting, where “tight” refers to the meeting being well-focused on a coherent and rather narrow topic. Other terms used to describe the better processes were “discrete rather than open-ended meeting topic”. It is not only the actual items under discussion that have an impact on the meeting’s success, but also the process by which they are discussed. Because certain aspects of communication are lost over the electronic link (some or all non-verbal cues), the channels that are left must be utilized more carefully.

## 3.3. Number of sites / individuals and length of meeting

The participants were largely in agreement that no more than five different sites should be used to ensure a successful video conference. Most of the managers preferred only two to four sites. If some of the distant sites had a system that was audio only, it was viewed as a definite handicap. When one or two sites had no video link, they were perceived by other sites as “left out of the meeting”: the meeting leader had to work extremely hard to keep them involved. Thus, whether or not all of the involved sites have video capabilities is an important issue regarding managerial use of VCS. Markus [10] reported that managers found E-mail useful only to the extent that others also had E-mail available. Such a “critical mass” concept certainly holds true for video conferencing.

The number of individuals who may be involved in a video conference should not exceed fifteen (the suggestion was for approximately five sites with two to three people at each): more than that was believed to be too many and led to an “out of control” meeting. However, for one-way information flow, larger numbers of participants could be accommodated. In that case the process more closely resembles a television broadcast than a business meeting.

The proposed optimal length for a video conference varied with the manager; however, many believed that two hours was best. The major reason given was that a video conference requires more concentration of the participants than a face-to-face meeting. Some said that an electronic meeting could last as long as four hours and still be successful. A few of the managers interviewed had participated in all-day VCS meetings and claimed that it was difficult to keep the participants focused after a few hours.

## 4. Conclusions

Based on the interviews, some conclusions can be drawn, although further study is warranted.

The first is concerned with media richness: a

VCS should provide a rich communication environment because it allows for multiple cues through multiple channels of communication, it provides immediate feedback, it allows for language variety and it affords fairly personal contact. However, it is apparently not as rich as one would objectively expect. Reasons include the inappropriateness of VCS for the creation of a team, for interaction, particularly around a controversial or sensitive topic, and for meetings with open ended topics or conflicting viewpoints. One manager commented that it was easier for individuals to lie over the electronic link than in-person and that the level of mutual trust and loyalty of participants while using a VCS was much less than found in face-to-face settings.

Maybe the technology has not yet progressed far enough to allow for a close simulation of the face-to-face situation. Also some managers in this study had primarily used the freeze frame rather than a full motion option. Several commented that a full motion system was a huge improvement over freeze frame (many viewed the latter as an audio system with FAX capabilities).

Thus, we conclude that in actual use, video conferencing is a moderately rich communication medium. It is at least as rich as other audio-only systems and may actually be richer.

Second, video conferencing seems to be appropriate for certain types of task. Uncertainty versus equivocality of the task is a major factor. Most participants either intimated or stated clearly that video conferencing is a better medium for information transfer than for complex discussion and decision making.

Uncertainty requires basic information exchange and transfer to answer pre-defined questions. Equivocality requires debate, discussion and social interaction to resolve multiple conflicting viewpoints and to enact a common frame of reference. Equivocality also requires participants to determine what questions need to be asked rather than to answer questions with objective information. Apparently video conferencing works well when meeting participants need to focus on specific drawings, documents and charts. These activities are appropriate for reducing uncertainty. On the other hand, VCS does not work well for brainstorming, or unstructured discussion and debate. In contrast with most group decision support systems (GDSS), which do work well for such tasks, there is no process structure built into a VCS. It may be the lack of structure rather than characteristics of the medium that explain this difference.

As an example: one manager held a meeting to evaluate different parts of the firm for participation in the Malcolm Baldridge award. He described this meeting as involving “turf issues” (i.e., conflicting views, debate, and argument). After meeting for several hours over VCS, the participants were not able to resolve the relevant issues and arrive at a decision. However, the same group met in a face-to-face mode some weeks later and were able to come to a decision within fifteen minutes.

Thus, we believe that VCS are better suited for tasks that involve the reduction of uncertainty than for those that involve the resolution of equivocality; moreover full motion VCS will be more likely to be successful than a freeze frame system.

In conclusion, some managerial guidelines are offered:

1. To achieve greatest success while running a video conference, managers should restrict their meetings to those with a clear agenda and non-controversial information exchange.

2. Managers should use full motion, not freeze-frame systems, for situations that require debate. Results are greatly enhanced for both systems but particularly for the freeze-frame case when meeting participants are already acquainted through a face-to-face relationship.

3. Meetings should probably not exceed two hours in length (and certainly not four).

4. All participants should be well-prepared for the meeting, especially the leader.

5. Material should be prepared specifically for the video environment (e.g., large type).

6. The number of people at each site should be limited, as also should the number of sites involved.

By increasing the effectiveness of the way in which managers communicate, firms should realize productivity benefits.

## Appendix 1

Interview Protocol

1. For what purposes (i.e., general types of meetings) have you used the video conferencing facility?

\- Freeze-frame or full motion?

\- Number of participants/locations involved?

\- Did you already know the person/people with whom you communicated?

\- For each use of the facility, what was the critical communication incident (CCI)?

\- How well did the video conference work for this CCI?

\- Did you consider other forms of communication for this CCI? If so, which ones? Why did you choose video conferencing over the other options, such as a face-to-face meeting, paper memo, E-mail or audio conferencing?

\- Would you make the same choice again? Why or why not?

2. In general, under what circumstances do you choose to use video conferencing?

3. How easy/difficult was it to use the video conferencing facility?

\- Difficulty in learning how to use it? How long did it take? Did you have to “rehearse” the meeting?

\- Did it get in the way of communicating? Did it help in communicating? In what ways?

4. Do you believe that use of the video conferencing facility improves your productivity? If so, how? Why?
