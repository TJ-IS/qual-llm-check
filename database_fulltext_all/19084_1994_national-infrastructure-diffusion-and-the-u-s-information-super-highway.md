# Research

# National infrastructure diffusion and the U.S. information super highway

William J. Kettinger \*

College of Business Administration, University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

## Abstract

Governments are playing an increasingly important role in the development of national information infrastructure (NII). This paper explores one example in the United States' effort to build an “Information Super Highway”; which may determine the way its citizens create and communicate knowledge in the next century. This NII will provide widespread access to private and public networks capable of transmitting data, voice, text, images and video. It will consist of user information appliances, local area networks, access networks, and regional and national networks with speeds of hundreds of gigabits per second. In the course of its development the U.S. Information Super Highway's scope has been broadened to include high-speed connections to public schools, corporations, and eventually homes. The building of national information infrastructure, raises important issues of ownership, management, operation, use, and economics. A diffusion model of NII is presented to address these implementation issues.

Keywords: Information super highway; Information infrastructure; Innovation and diffusion; Telecommunications; National Information policy; Wide-area networks; Inter-organizational systems

## 1. Introduction

A nation's infrastructure is defined by its basic communication and transport capacity. Many governments have been the source of funding for large scale or risky infrastructure projects (e.g., the U.S. highway system). Porter [27], states that:

"A nation's industry depends on a modern and improving infrastructure. This is particularly true in advanced transportation, logistics and telecommunications, all integral to introducing modern technologies and to competing in foreign markets. Both firms and governments have a responsibility in creating and upgrading infrastructure.”

The private sector has long recognized the critical role that technical infrastructure plays as a strategic enabler of growth and structural reconfiguration $[3,29,42]$ . As computer and telecommunication technology has progressed, many governments are now refocusing their attention from traditional “brick and mortar” infrastructure development to electronic communication and transport projects $[14]$ .

One example of this phenomenon is in the United States' effort to establish a national information infrastructure (NII), or "Information Super Highway" as it is often called, which may determine the way its citizens create and communicate knowledge into the next century. The U.S. High-Performance Computing Act of 1991 called for the establishment of a National Research and Education Network (NREN). The resulting telecommunications infrastructure interconnects centers of research and education for the exchange of ideas, databases, scientific instruments, and computing. In enacting this legislation, the U.S. Congress took a major step toward formalizing a national information infrastructure. There is now strengthened support in the United States for broadening the scope of the NREN objectives to connect all U.S. citizens to an information super-highway. The Clinton Administration has proposed a nearly \$5 billion budget over the next four years to build an information super highway that would enable users to move ideas, pictures, and data-quickly and easily all over the world.

The push for the U.S. Information Super Highway Project is being led by public and private sector leaders who point to the growing importance of the information processing sector of the U.S. economy and the fact that other leading economic nations, such as Japan, Germany, France and the United Kingdom, are proceeding with construction of national computing and communication infrastructure projects; e.g., the Japanese have committed to the fiber-optic connection of every home, business, school, and research laboratory by the year 2015. They have already implemented the Asia Pacific Interactive Communication Network linking elementary and high school students to networks in 108 countries [35]. Even the small city-state of Singapore has made substantial efforts towards establishing an NII, first in the realm of international trade with an EDI-based network (TRADENET) and now through similar computing and telecommunication networks to support health care, retailing and manufacturing [16].

Given the degree of international competition and the rapid advance of technology, many nations are now determining what their NII should contain and how it should be structured.

Key issues to be resolved include:

\- How is a national information infrastructure developed? What are the phases of development?

\- How is a national information infrastructure managed? What organization and management structure is needed for a national information infrastructure?

\- What is the scope of a national information infrastructure? Only the scientific elite? All of higher education? Kindergarten through high school (K-12)? Private sector research and development? All commercial users?

\- What applications and services are provided by such infrastructure? High-performance scientific computing? Electronic-mail? Library access? Electronic publishing? Instruction? Commercial IS?

\- How is such infrastructure funded? Federal government? Universities? Public education? States? Private sector? Individuals?

In examining the case of U.S Information Super Highway's development, this article will explore these issues as they relate to an NII's growth and development.

## 2. The path to a national information infrastructure

The new technology of the proposed U.S Information Super Highway would propel television, telecommunications, computers, publishing, and information services together to form one interactive information/entertainment industry. A national data network is envisioned stretching from coast to coast providing a multi-gigabit resource for videoconferencing, telecommuting, telephone and workstation integration, Broadband Integrated Services Digital Network, image communication, multi-document storage, and retrieval, and multimedia communication [8]. John Sculley, former chairman of Apple Computer, estimates that revenue generated by this interactive information industry could well reach \$3.5 trillion worldwide by the year 2001.

In relative terms, the U.S. Information Super Highway has a short history. Its seed can be found in earlier U.S. federal government subsidy for high-speed, heterogeneous data networks that supported a rapidly growing web of global research communication. Early academic and research use of inter-organizational networking can be traced back to the scientific networks of the 1970s [22]. The use of these was limited to specific research purposes (e.g., ARPANET, CSNET, SPAN, HEPNET) [28]. Although many of these networks existed prior to BITNET, it was this network's introduction in the early 1980s that can be attributed with wide-spread adoption of inter-organizational computer communication among the large university community [11]. Because university faculty were not directly charged for use of BITNET, they began to view inter-organizational networking as a "free good." But, while BITNET's store and forward messaging approach satisfied simple messaging requirements, many converts soon decided that they required interactive access to remote computers. At the same time, new applications emerged requiring the transmission of graphics, video and voice. Development of alternative networks became necessary.

The U.S. National Science Foundation Network (NSFNET) $^{1}$ was established by the National Science Foundation (NSF) in 1984 to provide a large-scale packet switching network to the university community and, in particular, to university faculty conducting research at NSF Supercomputer Centers. The design of NSFNET was based on the TCP/IP protocols originally developed for the U.S. Advanced Research Projects Agency network – ARPANET. $^{2}$ NSFNET grew into a multi-node, high-speed network backbone connecting regional and state-wide networks. These networks were formed and managed by independent organizations, most of which were originally funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation. In addition to domestic access to the Internet $^{3}$ , backbone connections were also made to Europe and the Pacific Rim.

Internet availability enabled scholars to adjust their research agendas and communication patterns. Researchers were thus able to route parts of their jobs to supercomputer centers. Additional benefits were achieved by on-line access to unique scientific equipment and information resources, such as The Library of Congress, the Congressional Research Service data bases, and the National Library of Medicine's Med-line [30]. As NSF's high performance computing "test-bed" applications progressed, new innovative computational science applications became possible, including: global change and biosphere simulations, severe weather forecasting, cancer gene data bases, air pollution, superconductor simulations, aerospace vehicle design, energy conservation and turbulence combustion simulations, and microsystems design and packaging [10].

NSFNET rapidly evolved into something much broader in scope. Communication via e-mail (estimated to be 80–90% of the traffic) became the largest network application [19]. Many proponents began to view NSFNET as a mechanism to achieve a “Collaboratory” or “invisible college” [18,43]. Schatz [32] identified an “electronic community system” of scientists studying a common model of an organism in molecular biology over the Internet; they were able to encode and manipulate knowledge and values necessary to function effectively. This community knowledge included both formal and informal data, results, literature and news. Access to wide-area information services (e.g., Gopher, WWW Archie and WAIS) $^{4}$ and bulletin boards have substantially broadened the communication function of the network.

Between 1988 and 1992, NSFNET went from a T1 backbone (1.55 megabits per second) connecting 400 universities and research centers, to a T3 (45 megabits per second) backbone connecting over 4500 universities, businesses, libraries, schools, museums and government agencies. During this period, data traffic over the NSFNET backbone increased from approximately 100 million packets per month to about 12.5 billion packets per month [24,25]. This phenomenal growth rate has continued, with volume doubling to 26 billion packets per month by February 1993 [26].

Increasing public awareness of the potential of high-speed networking broadened interest in the NSFNET beyond the scientific and research communities to schools, libraries, government, business, and individuals. Potential network information service providers, such as the not-for-profit (libraries) and for-profit (publishers) information service voiced their desire to be active players in the NII design and decision making. Interest was also particularly strong for high school science and mathematics education $[20]$ ; e.g., the Cornell Theory Center's “Superquest” high school supercomputing challenge allows student teams to compete for the best scientific project using Cornell’s NSF Supercomputing Center. In 1989, realizing the needed emphasis on education as well as research, the U.S. Federal Research Coordinating Committee renamed the network the National Research and Education Network (NREN) and projected that the population of active NREN users would increase from an estimated 1 million in 1990 to 4–6 million (approximately 1/2 the U.S. academic and research community) by the year 2000 $[36,37]$ .

It has been estimated that the federal contribution supporting the NSFNET backbone represents only 10 percent of the total U.S. network infrastructure investment made predominantly by universities, states, and the private sector [17]. Since the mid 1980s, U.S. colleges have made tremendous financial investments in faculty and student workstations and network connections. Mid-level networks have also moved away from federal subsidy to reflect a more commercial-like model. For example, the California Education and Research Federation Network (CERFNET) that connects into the Internet had, in 1992, more than 70 members including grade schools, high schools, universities, industrial facilities, libraries, and hospitals, as well as government facilities. All its links are leased from local and long distance telecommunication carriers. CERFNET has also established international Internet connectivity to organizations in Brazil, Korea, Mexico and Venezuela.

Continued network growth has created a commercial sector of technical service providers. In 1990, IBM, MCI and MERIT formed the not-for-profit consortia, Advanced Network and Services Inc. (A.N.S.), to manage and extend the NSFNET's capabilities to commercial companies. Alan H. Weiss, then president of A.N.S. stated, "The demand for networking is sky-rocketing...it has grown more than 500 percent in five years, and still touches only a small part of the people and institutions in the education and research community with a need to connect" [38]. By 1992, it was estimated that the domestic commercial market for Internet product and services exceeded four billion U.S. dollars and was growing at the rate of seventy-five percent per year. U.S. telecommunication vendors such as AT&T, MCI, the Regional Bell Operating Companies, and Sprint became actively involved in developing new high-performance network products. Private companies, such as Alternet and Performance Systems International Inc., began offering both common carrier, software, and consulting services to businesses that could not afford, or did not qualify for, previous NSFNET connection. Consultants and software developers, such as Bolt, Beranek and Newman, Inc. and The Center for National Research Initiatives, continue to be actively involved in further enhancement of network software to provide greater capacity, improved network management, and ease of use.

In a direct move to privatize the delivery of Internet services, PSI, UUNET Technologies Inc., and General Atomic Inc. formed the Commercial Internet Exchange. Under a cooperative agreement, each vendor provides complete Internet traffic exchange regardless of network source. These firms provide a large part of the commercial TCP/IP Internet services in the U.S. and are not subject to government mandated restrictions of “Appropriate Usage Policies” outlined for NSFNET use. Susan Estrada of General Atomic stated, “We have structured this agreement so that the customers remain our focus, rather than government rules and subsidies.” Martin Scoffstall, PSI’s Vice President stated, “The CIX will help to reduce the uncertainty some users expressed concerning government control, such as a government owned or sanctioned monopoly” [33]. In addition, A.N.S. operates a virtual, independent network over the same physical equipment as the NSFNET to allow commercial users to make use of high-speed Internet connections.

In January 1993, Stanford Research Institute estimated that both the public and commercial Internet connected more than 1.3 million hosts around the world. Growing along side the Internet are the tens of millions of users of packet data networks such as Sprintnet, British Telecom Tymnet, and the CompuServe Packet Network plus the tens of thousands of companies worldwide that link employees with private local and wide area networks that have Internet connection. More recently, cellular radio networks are providing Internet interconnectivity to notebook computer users. Finally the telephone companies will soon offer digital information services that effectively will make them large inter-networks.

During the network growth, there has been a tandem effort in the U.S. Congress to establish a national information infrastructure and provide it with financial support. Since the 1970s, Vice President Albert Gore, has been an advocate for an “information super highway linking educators, students, scientists, and business people of this country” [9]. In 1989 and 1990, he waged unsuccessful legislative campaigns to establish federal funding for such a project. In 1991 he resubmitted a bill (S. 272) that would “establish a high-capacity national research and education network … and … develop an information infrastructure of data bases, services, access mechanisms, and research facilities” This legislation eventually passed and was later signed into law [39,40]. This outlined a five year effort leading to a national gigabit per second network in 1996 which will link research and educational institutions, government, and industry and eventually provide public access to information.

Under the law, the federal government will continue to provide subsidies to support the expansion of the national research and educational network. However, based on the provisions of this law, the network has reached a juncture that will mean significant change including:

\- The network will be gradually moved to private sector ownership.

\- Access that was once exclusively oriented to scientific research will be expanded to include libraries, educational institutions, and businesses.

\- Services that were previously viewed as a “free good” will be made available on a charge basis.

\- Operational procedures and standards will be devised in cooperation with an expanded group of actors and operated by the private sector.

There is now a heightened effort to expand the original NREN legislation to engender the development of a National Information Infrastructure. In an economic statement $[4]$ , the proposed new programs would include:

\- Implementation of the high-performance computing and communications program, which would fund research and development focused on creating more powerful supercomputers and increasing the speed of the national network. The bulk of the network's technology will be purchased and constructed on a competitive basis but leveraged by federal policy and technology test-bed development.

\- Creation of a task force on information infrastructure. In an effort to create a consistent and stable regulatory environment that will facilitate private sector investments necessary to build the network, a high-level inter-agency task force within the National Economic Council will work with Congress and the private sector to find consensus on and implement policy changes needed.

\- Creation of an information infrastructure technology program. This will assist industry in the development of hardware and software to apply advanced computing and networking technology in manufacturing, health care, education and libraries.

\- Providing funding for networking pilot projects through the National Telecommunication and Information Administration (NTIA) of the Department of Commerce. NTIA is to provide matching grants to states, school districts, libraries, and other non-profit entities to purchase the necessary hardware.

\- Dissemination of federal information. This position is to disseminate governmental collected data more expeditiously. In particular, the administration will use new computer and networking technology to make economic and environment data readily available.

The vision of the proposed NII is one of a universally accessible widely distributed, private and public interconnected networks that permit access to a variety of databases and that can transmit voice, text, images, video, and virtually any other format for depicting information to anyone, at any time, in any place. The NII scope will range from supercomputer and research centers requiring gigabit data transmission speeds, to business user needs for highly reliable multiple megabit speeds, and to users in homes and schools, whose needs may only be in the kilobit range. Finally, because the technical skill set of the NII uscrs will be extremely diverse, efforts are proposed to educate the citizenry concerning usage and benefits of such an infrastructure.

## 3. Applying a diffusion framework to the evolution of an NII

Considerable past research has focused on the transitional process whereby a technology moves from conception to maturation. Since the 1930s economists, such as Schumpeter [34], have recognized technology as a central factor underlying political and organizational dynamics and as a critical determinant of group and individual behaviors and outcomes. Technology is conceptualized as a set of interdependent and hierarchical systems developed by an inter-linked community of researchers and practitioners. This definition fits well with the notion of NII as a grouping of diverse information and communication technologies that make up infrastructure. NII consists of user information appliances (computers, personal assistants, FAX machines, etc.), local area networks (LANs), and access networks, regional and national networks embodying various technologies, with speeds in the hundreds of gigabits per second, vendor systems and databases. Researchers such as Andersen and Tushman state that technology moves through a cycle of rapid growth and tremendous change, followed by a period of incremental improvement until it reaches a mature state, after which it will be replaced by a new technology [1].

It is widely held that diffusion of technical “innovation occurs through distinct stages based on communication, over time, among clements of a social system” [31]. Technical innovations are communicated from sources to potential innovators through communication channels (friends and colleagues, magazines, group meetings, etc.). Potential adopters may differ in their innovativeness or the extent to which they are capable and motivated to adopt an innovation. The rate of adoption will be affected by differences in innovators. The social system in which an innovation is communicated can be made up of any grouping of individuals or organizations that possess similar knowledge and goals (e.g., professional associations, work groups, schools, etc.). Communication about the value of an innovation may grow exponentially across overlapping social systems.

Diffusion of innovation theory has been used to explain the adoption, implementation and use of information technologies by individuals, organizations, and industries $[2,5,12,21,23,44]$ . Research on technical substitution and diffusion shows that new technological paradigms follow a classic logistical “S” curve $[41]$ . Ultimately, to be successful an innovation must have a perceived relative advantage over the technology it supersedes. Compatibility with existing values, past experience, and the need of adopting individuals and organizations may also be important. Ease of use or complexity of the technology may affect diffusion rates. A perceived difficulty in adopting the technology may delay implementation. However, the ability to try out the technology on an experimental basis lowers the risk. Finally, the extent to which the value of the technical innovation is observable accelerates diffusion.

In 1991, Mitchell and Zmud mapped the development of the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) industry from infancy to maturity. $^{5}$ They present a diffusion model of infrastructure whereby user demand is measured against stages of infrastructure investment: development, launch, growth, maturity, and technology substitution. The development of the NII may be viewed as moving through a similar “S” curve with corresponding growth in the number of network users, traffic, and/or costs. NII development and distribution can occur from numerous locations: sources can be national efforts supported by grants and contracts; consortia of both public and “pioneering” private sector organizations established to leverage research investments; and, once viable markets are established for technologies that make up an NII, new industries may emerge. It can be anticipated that incremental modifications will be made to all technologies to ensure their continued competitive viability until such time as they are replaced by technical substitution. As an NII matures, pricing and saturation may impose limits on growth.

## 4. The how, what and where of the NII

When national infrastructure development is viewed as a process of innovation diffusion, important who, what, where and how issues arise at different phases of development. These have crucial policy implications. The McFarlan and McKenney diffusion process model will be adapted to help describe four phases of NII development:

\- Phase 1: Technology identification and initial investment. This involves early development and learning technologies that make up the infrastructure; short-term technical problems are numerous and long-term understanding is needed. Funding may be precarious and subtechnologies tend to emerge as individual research or technical projects. Through technical experimentation, substantial technical expertise concerning the technology is developed.

\- Phase 2: Technological learning and adaptation: This is characterized by heightened broad-based user awareness. The infrastructure technologies are subject to user-oriented experimentation concerning profitable applications. However, problems in recognized relative advantage (usefulness), of ease-of-use, and of compatibility may result in a high level of risk. Governmental subsidy or consortia will be needed to incubate the technologies. If they are nurtured successfully, usage will tend to increase and commercial viability will be realized.

\- Phase 3: Rationalization and control: During this phase, the applications of the infrastructure are clearly specified and cost-effective implementations of the infrastructure technologies are undertaken. The levels of risk have been minimized and healthy competitive markets or industries emerge around the infrastructure. A major challenge is the development of appropriate tools and controls to ensure that the infrastructure is used efficiently. Development of formal standards, cost-benefit and pricing mechanisms are needed.

\- Phase 4: Maturity / wide-spread technology transfer: The infrastructure has now essentially passed through the gauntlet of organizational and individual learning, with technical skills, user awareness, and management controls in place. The information and communication products of this infrastructure are generally available and based on market-based pricing conventions.

When a NII is based on existing networks and information resources, used for commercial and personal applications, open to incremental technology upgrades and new users and transparent in its telecommunication protocol, the four phases of the NII's past and future development may occur: see Figure 1. By using this framework we can begin to address how national information infrastructure is developed, how it is funded, who has access, what applications are offered, and how it is managed.

## 4.1. How is a national information infrastructure developed?

Phase I from about 1970-1984 was characterized by the technologies of the research and special purpose networks available at that time with serial applications. Phase II, from 1984 to 1992, applies to the development and expansion of the backbone and regional networks, and the many state and local networks. Phase III, which may take until 1998, might be described as controlled growth of the a national research and education network (NREN) with delivery of service offered by privatized regional network utilities and commercial vendors [6,7]. In Phase IV, current users will be joined by businesses, public schools, etc., forming a larger matrix of information infrastructure users.

## 4.2. What is the scope (users and applications) of a national information infrastructure?

During Phase I the principal users were researchers and scientists. A series of basic applications evolved, with electronic mail being common. Additional applications, such as remote log-on to distance and inter-organizational computers and file transfer, grew in ease of use. The maximum available bandwidth was 9.6 thousand bits per second. In Phase II, network speeds were increased to T1 and T3 service. Directory assistance, network file and information servers, and network management and security methods were devised to keep this decentralized grouping of thousands of networks operating. These efforts led to exponential growth in usage. Legislation was passed to supply network and information capabilities to libraries, secondary schools, and businesses.

By the end of Phase III we should see widespread availability of gigabit network speeds for backbone communication and at least T1 speeds over twisted-pair copper wires for most members of the NREN. Transmission of interactive voice, video, text, and image information at relatively low cost will take on increased importance. Entertainment and electronic publishing applications will begin to proliferate, with the potential for information being supplied based on preassigned presentation formats. Transmission of digitally captured and preserved documents may become more popular than FAX. However, copyright and distribution mechanism laws will need to be expanded or modified to allow such innovations as customized printing and publishing.

As the NII becomes a commercially delivered infrastructure, business information system applications will have access to high-performance networking. By the Maturity Phase IV, fiber optic and satellite communication and wireless broad band technology will come close to making the NII a universally accessible network that can “transmit voice, text, images, video, and virtually any other format for depicting information, to anyone, at any time, in any place.”

## 4.3. How is such infrastructure funded?

Phase I funding tends to be uncertain and dispersed: the individual technologies tended to be designed to meet specific parochial needs, with funding coming from those directly benefiting or from research and development (R&D) resources. In Phase II, user realization of the potential for high-speed, computer-based communication of TCP/IP networks placed pressure on the federal government to subsidize the NSFNET backbone and Regional Internets. The 1991 NREN legislation recognized the need to broaden the scope of the NSFNET to educational and business users.

In Phase III, commercial suppliers of communication and information services are pressuring government to privatize all Internet services. In recognition of this migration, NSF has proposed to end subsidy of Regional Internets by the 1998. The federal government would no longer operate networks or provide direct subsidy to Internet providers, but would provide direct subsidy to individuals and organizations to assist in payment for commercial Internet usage for “social welfare” or the “public good” (e.g., direct subsidy to public schools, libraries, basic researchers, etc.). The U.S. government, believing in the merit of this approach, has committed to provide funding for networking pilot projects through the National Telecommunication and Information Administration of the U.S. Department of Commerce. They will provide matching grants to states, school districts, libraries, and other non-profit entities so that they can purchase the computers and networking connections for distance learning, etc.

In Phase III, pricing mechanisms must be devised. While “packet accounting” over the NSFNET shows that user demand has grown rapidly, it must be remembered that NSFNET was heavily subsidized and generally considered a “free good.” At this point it is unclear how user demand would be affected by a charge-based system. Vice-President Gore, comments that it is “the classic chicken and egg problem: The NII won’t be economically viable until a market for it develops – but the market will not develop until users can experiment with the higher speeds and greater capacities to learn which new products and services are available and needed” [13].

Several analogies have been developed. One would treat the network as a library, allowing unlimited use to institutional subscribers. This guarantees equal access within organizations but eliminates individual choice. Another is that of the commercial electronic publisher that bills on a fee-for-service basis. Then, pricing is based on connect time, unit or record charges, “hit” charges, line speed charges, or CPU charges, with price being strictly determined by the marketplace [15]. A final approach is a regulated private utility, similar to the energy and telecommunication industries. This would guarantee users affordable and predictable prices; these would be initially subsidized to cover up-front capitalization, with user payment based on per unit of consumption and minimal hook-up costs.

During Phase III, the Regional and state based Internets began entering into partnership with telephone, and potentially cable providers, to form entities similar to privatized utilities. These utilities are subject to some form of rules and regulation controlling pricing schemes to ensure “fairness in rates.” It is possible that, in Phase IV, these utilities will be substantially deregulated. Then, the NII would operate in a free market.

4.4. How is national information infrastructure managed?

In Phase I the management approach was ad hoc, with specialized networks emerging to meet special needs. Users developed technical solutions to the problems that arose with new and un-tested networks. Phase II management was learning-driven with subsidized access to ever-improving user services. The focus was not on any concerted effort to deliver a product, but to establish a critical mass of users; efforts focused on development and expansion of the NSFNET and Regional Internets. A governmental subsidized university/corporate consortia was formed to operate the NSFNET backbone. In Phase III, the management of a NII will depend on the nature of the services offered and the subscriber base. Delivery of Internet service is moving to privatized regional network utilities and commercial vendors. The federal government will probably focus on its regulatory responsibilities. Federal government agencies may seek to influence governance regulations that best supports their missions. In addition, standards bodies may be employed in setting operating procedures for NII service delivery. Phase IV may see information infrastructure management structures that are no longer merely national, but reflect an international information infrastructure, with national information policies being weighed against international policy criteria.

## 5. Conclusion and recommendations

The U.S. Information Super Highway stands as only one model of information infrastructure support integrating research, educational, and economic development goals. However, as other countries continue, or begin, defining their public policy concerning national information infrastructure, they might consider some of the early lessons learned from the U.S. NII initiative:

\- Governmental support of basic research in experimental high-performance networks appears to be important in maintaining national competitiveness.

\- NII development can focus on applications and services that meet the needs of a broad range of users in education, health care, and manufacturing, and other industries critical to a country's economy. A basic premise of the U.S. NII is universal access for homes, business, public sector agencies and organizations.

\- The NII should build a nation's human capital. For example, the U.S. “Nation-wide Connectivity Program” will subsidize connection of public schools, community colleges, libraries, museums, agricultural extension programs and community learning programs to allow broad levels of utilization by the members of society.

\- Joint ventures, consortiums, and strategic partnerships are important in risk sharing and capitalization in migrating new technologies and applications to a commercial NII marketplace. For example, a joint government, university and commercial partnership has been proposed to develop a new generation of user-friendly software to make navigating the network easier and more productive.

\- Ultimately, a competitive, market-based approach should provide a powerful mechanism for organizing the technologies that make up a national information infrastructure. However, the decision to move the NII investment from public to private initiatives is not easy to make. In the case of the U.S., there are still questions of demand, pricing, and ultimate benefit. The events that unfold over the next ten years should provide a valuable case study to facilitate better understanding of national to private sector infrastructure technology transfer.

\- Governments may have to continue to provide direct subsidy to research and educational users. These funds may be carefully targeted to meet societal needs by those end-user communities that can not afford to take advantage of the benefits that the infrastructure can provide.

\- Government should encourage interconnectivity and interoperability with an NII through its involvement with licensing and standards bodies. This will encourage alternative network suppliers to connect seamlessly with one another, resulting in a wide array of competitive, commercial choices. Ultimately, the NII should have improved reliability and operating standards similar to the traditional telecommunication services today.

\- In general, government and industry should strive for a framework that promotes fair and open competition, encourages innovation and allows for effective participation among all.
