# Can information technology improve managerial problem finding?

Lutfus Sayeed $^{a,*}$ , Harvey J. Brightman $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business and Economics, P.O. Box 6025, West Virginia University, Morgantown, WV 26506-6025, USA $^{b}$ College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

## Abstract

The study investigated the use and effectiveness of computerized information and communications support in 90 problem and opportunity finding episodes within the banking industry. In 76 problem finding episodes, branch managers used some form of computerized information and communications support. The descriptive data suggested that branch managers frequently use Management Information Systems and Transactions Processing Systems reports to identify both operational and strategic problems. When managers used computerized information or communications support tools to find potential problems or opportunities, they (1) found potential problems and opportunities in a more timely fashion, (2) used inputs from a larger number of departments, and (3) sought information from more sources. Surprisingly, when compared to non-computerized support, computerized information or communications support did not produce more accurate problem finding. The study suggests organizational and managerial practices that can enhance information support of problem finding. Finally, the potential for additional research is presented.

Keywords: Impact; Decision making; Problem finding; MIS/DSS; CMCS; Diffusion; Benchmarking

## 1. Introduction

Several researchers have suggested that computer technology affects organizational and managerial decision making $[16,23,41,56]$ . Recently, Huber $[24]$ hypothesized several testable propositions about the impact of information technology (IT) – information and communications support tools – on decision making. Huber suggested that computer-assisted information and communications support should improve decision making.

The present study investigates the impact of IT on the first phase of decision making – the intelligence, or problem and opportunity finding, phase, an area in need of significant improvement [26]. Intelligence includes sensing, defining, or clarifying potential problems and opportunities by an individual manager.

The present study is important because of three reasons. First, organizations and individual managers must engage in intelligence activities $[2,12,24]$ . Organizational intelligence is a proactive decision making approach by organizations that results in early problem detection, reduces environmental uncertainty, improves decision making, and provides a competitive advantage in the market [15,55]. Organizations must constantly seek cues that indicate unfolding problems and opportunities deserving managerial attention. Several researchers mention organizational intelligence as the first phase of organizational decision making [12,14,17,20,22,26,27,33,36,47]. Daft and Weick [14] assert that individual managers perform organizational intelligence activities. Therefore, one way to study organizational intelligence is to investigate the individual manager's intelligence activities [26,33], an essential managerial function [25,29,30].

Second, there have been few empirical studies on managerial problem and opportunity finding. The research on managerial problem finding can be categorized into three areas: (1) untested theoretical models $[11,26,49,50]$ , (2) empirically tested a-priori models $[32–34]$ , and (3) models derived from empirical findings $[39,40,43]$ . Except for Nutt $[40]$ , the empirical studies were done prior to the information technology revolution of the 1980s. Furthermore, Nutt did not address the effects of IT on managerial intelligence. The present study adds to the earlier research by investigating the impact of information technology on managerial problem finding.

Third, previous research has not addressed the impact of IT on managerial problem finding though several studies have investigated the effect of decision support systems on decision making $[3,46,48]$ . The present study tests Huber's $[24]$ theoretical assertions that IT enhances the four intelligence success measures of (1) timeliness, (2) accuracy, (3) comprehensiveness, and (4) availability. Huber's propositions address four possible, though not exhaustive, impacts of IT on managerial intelligence.

Following Huber's [24] lead, we distinguish between IT tools that support information processing and communications in organizations. Information support refers to computer-based applications to support structured and unstructured decision processes. Communications support refers to computer or telecommunication-based applications for communication purposes. Huber drew the distinction between these IT tools because he hypothesized that the effects of information support on decision making may be different than those of communications support on decision making.

We tested the following four hypotheses based on Huber's assertions.

H1: Compared to non-computerized support, the use of information support and communications support generates more timely managerial intelligence.

H2: Compared to non-computerized support, the use of information support and communications support generates more accurate managerial intelligence.

H3: Compared to non-computerized support, the use of information support and communications support generates more comprehensive managerial intelligence.

H4: Compared to non-computerized support, the use of information support and communications support generates more available managerial intelligence.

This study also addresses three exploratory questions not raised by Huber:

1. How frequently do managers use IT to find potential problems and opportunities?

2. Does the frequency and level of IT support differ for operational and strategic problems?

3. What IT applications enhance problem/opportunity finding?

We raise these exploratory questions because the answers will be helpful in promoting effective use of IT in problem and opportunity finding which will be a major function of IS departments in the 1990s [57].

## 2. The study

Branch managers from two large (Fortune 500) commercial banks in the Atlanta metropolitan area participated in the study. The banks' assets were 30 billion dollars and 50 billion dollars respectively [52]. One year before the study, one bank had merged with another bank of similar asset size while a national holding company had acquired the second bank.

We selected branch managers for two reasons: 1) a pilot study confirmed that they used a range of information and communications support tools, and 2) all worked within the same industry and had similar management responsibilities. By selecting only bank branch managers we minimized the impact of two major extraneous variables – industry classification and the nature of managerial responsibilities. However, selecting branch managers may limit the generalizability of our findings. Thirty (15 from each bank) of 36 branch managers who were contacted by mail participated in the study. The sample which included 14 women had an average of 17 years of banking experience.

Each interview lasted about 45 minutes to an hour and was audio taped when permitted (28 managers allowed the interviews to be taped). After responding to clarification questions, the interviewer asked the manager to describe how s(he) had sensed and ultimately resolved two to four potential problems or opportunities within the past six months. The interviewer allowed the manager to speak freely, probing only intermittently for necessary clarification. The respondents used documents such as office memos, loan request applications, letters exchanged, computer printouts, computer screens etc. in helping them accurately recollect past events. Altogether, we obtained data on 90 completed episodes. The episodes included detecting bank fraud, granting credit to commercial and individual clients, meeting commercial credit goals for the branch, restructuring existing operations, personnel problems, etc. Some episodes will be described later in this paper.

After obtaining a detailed description of the problem or opportunity, the interviewer asked the branch manager to evaluate each detected problem or opportunity along intelligence timeliness and accuracy. The other intelligence success measures – comprehensiveness and availability – were determined from the interview transcripts. Previously, researchers did not propose any problem finding success measures. While Huber [24] is the first to suggest these success measures, we had to develop operational definitions for Huber had not provided these. Future research may further refine our operational definitions.

Despite some limitations discussed later, Mintzberg et al. [39] recommended interviewing as the most suitable data collection method for studying decision making activities. Further, all previous studies on problem finding have used interviews where subjects recollected past problem finding activities $[26,33,40]$ . Therefore, the present study is consistent with methodologies of previous studies.

## 2.1. Dependent variables: managerial intelligence success measures

Timeliness. We defined timeliness as the approximate length of time between sensing a problem and its actual onset. Timeliness was treated as a three level categorical variable. 'Far in advance' meant that the manager sensed the potential problem early allowing sufficient time to develop a well thought-out action plan. 'Just in time' meant that the potential problem was about to occur and thus an action plan was hastily developed. 'Rather late' meant that the problem had blindsided the branch manager and (s)he would have to solve the problem "on the fly."

Accuracy. We defined accuracy as the degree to which a suspected potential problem or opportunity was correctly identified. Accuracy was treated as a three level categorical variable. 'Accurate' meant that the manager's potential problem assessment totally matched the subsequent problem. 'Somewhat accurate' meant that the manager's assessment partially matched the subsequent problem. 'Not accurate' meant that the manager's assessment did not match the subsequent problem.

Since timeliness and accuracy are subjective evaluations, the respondent's assessments were necessary as the problem finder, i.e., the respondent, is the best judge of these intelligence success measures. At the same time, the respondents could bias the data by providing socially desirable responses while assessing timeliness and accuracy. We checked for this possibility before drawing any conclusions from analysing these two variables. The data analysis section will elaborate on this.

Comprehensiveness and availability. Comprehensiveness and availability were determined from the interview transcripts and the interviewer did not reveal to the respondents that these variables were intelligence success measures. Therefore, these measures were less subject to the socially desirable response bias. We defined comprehensiveness as the number of different departments involved in the problem sensing episode. Different departments often have different views of the same problem [1]. We defined availability as the number of information sources accessed in sensing and clarifying the potential problem or opportunity. Information sources were human (customers, coworkers, consultants etc.) or non-human (databases, computer models, documents etc.). Comprehensiveness and availability are interval-scaled variables.

## 2.2. Independent variables: levels of information and communications support

To test Huber's four hypotheses, we identified different levels of information and communications support. We classified information support along the Gorry and Scott Morton's taxonomy [21]. The level of computer-generated information support was treated as a three level categorical variable. The ‘none’ support level meant that the branch manager did not use computerized information support to sense a problem. Examples are letters from colleagues or supervisors, and office memos or reports not generated and distributed by the computer. The ‘low’ support level included systems that support structured decision making – TPS or MIS. TPS and MIS reports included deposits, withdrawals, commercial accounts, non-commercial accounts, delinquent accounts, commercial and non-commercial loans, teller outages, comparison of budget expenses versus actual expenses, internal economic projections about the region, competitor banks' positions and strategies, etc. The ‘high’ support level included in-house developed information systems that support unstructured decision making – decision support or executive information systems. One DSS model helped managers design their own investment packages for depositors with deposits over \$50,000. It also provided a spreadsheet for quick ‘what if’ analysis. One EIS feature included information about commercial loan targets of the branch/area for a period and actual loans made by the branch/area. When managers used more than one information support level, we recorded the highest level of information support. In summary, the 'low' and 'high' levels involved some form of computer support while the 'none' level did not.

The communications support field does not have a taxonomy equivalent to Gorry and Scott Morton's. Thus we used an ad-hoc classification scheme. The independent variable, level of computer-based communications support, was treated as a three level categorical variable. We classified computer mediated communications systems (CMC) and the on-line systems as the 'high' support level. CMC refers to a broad range of communications systems that (1) allow distribution of text-based and voice-based messages among groups of people, (2) facilitate a group of people to simulate a conference through terminals, or (3) let users append or post messages on a shared file [53]. CMC includes the electronic mail, the voice mail, the computer conferencing, the computerized bulletin boards and facsimile. The online systems were transaction databases linked through communication media and therefore, were classified as 'high' communications support level. At several branches of both banks each branch manager had on-line access to at least two external transaction databases for evaluating commercial (Dunn and Bradstreet) and individual (TRW or Equifax) credit. Desktop personal computers provided access to a network connecting the branches under one area manager. The area managers had access to a network connecting all area managers in the city. We classified non-computer but automated modes such as the telephone and PBX (it was not computerized at either bank at the time of the study) as the 'low' support level. The 'none' support level included regular mail, face to face conversation, and office memos distributed manually. When managers used more than one communications support level, we recorded the highest support level used. In summary, the 'high' level of communications support involved some form of computer support while the other two communications levels did not.

## 2.3. Covariates

Although industry and management responsibility had been eliminated as potential covariates through the choice of branch managers, we included three other covariates – type of problem (strategic vs. operational), organization size, and the years of experience of the manager.

Strategic problems are more complex than operational problems. Strategic problem finding may require more managerial time and information, thus affecting the four success measures. Problems with day to day or short term impacts are operational, and problems with long term impacts are strategic in nature [6]. Two judges, the senior author and a graduate business student, independently categorized each problem episode as either strategic or operational. Cohen's Kappa [9], an inter-judge reliability index, of 0.99 indicated strong agreement between the judges.

Different size firms may deal with problems of differing complexity or provide different levels of skill training to its employees. The number of employees in the branch represented firm size. Also more experienced managers may outperform less experienced managers. Managerial experience was recorded as the number of years in banking.

## 3. Data analysis

The present study included three exploratory research questions and four hypotheses. We analyzed the first two exploratory questions using frequency counts and narrative descriptions of some problem finding episodes. For the third exploratory question, we followed Miles and Huberman's [37] recommendations and reviewed interview transcripts seeking descriptions of managers using IT which matched theoretical descriptions of effective problem finding [26]. Huber's four hypotheses were analyzed separately using quantitative techniques, namely, log-linear analysis and analysis of variance. The dependent variables were the intelligence success measures. The independent variables were the levels of information and communications support.

3.1. Exploratory question 1: how frequently do managers use IT to find potential problems and opportunities?

Table 1 cross-classifies the 90 problem finding episodes over three levels of information and communications support. Table 1 shows that managers used IT in 76 of the 90 episodes (84%). Automated communications support (telephone and PBX) and TPS- or MIS-based information support were the most common form of support (27%). The second most common combination of IT support was on-line or CMC-based communications support and TPS or MIS-based support (22%). In 14 episodes, managers used no computerized information and communications support.

Following is a brief description of two problem finding episodes that represent the most commonly-used combinations of IT support.

Low Information Support and Low Communications Support. A long-standing customer approached a different branch to open a new commercial account. The branch manager reviewed the summarized reports of the customer's transactions over the last three months (a MIS report – low information support), and talked to the manager of the customer's previous branch over the telephone (low communications support). The reports indicated a slowdown in the customer's cash flow. Also, the manager of the previous branch stated that the customer had recently requested an extension of his credit line but the branch had rejected his application because of low cash flow. The branch manager subsequently refused to open the commercial account.

3.2. Exploratory question 2: does the frequency and level of IT support differ for operational and strategic problems?

Two judges, the senior author and a graduate business student, independently categorized each problem episode as either strategic or operational using Brightman's [6] definitions. Cohen's Kappa [9], an inter-judge reliability index, of 0.99 indicated strong agreement between the judges.

Table 2 indicates that strategic problems more frequently required computer-based information support than operational problems. Fifteen of the 16 strategic problems (94%) required computer-based information support. Only 57 of the 74 operational problems (77%) required computer-based information support. While the respective sample sizes are too small to use statistical methods, the difference appears worthy of further study. The frequency of computer-based communications support did not differ between operational and strategic problems.

An unexpectedly high percentage of strategic problems (five out of 16) were identified without any automated or computerized communications support. In all five episodes, managers became aware of the strategic problem or opportunity during area and branch managers' meetings. For example, an area manager informed the branch managers about the potential opportunities in commercial accounts resulting from the impending bankruptcy of the Bank of Credit Commerce and International.

## 3.3. Exploratory question 3: what IT applications enhance problem / opportunity finding?

To improve intelligence activities, Kiesler and Sproull [26] suggested that managers and organizations should (1) plan, (2) learn from experience, (3) increase the speed with which they receive information, and (4) increase the range of information they receive. During data analysis, we sought IT applications that promoted these four activities.

IT Applications Promoting Better Planning. We found five IT applications that helped managers and the organizations plan effectively. First, a recently installed in-house system (developed by the acquiring firm) provided a database of calls made by branch managers, under an area manager, to potential commercial clients who bank with competitors. Branch managers shared the database through an electronic bulletin board. The database recorded the date and a brief description of the calls, and allowed the area managers to monitor progress of potential commercial accounts.

Second, an EIS provided branch managers with comparisons of actual and targeted commercial loan amounts by the branches under one area manager for multiple time periods. This IT application helped branch managers decide on how aggressively they should pursue commercial accounts in the future. Besides these IT applications, three MIS and TPS reports were also useful in planning. These included: 1) exception reports for withdrawals in excess of \$10,000, 2) outage reports for variations between the teller's end of day cash and the actual cash on hand, and 3) variance reports that compared budgeted and actual expenses.

IT Applications that Help Managers Learn from Experience. Kiting, a major fraud in commercial banking, involves frequently ‘floating’ funds among two or more checking accounts in different branches or banks and withdrawing the funds after concentrating them in one account. Two months after the installation of an electronic bulletin board, a branch manager used e-mail to inform other branch managers in the same area about potential kiting suspect(s). This timely communication led to early detection of several fund transfer frauds. As a result, the area managers formalized the practice by daily posting suspicious account numbers on the bulletin board and sharing the information with other area managers through an electronic bulletin board. This practice has significantly reduced kiting. Other banks are presently discussing implementing similar bulletin boards.

Formal training sessions and newsletters from IS departments also help managers learn to apply IT tools in intelligence activities. Subsequent to the merger and acquisition in both banks, the branch employees were formally trained on a new in-house information system containing DSS and EIS capabilities and a communications network for the managers. Also, IS departments in both banks started publishing a monthly newsletter containing changes and new uses of the information systems since the organizational changes. Several branch managers mentioned that the newsletter and training were helpful in using the recently installed systems. Also, the newsletter publicized the accidental discovery described above. Therefore, formal training and newsletters indirectly promote effective uses of IT tools in intelligence activities by providing training and relevant information on recently installed systems.

IT Applications That Increase the Speed of Information. The exceptions report flagging larger than \$10,000 withdrawals, the monitoring of kiting through the e-mail/electronic bulletin board, and the on-line transactions databases for credit evaluations have significantly increased the speed of information in intelligence activities. These systems and reports allow branch managers to investigate potential problems or opportunities more quickly, before they become crises.

IT Applications That Increase the Range of Information. The DSS feature in the recently installed in-house systems in both banks and the computer networks connecting area/branch managers have increased the range of information in intelligence activities. The DSS provides managers with competitors' interest rates from the previous day. The systems also provide spreadsheet features for quick 'what-if' analysis. These features increase the range of information in problem/opportunity finding. Also, the computerized networks make it easier for area and branch managers to share intelligence information with each other.

Table 4 shows that the level of information support variable is significant (p < 0.0335). Huber's timeliness hypothesis is partially supported. The parameter estimates of the log-linear model indicated that overall, the branch managers were 2.35 times more likely to find problems 'far in advance' than either 'just in time' or 'rather late'. Branch managers were 4.75 times more likely to find problems 'far in advance' than 'just in time' or 'rather late' when using computerized information support. Branch managers were only 1.16 times more likely to find problems 'far in advance' than 'just in time' or 'rather late' when they did not use computerized information support. In conclusion, the log-linear model showed that use of computerized information support was associated with a fourfold increase (4.75 vs. 1.16) in intelligence timeliness.

We were concerned about the possibility of socially desirable responses biasing our data for the first two dependent variables - accuracy and timeliness. Table 3 shows that respondents rated problem sensing timeliness as "rather late" in only nine of 90 episodes. Therefore responses on timeliness may contain socially biased responses.

## 4. Discussion

Our findings indicate that:

1. Branch managers frequently use computerized information and communications support in their intelligence activities.

2. Branch managers use MIS-based and TPS-based reports to find both strategic and operational problems.

3. IT applications enhance managerial intelligence by promoting planning, learning from experience and quick information access from a large number of sources.

4. Huber's four propositions were partially supported.

Frequency of IT Use. From Table 1, branch managers used computerized information and communications support to find problems and opportunities in 84% (76) of the episodes. This occurred because many daily tasks of branch managers, such as credit evaluation, communication with area and other branch managers, designing investment packages, following key banking indexes, etc. – have been computerized. However, in 14 of 90 episodes managers sensed problems and opportunities without computerized information and communications support. Informal sources that include gossip, lunch conversations, personal contacts inside and outside the organization, trade shows etc. are still important [38]. A branch manager uncovered a long-standing disciplinary problem with one teller in a walk-about – 'management by walking around' [42]. Therefore, managerial problem finding will always include information from non-computerized sources even in highly computerized environments.

IT Use in Strategic and Operational Problem Finding. From Table 2, MIS or TPS reports were most often used for sensing both operational and strategic problems. Telephone or PBX were most often used for sensing operational problems. However, managers frequently (31%) used no computerized or automated communication support for strategic problems or opportunities.

Though based on only 16 strategic problem/opportunity finding episodes, managers received information from group meetings with other branch and area managers in five of these episodes. The problems/opportunities involved customer concerns about the bank's name change, bankruptcy of a competitor, marketing plans based on a market survey for introduction of an upscale product, the need to generate additional commercial loans, and ways to extend new services to current account holders above a certain deposit level. Given the nature of these problems/opportunities, formal meetings are an effective communication medium because face-to-face meetings provide richest communication cues [13].

IT Applications That Enhance Managerial Intelligence. Analysis of the third exploratory question generated several insights. First, organizations must establish work practices and routine reports that promote managerial intelligence through IT use. Monitoring of daily withdrawal transactions through an exceptions report helped find potential problems or opportunities with customers' existing portfolios. The report on teller outages identified problems with a teller's daily cash accounting. The monthly report comparing allocated and actual expenses helped detect potential budget problems. Detection of problem cues – large withdrawal, large or persistent teller outages and large actual expenses – activated organizational actions, which are consistent with Kiesler and Sproull's [26] description of problem sensing.

Second, as users become familiar with the computerized systems, they accidentally discover new ways to find problems and opportunities. Discovering the use of e-mail to prevent kiting is an example. This reinforces Franz, Robey and Koeblitz [19], and Markus and Robey's [35] argument that the impacts of information systems evolve over time. Organizational and individual work practices interact to produce unforeseen IT applications. In the present study, an individual branch manager used e-mail to prevent bank fraud and this practice subsequently became a standard operating procedure.

Third, the above example also indicates that organizations can ‘benchmark’ a successful managerial practice by transforming an accidental discovery into a standard operating procedure [7]. Further, this example illustrates how organizational intelligence sometimes develops from an individual manager's intelligence activity and becomes permanent in organizational memory [14].

Fourth, formal support of users through IS newsletters and training help managerial intelligence. Both banks had been acquired, and thus were of sufficient size to provide more formal IS support such as periodic newsletters and formal training sessions $[31]$ . Such formalization of user support by IS departments appeared to help problem and opportunity finding. Further, careful planning of IT implementation activities can promote problem and opportunity finding by including training on IT features helpful in managerial intelligence $[44]$ .

Fifth, computerized information and communications support provides a larger organizational memory [24], and improves intelligence information storage and retrieval – two essential problem finding facilities [26]. The database of branch managers' calls to potential customers enlarged organizational memory and improved storage and retrieval of intelligence information.

Finally, computerized information and communications support can incorporate external information useful in managerial intelligence. For example, in-house systems at both banks contained information about interest rates competitors offered on the previous business day. Though incorporation of external information in IT is difficult $[5]$ , it can be done, and is useful in problem and opportunity finding.

Evaluation of Huber's Propositions. The results do not provide evidence for Huber's assertion about the impacts of IT on intelligence accuracy. Even without considering the possibility of socially desirable responses, there was no support for a positive technological impact on intelligence accuracy.

Computerized information support enhances intelligence timeliness. Though the possibility of socially desirable responses weakens the positive finding on timeliness, the results are consistent with the third exploratory question discussed above. It indicated that organizational practices built around IT applications and MIS- or TPS-based reports enhance managerial intelligence timeliness. For example, the monitoring of daily withdrawal transactions through an exceptions report, increases the speed of detecting potential problems with an existing account. Also, the use of e-mail to prevent kiting is effective because branch managers can share information about suspicious accounts quickly and at the right time.

The findings on comprehensiveness and availability are convincing since socially desirable responses did not weaken these measures. In addition to partially supporting Huber's assertions, statistically significant findings on comprehensiveness and availability show that IT provided a wide range of information in problem finding as recommended by Kiesler and Sproull (the third exploratory question). Almost one more department provided input for potential problem finding when computer assisted communications support was used. Also, managers used one more information source when they used computerized information support in finding a potential problem or opportunity.

## 4.1. Limitations of the study

The study has three limitations: 1) it was conducted at only two banks, 2) interview data were used, and 3) the accuracy and timeliness dependent variables were subject to the socially desirable response bias.

First, the respondents worked in highly automated environments of two Fortune 500 banks. The findings are based on problem and opportunity finding in such environments. Also, both banks had undergone recent organizational changes.

Second, Mintzberg et al. [39] noted that data collected through interviews may contain two types of errors – distortion and memory failure. The present study minimized these errors by focusing on problem finding episodes in the recent past. However, recollection recency does not eliminate such errors altogether.

Third, the face to face interview may cause the interviewees to respond in a socially desirable manner. Given the large number of ‘accurate’ and ‘far in advance’ responses, it is possible that some respondents exaggerated their success levels while recalling the accuracy and timeliness of problem finding.

To overcome the study's limitations, researchers should conduct a longitudinal study in different environments and adopt a “process” approach, that is, collect qualitative and longitudinal data [35]. Such a methodology will better facilitate observing impacts that evolve from prolonged use of IT.

## 5. Conclusions and recommendations

How can the findings from the present study enhance managerial and organizational intelligence? Cougar [10] suggests that organizations can enhance intelligence activities through IS planning. We recommend the following three IS planning practices.

First, IS departments must identify and computerize routine intelligence activities; that is, where the relevant problem stimuli are well developed and interpretation is straightforward [26]. Pounds' [43] suggestions may be helpful in identifying the nature and type of stimuli. He reported that managers find problems when they detect deviations from four organizational and mental models – historical, planning, extra-organizational or other people's. Of these four models, problem stimuli are usually most well developed for historical and planning models.

Second, IS departments must encourage and disseminate innovative uses of IT in problem finding. Camp [7] suggests that “benchmarking” of successful managerial practices adds to organizational effectiveness. Formal training sessions and IS newsletters can publicize and motivate innovative intelligence practices using IT.

Third, IS departments must acknowledge that not all problem sensing can or should be computerized. The findings suggest that (1) some intelligence activities will be impossible to computerize (e.g., “management by walking around”) and (2) some intelligence activities should not be computerized. When organizations seek to uncover strategic problems or opportunities, face-to-face meetings may be the best way to communicate. Computerized communications can filter out social context cues such as nonverbal information [51], and may dilute the emphasis organizations must place on strategic opportunities.

We recommend two directions for future research: 1) examine the impact of IT on organizational intelligence rather than individual managerial intelligence, and 2) examine the impact of IT on the two remaining phases of decision making, namely, design and choice. First, more research is needed to analyze how IT can enhance organizational intelligence. Daft and Weick [14] defined organizational intelligence as consisting of three interactive stages – scanning (data collection), interpretation (data given meaning) and learning (action taken). Future research should address how IT can improve the three stages at the organizational level. Second, future research should address the impact of IT on the design and choice phases of decision making. Several researchers have suggested that computer technology affects organizational and managerial decision making [16,24,41,56]. Findings from the studies on impacts of IT in the intelligence, design and choice phases can lead to designing and implementing effective systems.

Finally, researchers and practitioners have argued for a better fit between organizational strategy and IT $[8,45,54]$ . Supporting organizational intelligence through IS planning and development activities will be one way to achieve a better fit.
