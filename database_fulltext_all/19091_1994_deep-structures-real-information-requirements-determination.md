# Deep structures: Real information requirements determination

Richard Leifer ${}^{a,*}$ , Sunro Lee ${}^{b}$ , Jeffrey Durgee ${}^{a}$

$^{a}$ School of Management, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, NY 12180, USA $^{b}$ Department of BIS, School of Business and Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## Abstract

We argue that a class of information is missing in the traditional ways of performing information requirements determination (IRD) activities. This “deep structure” information consists of the values, beliefs and unwritten rules in an organization. Previous research shows that failure to identify this is one of the reasons why information systems (IS) fail. Here, therefore, focus group methodology is suggested as a preprocess for IRD processes for uncovering latent information. Further, in order to see how effective the focus group method is for eliciting deep structure information, a laboratory experiment was conducted. The results suggest, first, that deep structure information is qualitatively different from surface structure information and second, that information gathered using the focus group methodology contains more deep structure information than that using traditional interviews. Implications are discussed.

Keywords: Requirement analysis; Deep structure information; Focus group

## 1. Introduction

The information requirements determination (IRD) process is the single most important phase in the systems development life cycle (SDLC), because it is in this phase that the problem is defined and the scope of the analysis is set. Thus, if the IRD is incorrect, the resultant system will be incorrect. Requirements determination or specification refers to the rather tedious task of explicitly identifying, cataloguing, and documenting systems' requirements. It has been well accepted by researchers and practitioners alike, that in order to construct successful MIS, accurate, relevant, reliable and valid information about the phenomena being modeled must be obtained from those who are the users of the system.

Typical of the techniques for requirements specifications or fact finding are: existing documentation, forms and files, observation of the work environment, questionnaires, and interviews $[14,37]$ . These techniques yield categories of information about: the organization (goals, structure, policies, objectives of functional units), people (authority and responsibility relationships, job duties, interpersonal relationships, information needs), work (tasks and work flows, methods, procedures, schedules, etc.), and the work environment (physical arrangements, resources available) $[35]$ .

One reason for the dissatisfaction by organizational stakeholders with their IS derives from the problem of specifications not being stated accurately and/or completely $[8]$ , and the problem of unstable requirement specifications due to the future information needs $[21]$ . Communication obstacles primarily occur because of cognitive limitations of humans as information processors, the lack of common language between systems analysts and users, and diverse interests of multiple users $[39]$ . In addition, although the user and the analyst may understand the sequence of words and their surface level of meanings, there may be misunderstanding about implications or unobtrusive meanings. Thus, there are two problems: users may not be able to state their needs or why they do what they do, because they may not be aware of them $[1]$ , and the words that are used by users to describe their world may not be understood because they are a dialect not fully comprehended by the systems developers.

It should be no surprise that techniques of gathering information determine the kinds of information gathered. Due to the traditional techniques, this information is generally characterized as the surface features of the organizational functions, rather than deep features, such as underlying norms, beliefs, and value judgement. But, such deep features tend to reflect users/analysts' ontological assumptions about the systems, but they are critical for successful implementation of IS [15]; hence latent means which implicitly guide the organizational functions should be uncovered during the IRD stage and captured in the system [38].

Our contention, therefore, is that there is a class of knowledge that is not obtained, because current methodologies are not designed to elicit it. This class can be revealed by exploring “deep structure” [12] $^{1}$ of organizational members’ language and their implicit meanings, such as values, beliefs, and norms, that are critical to effective organizational functioning and hence IS functioning. Therefore, this paper presents not only an argument for the importance of understanding the deep structure of the organizational level of communications, but also the results of an exploratory study designed to demonstrate the usefulness of both the methodology and the knowledge.

The basic problem addressed, which remains to be solved for MIS or organizational control alike, is how to gain an understanding of the underlying rules that govern organizational behaviour. These, in turn, influence MIS development and use.

## 2. Surface and deep structure control

The conventional view is that rules direct or control organizational behaviour. In order to elicit these rules, we ask people: What rules do you follow in doing your job? and What information do you need in doing it. We also ask them to provide their job descriptions and we ask them what is expected of them. Unfortunately, their answers may not reflect the theories-in-use which actually governs their actions, but espoused theories of actions for certain situation [2]. This indicates that most of the work in an organization goes on without formal rules or directives. It has been estimated that these “residual” activities (e.g., habit, automatic behaviour, scripts or routines) may constitute 80% of a person’s behaviour [33].

Upon what do these habits depend? They can be observed as the actions that take place due to organizational events: they result from the (possibly hidden) values, organizational vocabularies, communication channels, programmed tasks, accepted ways of doing things, interpretations of events, status and prestige symbols, etc. Such mechanisms affect organizational behaviour by limiting information flows, controlling premises of decisions, setting up expectations to highlight some aspects and play down others, and limiting the search for problem solving alternatives. Thus, these processes ensure relatively predictable and consistent behaviour and solutions by shaping and controlling it without reference to conventional rules and commands. In other words, these are latent or unobtrusive means of controlling behaviour. Further, an organization can be defined as a system of social norms, and a complete picture of them defines the essential information flow that should be captured in IS $^{2}$ .

In summary, all organizations utilize formal rules and supervisory direction, but they also depend on the more subtle and unobtrusive controls of jargon, norms, and beliefs. But, these influential yet unobtrusive aspects of the organization are such a function of habit and unquestioning influence that they are relatively difficult to discuss or identify. However, due to their importance, it is incumbent upon systems designers to somehow take them into account when developing IS. The consequence of not doing so is that resulting systems may contradict the unobtrusive aspects of the organization. This leads to users rejecting the system.

## 3. Surface and deep linguistic structure

Users may be aware of their obvious needs (due to surface structure controls) but may not be aware of those emanating from unobtrusive controls. A consequence of this is that users may find it difficult to determine and express their requirements. Furthermore, users define and describe their needs in terms they understand and with their own meanings, which stem from the jargon of the members of the department or organization. However, because the systems designer is generally outside the unit, the words and terms may be ambiguous. The user-analyst communication gap arises due to differences in tasks, responsibilities, education, reporting departments, etc.

Language is used in two senses to represent experience. First, language creates a model of experience based upon our perceptions of the world. Second, we use language to communicate this model to each other [3]. When communicating, we are usually not aware of the process of selecting words to represent our experience nor are we conscious of the way in which we order and structure these words. However, although we have little or no consciousness of the process, it is highly structured. That is, our language is a system, it has structure and there is some set of rules which identify the sequences of words that will make sense. Even though we are not normally aware of the structure in this process of representation and communication, it can be understood in terms of regular patterns by native speakers.

As native speakers of a language, our ability and experience in using our language system to represent and communicate is so extensive that we have consistent intuitions about that process. This behaviour is rule governed behaviour. The difficulty is that although the use of language is rule governed, we may not be conscious of or able to articulate the rules that are used. Extrapolating this to the user/analyst interface suggests that users and analysts are native speakers in different domains. Thus, the analyst may misinterpret or misunderstand the implications of the language spoken by the users.

This is analogous to organizational control. One element of an organizational control system is a set of unobtrusive controls: rules that govern behaviour, the existence and the meaning of which may not be consciously available to those who are governed by the rules. Thus, unobtrusive control rules are knowledge representations of meanings that we attribute to events that guide and shape our behaviour [23]. That is, the unwritten rules of organizational functioning act to guide behaviour by determining and defining appropriate and inappropriate behaviour. These, such as beliefs about technology [17] or about ways of using technology, potentially impact decisions about the kinds of systems to implement in a particular organizational setting.

## 4. Review of IRD processes

Prototyping and its variations have been practised to address issues associated with communication gap, while sociotechnical approaches, soft system methods (SSM), cognitive mapping, and organizational learning methods have been utilized to address the need to elicit deep structure of organizational functioning.

Prototyping methodologies [6] have been advocated for determining more precise user specifications and consequently developing more acceptable systems. However, the criticisms of traditional IRD practices are valid criticisms of prototyping, since iterative prototyping emphasizes design aspects of systems (i.e., mostly user interface design, shallow prototyping) and reduction of the development time due to consideration only of the surface level of user knowledge, rather than the deep structures.

More recently, the concept of group processes, such as cooperative prototyping $[7]$ or software storming $[19]$ , has been incorporated into software development. These approaches help resolve conflict at the surface level by sharing diverse functional knowledge. They also improve system functionality and usability by having prototypes that are practically tested, thereby maintaining the idea of work-like evaluation rather than a shallow prototyping, and contribute to better integration with other subsystems. A major difference between cooperative prototyping and software storming is, however, that (unlike cooperative prototyping, where users actively participate in the analysis and design phase) software storming is performed amongst system developers on narrowly focused problems. It therefore tends to neglect the negotiation process with users whose goals and objectives may be different.

Sociotechnical approaches claim that the information system has to be compatible with the surrounding social system, accounting for prevailing social values and norms and elusive social needs (e.g., [8]). However, such values and norms are not clearly represented in the surface structure, but obscured and latent in the deep structure. Participative design method (i.e., ETHICS) [32] is intended to meet such a need by facilitating mutual learning among users and analysts through joint change analysis. ISAC proposed by Lundberg et al. [25] also views analysis and design of IS as a communication and learning process but is more geared toward utilizing local/tacit knowledge about activities.

More significantly, Soft Systems Methods (SSM) [10,11] emphasizes a collective interpretation of phenomena. By strengthening participation and learning, SSM provides stakeholders with opportunities to explore subjective issues, such as value judgements and statements of beliefs, as well as objective issues, such as formal procedures, functions, and subsequent information flows. Similarly, Salaway [36] applied organizational learning method to information systems development in order to increase the effectiveness of user/analyst interactions. Lyytinen [26] points out that sociotechnical and learning approaches tend to lack specific strategies for both resolving conflict and structuring processes.

In ill-structured problems, a cognitive mapping technique has been utilized for deep knowledge, such as causal relationships among critical decision factors $[30]$ . The utility of deep knowledge has been demonstrated for stabilizing expert systems, which are often brittle $[4]$ . The cognitive mapping approach assumes that problems are already identified by users, and that analysts can easily derive causal structure (knowledge) from users about the problem area. This assumption may not always be true and may lead to type III errors caused by solving the wrong problem.

In sum, as shown in Table 1, several methods have been developed for IRD. Each supports different activities during the IRD or design phase and each can play complementary roles for one another, depending on the characteristics of the information required. The social context of IS has long been considered important for their successful implementation, and a learning process has been suggested as a critical stepping stone. But, the importance of linguistic deep structures is yet to be recognized. Also, if language and behaviour are partially controlled by unconscious rules under deep structures, and if the task of the analyst or knowledge engineer is to elicit those rules in order to model behaviour, then strategies (methodologies) for derivation and understanding of deep structures need to be developed and should be studied before structuring data/control flows.

It therefore seems that there is a need for a methodology that can provide deep structures. A viable and proven method for tapping deep structures and unobtrusive rules is the focus group technique, often used in marketing research [9].

## 5. Eliciting deep structure information and unobtrusive rules

Traditional IRD methods may be appropriate for eliciting surface structure information: it is conscious, available, and observable. However, they cannot tap deep structure information. However, deep knowledge may be made available for discussion via a group based, facilitator run meeting called a focus group $[31]^{3}$ . Since the analysts are not “native speakers” of the particular group or organization of users, the process reveals the unobtrusive rules, as well as the meanings attributed to the language.

Recently, the method has been applied to information systems design and showed some benefits, such as for eliciting better original responses from experts in the case of knowledge acquisition for expert systems $[29]$ and more appropriate (relevant) information in the case of the interface design for data base management systems $[27]$ . In the context of IRD, however, potential contributions have yet to be demonstrated.

The focus or group depth interview is a loosely structured interview conducted by a trained moderator among a small number of respondents simultaneously [20]. The technique has its origins in group therapy methods, as used by psychiatrists; its premise (from an MIS perspective) is that it is important to listen to the way that users describe their needs and situation in their own vernacular and from their own perspective. It emphasizes flexibility and the discovery of the unexpected, with a free flowing group discussion bounded by set of topics discussed in a preinterview. There is typically a synergism and snowballing effect, where the group will produce insights and information unavailable to them individually.

Participants or respondents should be members of the organization and user representatives. The setting should encourage a relaxed feeling, where informal and spontaneous comments are encouraged. The typical focus group interview lasts $1\frac{1}{2}$ to 2 hours; time is needed to establish rapport with respondents and explore in depth their beliefs, feelings, ideas, and insights.

The moderator's role is critical: to maintain a high degree of interaction among the participants and to excise to irrelevant discussion. For most applications, the typical systems analyst is not trained for this task; focus group training or a trained focus group moderator should be used.

The objective of IRD using focus groups is for analysts to understand user needs from their perspectives. It is the interactions of the respondents that results in a shared knowledge of the world: the meeting becomes a discussion of deep structure material, with the analyst able to interpret it. The resulting data are then subject to verification and clarification in subsequent discussions.

## 6. Typology of knowledge types and task characteristics

Both surface and deep structure information are necessary for adequate IRD modelling. Figure 1 showed when surface and deep structure information are necessary. Surface structure knowledge is largely concerned with how activities or decisions are accomplished: how transactions are processed, how information is transmitted and to whom, or, in general, how operations are accomplished. This requires traditional techniques of interviewing, observation, records, or questionnaire. Deep structure knowledge is concerned with why activities or decisions are accomplished the way they are. This involves understanding the right way to perform tasks or the agreed upon values. Such agreements describe why the organization performs its tasks in the particular manner. It also suggests that there are basically two types of task activities: task specific, such as routines for processing orders or requests for service, and task generic, such as understanding the systems for handling employee grievances, processing checks, or dealing with customers. ## 7. Systems analysis and design project study

## 7.1. Methodology and hypotheses

A semester long course in Systems Analysis and Design used the Valley Industries case from $[37]$ . This case has several parts, including a good description of the organization, the people, the situation, jobs, problems, etc. It has a number of parts illustrating various aspects of the system design life cycle. The case materials also include a set of questions to be used for interviews with relevant personnel as well as data presumably gathered from them. In addition, company records, background, and operating information was also given.

Role playing was used to add a part involving a focus group session as additional materials. A study to compare information gathered using focus groups with that gathered using company records and interviews was then conducted as part of a class as follows:

1. A set of six students volunteered to role play middle managers of Valley Industries. These students in the systems analysis and design class were intimately involved with the case, as they had just completed feasibility study of it.

2. Each student was given a fictitious role. These covered the major functional responsibilities. Students were told to play the role as if they were actual members of the company.

3. With a trained focus group moderator (the third author) the group met for a little over one hour to discuss the company, its problems, personnel, plans for the future, the work environment, its culture and what was valued in the company. The focus group was videotaped.

4. The video tape was then shown in the systems analysis and design class five days later. All the students (n = 14), including those who had participated in the role playing, completed open ended response forms (see Appendix) designed to elicit information in terms of three levels of knowledge: surface structure, subsurface structure, and deep structure. Each of the three types of knowledge were thoroughly explained before running the videotape. Examples were offered to ensure that all the students understood what was meant by the three levels $^{4}$ .

5. The response forms were randomly divided into two sets. The first set was content analyzed and a provisional categorization determined. Using the categories as criteria, the second set was analyzed using the categories derived from the first set. This was done to test the adequacy of the initial categorization (to ascertain category stability). It provides a rough validity check comparable to the “triangulation” method used in ethnographic research [5], where independent interpretations are compared with each other and with field notes to validate key interpretations.

6. Once it was determined that the categories were stable, the entire sample was pooled and a frequency table was developed for each item at each level. Thus, for the first surface issue, accounts receivable problems, a count was made of the number of times this problem was mentioned across the entire sample. The frequency table was used to identify the count when used as surface, subsurface, or deep structure issue.

7. Information gathered using the focus group methodology were compared to that gained in interview data and company records (from the case).

Guiding the study were the following hypotheses:

H1: Information about Valley Industries gathered using focus groups will have more deep structure material than information gathered using interviews and company records.

H2: Deep structure information will be qualitatively different than surface structure information.

## 7.2. Results

1. A content analysis of the first set of response forms generated by the judges (i.e., class members) were made. Surface structure information issues are concerned with accounts receivable problems, inefficiency of the manual order processing procedures, loss of orders, backlog, lack of on-tine capabilities and poor paper flow. Subsurface structure information issues are concerned with the high growth rate and implications for the organization, job security of bookkeepers when the system is computerized, lack of interaction between departments, high workload and high stress, lack of delegation by the president, and the disorganized and decentralized organization. Deep structure information issues are concerned with the confused culture (good communication but poor cohesion, low morale but high stress, president in charge makes most of the decisions but work is decentralized), management seemingly not concerned with the growth, centralized power, the company as the president's toy, and distribution of power and decision making when a computerized system is implemented.

Based on the above findings, the two hypotheses are supported. Information gathered using the focus group methodology contained deep structure information while the information gathered using traditional interviews did not elicit deep structure information at all. Further, deep structure information was shown to be qualitatively different from surface structure information. These results suggest that focus groups can be an important technique for information requirements determination aspects of systems analysis and design.

## 8. Discussion

Motivating this study was the basic assumption that deep structure information would be helpful in constructing more effective information systems: ones that will more closely fit with critical aspects of the organizational system. This improved organizational validity $[28]$ should yield greater acceptance and use of the developed system. The aim of the study was to show how to obtain deep structure information using the focus group technique. It was also noted that interviews might surface some deep structure information, if those questions were asked, but since there is no group meeting, the analyst would be left with bits and pieces of information rather than a whole.

How this information is used and the difference that it makes in the design of systems remains to be shown. Some writers, however, indicate that systems design is a political process $[28]$ and that successful systems should match the organizational context of use $[22]$ . More recently, Davis et al.'s empirical study $[15]$ showed that lack of deep structure information, such as analysts/developers' theories-in-use, is one of the critical reasons why IS fail.

Apparently, focus group methodology is a viable alternative for eliciting deep structure information. Also, deep structure information is not a substitute for surface structure information, though it is evident that there is an overlap. Surface information from both techniques were consistent, although the scope, depth and detail from traditional IRD techniques was substantially greater.

However, conducting a focus group requires skills and abilities that are not usually in the behavioral repertoire of a systems analyst. Moderating a focus group requires group process leadership and direction of group communication. Further, since the data is qualitative in nature, an ability to interpret ambiguous statements is necessary. One option is to include a focus group moderator (who is not an systems analyst) as part of the systems development project team. As White and Leifer suggest, a successful project team should contain people with a range of skills, especially communications and group process skills [41].

There may also be a problem in the interpretation and understanding of deep structure information. The systems analyst is then like an anthropologist trying to make sense of a foreign culture. How do we know if the interpretations are correct? By using multiple focus groups we can introduce initial interpretations to subsequent sessions for reaction and refinement. Of course, it is also possible to feed finding back to organizational members for corroboration.

Interpreting deep structure information may also put the systems designer in a role conflict situation. Deep structure information may suggest a design inconsistent with technical requirements stemming from the surface information. The conflict is between the role of the technologist (whose goal is to design the system most technically compatible with task requirements and other surface information) with the role of the behaviourist (whose goal is to design the system most compatible with the value and social context of the organization). In order to foster better understanding of designers' roles during systems development, Hirschheim and Klein suggest that it should be necessary for them to explicitly document the underlying assumptions about their design [18]. Realistically, however, the career path of a systems designer suggests that the most technically proficient programmer is promoted to be an analyst. Expecting this person to “compromise” a technical solution for a more organizationally acceptable one, requires a mind set change, notwithstanding the call for an increased business and organizational orientation.

Concern about validity is obviously important. If the focus group technique is used along with other IRD techniques, nomological validity $[13]$ can be demonstrated by analyzing the nature of information resulting from different IRD methods. That is, nomological validity is demonstrated if information is consistent with other information to which it is conceptually related. Further, we recognize the data presented here are somewhat artificial since we have not used a “real” system development situation, nor were the focus group participants real organizational members. In applied situations, perhaps other variations on the focus group theme might emerge, allowing deep structure information to arise.
