Case Study

# Managing information systems within Japanese companies in Europe: An empirical study

Peter T. van den Hoven, Frank P.A. van Valkenburg, Michael S.H. Heng \*

Department of Information Systems, Faculty of Economics, Business Administration and Econometrics, Vrije Universiteit, De Boelelaan 1105, 1081 HV Amsterdam, Netherlands

## Abstract

This paper examines the influence of the culture of Japanese companies in Europe on the information systems (IS) departments of thirteen Japanese companies in Belgium and the Netherlands. Japanese culture is normally considered to involve groupism and perseverance. In the subsidiaries that Japanese companies set up in Europe, much of that culture prevails. Characteristics of these companies are consensus decision making (nemawashi), continuous improvement (kaizen), regular change of president, strong informal communication, loyalty to employees, and lack of written long term plans. The IS departments of the companies are generally characterized by a lack of formal information planning. They are acting reactively only. When the companies introduce Information Planning, and perform it in the typical Japanese way of continuously improving it (Information Planning kaizen), it allows the companies to optimize their IS departments, making them more up-to-date and aware of new opportunities.

Keywords: Information systems; Information planning; Culture; Japan; Europe; Management

## 1. Introduction

For several months we have considered the question: What is the influence of Japanese culture (and management style) on information systems? We conducted an empirical study with thirteen companies in the Netherlands and Belgium to answer this question. However, for reasons of confidentiality, their identify will not be disclosed. The people we interviewed in these companies replied: “I don’t believe that there is an influence” when we explained the purpose of our study. Most also assured us that there was hardly any Japanese influence in their company. At first, these remarks really raised doubts, and we wondered if we had chosen a suitable topic. We persevered, however, and the answers ultimately suggested otherwise. It is interesting to see that people who have been part of a culture for a substantial time cannot review it objectively.

For various reasons Japanese companies have set up subsidiaries in both North America and

Europe. Most people would agree with the proposition that the Japanese have a distinct way of doing business. One can even say that Japanese culture has features which are distinctive from Western culture. Perhaps specific Japanese features have contributed to the success of their companies where-ever they operate and their European (or North American) employees may adjust to working within this culture in the subsidiaries.

All three individual aspects (company culture, information systems, and Japanese culture) have been covered extensively in research and theory. However, the overlap between these area's, especially mutual influence, has hardly been explored. The influence of Japanese national culture on organizational behaviour in Japanese companies is enormous, and culture partly determines the behaviour of individuals within an organization, including the information systems (IS) department. According to Hofstede [2], culture effects several subsystems in organizations such as human resources, automation, marketing, finance and especially accounting:

“Accounting- and information systems are products of a culture, that reflect and record fundamental cultural presuppositions.”

Here we attempt to identify the effect that the culture of Japanese companies in Europe has on the IS department. Hopefully the exercise will allow us to identify opportunities for improvement in the IS department. We ask:

‘In what ways is the information systems department of a Japanese company in Europe influenced by the company culture?’

However, we had to set some restrictions on our research. First, ways to change a company culture will not be examined: attitudes and behaviour can be very important to the information supply, especially when introducing new systems and technologies. However, changing a culture is difficult. Therefore we only give a few suggestions for change in both culture and IS departments. The emphasis is on recording the cultural influence on the IS department within an organization.

Secondly, we did not investigate the total information supply function of organizations: we only deal with the IS departments of the companies, including issues such as automation, system development, outsourcing, communication networks, etc.

## 2. Japanese companies in Europe

The Japanese companies are characterized by strong employee loyalty, horizontal consensus, continuous improvement of products and processes, etc. The bottom line of all these characteristics is twofold: groupism and perseverance. A question arises: “How do Japanese companies maintain these typical cultures in their European subsidiaries?” Our literature study suggested a mixed Japanese – European culture $[8,10]$ . In order to determine how such a mixed culture works in practice, we visited the 13 companies and interviewed people from each organization. We also asked several members of each organization to fill in a questionnaire about organizational culture (see the Appendix). Before starting to describe the cultural characteristics of the companies we first deal with the general differences of the companies we have investigated.

## 2.1. Companies

The 13 companies are all either totally Japanese or majority Japanese owned. They are active in diverse businesses; e.g. automobiles, electronics, computers, office machines, machinery, etc. Some of the companies are competitors, but most do not have much to do with each other. Some have only recently been taken over, and thus Japanese influence was hardly noticeable. Other companies are far more Japanese. Some larger companies that have been operating for several years have many Japanese employees. Thus the organizations vary. They also differ in their openness. Some people we interviewed talked about everything we asked and even gossiped, while others refused to answer some questions. It appears that this depends mainly on the attitude of management on giving information to outsiders.

## 2.2. Management

First, we were interested in those positions which are filled by Japanese and European managers. All companies have a Japanese President. If the company employs a Vice President, he (never she) is Japanese also. Furthermore, almost all companies have several other Japanese in high positions; e.g., on the Board of Directors or just below. In several cases, most of the higher management positions are filled by Japanese. However, this does not mean that all Japanese employees in the companies occupy management positions. In fact, all the companies have several Europeans in management positions. In most cases the number of Europeans in (top) management positions appears to increase with the length of time the company has been established in Europe. In these cases, Japanese top management starting up the company established good communication lines with headquarters in Japan. It enables European managers to take over their places while using the established communication lines and contacts with the managers who have now returned to Japan. In a few companies all important management positions are still occupied by Japanese.

In several companies we noticed a significant use of Japanese ‘shadow managers’ as advisors to European managers at all levels. These have more authority than their European counterpart due to their contact with Japanese top management (or Tokyo). This phenomenon has been discussed by Rehfeld [9]:

"Some people point to the use of ‘shadow managers’ as a clear indication of the lower status of non-Japanese managers. Shadow managers play the role of informally communicating with Tokyo (...) What it really means is informing Tokyo of what is going on and what requests will be made (...) I’ve always experienced this role as a positive one. But I’ve heard that other Japanese companies use shadow managers to relay what amounts to orders from Tokyo through the assistant general manager to the general manager and then to the division, effectively depriving the non-Japanese executive of any real decision-making power."

Apparently the Japanese do not trust westerners in the same way as they trust one another. Trust is the basis for doing business in Japan. In western cultures, however, it is not unusual to break promises, be unreasonable in negotiations and put pressure on business partners. We believe that the Japanese consider westerners to be gaijin (outsiders) even when they work for the same company.

One very important characteristic in all companies is that Japanese top managers remain in office for 2–5 years. This applies, in all but one case, to the Presidents, and also to other highly placed Japanese managers. Presidents are said to be sent with a mission from Japanese headquarters which only he knows. The President will focus on this mission, leaving long-term structural issues to (mostly European) employees who remain within the company for a longer time. Due to this regular change of leadership, employees experience a lack of stability in leadership style and work. Furthermore, the employees believe that this is not efficient. Because presidents' times never overlap, an old president hardly ever has the opportunity to pass his experience of the job on to the next president.

## 2.3. Decision making

Interviewees told us that top management did not make big decisions alone. If decisions are made, they are made by means of nemawashi (joint decision making), leaving no one responsible for decisions. In these companies, decisions are often made by informal consultation among managers. This process involves both Japanese and European managers, and in most cases horizontal consensus is officially attained. However, we sense that not everyone totally agrees with decisions made this way, although formally they do! The decisions seldom propose radical shifts in the way of doing business. Thus organizations change little from year to year. Some people, on the other hand, judged their companies to be quite hectic: their companies were continually changing. However, they turned out to be functional, with business processes remaining constant. The fact that decisions, on management level, are often made by a group points to an employee-oriented rather than job-oriented culture.

## 2.4. Goals, strategy, and planning

Most companies focus more on market share than profitability, though the latter is still important. These findings clearly agree with reports in literature: Japanese companies focus more on expansion. A reason is that Japanese companies are controlled by industrial groups. Evidence for this is the fact that Japan has the lowest dividend pay-out rate in the world. Rehfeld [9] says:

'The Japanese don't like business that aren't profitable... But it's okay to make a few percentage points less and spend money on promotions and other things...' This phenomenon is also discussed in The Economist [1]:

“Your rules of competition are different from ours”, one European executive complained: “the Japanese should stop trying single mindedly to produce the best goods at the cheapest prices to win the biggest market share”.

Some large organizations have clearly stated goals, strategies and policies, formulated with Japanese headquarters. Striking, in more than one case, is the absence of a long-term plan.

## 2.5. Communication

Almost every company had strong informal communication. An employee said he could learn more about the organization from one informal dinner with a few managers than all the formal meetings. In one company, the Japanese are known for staying in the building after all Europeans have left. This is when they discuss the company.

Several companies we approached hesitated to participate in our project. In fact, in every case we spoke to a Japanese representative, we encountered opposition. Several Europeans were also unwilling to help. One person told us that an employee from the company had almost been fired when he had hired an external consultant to solve a problem. Japanese managers reproached him for having no confidence in the capacity of their own employees. We also noticed that top management often does not release new strategic plans until just before their execution. This kind of secretive behaviour suggests a closed company culture. People who did contribute to our research specifically told us not to mention their names and companies. Perhaps the cultures of these companies were not quite as open as they appeared to be.

## 2.6. Change

The way to introduce change in Japanese companies is to do so gradually. Changes or improvements should be introduced step by step (or in Japanese, kaizen [3]). Sometimes step-by-step improvement is literally applied: the company performs kaizen-projects to gradually improve business processes. Other companies, though less formalized, employ ways of gradually improving their businesses as well. The success of this depends highly on employee-involvement. When employees are too ambitious, and want to change half of the organization at once, they will generally not last very long with the company.

## 2.7. People

Almost all the organizations studied have two separate social groups: the Japanese and the Europeans. It appears that the Japanese socialize among themselves, both at work and after work. In one organization we were told that the Japanese thought the Europeans were silly, and vice versa. This does not mean, however, that the two groups cannot work together: generally they do. Some organizations show another separation: office- and workfloor-personnel. Then both groups have practically nothing to do with each other. The companies with the most Japanese influence have a paternalistic or family atmosphere: people are proud to work for their companies. They feel at home and are well paid, so they do not feel the need to look for another job. We found that a person is seldom fired, but is transferred to another job or department. When the company does not perform well, Japanese headquarters usually sends a team of Japanese advisors to support top management.

Japanese companies in Europe appear to be parochial rather than professional: people identify themselves more with the company they work for than their profession. The fact that the people in the organization are important, not so much their results, is another reason why the companies are generally more employee-oriented than job-oriented.

## 2.8. Authority structure

Two of the companies with several divisions have a very complex authority structure. The mother company has set up two or more subsidiaries, each performing different business tasks. Several managers in them perform more than one job, often in two (or more) subsidiaries. Japanese managers appear to be doing as many as three or four jobs at a time. Besides these ‘multiple jobs’, there is much job rotation amongst people and positions in the companies. As mentioned before, people are transferred to another job when they do not function adequately. But also when people do their jobs well, they will often be transferred to other jobs, sometimes to another subsidiary, sometimes even abroad. The vague authority lines are characteristic of Japanese companies, and apparently also of Japanese companies in Europe.

## 3. Culture and information systems

Company culture partly determines the practices of people in a company. Here, we have restricted our study of cultural aspects to the IS departments of the organizations. We have located several common features within the IS departments investigated.

## 3.1. General characteristics

All thirteen IS departments are staff oriented, in most cases reporting directly to the Board of Directors or through a Finance Manager. In most cases, there is one section for system development, and another for technical issues. Hardly any IS departments have a Japanese manager.

This suggests that they do not attach strategic importance to the IS department. Japanese IS professionals communicate frequently with local IS departments by means of conferences or informal contacts. The IS professionals constitute approximately 5% of all employees. Large projects are often supported by external people, which is a costly solution. In general IS departments operate quite independently with the instruction: fulfil user requirements.

## 3.2. Strategy and planning

Information planning and information strategy formulation have been widely discussed is IS literature. See for example $[4–6]$ . The practice is normally accepted activities in IS departments; by 1991, of all companies in the Netherlands, 74 percent had an information plan $[7]$ . From the companies in our survey however, only two of the thirteen companies use information planning for following business goals and new developments.

There may be several reasons why only a few Japanese companies in Europe perform formal information planning. Possibly there is no strategic importance attached to IS departments. Furthermore, if there is a concrete long term plan, it is seldom formally approved and disseminated. A second factor may be the lack of knowledge of IS managers of the secret strategic plans. However, execution of these plans usually requires IS/IT support. The IS department usually knows about secret plans through informal contacts, but this information cannot be used for information planning. For an IS manager in an organization with Japanese management, this makes information planning very hard to realise and maybe useless. One IS manager put it this way:

“It is as if there is a Japanese cloud hanging above Europe. Suddenly automation projects are falling out of this cloud. These projects have to be executed by us.” Thus the IS department is only reacting in a passive way: this implies enormous time pressure.

## 3.3. Coordination

Several larger companies operate as the European headquarters of a global organization. An important activity of their IS department of these companies is coordinating European activities. However, because there is lack of information planning another means of coordination is used: setting global guidelines. These are determined at global conferences, with Japanese headquarters, and distributed throughout Europe by meetings in which every aspect of IT applications may be discussed. In general, European coordination consists of distributing global guidelines, coordinating European projects, and trying to prevent ‘reinventing the wheel’ in several locations; for example, by exchanging certain applications among the organization.

Coordination of international activities in multinational companies is difficult. In Japanese companies, coordination is even more difficult because there is a strong informal communication network. Interviewees in one company admitted that they had even developed informal ‘grey’ systems without management’s knowledge. Another implication of the informal network is that networks are based on social groups.

For IS/IT matters, managers are used to communicating informally with their Japanese colleagues. Therefore IS managers have to get used to nemawashi as well. An advantage of this complex informal communication is that more people are involved and this can make implementation easier.

Coordination is necessary to reach agreement on the role each party is to play in cooperative activities or projects. A basis for these roles is that the set of underlying responsibilities are not always clear. One of the organizations in our investigation had the authority structure shown in Figure 1.

The bold lines indicate formal responsibilities: thus the IS department is formally only supporting the European Division. As the open lines indicate, the European headquarters has also informal influence on it. The third and final party is the IS Division in Japan. They have the largest influence on IS departments, by choice of the IS department itself; there is no formal authority.

## 3.4. New technologies

The speed with which a company applies new technologies is very important. EDI, CASE tools and 4GL's are recent examples of change. In most companies with Japanese influence, applying new technology is different from the other companies. The new technologies are only investigated and applied in Japan. Only after that will they be implemented in subsidiaries. All people from IS departments said that they would rather wait for a successful implementation of technology in another company: taking risks is not the practice in Japanese companies, and thus not in IS departments either. This attitude can be derived from the decision making process and Japanese perseverance.

In two of the companies, employees explicitly mentioned that some project proposals were rejected by Japanese management because they were narrow minded. In one case, an inquiry with potential users to determine which system they would prefer. The findings did not agree with the system that the Japanese manager had chosen. Eventually the inquiry was repeated so that its results agreed with the ideas of the manager.

Introduction of new technologies often requires gradual change in Japanese organizations. Applied to IS departments this implies that the technical infrastructure is to be gradually improved. However, none of the companies used Japanese methods, like kaizen or Quality Control, for improvement in the IS department, while such methods are used in the primary processes.

## 3.5.IS activities

Culture partly determines the attitude of the organization towards fulfilment of user requirements and towards the outsourcing of IT/IS activities. An anecdote illustrating this influence is the story of a Dutch EDP manager who has worked within a Japancsc IS department. The methods are all group oriented: hard working, persevering, and supplementing each other. When the Dutch visitor objected that this was inefficient, the Japanese reminded him that the desired idea would come up eventually. According to the Dutch manager this approach obstructed the innovative power of the organization. Another fascinating observation is that most system developers perform their activities within all system development phases, whereas most western organizations have specialists in information analysis and programming. Japanese organizations try to create generalists by job rotation. Only two of the 13 organizations use a standard methodology for system development. The problem in another organization was that management talks about a methodology and its advantages, but does not implement it: Japanese do not easily implement a standard method, because it stimulates working individually, and this is in conflict with Japanese culture.

Almost all these companies hire external IS people for large projects. In some organizations they even make up the majority of the department. We expect the external agencies to be predominantly Japanese as Japanese companies are closely connected, but this is the case in only a few organizations. Buying Commercial off the Shelf (COTS) or standard packages is not very common in the 13 companies. Only financial applications are COTS and a not-invented-here idea exists.

## 3.6. Historical constraints

The applications are largely the same as those developed by Japanese at the time the company settled in Europe. These systems are usually very poorly documented. Moreover, if these systems were developed some time ago, as is often the case, they are batch oriented and they become more and more complex, as a result of maintenance and improvement. While documentation was poor in the beginning, it has become worse. The basis for the companies' information systems remains Japanese originated, batch applications. In only two cases had the company's IS department partially rebuilt a batch system into an on-line system. Incidentally both companies did this for the invoicing system; it generated invoices at night after collecting data in the day time.

## 3.7.IS atmosphere

Most IS departments operate quite independently: they are considered to be pure staff departments. Not one single IS department receives instructions from top management regarding informational aspects. As one employee told us, Japanese top management did not attach much strategic importance to the IS department, leaving the IS people free to perform their jobs in the way they thought best. This may be due to the short term presence of the presidents. Meanwhile, people feel relaxed, without much pressure to attain outstanding results. However, there is always some friction between users and IS professionals. Fulfilling the user requirements is the basic goal for IS departments. Because chargeback is seldom employed, users define requirements without being aware of the actual costs.

## 3.8. Attitude towards IS / IT

There are three important questions that must be answered:

1 How important does (top) management think IS and information planning are?

· is there an information strategy and/or plan?

\- where is the IS department in the organization?

\- what is the structure, size, and budget of the IS department?

\- what is the time horizon for automation, project and information planning?

\- are there strategic IT-applications?

2 What is the attitude of management and IS departments towards projects and systems? Is there:

\- cost transfer for IS projects to users?

\- project audit (before and after project delivery), and if there is, does it comprise both technical and social aspects?

• priority setting for projects; how is it done?

3 What is the attitude of management, IS department and users towards information systems? Should information systems be:

· open or closed?

· complicated or simple?

\- functional or fanciful?

\- cheap or expensive and of a better quality?

Management usually attaches no strategic importance to IS and information planning. Strategic applications of IT are absent. The IS department is always a staff department (sometimes under the authority of the Finance department), only serving end-users, and the time horizon for information projects is usually short to medium term. Management does, however, attach operational importance to IS. Most IS departments are large and have an adequate budget.

Most companies have no cost payback scheme. In all but one company, there is no project audit, to determine whether a project has been successful or not. No company has seriously performed feasibility studies to determine the relative importance of different projects.

In most companies, there is no formal statement as to how IS should be developed, or how they should operate. The fact that most systems are based on the original Japanese developed systems causes them to be more closed than open and more complicated than simple. The philosophy of management is that there has to be enough IS support. Therefore the core processes of the business must come first, which leads to user requirements being almost sacred. But because users and IS professionals often do not understand each other, systems are often more fanciful than functional. Japanese companies in Europe consider information systems to be only of operational importance. A shift in attitude towards strategic importance is, in our opinion, desirable.

## 4. Conclusion

We start by some suggestions on the alignment of the information systems department and the culture of the organization, stimulating improvement in both composition and working-methods. The companies of our investigation all face a cultural gap. We see the Japanese company with its Japanese top managers on one side and the European (Western) employees and managers on the other. We believe that in order to make the company more effective, both parties will have to adapt. Of course this has happened in the past, but more improvement is needed.

## 4.1. Top management

All companies have a Japanese President, and all but one of them stay in their positions for no more than 3–5 years, presumably for two reasons: tax advantage and job-rotation. This change causes concern and instability amongst company employees. The consequence is lack of structure and policy in the IS departments, causing them to be pure staff departments.

There were two interesting exceptions in the stability and the function of the IS department. One company has had one Japanese President for almost 10 years. The other one has had one (Japanese) Vice President for 17 years, and he is responsible for all operational issues. These Japanese managers become more and more European, which results in the companies changing. This in turn leads to increased awareness of the importance of IT within the organization. If the company is managed consistently it can focus on structural and long-term issues and the IS department can undertake actions to attune the department to future information needs. Furthermore it can examine the possibilities for new technological developments.

## 4.2. Nemawashi and kaizen

Most Japanese companies have an idea of the future direction, but do not explicitly present this on paper, especially for information needs. Very few companies have an actual information strategy, and new developments were only introduced coincidentally and on an ad hoc basis. In an organization with a (partial) Japanese culture however, it is not very easy for an employee to be clearly ambitious: changes are only introduced gradually (kaizen), and big decisions are only made by consensus, with a lengthy nemawashi process.

If people in the IS department are ambitious, and want to be successful in their jobs and with their department, they can only do so by adjusting to the Japanese culture. The only way to force decisions is to qualify oneself in nemawashi, but changes will only be introduced gradually. Most IS departments are still quite conservative, and have a reactive function rather than a proactive one. We believe that an IS department, managed or supported by ambitious and flexible people with ideas and vision, can contribute to a proactive IS department and thus to the success of the company.

## 4.3. Flexibility

That people have substantial freedom to perform their individual tasks is typical for Japanese companies in Europe. On many occasions a description of responsibilities and performance standards does not exist. The lack of responsibility descriptions on departmental level is an obstacle for coordination: many parties are involved in IS matters. Many westerners feel comfortable with good job descriptions and performance standards. Lack of them allows people substantial freedom, which most people appreciate, and this may result in enormous flexibility. Employees should adjust themselves to the situation, and discuss their activities and goals with each other. For IS departments this may allow an enormously flexible way of working. Training IS people is a good way to persuade them to optimize their activities. If IS professionals are not stimulated to adjust, the IS department is likely to be inefficient.

## 4.4. History

More than a few companies have information systems that were developed by Japanese programmers several years ago. As we pointed out earlier, these systems have limited documentation, are batch oriented, complex, inflexible, and require an enormous maintenance effort. However, Japanese companies are oriented towards gradual improvements rather than radical change and this apparently holds for the technical infrastructure and information systems. Most of the Japanese companies in Europe do not extensively employ modern technological opportunities. We believe that the information systems in several companies are historically fixed and that modernization of infrastructure and systems is possible and at least worth consideration.

Prior to this he worked as a software engineer at ITT Nederlands and as a research scientist at TNO, a Dutch national applied research institute. His research interests centre on theory of knowledge, artificial intelligence, information study and innovation involving the use of IT. He obtained a degree in Physics, a post-graduate diploma in Education, and a degree in computer studies specializing in artificial intelligence. His publications have appeared in Future Generations of Computer Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Proceedings of IFIP, Proceedings of European Conference on Artificial Intelligence, and Proceedings of SISnet European Conference on Information Systems.
