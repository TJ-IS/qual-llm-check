# A survey of rural small business computer use: Success factors and decision support

Vincent S. Lai

Department of MIS/MS, College of Administrative Sciences, University of Alabama in Huntsville, Huntsville, AL 35899, USA

A survey was conducted on 500 rural small manufacturing businesses to investigate their perception of the impact of computer use on decision performance. Two key findings emerged. First, computer systems failed to provide adequate support to many decision activities. Second, executives in such businesses were satisfied with their computer systems. However, many did not perceive a positive correlation between computer use and cost efficiency. The survey also tested the relationship between organizational characteristics and success of computer use. The survey showed that MIS ranking function, age of business, and EDP experience were significantly associated with success of computer use.

Keywords: Small business; Rural business; Computer use; MIS success; MIS evaluation

## 1. Introduction

The introduction of microcomputers into small business has had a tremendous impact on their ability to operate efficiently and enhance the decision making process through the computer-based information systems (CBIS). Prior research on the influence of computer use on decision performance has almost exclusively been conducted in large organizations. Their findings, though can be used as references, cannot be generalized to small organizations. DeLone's [2] [3] and Raymond's [10] empirical investigations, which focused on studying the impact of organizational characteristics on computer use and MIS success in the context of small businesses, marked a new research direction. However, research that explores the impact of computer technology in the context of rural, small business is still limited.

## 2. Research questions and hypotheses

The utilization and impacts of computer technology may vary, depending on the context of the application. This study deals with the application of computer technology to decision making in the rural small business context. Specifically, the study was designed to collect information and answer some questions: the first is:

i. How do business executives use computer technology to support their decision making process?

In addition to decision support, it is important to assess how business executives perceive the impact of computer technology on the decision making process. The perceived effectiveness and efficiency of computer use are particularly relevant. These considerations lead to the second research question:

ii. What are the perceived impacts of computer use to decision making process with respect to effectiveness and efficiency?

If computer technology is perceived to have impact on the decision making process, then it is important to find out specific ways in which computer technology helps (or fails to help) rural business executives in decision making. Such regard leads to the third question:

iii. How does computer technology facilitate the decision making process of rural business executives?

In addition to the exploration of the impact of computer technology on decision makers, a research model (Figure 1) with a series of accompanying hypotheses is proposed as a means to explain the relevance of business characteristics on the success of computer use. Based on earlier work, the measures of system success for small businesses included user satisfaction and the extent of computer use. Our study adapted these two variables as surrogate measures of computer use success.

The hypotheses were based on previous studies (e.g., [2] [10]), they are:

H1: The size of the business has a positive impact on the success of computer use.

H2: In-house applications development is positively related to the success of computer use.

H3: EDP experience is positively associated with the success of computer use.

H4: MIS ranking function is positively associated with the success of computer use.

H5: Age of the rural business is positively related to the success of computer use.

## 3. Research methodology

Two small businesses were used as pilot sites for testing and improving the survey questionnaire. Two group interviews had been conducted at these companies to identify key issues that would affect computer technology usage. Following these interviews, the survey questionnaire was developed and discussed with the company executives to obtain feedback on content validity. This provided an estimate of the time required for completion and also in the addition, removal, and rephrasing of several items.

Fig. 1. Research model.

After revising the survey instrument, a full scale pilot test was administered to thirty rural, small businesses in Texas. Cronbach's alpha was calculated for multiple item measures in order to assess the questionnaire's validity. The results of this analysis indicated reliability significantly higher than the 0.7 suggested by Nunnally [8], thereby implying an adequate level of internal consistency.

The full scale survey was administered to a sample of 500 small businesses randomly chosen from American Business List; this satisfied the following criteria:

(1) no more than 50 employees and

(2) annual sales of \$3.5 million or less.

Four weeks later, a follow-up mailing was sent to the companies which had not responded to the first mailing. Seventy-two companies responded to the first mailing while forty-eight more responded to the second. Of the 500 questionnaires, 35 were returned with “no forwarding address”. There was therefore an overall response rate of 25.8 percent.

## 4. Results analysis

Only eighty-two (68.3%) of the rural small businesses has adopted computer technology as a part of their daily operations. However, this number may be inaccurate because companies using computer technology tend to response more frequently than those that are not using. Therefore, the data on percentage use of computer technology is probably biased upwards to a considerable extent.

Among the adopters, 64% had used computer systems for five years or less. Only 24% had used computer technology for more than 10 years. The responses of these eighty-two adopters form the information base.

## 4.1. Decision support

The small business executives were asked to identify the computing resources that were employed to support their decision making. Each of the respondents was asked to select more than one answer from the list of computing resources. These answers were then tabulated and categorized. The hardware resources deployed in the respondents' organizations were identified as falling into four categories: personal computers, workstations, minis, and mainframes. As for the software resources, an analysis of respondents' selections suggests that they fall into four categories – word processing, accounting, sales, and decision support packages. Table 1 details this information.

One of the measures of computer support in decision making is the degree of deployment of decision support tools. The results in Table 1 depicted that most rural small businesses have largely adopted transaction applications, such as word processing and accounting packages, to improve operational efficiency. However, the more advanced and diversified applications that support functional and strategic decisions are less usual. This finding is consistent with Raymond's study [10] conducted in the urban setting.

For the organizations that have implemented decision support applications, the respondents were asked to indicate how, as well as how often, the applications were utilized to support their decision tasks. Their responses were then categorized into the major decision activities, as illustrated in Simon's decision model. In this process, three judges independently reviewed and classified each response on the usage pattern addressed. If any of the three judges classified a response differently, the response was discussed by the group until a consensus was reached. Once all the responses were categorized, the usage frequency was compiled to determine how often the applications were used to support decision tasks within the prescribed usage pattern. ## 4.2. Perceived impacts of computer use on decision making

The survey data indicate that seventy (85.4%) rural small business executives considered a computer-aided approach to decision making to be beneficial. Forty-one (58.6%) revealed that they rely on computer output for their decision making. Among these respondents, thirty-six (87.8%) perceived the computer-aided decision process to be an improvement over a manual process. Only three (7.3%) of the respondents who had adopted the automated approach were dissatisfied with the computer-aided process.

The variables used to evaluate the perceived impacts of a computer-aided approach were effectiveness and efficiency. Hamilton and Chervany [5] stated that user satisfaction is the most useful assessment of system effectiveness, while Mocker [7] believed that cost and capital efficiency are important indicators of efficiency. Thus, user satisfaction and cost efficiency were used as surrogate measures of impacts of computer use on decision making.

## (i) Effectiveness

Sethi et al. [11] has critically evaluated the ranking criteria and effectiveness index developed to measure information systems. Szajna [13] reminded researcher to be cautious in choosing a relevant measure of effectiveness because different independent variables will influence effectiveness in different ways. Bailey and Pearson [1], Ives, Olson and Baroudi [6], and Raymond [10] have developed instruments of a similar type to assess system effectiveness in the context of businesses. Our survey adapted their instruments by eliminating the items inapplicable for rural small business, and then we applied the revised instrument to these businesses. The results, which were measured on a 7-point Likert scale, are exhibited in Table 3.

The findings show that the rural executives who were surveyed are very positive about their computer use. They are comfortable with the system's output quality (overall average of 5.0), except in the areas of completeness, reliability, relevancy, and precision of reports (all below 5.0). This seems to be of concern because the quality of the decisions relies on these attributes. If nothing is done to improve the output quality, such as acquiring advanced decision tools or employing competent computer professionals, the executive's decision performance may be degraded.

It is obvious that rural small businesses are happy with the user-system (overall average of 5.78) and user-EDP staff relationships (overall average of 5.53). These positive attitudes may all be attributed to the small, informal, and personal working environment rural small business always demonstrate.

## (ii) Efficiency

Virtually all decisions made by executives are designed to improve either effectiveness or efficiency. These, in an organizational setting, demonstrate different traits: effectiveness focuses on addressing system outputs and measured by user satisfaction; efficiency addresses the use of resources to produce results. Measuring computer use efficiency is difficult because the impact of computers on firm level performance is generally indirect. Computer use only impacts certain intermediate output variables, which in turn are transformed to realize firm performance. In our study, the information on computer use efficiency was collected from return on sales, return on assets, and return on investment. A perceived positive impact on these three indicators implies computer use efficiency.

The results in Table 4 indicate that a majority of respondents (78%) perceived computer use to have a positive impact on cost efficiency. However, 22% exhibited a less positive attitude toward it. The explanation is possibly due to the weak link between computer use and cost efficiency. The contributions of information technology to these factors are implicit and cannot be measured directly. When people cannot relate computer technology to cost efficiency, they may not give high marks on these issues.

## 4.3. Adequacy of computer-based systems

In addition, the survey also attempted to determine the executive's perception of decision support resulting from computer use. The Simon decision model was applied to investigate how adequately computer systems support each decision activity in the decision making process.

The overall results presented in Table 5 indicates that the executives did not believe their current computer use had provided strong support in improving their decision performance. The high standard deviations associated with most decision activities also illustrate general disagreement among the executives.

Among all the decision activities, the executives, as a whole, believed they received the most support in performing sensitivity analysis during the design phase; this may be attributed to the capability most spreadsheets possess. Additionally, the executives exhibited positive attitudes, though not strong, toward the support they received in undertaking activities in outcome prediction, solution selection, plan formulation, outcome measurement, and information scanning.

Not surprisingly, the executives are most disappointed with the support they receive during the intelligence and choice phases. This is because intelligence activities are unstructured and choice activities are judgement-oriented. The existing applications, which most rural small businesses adopted, are inadequate to satisfy these requirements. Only the application of Management Support Systems, such as Decision Support Systems, Executive Support Systems, or Expert Systems, would meet these needs.

## 4.4. Hypothesis testing

In this study, a research model was proposed to evaluate the significant organizational factors that affect success of computer use in the context of rural small business. The hypotheses that are associated with this model were tested using regression analysis. Table 6 presents the results.

H1: The size of the business has a positive impact on the success of computer use.

Many previous studies [4] [12] have indicated that the size of organization has a positive impact on the success of computer use. However, this hypothesis is not supported here. The major reason is that businesses used in this survey are homogeneous, with small variations in employees and annual sales.

H2: In-house applications development is positively related to the success of computer use.

Conceptually, with the existence of an in-house development team, the chance of computer use success should be a lot higher. However, the survey results indicated that this hypothesis was not supported at the 5 percent significance level. A further analysis by decomposing computer use success (dependent variable) into user satisfaction and extent of utilization indicates that user satisfaction is significantly related (p-value = 0.001) to success while the extent of system utilization is not. The reason that user satisfaction and success are positively related is that information needs of a small business are better met by the development staff if the applications can be developed in-house.

H3: EDP experience is positively associated with the success of computer use.

Powers and Dickson [9] perceived EDP experience to be a significant determinant of success for computer use. Raymond tried to verify this perception, but found that small business executives tended to be less satisfied with MIS in firms with greater EDP experience. His explanation was that these users would have higher expectations, thus causing them to be more critical. In our study, EDP experience was found to be positively associated (p-value < 0.05) with the success of computer use. This result supports the argument that the greater the experience, the less difficulties the user will have in overcoming issues related to computer implementation and operation. As a consequence, the chance of success is greatly enhanced.

H4: MIS ranking function is positively associated with the success of computer use.

Many researchers believe that the higher the organizational level of the MIS function within an organization, the greater the chance for computer use success. Ein-Dor and Segev [4] and Raymond [10] verified that success of computer use is significantly related to location of the MIS function in the organization. This study supports their findings and provides further evidence that, in the context of rural small businesses, MIS rank is positively associated with success of computer use.

H5: Age of the rural business is positively related to the success of computer use.

It is postulated that when a business becomes more established through years of operation, its structure becomes more stabilized, integrated, and coherent. As a consequence, the company will be more ready to accept new information technologies. This study tried to validate this postulate and concluded that age of small businesses is, indeed, positively associated with the success of computer use. In other words, small businesses with a longer history of computation are more likely to accomplish success in computer use than their counterparts with a shorter history.

## 5. Implications for researchers

This research has extended the investigation of computer use in small businesses to the rural setting. No research has previously attempted to validate and extend computer use success model in this direction.

This research only evaluates success factors for computer use in the manufacturing industry. The service and other industries may exhibit different response patterns to the independent variables in our study. Hence, future research is required to validate and investigate the success model elsewhere. Undoubtedly, the success factors for computer use in large, small, urban, and rural companies are very different.

The research indicates that EDP experience is positively related to the success of computer use, however, Raymond's study indicates a negative relationship. These contradictory findings may be an indication of measurement errors, approach adopted, or samples selected. No matter what causes inconsistent results, the issue needs to be re-examined and, perhaps, resolved in future studies.

Small rural businesses are always the most neglected research domain. However, the importance of small businesses to the U.S. economy is clear. They not only contribute to a large percentage of the country's GNP and employment, but also account for the strongest force in maintaining the prosperity of the local economy. Their success and continued growth are of utmost importance to the national economy. More studies should be conducted to investigate their survival needs. Exploring the success factors for computer use is only one of them.

## 6. Summary

This study has provided insights into exploring MIS utilization in rural small businesses. The results indicated that computer use in these businesses is still confined to operational activities: the computer has not been used as an information tool to improve decisions performance. Most of the MIS used in these businesses were low level applications. Management Support Systems have not been adequately implemented to support semi-structured and unstructured decisions. Although executives were satisfied with their current computer use and relationship with EDP staff, they did not perceive the computer as a strategic tool for the business.

In exploring the impacts of organizational characteristics on computer use among rural small businesses, it was found that when the MIS function is ranked higher in the organization, the chance of success of the computer use is higher. Additionally, the survey indicated that the age of the business and length of EDP experience have positive relation to the success of computer use.
