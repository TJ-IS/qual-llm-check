# Benchmarking human resource information systems in Canada and Hong Kong

Maris G. Martinsons \*

Pacific Rim Institute for Studies of Management (PRISM) and Business and Management Department, City Polytechnic of Hong Kong, 83 Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong

As local and regional economies become more competitive and interdependent, organizations can benefit from benchmarking their use of information technology (IT) on a global basis. A recent study compared the degree and sophistication of information systems (IS) in a fast developing economy, Hongkong, with that in a more developed one, Canada. Research of nine human resource management (HRM) activities found that while Canada had established a lead over the newly industrialized Asian economy in routine applications, this was narrowing. However, Hong Kong still significantly trailed its North American counterpart in IS sophistication. Management vision and support, skilled developers and a business-driven approach were critical for successful computer-based human resource information systems (CHRIS). Regulatory requirements as well as organizational and cultural factors accounted for many of the differences in IT applications between Canada and Hong Kong. The results provide a basis for management to review their own CHRIS and help to identify directions for further research on this topic.

Keywords: Benchmarking; Hong Kong; Canada; Economic development; Comparative management; Human resource management; IS sophistication; Technology and development; Human resource IS; Performance evaluation

## 1. Introduction

Organizational improvement is no longer just a management challenge, but a basic necessity for competitive survival. Information about a firm's relative performance and comparative practices is commonly used to help focus and facilitate essential improvement initiatives [27]. Such benchmarking forces an organization to critically examine its operations and often stimulates the refinement or overhaul of business processes and systems. Benchmarking data can be the basis for emulating exemplary performers, capitalizing on new business opportunities and constructing defenses against competitive threats. With local and regional economies becoming more interdependent, the scope of such benchmarking now has to transcend traditional geographic and political boundaries. The situation confronting Canadian and Hong Kong businesses offers a good example.

He received his engineering and postgraduate management degrees from the University of Toronto in Canada, and subsequently took on a progressive series of responsibilities in North America. More recently, he has been active in fostering business relationships, research collaboration and management development activities across national

Over the past few years, business and cultural ties have steadily and significantly increased between Hong Kong and Canada. This has been due to: large-scale migration to Canada in advance of Hong Kong's political transfer from Britain to the People's Republic of China (PRC) in 1997; enactment of a free trade agreement between Canada and the United States, which is the largest export market for Hong Kong; and globalization of business activities in both economies.

With the growth of Hong Kong-Canadian business relationships (as customers and suppliers, competitors and collaborators), managers on both sides of the Pacific Ocean seek to better understand their counterparts - what they are doing, how they are doing it, and the rationale for their activities. Unfortunately, such information remains in short supply. Although Asia and North America have featured prominently in the comparative management literature, it is Japan and the United States that are invariably considered on the basis of socioeconomic, cultural and political factors. Smaller economies on both continents have been largely ignored. This lack of benchmarking data, at even the macroeconomic level, inhibits the planning and policy-making process for both private- and public-sector organizations.

Canadian per capita gross domestic production (GDP) and living standards are among the highest in the world, rivalled only by the United States, Japan, and a few Western European countries $[51]$ . However, the real growth of the Canadian economy has averaged only about 3.5 percent per year since 1971 $[33]$ . The North American country is characteristic of a stable and well-developed economy.

Hong Kong, one of the four Asian Dragons (along with South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore), is a newly industrializing economy. Although considerably less developed [44], its growth rate in recent decades has been among the best in the world, with gross domestic production rising about 8 percent per year [51]. Other countries in the region (such as Malaysia, Thailand, the PRC and Vietnam) are now striving to follow its development path. As Southeast Asia industrializes, there is a wide interest in benchmarking its progress. The evolution of an individual economy may be considered from two frames of reference: the world's most developed economies and other economies in the region. A comparison of Hong Kong against Canada incorporates the first of these reference frames.

## 2. Information Technology in HRM

Information technology (IT) is still a relatively young technology which has been rapidly diffused across international borders $[28]$ . A recent study sought to benchmark the use of IT in Hongkong and Canada. Although benchmarking is also suitable for considering the cross-functional performance of the organization, the focus of this study was one specific area – human resource management (HRM). People and information are two resources which can dramatically affect the overall performance of a business. Each can be the source of competitive advantage $[1,36,45]$ but business success typically requires superior management of both $[15,21]$ . Equally important is a synergy between the two. This is most visible in the HRM area, where IT can be used to develop new products and services and increase competitiveness $[2]$ .

The tremendous amounts of paper and data associated with personnel management made it an ideal candidate for early automation. The first computerized employee record keeping systems were already introduced in the early 1960s. Transaction processing applications, for payroll and benefits administration, soon followed [43]. Computer-based information systems became critically important as the HRM concept evolved and was being adopted in North America and Europe. This phenomenon was first observed in larger organizations [5,12,25] and subsequently in smaller ones [42]. Anecdotal reports suggest a similar progression in Asia [21,39].

Contemporary IT enables sophisticated data manipulation, comprehensive yet flexible reporting, decision-making support and even expert-level advice. Its use for HRM gives rise to a computer-based human resource information system (CHRIS). A CHRIS has traditionally supported the administrative activities related to staffing, compensation, performance review, and training $[31,34,43]$ . It has been widely applied to less-structured tasks, such as recruitment and manpower planning $[12,21]$ . The use of a CHRIS to match people to specific jobs, distribute staff rewards, design career development programs and evaluate staff development programs has also been reported $[31,34,45,47]$ . Exception reporting systems can help avert potential crises by highlighting internal HR problems and identifying prospective moves by competitors $[13]$ .

A CHRIS can increase employee productivity, provide organizational synergy by integrating manpower processes and policies, and facilitate process redesign $[2,26]$ . HR professionals may expect greater job satisfaction and recognition and better opportunities for career advancement $[42,48]$ . However, these benefits can only occur when IT implementations successfully address real and pressing business problems. The absence of an appropriate business strategy $[20]$ , the patchwork nature of design $[19]$ and the disjointed nature of applications development $[5]$ are among the reasons cited when the value of CHRIS is not realized.

The relationship between human factors and IT success has been widely researched $[4,15,35,50]$ . However, there is evidence to suggest that HRM has been a laggard in adopting IT $[7,29]$ . Explanations offered for this phenomenon include: a weak and often poor relationship between HR and IS groups in the organization $[4,5]$ ; the difficulties system professionals have in understanding the nature of CHRIS requirements and priorities $[7,50]$ , and the fact that general management often does not view the HRM function as being strategic to the organization's objectives $[7,13,40]$ .

CHRIS managers only came into existence during the 1980s and their organizational power and influence remains limited [6]. With recent budget squeezes, rapidly evolving statutory requirements and a renewed need to justify their organizational role, the HRM area has belatedly begun to increase its assimilation of IT [12,47]. The global market for human resource software was forecast to rise from US\$398 million in 1989 to US\$730 million in 1993 [40].

The impact of the massive investments in IT on HRM activities is far from clear. There has been little published research and a scarcity of systematic or theoretically-grounded studies related to computer-based human resource information systems. This has been partly due to difficulties in measuring HRM performance $[1,2]$ and the absence of standardized frameworks for assessing IT value [3]. While the information management research community is alerted to these shortcomings, a recent exploratory study had more modest aims:

(1) To benchmark the present use of IT for HRM activities in Canada and Hong Kong;

(2) To identify future priorities for CHRIS enhancements in the two economies;

(3) To account for observed CHRIS differences between Hong Kong and Canada;

(4) To profile exemplary CHRIS practices in the two economies.

This research builds upon discussions of the relationship between technology and economic development $[24,28,30]$ . Four hypotheses were tested:

H.1 IT use is greater in the more developed economy (Canada);

H.2 IT sophistication is greater in the more developed economy; and

H.3 the IT application rate is greater in the faster developing economy (Hong Kong).

H.4 larger organizations make more use of IT.

## 3. The Benchmarking Study

## 3.1. Areas of Study

Ein-Dor and Segev [10] suggest that IT use in an organizational unit may be characterized by two factors. The reported study employed this framework, considering both the degree of technology used and the sophistication of IT applications. The independence of these two factors may be illustrated by considering a pair of examples. Limited IT can support an unstructured management activity which requires considerable expertise, such as personnel evaluation and human resource planning, producing a sophisticated application. Alternatively, very extensive use of IT may involve simple record-keeping and routine reporting, creating an unsophisticated application.

The HRM function may be defined to include the recruitment, placement, evaluation, selection and development of employees. Nine of the most common and important HRM activities were selected as the basis for this investigation. Four of them, the administration of payrolls and benefits, and the record-keeping related to employees and their absences, are relatively unsophisticated. Four other activities, namely recruitment and personnel selection, staff training and development, performance appraisal and manpower planning are considered to be more sophisticated. For them, timely information is crucial to unstructured decisions (often involving professional judgment) which typically lead to significant impacts. A ninth activity, budgeting, was studied as an indicator of financial planning and control within HRM. A classification of these activities appears in Table 1.

## 3.2. Methodology

The degree and sophistication level of IT usage for managing human resources in Hong Kong and Canada were examined using a two-stage data gathering process. The initial, largely quantitative data was obtained from comprehensive questionnaires which combined forced-choice and open-ended questions. This survey instrument was pretested with a small group of HR managers in Hong Kong. Follow-up research was undertaken to obtain more detailed and explanatory data from representative organizations. The Hong Kong part of the study was initiated in late 1988 while the Canadian group was first surveyed during the first half of 1989. Differences due to this small offset in the sampling periods are assumed to have a relatively insignificant effect on the results. The extensive follow-up research for both economies was completed by early 1992.

Among Hong Kong organizations, 138 usable responses were received from the 990 comprehensive questionnaires which were initially distributed to larger companies (over 200 employees). A shorter form, sent to 1983 randomly-selected smaller companies (100–200 employees), yielded 223 usable responses. The results obtained from the total of these 361 Hong Kong organizations, representing a cross-section of businesses in that economy, were compared with a total of 118 usable Canadian responses. Fifty-seven of the latter responses were obtained from a mail-out of 75 questionnaires that had been sent directly to pre-selected Canadian members of the human resource systems profession in a cross-selection of larger companies (again over 200 employees). Shorter survey forms, sent to a random sample of 300 smaller businesses across Canada, provided a further 61 responses. The overall response rate of about 15% is typical for this type of survey [16,49]. Our analysis of early and late respondents (the latter being a surrogate for those not responding) failed to find any evidence for a non-response bias.

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. Applications

The results indicate broad similarities in the use of IT for managing human resources, but also reveal some key differences in the degree and sophistication of CHRIS between the two economies. As shown in Table 2, most of the surveyed companies in both Hong Kong and Canada have applied the technology to their HRM activities.

A CHRIS was significantly more common (at a 95% confidence level) in larger companies than in smaller ones. In Hong Kong, a CHRIS was found to be 33% more likely among the larger firm sample, while the comparable figure in Canada was 24%. The most prevalent IT usage in HRM was among large organizations engaged in the following sectors of both economies: financial service, real estate, and hospitality (especially the air travel and hotel segments).

The results indicated that Canada had a longer and more distinguished history with CHRIS. Only 15% of these systems in Hong Kong had existed for more than five years, compared with 42% in Canada (see Figure 1). In 1989, the 67% using a CHRIS in Hong Kong was less than the 84% in Canada, but up significantly from 59% in the Asian territory a year earlier [41]. Our study found that Hong Kong was applying IT to HRM activities at a higher rate than Canada. This trend appears to be continuing into the middle 1990s.

About one-half of those without a CHRIS were planning to implement one within the next three years. Pressing needs to reduce turnaround times for queries from managers and customers and the desire to use a common information base across the company in considering strategic issues were the most frequently cited justifications. Our follow-up research indicated that, while less than half the plans were fulfilled on schedule, the reasons for this were quite different in the two economies. Canadian businesses cut back on project spending and deferred new CHRIS developments during an economic recession. Meanwhile, the presence of a “brain drain” in Hong Kong, related to its 1997 political transfer, made it difficult to staff CHRIS projects. The inadequacy of financial resources in Canada and shortages of requisite human resources in Hong Kong may be considered as typical bottlenecks for technological progress in developed and developing economies, respectively [24,30].

## 4.1.1. Current Applications

The application of IT to the nine aforementioned HRM activities is reported in Table 3. The most common and also the most useful applications in both economies were related to payroll administration and personnel record-keeping. More than 80% of the responding organizations in both Hong Kong and Canada used such applications.

Benefits administration was the only “unsophisticated” application area where Hong Kong businesses significantly trailed their Canadian counterparts (at a 95% confidence level). This difference could be explained by the more comprehensive social programs, greater management-labour interaction and underlying regulatory requirements in Canada. Only 41% of Hong Kong respondents listed budgeting among their three most useful existing applications or most desirable additions, significantly less than the 63% doing so in Canada. Shorter planning horizons, greater decentralization of HRM activities in Hong Kong, and more evolved financial planning and control in Canada were found to account for this difference.

Hong Kong also lagged in two “sophisticated” application areas: computer-supported performance appraisal and computer-aided training and development. Canadian use of IT was about twice as likely in these two areas. This is consistent with the informal nature of both employee evaluation and management development in Chinese business [37, 38]. Hong Kong counterparts do relatively more IT-assisted recruitment. There is a greater need to recruit new staff in the highergrowth economy, as the skilled labour market is tight and the employee turnover rate is relatively high $[14,17,37]$ . Hong Kong personnel managers were willing to make IT investments in recruitment and selection because improvements in these activities were considered critical to overall business performance.

Canadian organizations had a diminishing edge in the overall degree of CHRIS usage, but a more significant and sustained lead in sophisticated applications. Such results reflect the changes occurring in the respective economies during recent years. The fast pace of Hong Kong's macroeconomic growth led to a higher rate of new business formation from an organizational base that was relatively smaller and somewhat less sophisticated than that in Canada [14,51]. In fact, the mean business size in Hong Kong has dropped from about 55 employees in 1950 to less than 20 employees in the late 1980s [38]. This does not mean that individual organizations are getting smaller, but simply that many new firms are entering the economy.

A top tier of the most progressive IT users in each economy, constituting about 15% of the total sample, were at similar stages of CHRIS evolution. They were typically larger firms with multinational operations. However, Canada had a second tier of organizations that were greater in number and further evolved in their CHRIS applications than their Hong Kong counterparts.

## 4.1.2. Desired Applications

The need for organizational improvement was clearly recognized by the vast majority of respondents. Current applications do not completely meet organizational needs. When asked to list a maximum of three additional desired applications, respondents in both economies identified an average of 1.9 new areas for using IT. These wish lists were typically headed by manpower planning and performance appraisal. Many respondents wanted greater flexibility in retrieving and using the data which was already part of a CHRIS. They wanted outputs to be upgraded from preformatted reports to interactive data retrieval and analysis. Greater integration between payroll and personnel data and improved line management access to relevant HR information were also frequently sought. A few of the larger companies were considering the outsourcing of their transactions processing activities in order that they could focus on strategic HR planning issues.

Significant numbers of both new and modified CHRIS applications may be expected in the near future. Most efforts will focus on upgrading the support for regular decision-making activities rather than providing better information for specific meeting preparation or crisis situation handling. In Hong Kong, where large-scale migration has resulted in a “brain drain” problem, increasingly sophisticated recruitment and manpower planning tools will enable better planning and control of scarce human resources. In Canada, the focus will be on greater use of IT for absence records and benefits administration.

## 4.1.3. Exemplary Practices

Canadian HRM is significantly more sophisticated in its use of IT, with nearly 60% of organizations applying it to at least one of the four non-routine activities. In Hong Kong, 61% reported that IT applications were limited to the unsophisticated activities, with the automation of clerical and operational tasks being common. While IT usage for record-keeping is similar, data analysis and modelling facilities were almost twice as likely (57% versus 32%) to be part of a Canadian CHRIS. This is consistent with the more common application of IT for performance appraisal, manpower planning and staff training in Canada.

Decision-support systems (DSS) and knowledge-based systems (KBS) are well-suited for manipulating the qualitative knowledge associated with organizational and management tasks $[18,20]$ . These systems support strategies based on quality, customer service and innovation by enabling people to work smarter and with greater vision $[2,22]$ . Brainstorming activities as well as decisions using dynamic and ill-defined rules can be supported by DSS $[2]$ . Meanwhile, KBS applications can leverage and formalize in-house expertise, thus enabling its retention even after the human experts leave the organization $[21]$ . Expert support systems can complement human professionals, freeing experts to do more challenging and higher-value activities. These two new-generation information technologies (NGIT) increase the potential value of a CHRIS $[21]$ .

The presence of NGIT was three times as common in Canadian HRM organizations, with 59% having at least one such application. Many ad-hoc information requests were handled by “intelligent” applications which combined expert systems technology with a report generator. Some of the more innovative KBS applications are listed in Table 4. In Hong Kong, two financial service firms were using KBS technology to assist with recruitment and selection activities. Those applications eased temporary work burdens, reduced the time for hiring replacement employees and served as a valuable training tool for the new hires [21]. About one-quarter of the respondents had created some form of an employee skills inventory which was used to support long-range succession planning.

Respondents were generally satisfied with their NGIT applications, although many reported that they had experienced difficulties in resourcing the development of these applications. Shortages of technical expertise as well as concerns about the economic and operational feasibility were the most frequently mentioned obstacles. This is consistent with other reported research $[2,23]$ . HR practitioners in Hong Kong considered more extensive use of DSS to be a short-term priority, especially since cost-effective packaged software solutions were increasingly available. For the longer-term, computer-based diagnostic tools to help prescribe staff development and interactive training packages were seen as potentially important contributors to organizational improvement.

## 4.2. The Technology

## 4.2.1. Hardware

As indicated in Figure 2, 54% in Hong Kong and 44% in Canada used microcomputers as their primary hardware. Even among larger organizations, PCs represented the primary CHRIS hardware for 37% in Hong Kong, but only 20% in Canada.

Many Canadian firms implemented a centralized CHRIS structure many years ago, when the underlying technology was less sophisticated and flexible. Today, with large and entrenched databases and applications, Canadian HR managers have relatively little incentive to modify this structure. The upgrading of these still-functional human resource systems is a low organizational priority in a stable business environment.

The personal computer is far and away the most popular medium for recently implemented systems in both countries (see Table 5). Local area networks have made it both practical and cost-effective to use a microcomputer platform for CHRIS. This is consistent with the reported trend towards the increased use of PCs for managing human resources [11,26,47].

## 4.2.2. Software

Software originating from the United States predominates in both Hong Kong and Canada. The relatively free trade practised by both economies has enabled U.S. suppliers to establish a very strong market position. A wide assortment of both traditional and fourth-generation language (4GL) software tools are used on the larger CHRIS hardware. The most popular mainframe and minicomputer-based products include third-generation language compilers and database management packages with SQL functionality.

Among those primarily using PCs, more than half in both Hong Kong and Canada employed the dBase family of products. High proportions use spreadsheet software, primarily Lotus 1-2-3 (37% of all CHRIS), suggesting that quantitative analysis is a significant aspect of contemporary HRM. The common listing of assorted word processing packages (94% of those with a CHRIS) confirms that this business area is no exception when it comes to this most fundamental business computing activity.

## 4.3. Systems Development

The state of a CHRIS is not only related to technology expenditures, but also strongly influenced by the technical sophistication of managers $[15,20,50]$ . Internal development or (at a minimum) internal participation in contracted development is more common for more sophisticated applications. Evidence for this relationship comes from the higher fraction of custom-developed systems and the greater IT sophistication in Canada. Only one CHRIS of every six in Canada is primarily the result of a pre-packaged product, compared with one of four in Hong Kong. Ratios of in-house to contractor-developed CHRIS were similar in both Hong Kong and Canada, with over two-thirds being primarily developed by internal staff.

Canadian HR managers were significantly involved in planning about 90% of new CHRIS implementations. They expected to maintain this percentage in the near future. However, the definition of information needs and the design of associated work procedures is predominantly left to line managers with some input from operational staff. Systems modifications typically supported the redistribution of HR activities in the company. In Hong Kong, CHRIS planning followed a similar pattern, although there was greater hands-on involvement by senior managers during the early stages of the development life cycle.

## 5. Conclusions and Recommendations

The findings support all four of the stated hypotheses. The use of IT was found to be more extensive and of greater sophistication in the more developed economy. However, superior economic growth in recent years has resulted in HRM organizations in Hong Kong applying IT at a significantly faster rate than their Canadian counterparts. This has reduced Canada's historic advantage. Although Hong Kong establishments have placed a priority on developing sophisticated applications, this gap is projected to continue over the next decade.

HR managers in larger firms have made the most extensive use of IT. Equal fractions of Canadian and Hong Kong organizations with more than 200 employees had a CHRIS. However, the overall CHRIS sophistication was significantly lower in Hong Kong. Quantitative analysis of the survey data showed that almost half the disparity could be attributed to organizational size. There are simply fewer very large firms in Hong Kong. The basic character of the economy (including industrial mix), the organizational structure and technical sophistication of management as well as the level of both foreign competition and ownership are also likely to be significant factors. This is consistent with the view that the structure, systems and management practices must fit the organization's state of development (an external fit) while complementing each other (an internal fit) [1]. Research of these specific relationships can help an organization to benchmark its current practices.

Hong Kong HRM organizations significantly lagged their Canadian counterparts in high-end applications using NGIT. Such developments typically require larger investments and have longer pay-back periods, based on less tangible and quantifiable benefits. These types of technology initiatives are less attractive in a dynamic and developing economy. The data indicate that Hong Kong will increase its edge in using computerized recruitment applications because of continued shortages of skilled labour and a shorter-term business focus. The Asian territory will also narrow its lag in computer-supported training and development, reflecting the growing influence of Western management practices.

The results indicate a tremendous amount of unrealized CHRIS potential, particularly in Hong Kong. Relatively few respondents are using IT to directly improve competitiveness. Such findings are consistent with a 1987 U.S. survey which found that while 91% of HRM professionals used IT, their applications were almost exclusively restricted to spreadsheet analysis, word processing and database management [46]. The use of DSS and KBS, to improve managerial decision-making and address the scarcity and fluidity of human expertise, respectively, are among the obvious future IT application areas within the realm of HRM.

Expert systems can be used to screen job applicants, develop complicated work schedules, recommend training programs, help employees to select from flexible benefits plans, advise on health and safety issues and enable performance problem analysis $[18,21,23,26,32]$ . Planning for such applications had become fairly common in Canada but was typically just beginning in Hong Kong. However, progress with both new-generation and more conventional IT could be hampered by shortages of systems professionals if end-user computing is not successfully encouraged.

Managers in both Canada and Hong Kong expressed a high level of interest in creating or enhancing their CHRIS. A few had already visited other organizations and/or applied their own benchmarking in an effort to make CHRIS improvements. However, significant number of respondents were unaware of, or had limited understanding of the wide-ranging benefits and issues involved with the assimilation of IT. Others were sceptical of the need for improvement.

In most of the exemplary performers, IT literacy and skills were integrated in the organization. Familiarity with good information management practices and knowledge of technology developments permeated the organizational culture, enabling the implementation of more strategic solutions to business problems. In fact, the push for continual improvement often came from the bottom of the management hierarchy. Management briefings, to emphasize the potential for applying IT, may be an essential step in getting greater value from a CHRIS.

## 5.1. The bottom line

The judicious implementation of IT can facilitate organizational change $[9,45]$ and enable the development and maintenance of competitive advantages $[2,36]$ . The superior use of technology and a talented workforce offers an attractive blend for organizational improvement. The greatest benefits occur when the use of information systems evolves from bookkeeping to managerial science $[20]$ . Many companies had significantly improved their organizational performance on an incremental basis by developing their CHRIS and focus or leveraging the activities of their human resources. Only in rare cases was IT facilitating more dramatic work redesigns or helping to break down functional barriers in the company.

Benchmarking human resource activities and related information systems can identify potential sources of competitive advantages and areas where improvements are imperative or possible. The use of a framework described by Broderick and Boudreau can help managers relate competitive objectives and IT investments $[2]$ while that developed by Martinsons $[22]$ would be useful in choosing among information systems assimilation strategies. A benchmarking study often precedes a major organizational change. An effective CHRIS provides its greatest value when planning and enacting that organizational change. IT applications should directly support HR activities, which in turn must respond to, and grow with, the organization and its ever-changing environment.

Implementation of a IT-based plan for organizational improvement is a major endeavour involving assorted resources and risks. However, the predominance of U.S.-produced software and imported hardware in Hong Kong suggests that technology acquisition would no longer be a significant barrier for developing a CHRIS. Although end-users are taking greater control and responsibility for their CHRIS [8], the supply of information system specialists is a potential bottleneck. The rate of IT application in HRM could now depend on the function's own ability to train or recruit technically-competent managers, employees and contractors. In Hong Kong, the shortage of senior technical professionals was found to have significantly impeded the development and implementation of new CHRIS applications. The migration of systems professionals and technical managers from Hong Kong is reducing IT skill shortages in Canada while exacerbating the problem in the territory. Similar predicaments could befall companies in other developing economies.

Political and cultural factors, rather than macroeconomic or technical factors, accounted for much of the difference in IT use between Canadian and Hong Kong HRM organizations. This implies that IT users, such as business managers and government policy-makers, rather than the IT providers, such as computer scientists and technology vendors, will largely determine the pace and nature of IT assimilation. Business pull will overshadow technology push. Organizational improvement is now imperative and IT can significantly enhance business performance. Managers are left to assess their competitive needs via benchmarking and move forward with IT.
