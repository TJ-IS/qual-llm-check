# The evaluation of strategic information system planning

G. Premkumar $^{a,*}$ , William R. King $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA 50011, USA

$^{b}$ Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA

The potential to obtain substantial benefits from IS planning, as well as the problems encountered in the planning process, have made the evaluation of IS planning an important issue for both researchers and practitioners. A conceptual planning model, which identifies the various components and links of the IS planning system, is used as a framework for evaluation. To validate the framework, a research model is developed and empirically validated using data collected from a survey of planning practices in organization. The results show that information inputs and planning resources are significantly related to the quality of the IS planning process, and quality of integration mechanisms moderates the relationship between information input and the quality of the planning process. Also, the quality of the planning process and quality of implementation mechanisms are significantly associated with performance.

Keywords: IS planning; Strategic IS planning; Evaluation of Planning systems; Planning system model; Planning and performance

## 1. Introduction

Over the last two decades the rapid evolution and growth in use of information systems (IS) technology has created a challenging environment for IS management. Information systems are increasingly being used to generate strategic advantage for organizations $[11,44]$ . Many firms are diversifying and starting new IS-related businesses based on their strengths in IS. Also, firms such as MCI, VISA and American Airlines are forming value-added business partnerships with other firms, based on synergies in information systems $[29]$ . These opportunities lead to a complex and dynamic environment for the IS function that is significantly affected by both the internal and external business environments.

Strategic IS planning, which includes all planning activities that are directed toward identifying opportunities for using information technology (IT) to support the organization's strategic business plans and to maintain an effective and efficient IS function, is being extensively used to deal with this complex environment. It has become a subject of considerable importance in IS research [6], as well as a key management issue for practitioners [41].

Although IS planning methodologies display face validity, their real validity and effectiveness has been empirically studied only to a limited extent; results have not conclusively demonstrated the efficacy of IS planning [27]. While researchers and consultants have highlighted the necessity to plan and have developed many planning models and methodologies, results from the field are far less encouraging. In a recent study, researchers found that while some firms have had success with the planning process, others have had major problems and some even had to shelve their planning process [15]. Organizations spend significant amounts of resources on IS planning, and therefore are increasingly interested in determining if the benefits are commensurate with the investment. The significant problems with the implementation of IS plans [32] have raised doubts, among some firms, about the quality of the planning process and its efficacy in firms. There is also some evidence that some of the best known and successful strategic information systems were identified and implemented without formal IS planning [49]. These factors have raised serious concerns on the utility of IS planning and made evaluation of IS planning an important management issue.

The simplest and most direct method to evaluate planning systems is to determine if it has an impact on an organization's financial performance. Such an approach, although attractive due to its simplicity, is fraught with problems. IS planning typically tends to provide more intangible than tangible benefits. Also, various other intervening factors influence a firm's performance. Such an analysis, also, does not provide any indication of the possible reasons for the presence or absence of a relationship between IS planning and a firm's performance. Hence, a more comprehensive evaluation of the IS planning systems is required.

This study develops and validates a framework for a comprehensive evaluation of IS planning systems. Its primary objectives are:

(a) to develop a conceptual systems model linking the various components of an IS planning system,

(b) to formulate an approach and develop a research model for the evaluation of an IS planning system, and

(c) to empirically test the model and establish the framework as a valid approach for evaluating an IS planning system.

## 2. Background

The concept of strategic planning for information systems has been discussed for more than two decades $[30,65]$ and various planning models and methodologies have been developed $[17]$ . Zani $[63]$ perhaps first provided the conceptual foundation by defining it as a top-down planning with emphasis on linking organizational business strategies to IS planning. McLean and Soden $[38]$ developed a comprehensive framework for IS planning, describing the different stages of planning and the content of the different levels of plans. King $[25]$ provided a methodology to link the “organization’s strategy set” with the “MIS strategy set”. Rockart $[50]$ developed the “critical success factor” approach to provide better focus for IS planning. Henderson and Sifonis $[20]$ provided a comprehensive top-down model of IS planning. Recently, researchers have questioned the traditional top-down model and have suggested other models $[10,13]$ , but they are yet to be empirically tested.

In the last decade, the development of strategic systems that provide competitive advantage, initiated a variety of process models and methodologies based on Porter's value chain and competitive forces model $[44,62]$ . These models are useful in proactively identifying independent strategic IS applications, but they do not provide much assistance in terms of developing basic information architectures, or developing a comprehensive plan that is integrated with business plans, with current IS applications, and with future IS projects. It is necessary to integrate this analysis with the traditional approach to derive maximum benefits from IS planning.

The methodologies that are used by vendors and consultants generally employ a top-down planning approach, and use the guidelines provided by the models and database theory as their foundation. Many of them rely on computer-based systems to capture and analyze the information collected in the various phases, develop an information architecture, and use that architecture for project identification and systems development $[3,21,35,58]$ .

The key issues, as identified by various researchers, are the requirement of significant resources for planning, the lack of information flow between business and IS planning leading to poor integration between the two planning systems, the lack of top management and user involvement, the lack of expertise among the planning staff, and difficulties in implementing the plans $[16,31,32]$ .

Karimi [24] explicated five major phases in IS planning and implementation: organizational analysis, strategy-to-requirement-transformation logical system design, logical-to-physical transformation, and system implementation. The first phase, analyzing business objectives and strategies and translating them into meaningful guidelines for IS planning, provides the critical information inputs and forms the bases for later stages of planning [65]. The increasing use of strategic information systems and inter-organizational systems that provide value-added business partnerships has made the information flow between business and IS planning systems and the alignment of the two systems very critical [5]. However, this information flow is a major problem in organizations. Various integration mechanisms, such as the participation of IS manager in strategic business planning, are used to facilitate this information flow.

IS planning requires extensive resources, but the availability of adequate resources has been found to be a major problem in organizations. Various software tools are used to improve the efficiency in resource utilization by providing better methods for collection and analysis of information [28].

The implementation of IS plans is also a major problem. Plans are shelved because of a lack of proper implementation plans and migration strategies, lack of resources, the enormity of the changes to existing systems, user and system development groups' resistance to change, and the existing backlog $[7,16,32]$ . Hence, proper review and feedback mechanisms and good implementation strategies are considered vital for the success of the overall planning system.

## 2.1. The evaluation of IS planning

There has been very limited research on developing models for evaluating planning systems. King [27] describes a comprehensive evaluation model that develops an overall evaluation profile based on an assessment of the various components and links of the planning system, rather than solely on the assessment of the impact on the firm's financial performance. It provides for an assessment of the inputs to the planning system, the quality of the planning process, and the impact of IS planning on business performance. This approach has diagnostic value as it helps to identify the weak links in the planning system using both internal and external standards. The components identified in the model are informational inputs, IS planning goals, planning resources, planning system, planning outputs, and business performance. Henderson and Sifonis [20] describe a model for IS planning that highlights the need for evaluating the internal consistency and external validity of the plans.

A few empirical studies have tried to relate planning to organizational performance. McKinsey and Company [37], found that firms that planned EDP activities were more successful (as perceived by top management) than those that did not have any formal planning. Subsequently, McFarlan [36] also found, in a study of 15 companies, a significant link between IS planning and organizational effectiveness. However, Selto and Grove [52] using objective financial measures, could not prove the existence of a relationship between planning and performance. Raghunathan and King [46] studied the link between planning performance (measured using user satisfaction) and the three phases of IS planning – strategic planning, systems planning, and plan implementation. They did not find any relationship between performance and strategic planning phase but found significant relationships with the latter two phases. Raghunathan and Raghunathan [47] found a significant relationship between IS planning system capability and planning effectiveness, which was measured as fulfillment of key IS planning objectives. Goodhue et al., [15] found that firms had mixed success with IS planning in a study of strategic data planning in nine organizations. The inconsistent findings may be attributed to the fact that IS planning is one of many factors that influences organizational performance. The studies also highlight the possibility of obtaining better results by using a more specific measure of IS's contribution to organization's performance.

The findings in IS planning are very similar to the findings in the evaluation of strategic business planning, where conclusive evidence of a relation-

ship between the formalization of business planning practices and financial performance has not been found [43]. This has been attributed to other intervening factors confounding the relationship and the lack of: consideration of intangible benefits, concern for implementation, and concern for changes in firm's strategies in response to changes in the environment. The inconclusive results in the evaluation of IS planning can also be attributed to some of these factors. Because of the inconsistent research findings on evaluation of business planning systems, researchers have suggested using more elaborate evaluation procedures and subjective performance measures to evaluate the impact of business planning systems [26,33,54]. King [26] developed a comprehensive framework for evaluating business planning systems that evaluates each of the system components and generates an overall evaluation profile. He claims that it has greater diagnostic value as it helps to identify the weak links or components in the system so that corrective action can be taken. Researchers have used subjective measures such as fulfillment of planning objectives and organization's competitive performance on salient performance measures as dependent variables to better account for tangible and intangible benefits, and control for the intervening variables [48].

Recently there has been increasing interest in evaluating the impact of strategic information systems on organization's performance [4,11,14]. However, these studies are limited by their focus on a specific system. It is necessary to develop a broader conceptualization of evaluation to better assess IS planning.

## 3. Conceptual model

A planning model, adapted from the models suggested by King [26,27], was used in this study to identify the critical components of the IS planning system. The model is shown in Figure 1.

The primary objective of IS management is to support the organization's pursuit of its business goals and objectives. The “information input” component represents the inputs on business goals and plans, and the IS mission. “Resources” represents the various non-information resources such as time and effort of top management, users, and IS and business planning staff, financial resources, and planning methodology [27].

The “Planning Process” converts the information inputs into a system of plans that provides strategic directions for the IS function $[8]$ . It analyzes the external/internal environment, the business plans, the technology trends, the users' information requirements, and develops an information architecture and a set of plans for managing the IS function.

“Outcome” is the impact of planning on the performance of the organization. It evaluates the organizational context of planning, as well as assesses the efficacy of IS planning $[27]$ .

There are links between the system components: (a) Integration, (b) Efficiency, and (c) Implementation. They are considered as facilitators that strengthen the relationship between the respective system components; e.g., participation of the chief IS executive in strategic business planning (integration) facilitates the transfer of “information inputs” to the “planning process”.

The link between “resources” and “process”, termed “efficiency”, refers to the use of productivity enhancement features such as computerized planning tools (CASE tools) to efficiently utilize the planning resources and improve the quality of the planning process [19].

The link between “planning process” and “outcome”, termed “implementation”, refers to various organizational mechanisms, such as monitoring and feedback systems, that are used to facilitate the implementation of the recommendations of the planning process and obtain the desired outcomes.

## 4. Research model

The research model shown in Figure 2, derived from the conceptual model, provides a framework for empirically evaluating the planning system.

The notion of evaluation used in the research model is to individually evaluate the various system components and linkages in the IS planning system and obtain an overall evaluation profile for the planning system. Hence, each component in the research model is operationalized as an evaluative dimension of the corresponding component/link in the conceptual model. For example, the “quality of planning process” in the research model is an evaluative dimension of “planning process” of the conceptual model.

The “links” between the system components in the conceptual model is best captured by the notion of a “moderator”, a variable that influences either the strength or the form of relationship between two variables [53]. For example, in firms where the “quality of integration mechanisms” is very good, we would expect a stronger relationship between the information inputs and the quality of planning process compared to firms where the quality is not as good. Hence, the three links are shown as moderator variables in the research model.

The research model is a framework for evaluation of IS planning systems. To determine if this approach to evaluation is a valid approach, the research model must be empirically validated. Hence, hypotheses were formulated concerning the relationships between the various components of the research model, and tested using data collected from IS planning practice.

Brief descriptions and rationales for each of the constructs in the model and the research hypotheses are given below.

## 4.1. Quality of the planning process (PROCESS)

The planning process is the set of activities involved in analyzing the external environment for opportunities and threats, assessing the internal environment to identify strengths and weaknesses, analyzing the business plan and its implications for the IS function, forecasting IS technology trends and their effect on the industry and the organization, identifying the users' information requirements, developing an information architecture, and developing a set of strategic programs and plans for managing the IS function. It is best evaluated in terms of the extent of detailed analysis of the various facets of planning, which is assessed by the “quality of the planning process”.

## 4.2. Availability of information inputs (INFOINPUT)

The business plans and IS mission must be evaluated for their availability and whether they actively guide the organization's action. The mere existence of plans may not be sufficient, as in some firms they may be mainly meant for “public relations” purposes rather than for guiding managerial choice. Also, they may not have been updated to reflect the changes in the environment [39].

Since business plans provide the primary directions for the IS function, the quality of these inputs can be expected to have a direct effect on the quality of the planning process. However, some researchers may argue that IS planning is more adaptive and incremental and may not be directly related to information input from business planning $[45,61]$ . A few empirical studies have found that some strategic IS applications have been developed in organizations outside the purview of business planning and IS planning process $[49,51]$ . In spite of some of these contradictory evidences, most IS planning models are based on a rational top-down approach to planning and commercial planning methodologies use a similar approach to planning. Absence of business information input has been found to have a significant impact on IS planning $[31]$ . Hence:

Hypothesis 1: The quality of the planning process will be positively related to the availability of information inputs.

## 4.3. Quality of integration mechanisms (INTEGRATION)

Poor communication by senior management of business information to IS planners could significantly affect the quality of IS planning. In some firms, business strategies and plans exist in the minds of a few senior managers and are not communicated to others in the firm. Researchers suggest the participation of the IS manager in strategic business planning and active interaction between IS and business planners as possible strategies to improve the communication flow between the two planning systems $[5]$ . Hence:

Hypothesis 2: Organizations with “higher” quality integration mechanisms will have relatively stronger positive relationships between the availability of information inputs and the quality of planning processes than those with “lower” quality integration mechanisms.

## 4.4. Adequacy of resources (RESOURCES)

The “adequacy of resources”, is best assessed in terms of two major dimensions – the quality and quantity of resources. While “quantity” relates to the degree of availability of these resources, the “quality” of resources measures whether they perform to the desired standards.

Many firms consider planning as a resource drain that affect their daily operations $[8,56]$ . Unfortunately, IS planning is found to consume extensive amount of resources $[32]$ . A lack of adequate resources could lead to the planning process being reduced in scope, or aborted in the later phases of planning (review, revision etc.), thereby seriously affecting the quality of the overall process. On the other hand, the involvement of top management in IS planning sends a positive signal to the users and generates a conducive planning climate, thereby improving the quality of the planning process $[1,16]$ . Some researchers have suggested shortcuts to reduce the resource requirements without significantly impacting quality, thereby indicating a lack of direct relationship between resources and quality of IS planning. However, the impact of some of these strategies on IS planning has not been studied. On the contrary, there is empirical evidence to indicate that inadequate resources was one of the major problems in IS planning $[32]$ .

A related issue is the quality of resources. If top management and users have a clear understanding of the potential of IS technology in their business operations they will be able to identify new opportunities and to provide useful inputs to IS planning $[23]$ . Also, the quality of the planning methodologies and the skills of the project leader have been found to enhance the quality of the planning process $[28]$ . Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: The quality of the planning process will be positively related to the adequacy of resources.

## 4.5. Efficiency (EFFICIENCY)

The lack of automation of planning has been cited as one of the major problems in IS planning. Productivity enhancement tools and integrated CASE tools not only improve the utilization of planning resources but also provide features that enhance the quality of the planning process. Since resources are limited, efficient utilization of resources should result in a better quality planning process. Hence:

Hypothesis 4: Firms with higher efficiency in the utilization of resources will have relatively stronger positive relationships between adequacy of resources and quality of planning processes than those with lower efficiency in the utilization of resources.

## 4.6. Performance

The ultimate performance of any planning must be reflected in the organization's performance. An important objective of IS planning is to integrate it with business planning to enable the organization achieve its business objectives and improve its performance. IS planning is also responsible for identifying strategic IS applications that should significantly improve the performance of the organization. The planning process helps the participants to better understand the potential of IS technology and perhaps use that knowledge for making more informed decisions that will have a long-term impact on the performance of the organization. Hence, it can be expected that the quality of the planning process will have a positive impact on the performance of the organization.

Despite its logical and intuitive appeal, there is only limited evidence linking IS planning to organizational performance. In strategic business planning researchers have had mixed findings in linking planning to objective financial measures, but have had better success with subjective measures of performance $[48]$ . Some researchers have questioned the payoffs from information systems $[55]$ . However, recent studies in IS have found a positive relationship between strategic information systems and organizational performance $[4,14]$ . Hence we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5: The quality of the planning process will be positively related to IS's contribution to the organization's performance.

## 4.7. Quality of the implementation mechanisms (IMPLEMENTATION)

The extent to which the strategic choices derived from the planning process is reflected in the final outcome is dependent on the quality of implementation mechanisms used in the organization. The results of an excellent planning process may be put in cold storage if suitable organizational control mechanisms are not in place. It is not only important to perform detailed planning but also ensure that the recommendations from the planning process are implemented to have the desired effects on the organization. IS planning implementation has been a major problem for IS managers. Hence:

Hypothesis 6: Organizations with better quality implementation mechanisms will have relatively stronger positive relationships between the quality of the planning process and performance than those with lower quality implementation mechanisms.

## 5. Research design

## 5.1. Operationalization of research variables

Based on the guidelines provided by Churchill [9], the research variables were defined as succinctly as possible with multiple indicator items. All items were measured on a seven point Likert-type scale.

Availability of information inputs (4 items) assessed the availability of information inputs and the level of dependence on these inputs for guiding managerial action. The adequacy of resources (7 items) assessed the quality and quantity of various resources (top management, user, IS staff, planning methodology) used in IS planning. Efficiency was assessed by a single item. The quality of integration (2 items) and implementation mechanisms (4 items) were assessed by the quality of the mechanisms discussed in the earlier section. The quality of planning process was measured using 18 items that evaluated the three major dimensions – analysis of external/business, internal, and technology environment – and also evaluation with respect to external standards of good planning practice.

Performance can be measured using either objective financial performance measures (such as ROI, income, sales etc.) or subjective measures. The inconsistent results from using financial measures and the degree of success in the use of perceptual measures in prior studies motivated us to use subjective measures that captured the respondent's perception of the contribution of IS to the firm's performance measures. The head of the IS function indicated the extent of IS's contribution to five major performance measures on a seven point Likert-type scale. We recognized that the variation in the focus of the firm could create problems in the measurement. For example, some may focus on using IS to increase its sales, while others may use it for cost reduction through improvement of efficiency in internal operations. Hence, respondents were also asked to allocate 100 points among the five performance measures based on their importance to the firm. IS Contribution to organization's performance was measured as an average of their contribution to the five measures, weighted by their importance rating. We recognize the possibility of some method bias from using a single respondent from a firm. However, since the respondents were senior executives we expect them to provide unbiased responses.

## 5.2. Data collection

Since a large proportion of studies on IS planning are based on case-oriented research, researchers have found it difficult to generalize their results across a wide variety of organizations. After evaluating the study objectives, and the concerns of external validity and generalizability, a field survey was determined to be the most appropriate research methodology for this study.

The data were collected using a survey questionnaire. It was pre-tested with advanced doctoral students for content validity, general format, and readability. Subsequently it was also pre-tested with three faculty. After incorporating the feedback from pre-testing, it was pilot tested with three senior IS executives in industry.

In order to ensure that the responses reflect the organization's perspective of IS planning and cover the strategic aspects of IS planning, the questionnaire was sent to the senior executives of the IS department, typically the Vice President of MIS or the Chief Information Officer (CIO). The target population included 720 firms, randomly chosen from The Corporate 1000 [59], a directory of the 1000 largest manufacturing and service companies in the USA. Two hundred and forty ninc responses were received for a response rate of $35\%$ . The sample characteristics are illustrated in Table 1.

The respondents were reasonably well distributed across industry groups and sales revenue levels, thereby providing greater generalizability of the results to the population of large manufacturing and service firms. An analysis of the respondents who provided their organizational title indicate that a significant number of them were, in fact, senior IS executives.

## 5.3. Validity and reliability of the research constructs

The constructs were tested for various validity and reliability properties. Validity is the degree to which an instrument measures the construct under investigation, reliability measures the stability of the scale based on an assessment of the internal consistency of the items measuring the construct [9].

The content validity of the constructs, which evaluates if all aspects of the constructs are being measured was established through the process of selecting the items and refining them through pre-testing and pilot testing.

Construct validity, which evaluates if all the items measuring a construct cluster together and form a single construct, was assessed by examining the correlation matrix of the items and by using principal component factor analysis with “varimax” rotation. The results of factor analysis for each of the constructs are given in Table 2.

They indicate that the items measuring most of the constructs (except one) exhibited sufficient validity. The items measuring the construct, “quality of the planning process”, factored into four separate dimensions that were easily interpretable and matched with the dimensions that we initially envisaged in the operationalization of the construct. To obtain an overall measure of the quality of the planning process, it was decided to aggregate the items measuring these four dimensions. Such an approach has been extensively used in MIS research, especially in the context of measurement of user information satisfaction $[22,57]$ .

## 6. Results

Multiple regression analysis was used to determine the significance of the relationships among variables. Two separate regression models were utilized – one for the input side and another for the output side. Since, both the models have moderating variables – quality of integration mechanisms and efficiency on the input side, and quality of implementation mechanisms on the output side – moderated regression analysis $[2,53]$ was used. In moderated regression analysis the additive and interactive models are compared to determine the significance of the moderating effects. In the additive model the moderating variable is considered as a predictor variable along with other predictor variables in a regression model. In the second model, an interactive term, obtained by multiplying the moderator variable with the predictor variable that it is moderating, is added to the earlier model and the regression model is estimated. In practice, instead of individually estimating the two models, the “forced entry” option in regression analysis can be used to estimate the effect of the interactive term in a single run. In order to overcome statistical problems of potential multicollinearity between the main and interaction terms in these regression models, it is preferable to examine the change in $R^{2}$ ( $AR^{2}$ ) and the statistical significance of this value as each independent variable is entered into the regression equation [40,60]. Table 3 provides the results of moderated regression model for the input side.

The overall explanatory power of the model was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001) with adjusted $R^{2}$ at 0.371. Three variables – resources, information input, and the interaction term between information input and quality of integration mechanisms (INFO-INTEG) – were found be significant providing support for hypotheses 1, 2, and 3. No support was found for hypothesis 4, relating efficiency with the quality of the planning process.

Table 4 provides the results of moderated regression model for the output side.

The overall explanatory power of the model was significant (p < 0.001) with adjusted $R^{2}$ at 0.221. Quality of the planning process was a significant variable providing support for Hypothesis 5. The interaction term between the quality of the planning process and the quality of implementation mechanisms (PROC-IMPL) was not found to be significant thereby not supporting Hypothesis 6. However, the quality of implementation mechanisms was found to be significant indicating the possibility that it may be a independent predictor of planning performance.

## 7. Discussion

The results provide some support for the proposed IS planning evaluation framework. Information input and resources were found to significant predictors of the quality of the planning process, with the quality of integration mechanisms moderating the relationship between information input and the quality of the planning process. Performance is associated with the quality of the planning process. Quality of implementation mechanisms, instead of being a moderator, is a predictor of performance.

## 7.1. Information inputs

The results confirm the importance of information inputs from business planning for IS planning. In most IS planning methodologies, the identification of business objectives and plans forms the first phase of the IS planning process. However, the biggest problem in practice is converting that information into a form that is useful for IS planning. Sometimes the business information is not sufficiently detailed to be of use in IS planning, or there is difficulty in seeing its relevance to IS operations. For example, a firm may be planning to use a product differentiation strategy to increase its market share but may not have any information on how the IS function can help in supporting that strategy, thereby making it difficult for the IS manager to see the relevance of the business plans to IS operations. This stems from a lack of understanding of the potential of IS technology within the firm, as well as the IS manager not able to appreciate the business implications of information systems.

Another important information input from senior management is the “technology vision” and a clear mission for the IS function $[64]$ . Unfortunately, in many organizations top management may not have a clear perception of the role of IS in their business operations and may still consider it as a staff support function that does not require their involvement or guidance. In such instances, IS planning will suffer from a lack of direction.

## 7.2. Quality of integration mechanisms

An organization must have good quality integration mechanisms to bridge the business and IS planning systems. The participation of the IS manager in business planning not only helps him/her develop a broader perspective of the organization and IS's role in the operations, but also provides him/her an opportunity to highlight the potential opportunities available with IS technology to help the organization realize its business objectives.

The development and use of inter-organizational systems and systems that generate competitive advantage make it important to have a continuous flow of information between the two planning systems, through active interaction between business and IS planners. Since information systems are increasingly being influenced by the external environment these integration mechanisms enable the planning process to react quickly to the changes in the business environment.

## 7.3. Adequacy of resources

The importance of resources need to be considered in an organizational context where planning is considered as a resource drain and planning resources are typically obtained by diverting resources from operational activities. The scarcity of human resources further strains the IS manager and may force him/her to either conduct the planning exercise without adequate resources, thereby affecting the scope and quality of the planning process, or forego planning and resort to “firefighting” as an approach to managing the function.

Other important resources, such as the time and effort of top management and users, are usually not within the control of the IS manager. New integrated CASE tools have reduced the planning resource requirements. Organizations are also experimenting with other strategies such as reducing the scope of the planning exercise, targeting only the high impact areas, using a phased approach to planning, and inbuilding some of the activities as part of routine activities.

A closely related issue is the quality of the resources. A planning participant who does not understand the potential of IS technology may not be able to provide the inputs that are most useful for IS planning. Also, an IS planner without a good appreciation of the business environment may not be very effective. Hence, appropriate training programs have to be provided to enable the participants to be effective in the planning process.

## 7.4. Efficiency

In this study, efficiency was not found to be a significant variable. This may be due to the simplicity of the measure or it may suggest that the high hopes that exist for automating planning processes are misplaced.

## 7.5. Performance

The fact that the quality of the planning process (PROCESS) is significantly associated with the two inputs, information input and resources, and is also related to performance, provides empirical support for the basic input-process-output relationship in the research model (Figure 2).

The significant relationship between the quality of the planning process and performance establishes the utility of planning. The planning process creates awareness of the potential of IS among the planning participants and generates sufficient interest and momentum to achieve the desired performance. The significant link between IS planning and business process redesign and organizational performance provides support for using IS to improve organizational performance [18]. The results also indicate that quality of implementation mechanisms is a significant predictor of performance highlighting the need to convert the strategic choices derived from the planning process into desired outcomes.

An analysis of IS contribution to the five organizational performance measures (Table 5), reveal that the highest contribution was toward improving the internal efficiency of operations, which is the traditional role of IS in automating clerical operations and improving office productivity. This is understandable since this is the most common justification that is used for getting systems cleared by a steering committee. IS planning is increasingly being used for business process re-engineering, thereby significantly improving the productivity of business operations [12]. Customer satisfaction is the next highest contribution, which reflects the service role of the IS function and the frequently used criterion for project prioritization and clearance. The contribution of IS to the three objective performance measures – sales revenue, market share, and ROI – were lower.

## 8. Summary

Planning is intuitively appealing and logical and hence its worth is often assumed. However, since planning often consumes significant resources and promises the potential of substantial benefits, it is essential to assess its utility to the organization. This study identifies the various components and links of the IS planning system that need to be evaluated, provides a model and measurement instruments for its evaluation, and empirically validates that model using data from practising senior IS executives. This study has also tested the relationships among the elements of the IS planning system that have heretofore been “known” only through prescriptions based on individual experience and case studies.

The instrument for evaluating the various components of the IS planning system may be used as a diagnostic tool to analyze the planning system and identify the weak system components and links. For example, if an organization finds that it has a very good quality planning process, but lacks in all the performance measures, then the quality of the implementation mechanisms could be evaluated to determine the causes for low performance.

The availability of an instrument for measuring IS planning quality should provide a good foundation for future research in this area. Future research could address some of the issues that were outside the scope of this study. For example, the study did not consider the effect of external environmental factors on the IS planning system. It would be useful to determine their impact on the planning process and planning performance. Another possibility is the study of the impact of integrated CASE tools on IS planning. These tools provide computer-support for all phases of system development and are believed to have significantly improved the efficiency in the utilization of resources, as well as the quality of planning. However, the preliminary findings related to efficiency in this study suggest that a more detailed analysis of their impact may be necessary to understand their role in IS planning.
