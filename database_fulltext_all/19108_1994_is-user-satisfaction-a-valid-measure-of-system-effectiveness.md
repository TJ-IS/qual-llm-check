# Is user satisfaction a valid measure of system effectiveness?

Amy W. Gatian

University of Tennessee, Knoxville, TN, USA

User satisfaction (US) is often used as a surrogate measure of information system effectiveness. If an effective system is defined as one that adds value to the firm, then an effective system must have some positive influence on user behavior (i.e., improve productivity, decision making, etc.). Advocates of US argue that there is theoretical support for linking attitudes (i.e., satisfaction) and behavior in the psychology literature. At the same time, there is evidence of increasing employment of US questionnaires in firms as a measure of system effectiveness. Yet there is surprisingly little information systems research linking user satisfaction with user behavior. In this study, measures of user satisfaction and system affected behavior are taken for an indirect and a direct user group of the same information system in 39 organizations. Results indicate that a relationship does exist between satisfaction and behavior for both user groups.

Keywords: User satisfaction; System effectiveness; Decision-making performance; User efficiency; Information quality; Information system success; User productivity; Indirect users; Direct users; User experience

## 1. Introduction

Many researchers and practitioners agree that emphasis in IS research has shifted from efficiency measures toward effectiveness measures, including user perceived effectiveness measures such as user satisfaction $[78]$ . Indeed, increasing use of user satisfaction (US) questionnaires in firms as a measure of system effectiveness is further evidence of this shift $[15,18]$ . In the information systems literature, two primary reasons for a shift toward US in particular are frequently mentioned. The first reason is that many believe in the psychological expectancy theory that attitudes (i.e., satisfaction) are linked to behavior (i.e., productivity) $[23,24]$ . More to the point, it is believed that satisfied users will be more productive. The second reason for the shift away from using efficiency measures in IS evaluation is that it has traditionally been very difficult to measure white collar efficiency or productivity directly $[75,81]$ .

If an effective system is defined as one that adds value to the firm, any measure of system effectiveness should reflect some positive change in user behavior, i.e., improved productivity, fewer errors or better decision making. The implicit assumption made by managers and researchers employing US questionnaires for system effectiveness evaluation is that satisfied users will perform better than users with poor or neutral attitudes toward the system $[3]$ . This is controversial, since surprisingly little information systems research exists linking user satisfaction with any measures of user behavior. The one possible exception to this is research attempting to link satisfaction with system usage, but system usage does not necessarily translate into improved productivity or effectiveness, especially where usage is mandatory.

Interestingly, while there is some evidence in the psychology literature that a positive correlation exists between job satisfaction and job performance, critics of US as a measure of system effectiveness point out evidence in the same literature that satisfaction and performance may not be correlated $[13,27,38,60,79,86]$ . Critics have also noted problems with assuming that user satisfaction and job satisfaction are equivalent. An employee can love their job and hate the information system, or hate their job and love the information system. Furthermore, some opponents point to evidence that collectively, the various and different US questionnaires do not all measure the same construct, and that improper choice of questionnaire and/or careless interpretations of results could cause managers and researchers to draw poor conclusions $[48]$ . For instance, should the managers of XYZ Co. employ an instrument that measures satisfaction with MIS in general or just the product produced by MIS? Also, what does it really mean to the managers of an organization if users are generally satisfied or dissatisfied with either MIS or its product? Clearly, satisfied users are better than dissatisfied users, but does user satisfaction translate into system effectiveness?

This study represents an early, if not the first, empirical study investigating the validity of US as a measure of system effectiveness by looking at the relationship between user satisfaction and user performance for a particular system. Within the limitations of this study, user satisfaction is highly correlated with two measures of performance: the system affected decision-making performance of users and system affected user efficiency.

The research focuses on the users of a college and university information system at 39 different campuses. Two groups of users were studied: those in the controller's office who are direct users and academic department heads who are indirect users. The direct users have the dual roles of suppliers to and customers of the system. They are suppliers because they are responsible for transaction processing and report generation. They are customers since they use output from the system for decision-making. The indirect users play only the role of customers of the system: they obtain information for decision-making.

Users in both groups were asked to assess, via questionnaire, their own satisfaction and the impact of the information system on their own decision-making performance. Also, measures of transaction processing correctness and report generation timeliness were collected as measures of efficiency for the direct users, since they are also responsible for these activities. Finally, based on findings from previous research, variables such as age, sex, education and experience with the information system were observed in order to control for their possible effects [85].

LISREL was used to analyze relationships among the variables. Data for the direct users and the indirect users were analyzed separately, since mean responses to questions were significantly different across groups. For each group, there was a significant relationship between US and decision-making performance. For the direct users there was also a significant relationship between US and processing correctness and timeliness. Experience was the only demographic variable with a statistically significant affect on these relationships.

## 2. Background

Perceived effectiveness measures such as user satisfaction (US) are popular in practice and research as measures of system effectiveness at all stages of system design, implementation and evaluation $[17,77]$ . Yet, the justification for the use of US questionnaires is based solely on extrapolations of theoretical arguments from other disciplines. These arguments have been challenged by some researchers. Furthermore, there is evidence of widespread confusion about which questionnaires to use and how to use them.

User satisfaction (US) was initially advocated as a surrogate measure for the utility or success of an information system by Nolan and Seward [62]. Subsequently, the list of researchers advocating development and employment of US measures has become extensive [e.g., 40,42,53,58,61,74,83].

The general argument employed by advocates for the construct validity $^{1}$ of US as a system effectiveness measure is that satisfied users should perform better than dissatisfied users and if the information system helps users perform better, the system is successful. There are at least three important reasons why we should take a closer look at the justification for measuring US as a surrogate for information system success.

First, many researchers point out evidence in the psychology literature that job satisfaction is not necessarily related to productivity and note the importance of using caution when interpreting the meaning of high or low US scores from questionnaires in research and in practice. Those concerned with the possible misinterpretation of US scores emphasize that high levels of user satisfaction do not guarantee changes in user behavior or system effectiveness. These authors refer to the works of Brayfield and Crockett [9], Locke [55], Vroom [88], and Iaffaldano and Muchinsky [37], pertaining to job satisfaction, in which results indicate little or no evidence that satisfied workers are more productive in terms of enhanced overall job performance. The implications of these findings are that expectancy theory alone may not be enough to support use of user satisfaction as a surrogate for system success.

Second, while findings from psychology studies should perhaps be more carefully considered in further US research, job satisfaction is not the same thing as user satisfaction. Employees can be very dissatisfied with their jobs or their positions in a company, but still be satisfied with the performance of the information system. Therefore, it is crucially important to study the relationships between user satisfaction and behavior affected by system use instead of relying upon evidence for or against the existence of such relationships from another discipline. An understanding of these relationships is important to the refinement and interpretation of US instruments.

Finally, we need to take a closer look at these relationships because there is increasingly greater use of a wide variety of user satisfaction questionnaires in practice to evaluate information system effectiveness at all stages of system design, implementation and evaluation. Improper choice of questionnaires and/or careless interpretations of questionnaire results could cause managers to make poor decisions.

Kim [48] discusses the problems caused by the variety of instruments currently in use. He suggests that while US instruments have frequently been misused in research and in practice, US measures may be successfully employed if the organizational setting and purpose of the investigation are carefully considered before selecting an instrument. Kim categorizes previously developed questionnaires as measures of attitudes toward MIS, satisfaction with information quality, or satisfaction with MIS effectiveness and suggests appropriate applications of questionnaires from each category. For example, if an employer is interested in measuring a user's general satisfaction with the MIS department's contribution toward organizational effectiveness, then a questionnaire measuring satisfaction with MIS effectiveness should be employed. However, if the purpose of the investigation is to determine the users' satisfaction with specific information provided by the system and employed by the user to perform some task, then a questionnaire designed to measure satisfaction with information quality should be selected.

Similarly, construct validation of US instruments from each of the three different categories would require the researcher to choose completely different comparative measures. Within this context, several independent studies should be conducted matching US instruments from each of the different developmental perspectives with carefully selected measures of effectiveness. Clearly, determination of the overall construct validity of user satisfaction as a system effectiveness measure cannot be done through an analysis of arbitrarily chosen or universal measures.

## 3. The study

This study is a step toward construct validation of user satisfaction as a measure of system effectiveness. If US is to be used as a surrogate for system effectiveness, it is intuitively appealing that satisfaction with information quality (US-information quality) would have a more direct impact on user performance, and thus system effectiveness, than satisfaction with MIS or MIS effectiveness. Therefore, of the three developmental perspectives, satisfaction with information quality was selected for this study. Further, proponents of US-information quality argue that cross disciplinary theoretical support for the use of instruments with this specific developmental perspective come from psychology studies [66,80], indicating a relationship between decision performance and availability of relevant information, and from the management literature [36], which links decision-making performance and overall organizational goal achievement. Thus, theoretically, users that are satisfied with information quality will make better decisions and by making better decisions a higher level of organizational goal achievement is attained.

Two groups of users of an information system were studied across thirty-nine different sites. US questionnaires measuring satisfaction with information quality and decision performance questionnaires measuring system affected decision performance were administered to both user groups. In addition, direct measures of transaction processing correctness and report generation timeliness were independently collected from the office of the direct users. These were used as objective measures of system affected efficiency for the direct users.

## 3.1. The system and its users

Participants in this study were college and university users of a financial accounting and accounts payable application system. This on-line system has been implemented in approximately 260 institutions, consisting primarily of colleges and universities in the United States. It was chosen for several reasons. First, it is well documented and is used by many universities, providing a large population of homogeneous users for sample selection. Also, since the system has been used by many organizations for several years, the study environment is more mature and stable than many system environments. Additionally, testing relationships in organizations using the same information system strengthens the internal validity of the study. Testing relationships across organizations with dissimilar information systems would introduce too many different moderating factors to draw conclusions from the results of the study.

Another reason for choosing this system is that there are generally two types of user groups within a university, allowing for examination of US and effectiveness from different perspectives. First, there are frequent, direct users within the administrative accounting department. Some of these, typically clerical employees, use the system entirely for transaction processing. Since one of my primary purposes is to analyze the relationship between US and decision performance, these users were not surveyed. The direct users surveyed in this study are divisional managers who are responsible for managing inputs and processing of information, but also use outputs from the system to perform various accounting functions, including preparation and analysis of budgets, miscellaneous financial reports and maintenance of payroll and various university-wide fund entities. The second type is indirect users, consisting of academic department heads and deans who use system outputs and simple data queries explicitly for supporting resource maintenance and distribution decisions for fund accounts including research and gift accounts and for preparing and maintaining departmental budgets.

## 3.2. Measuring user information satisfaction

The Jenkins and Ricketts questionnaire, a Likert-type scale instrument, was chosen as the most appropriate instrument to measure US-information quality. Several questionnaires have been developed to measure US-information quality [e.g., 42,53,63,82].

Of the questionnaires measuring user satisfaction with information quality, the Jenkins and Ricketts questionnaire is the most rigorously tested and the most frequently cited and used $[22,45,47]$ . Further support for this questionnaire was confirmed by the fact that users from ten universities participating in a pilot test of the survey instrument had no difficulty in understanding or completing questions taken from it $^{2}$ .

## 3.3. Measuring performance

Decision-Making Performance. To be consistent with the portion of the survey instrument measuring satisfaction, Likert-type scale questions were designed to measure how the system affects decision-making performance. Extensive unstructured interviews with a sample of users from both user groups were conducted to identify specific categories of decision types for each user group. An extensive review of the literature dealing with white collar productivity and information system effectiveness was conducted to determine the best way to articulate the decision performance questions. A questionnaire developed by Sanders [73] was used initially as a model for phrasing questions in this portion of the survey, because it includes several Likert-type scale questions designed to measure improvements in decisions similar to those made by users in this study. After the first draft of the entire survey instrument was complete, two pilot studies were conducted to test the content validity and readability of the instrument. Comments and suggestions from participants in these studies were used to improve the content and structure of all questions before distributing the questionnaire in the primary study. Feedback from participants also provided assurance that the list of decision types was complete and representative of decisions made using this system's output at most colleges and universities. Cronbach's alpha test revealed coefficients ranging from 0.94 to 0.98, providing assurance of the internal reliability of this portion of the questionnaire. ## 3.4. Personal variables

The remaining questions in the questionnaire consist of descriptive and personal variables. Personal questions were included based upon the results of an extensive meta analysis conducted by Zmud [89]. By synthesizing the results from previous research, he concluded that several demographic variables may have an impact on overall MIS success. Specifically, past research suggests that males, older individuals, and less educated individuals exhibit less positive attitudes toward MIS. O'Reilly [63] also found a negative correlation between level of education and frequency of information file use. Questions were therefore included for sex, age and education. It was anticipated, however, that the users in each of the groups in this study would have similar education levels (i.e., doctorate degrees for department heads and bachelor's or master's degrees for managers) and education level might not have an effect on the results of this study. It was also considered that the level of experience each user has had with the system and with similar information systems could affect user satisfaction. Therefore questions were included to measure the length of time respondents had used the system, length of time they had used similar systems, and frequency of use. All of the descriptive and personal questions were included to test for any moderating effects on the relationship between satisfaction and decision performance or efficiency.

## 4. Methodology

Six questionnaires were distributed through the controller at each of 100 colleges and universities. It was established, through informal interviews at a regional support meeting, that most controllers felt the results of this study could immediately help them improve satisfaction and performance at their university. Since the controllers attending the meeting generally showed enthusiasm for the study, it was believed that a higher response rate would be obtained if the controllers were responsible for distribution and collection of the questionnaires than if participants were individually responsible for returning them. The list of administrative accounting variables used for computing the efficiency ratios was included in the packet sent to the controller along with explicit instructions for completion of all materials.

Three questionnaires were to be distributed to the managers of General Accounting, Accounts Payable, and Financial Reporting within the administrative accounting office of each participating university. Employee titles were specified in advance to reduce the potential for selection bias on the part of the controller. For example, if the controller had been told to distribute the questionnaires to any three employees in the office, there might be a tendency to choose the three people who have used the system the most, or are the most satisfied with the system. The three particular positions were chosen because they are the primary managerial users of the system in most university administrative accounting offices.

Questionnaires were also distributed through the controller to the heads of the Biology, Accounting, and Chemistry departments. The rationale for choosing these was similar to that used in choosing the controller's office managers. Prior selection was made to reduce the potential for bias, and the three particular departments were chosen because they exist in most universities. If the college or university did not have one or more of the academic departments mentioned, the controller was asked to distribute the questionnaires to another department, and to make a note of the participating department as well as the department it was replacing. However, since three of the most common academic departments for colleges and universities were prespecified, substitution was minimized.

After the initial mailings and a series of follow up letters and telephone calls, a total of 39 colleges and universities (39%) returned packets with completed questionnaires. However, some of the schools that responded did not send back all six of the questionnaires that were originally mailed to them. Completed questionnaires were received from 108 administrative accounting managers and 79 academic department heads for response rates of 35.7% and 25.6% respectively. T tests revealed no difference between mean responses from participant responses received before versus after the follow up dates.

## 5.1. Research hypotheses

While both groups use output from the system for decision-making, direct users are also responsible for managing the input and processing of accounting transaction data. In this study, the indirect users only interact with the system to generate reports for decision-making. Further, the indirect users' only control over report content is to discuss proposed report changes with systems personnel. In this context, user performance related to information system utilization may be measured by changes in decision-making performance as a result of output interpreted by indirect users. Since the direct users manage inputs and processing as well as utilize outputs for decision-making, direct user effectiveness may be measured by changes in decision-making performance from output utilization, as well as by data processing correctness and report generation timeliness (i.e., efficiency). Therefore, the following hypotheses are formulated to test the relationship between US-information quality and user performance:

H1: There is a positive relationship between US-information quality and decision-making performance for indirect users.

H2: There is a positive relationship between personal variables, including experience, age, education and sex and US-information quality for indirect users.

H3A: There is a positive relationship between US-information quality and decision-making performance for direct users.

H3B: There is a positive relationship between US-information quality and efficiency for direct users.

H3C: There is a positive relationship between decision-making performance and efficiency for direct users.

H4: There is a positive relationship between personal variables, including experience, age, education and sex and US-information quality for direct users.

The hypotheses for the different user groups (i.e., direct and indirect) were tested separately, because an initial analysis of the data revealed significantly different mean responses from the two groups.

## 6. Data analysis

LISREL VII [43,44], a statistical package designed to analyze covariance structures was used to test the relationships for each user group. LISREL can be conceptualized as a combination of factor analysis and multiple regression. Latent variables, or hypothetical constructs such as satisfaction and decision-making performance, are unobserved variables whose value is inferred by measuring several observable variables. LISREL is analogous to factor analysis. LISREL also measures the strength of relationships among various latent variables in a fashion similar to multiple regression. Further, LISREL provides the user with something that factor analysis and multiple regression do not: an assessment of the overall fit of the user's model. Fit is measured by the goodness of fit index (GFI) and the root mean square residual (RMSR). GFI is a measure of the combined relative amount of variance and covariance accounted for by the model and RMSR represents average residual variance and covariance. ## 7. Discussion

Overall, this research provides support for the construct validity of US-information quality as a measure of information system effectiveness. Specifically, the following relationships were revealed:

\* The statistically significant and strong relationships revealed for both user groups between US-information quality and decision performance support the psychological theory that availability of relevant information improves decision performance in a modern information systems setting.

\* The statistically significant relationship between US-information quality and efficiency provide further support overall for the construct validity of US-information quality as a measure of system effectiveness. This suggests that satisfied users may be more productive, at least in terms of data processing correctness and report generation and distribution timeliness.

\* Investigation of the relationship between personal variables and US-information quality revealed statistically significant results for only experience levels of direct users and satisfaction. This could be explained by the fact that direct users have more diverse experience, since they process transactions as well as utilize outputs. Experience from utilization of system outputs only is probably less likely to influence overall satisfaction with the system. All correlations are very small and path coefficients calculated in a preliminary analysis revealed no other significant relationships.

## 8. Recommendations for future research

This study represents a systematic effort toward construct validation of US as a measure of system effectiveness. Clearly additional studies are needed. While there are at least three different conceptualizations of the user satisfaction construct, this study looked only at US-information quality. It was selected because it seemed that the quality of the information product would be the most likely to have a direct impact on decision performance or efficiency. Any practitioners or researchers employing user satisfaction as a measure of information system success in future research should consider the underlying conceptual development of the various US instruments before selecting one. Studies designed to empirically link US with measures of system effectiveness will help users of US questionnaires assess the meaning and value of measuring US in their organizations or studies.

In order to establish the construct validity of a measurement of any phenomenon, it is obvious that more than one study must be conducted and the phenomenon needs to be studied in different settings. For example, exploring the relationship between satisfaction with information quality and decision performance in for-profit organizations could uncover completely different relationships from those found here. However, analyzing the relationship between two specific phenomena requires control over environmental factors.

Researchers could also perform laboratory experiments where participants have a decision-making task to perform using information from a system having both good and bad decision outcomes. Once the users have performed the task, the relationship between their satisfaction and performance on the task could be measured. However, this requires sacrificing external validity for greater internal validity.

## Appendix A

Summary description of measures employed in LIS-REL analysis

Latent Variables - Latent variables are unobservable constructs, typically psychological or behavioral constructs, whose "reality" is inferred from observable variables.

Parameter Estimates - Generally, parameter estimates are analogous to path coefficients or beta weights. Considered in conjunction with the intercorrelations among variables, the reader can interpret individual relationships within the model in much the same way as one would analyze individual relationships among variables in multiple regression analysis.

Standardized Solution - Parameter estimates may be reported in many ways in LISREL analysis. Here, the latent variables from the Standardized Solution (SS) are reported, in which the latent variables are scaled to have variances equal to one (range from -1.0 to +1.0).

Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) - The GFI is a measure of the relative amount of variance and covariance jointly accounted for by the model. Generally a $\mathrm{GFI} > 0.90$ is strong evidence the overall model being tested fits the data very well.

Adjusted GFI (AGFI) - GFI is independent of sample size. AGFI represents the GFI adjusted for degrees of freedom. Chi square is also reported sometimes, but its magnitude is a function of sample size and with large N, as is the case in this study, chi square will almost certainly by significant, and thus meaningless.

Root Mean Square Residual (RMSR) - RMSR is a measure of the average of the fitted residuals and can only be interpreted in relation to the sizes of the observed variances and covariances or the GFI. In general, the smaller the residuals, the better the fit. RMSR $= < 0.10$ or 0.11 indicate an excellent fit.
