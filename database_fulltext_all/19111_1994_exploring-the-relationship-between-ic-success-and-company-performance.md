# Exploring the relationship between IC success and company performance

Tor Guimaraes

Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN, USA

Introduction

Magid Igbaria

Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, USA

As the importance of end-user computing continues to grow, so does the importance of supporting and managing the related end-user activities. To accomplish this, many organizations have established Information Centers. The major hypothesis in this study is that there is a direct relationship between IC effectiveness or performance level, and two major dependent variables: (1) the benefits or payoffs that the organization has derived from end-user computing activities and (2) the organization's business performance. A sample of 252 internal auditing directors provided strong evidence for these relationships; further, the results suggest that the payoffs from end-user computing vary widely among companies and that there is much organizations can do to improve performance in this important area.

Keywords: End user computing; Information center; Critical success factors; Company payoffs; Company performance

## The theoretical framework

The basic hypotheses in this study are that IC performance is directly related to EUC success and that EUC success is directly related to company performance.

As the level of EUC activities in an organization grows, so does the need for some types of control (i.e. acquisition policies and procedures, sharing of resources, quality of systems and information) and end-user support [29,38]. Leitheiser and Wetherbe [30] proposed the notion of service support levels as “formal divisions of responsibility between end-users and MIS departments” as the basis for effectively managing EUC in organizations. The idea hopefully will lead to several advantages to the organization: freedom of choice for end-user managers, focusing of IS department’s attention on providing service to end-users, reduction of “finger pointing,” a structured approach for supporting end-users, incentives for end-users to follow established guidelines and procedures, and better means for coordinating EUC activities.

Starting in the early 1980s and increasing steadily, a variety of support mechanisms became available to the end-user community in many organizations, and the level of support was directly related to the level of control exercised by IS departments [20]. Information Centers were being strongly recommended as necessary for EUC management and support [14,18,19]. In those days, however, less than 60 percent of Fortune 500 companies, and less than 8 percent of all US companies, had “established a minimal set of microcomputer policies” [42].

## IC success

Some organizations have already disbanded their ICs, suggesting an alternative way to managing EUC. This implies a need to investigate IC performance and its value to the organization. Undoubtedly, ICs have evolved over time and are quite different across organizations in terms of their sophistication, size, location, modus operandi, and the variety of services provided to end-users $[17,6]$ . In this study, ICs are very broadly defined to include any group providing support for microcomputer-based EUC.

The most comprehensive and well known attempt at creating a measure for IC performance is the collection of IC Critical Success Factors (CSFs) developed by Magal, et al. [32]. A list of 26 factors were identified from the literature and factor analyzed to produce the five groups shown in Table 1. These 26 CSFs were used to measure IC success in this study.

These measures focus on individual systems and, unless data can be collected on a representative set of systems per company, are unsuitable to assess EUC success from a company-wide perspective. Suitable measures for EUC's impact on the organization had to be found elsewhere.

Two concepts can effectively reflect the contribution of EUC to the overall organization and its strategic mission. One represents the extent of improvements in EUC capabilities to support strategic management; the other focuses on how well EUC helps fulfill company objectives. Both reflect the overall success of EUC. Based on an adaptation and integration of the extensive literature on systems, Venkatraman and Ramanujam [39] conceptualized both dimensions and used them to evaluate the success of planning systems. They rationalized the two constructs as follows: "While the degree of improvement in the system's CAPABILITIES reflects the 'means' or the process aspect of the concept of planning system success, OBJECTIVES, as a dimension, is intended to tap the 'end' or outcome benefits of planning."

This framework has been used for examining the impact of EUC key business objectives on EUC capabilities to support the management of an organization, and, concurrently, for measuring the extent to which EUC capabilities and objectives are fulfilled. Individual end-users benefit from information technology. From a company perspective, the indicator for EUC success depends on the degree it improves six key business objectives: enhancing management development, predicting future trends, evaluating alternatives, improving short- and long-term performance, and avoiding problem areas.

In addition, it is important to examine the degree to which EUC helps in overall strategic management. These represent the ends and means (or output and process) perspectives for evaluating EUC success. It can be seen as a company-wide system that supports efficient and effective end-users operations and the strategic management of the organization. Its capability in supporting company management is measured along the following twelve dimensions:

\- anticipating surprises and crises,

\- identifying new business opportunities,

\- identifying key problems,

\- fostering managerial motivation,

\- enhancing the generation of new ideas,

\- communicating top management's expectations throughout the organizational structure,

\- fostering management control,

\- fostering organizational learning,

\- communicating line managers' concerns to top management,

\- integrating diverse functions and operations,

\- adapting to unanticipated changes, and

\- enhancing innovation.

We hypothesize that the EUC capabilities dimension has a direct effect on the objectives dimension. This hypothesis is based on the notion that the EUC “means” lead to the EUC “end.”

## Company performance

Company performance has also been measured in many different ways [e.g., 37,40]. Many authors have used single items to measure company performance, such as company profitability (return on total assets) [35]. Given the wide variety of ways in which information technology may contribute to a company's performance and the importance of content validity for such a significant measure, we chose a multidimensional scale. Gupta and Govindarajan [21] proposed 12 indicators for assessing company performance: sales growth rate, market share, operating profit, rates of profit to sales, cash flow from operations, ROI, new product development, new market development, R and D activities, cost reduction program, personnel development, and political/public affairs. These indicators were used to measure company performance in this study.

## Method

## Overview of the survey procedure

This study was designed as a comprehensive survey of EUC problems and payoffs in business organizations. A questionnaire was developed for collecting data from a large group of internal auditing directors (IAs). They were asked to gauge the extent of their EUC problems and payoffs. IAs were chosen as respondents; they generally serve two roles – as end-users and as auditors who are likely to be aware of the problems and benefits from EUC activities. Furthermore, the group is relatively homogeneous, and this strengthens internal validity of the data collection instrument. We felt that a survey of end-users or their support personnel would have greater likelihood of bias.

The survey questionnaire was pretested for content validity by six IAs before it was finalized. Several meetings were held to refine the instrument, particularly to examine its readability and reflect the auditors' preferred phraseology. This process improved the wording of many questions.

The final form was distributed to the IAs of 950 organizations randomly selected from a list of approximately 4,000 members of an Internal Auditors Association. This sample should represent a wide variety of organizational settings: small and large companies, and different industry types. Participation was voluntary; the cover letter assured confidentiality of the responses and that only summary information would be published. The survey was accompanied by a postage-paid envelope addressed for direct return.

## Sample description

Through the procedure just described, of the 950 IAs selected, 252 surveys (26.5 percent) were returned. This appears to be consistent with other mail surveys [24,16]. However, it was still necessary to determine the representativeness of the sample. Chi-square tests were used with a sample of non-respondents to check for the possibility of non-response bias. The results of this test support the conclusion that the companies in the sample are quite similar to those in the total population with respect to size and type.

The demographics of the responding firms are presented in Table 2. Relatively large IS budgets (over \$25 million) were reported by 78 percent of the respondents. In addition, more than half of the firms had more than 2,300 personal computers; and nearly 25 percent had more than 6,500 PCs. Most of the EUC support activities are provided by the IS department. They include help desk, coaching, training, maintenance, communications support, and other activities. It was also reported that the user department of most of the responding firms was responsible for backup and recovery and in some cases for coaching and training.

The use of previously validated measures is methodologically a major strength of this study. The difference in scales range, 1 to 7 for IC success and 1 to 5 for EUC success and for company performance, does not represent a problem. Furthermore, to change a scale would undermine its validity. A potentially more serious problem is the subjectivity of the responses. This is a problem common to all empirical research. Its complete elimination is dependent on the availability of objective measures which in most cases are non-existent, very difficult or impossible to implement in practice. This “common method variance” due to self-reporting bias can be a critical problem if the researchers are not careful with their data collection instruments and process. The risk factors involved are: the use of single-item measures, measures not previously validated and/or which lead to biased data. Two of the most relevant articles on this issue are by Spector [36] who directly addresses it, and by Huber and Power [22] who provide a very useful discussion of what causes inaccurate and biased data, and the many things which can be done to manage the problem. We believe this study has effectively dealt with this problem through the use of previously validated multi-item scales and through the re-testing and corroboration of scale validity. Further, the relatively large standard deviations suggest that responses showed no systematic bias toward the center of the scales or indicate no unduly favorable views about the participating organizations.

## IC success

IC level of success was measured with the 26-item scale using a data collection instrument that asked individual IAs to indicate the level of their IC's performance along each of the CSFs. A seven-point Likert scale was provided, with response options ranging from (1) extremely low to (7) extremely high. The measure of CSFs incorporates five groups of factors important to IC success: (1) commitment to the IC concept; (2) quality of IC support services; (3) facilitation of end-user computing; (4) role clarity; and (5) coordination of end-user computing. The five items in each of the groups labeled “commitment to the IC concept” and “role clarity” had reliabilities of alpha = .81 and .78, respectively, the six items of “quality of IC support services” and “facilitation of end-user computing” had alpha reliability estimates of .85 and .86, respectively, and finally the four items of coordination of end-user computing had alpha of .77. Table 3 presents the matrix of intercorrelations among the five factors. In view of high average correlation among the factors, a second order factor analysis was conducted to test for underlying homogeneity in the five factors. The results showed a single factor solution (eigenvalue of 4.12), which accounted for 82.4% of the explained variance. Therefore, the 26 items from the five factors were aggregated and averaged to create a composite measure of overall CSF for IC. The alpha reliability coefficient of the resulting 26-item scale was.96, confirming the homogeneity of the items and the appropriateness of combing them.

## Company performance

As mentioned earlier, company performance was measured by asking the internal auditing directors to rate the company performance over the past two years with respect to: sales growth rate, market share, operating profit, rates of profit to sales, cash flow from operations, ROI, new product development, new market development, R and D activities, cost reduction program, personnel development, and political/public affairs. The rating options ranged from (1) not at all satisfactory to (5) outstanding. For each respondent, the ratings for the 12 factors were averaged to produce the measure of company performance. The alpha reliability coefficient for this scale was.91. Given the high reliability coefficient for this scale and the absence of reports on a priori factor groupings within this measure, no factor analysis was undertaken.

## Discussion and conclusions

Information Centers represent organizational commitment to nurturing and providing support to end-user computing in the organization. As end-user computing grows, its impact on company performance is expected to grow. Concurrently, end-user computing demand for training, data administration, help desk services, coaching, and a host of other support and management activities will also grow. There are many ways for organizations to provide these services: informally or via formal support groups, and with resources controlled by IS, end-user, or standalone organization units, or with a wide variety of service menus, operating procedures, operational and managerial control. Regardless to how, end-users need support lest a host of computing problems plague the end-user community in insidious ways [e.g., Davis 1982].

The IC important areas of activity (CSFs) have been shown to be strongly related to the level of company payoffs from user computing and, to a lesser extent, to company business performance. Organizations with heavy end-user computing activity should ensure satisfactory performance in CSF areas.

The relatively high correlations among IC performance ratings in the different areas suggest that companies tend to be doing either relatively well or poorly in all five areas. One may surmise that once an organization is aware of the need to support and manage end-user computing and develops resources to do so, they perform a wide variety of activities and services. Though companies surveyed are doing less than well, the moderately large standard deviations indicate that some are performing significantly better than others.

In terms of company payoffs from end-user computing, the organizations, on the average, have shown only “little improvement.” However, the large standard deviations again indicate that some companies are benefiting. On the average, the impact (payoff) of EUC can be felt on a wide range of business activities. Microcomputers and related technology are apparently much more than just toys and status symbols.

Last, IC performance is strongly related to the payoffs the company has derived from EUC. Companies performing well are likely to have mastered the implementation of new technologies. Therefore, one may speculate that these companies have learned to derive greater payoff from EUC by focusing attention and resources on the performance of the important IC functions.
