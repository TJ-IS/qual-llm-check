# Factors affecting the implementation outcome of a mainframe software package: A longitudinal study

Edward G. Cale, Jr.
and Steven E. Eriksen

Babson College, Babson Park, MA, USA

A framework for predicting the likely short and long term outcome of an organization's effort to implement an off-the-shelf software package is proposed and then field tested. The framework considers the initial fit of the proposed system from the perspectives of both the manager/end users and members of their information systems staffs. Due to moderate collinearity among the independent variables, the field test data were analyzed using ridge regression. It was concluded that the short term success of an implementation effort was determined mostly by three factors, as viewed from the perspective of the potential user of the application. Those three factors, in order, were: the relative advantage of the application to the user, the compatibility of the application with the work norms of the user, and the applications lack of complexity or difficulty in use by the potential adopter. Over the long term, while these factors remained significant for continued success, it was important that the application also not be overly complex from the perspective of the information systems support staff. These findings lead to a strategy for software package evaluation and implementation. First, a thorough understanding of the impact of an application on its intended end-users is critical to successful implementation. This understanding is similar to the insight gained from the systems analysis process in custom software development. Second, even in cases where an application fits well with user needs and capabilities, long term viability is also dependent upon a competent and capable information systems support staff.

Keywords: Implementation; Success factors; Software packages; Systems implementation; Implementation strategy

## Introduction

The process of implementing computer-based applications in organizations has received significant attention in recent years. In most published studies of the implementation process, the authors have concentrated on the “normal” case in which organizations are designing, developing and implementing custom systems for their own use. However, an increasingly common method for acquiring computer-based applications is to purchase off-the-shelf “canned” software from vendors who have designed and developed their systems for general use [1], [9]. In this circumstance, the potential implementor of the system has little, if any, contact with the system designers, and the classical system development cycle is substantially modified, if not altogether short circuited.

In this article, a framework for predicting the likely outcome of an organization's effort to implement an off-the-shelf software package is proposed and then tested. Adapting a classic model from the diffusion of technology literature, this paper concentrates on the identification and measurement of the general characteristics of an application which significantly affect the fit between that application and a potential implementor, both in the immediate term and over a time span of several years.

## Framework motivation

A fundamental assumption underlying much of the implementation literature is that the implementation process is one of the final steps in the lengthy and formal system development cycle. As such, the implementation phase is closely linked to prior phases. Indeed, “the design and implementation are inseparable and evolutionary” [13]. Geisler and Rubenstein [7], Ives and Olson [12], Ginzberg [8], Lucas [14], Mumford [16], Davis [4], and a host of other information systems researchers and practitioners suggest that a major key to successful implementation is the establishment of a close working relationship between system developer and end-user. Through this relationship the analyst will come to understand the unique needs and capabilities of the end-user, and will consequently design a system which closely fits the needs of the client. Additionally, through this same interchange, the end-user will come to understand the nature of and reasons for any limitations present in the system, and will become increasingly committed to the success of the system as the design evolves. In keeping with this philosophy, much of the implementation literature to date has dealt with implementation as a broad process combining application development and introduction to end-users.

However, the recent explosion in the use of canned, off-the-shelf software packages has driven a significant wedge between system developer and system user. In many cases, the actual end-users of a package have absolutely no contact with the analysts who designed and developed the application. Thus, for the increasingly common special case of implementation of off-the-shelf software packages, much of our understanding of how to successfully manage the process is rendered moot. Rather than depend upon a close working relationship between developer and client to ensure an appropriate system, we must look for other methods for ensuring the appropriateness of acquired systems.

Without the luxury of custom development of applications software to perfectly fit the needs and capabilities of users, a key question becomes, "How do we ensure that there is an appropriate fit between an existing package and a given user?" Given alternative software packages, what factors should a potential implementor consider in making the decision as to which package to adopt? In this article, a framework is developed and tested for measuring the quality of fit, both in the short term and over an extended period, between an existing application and a potential end-user. It is our hypothesis that this fit will play a major role in determining the likelihood that a potential adopter will, in fact, successfully implement and use the given application.

In this instance, the implementation of a mainframe-based system is examined. Despite recent heavy attention paid to microcomputer based systems in the literature, mainframe-based systems continue to form the foundation of transaction processing and management reporting systems in large firms. Also, the expense and complexity of implementing mainframe-based systems makes them particularly worthy of examination. In addition, as the emerging client/server architecture continues to attract larger and more complex applications, we would expect that many client/server applications would share characteristics with mainframe based systems: they will require professional technical support for their acquisition, installation, implementation, operation, and maintenance, or some part thereof.

## Receptivity framework

Given the paucity of published research directed specifically at the implementation of existing software packages, in the development of the framework for this research it was necessary to examine literature from outside of the information systems implementation field. While the general field of research concerned with the diffusion of technology covers a wide array of situa-

tions, a number of works have very specifically addressed the issue of measuring the fit between a given innovation and its potential adopter.

In their review of the diffusion of technology research, Rogers and Shoemaker [17] surmised that there are five basic characteristics of an innovation which, when viewed from the perspective of potential users, determine the likelihood of acceptance. Those five factors are:

1. Relative Advantage: The degree to which the innovation is perceived by its implementor to be superior to the practice which it is to supersede is a positive force for successful adoption.

2. Compatibility: The degree to which the innovation meshes with the current practices, procedures, and norms of the adopter is a positive force for successful implementation.

3. Complexity: The degree to which a potential adopter finds the innovation to be difficult to understand or use has a negative impact on successful implementation.

4. Trialability: The degree to which a potential adopter is allowed to experiment with an innovation prior to final commitment has a positive impact on successful implementation.

5. Visibility: The degree to which the benefits of adopting an innovation are visible and apparent to the subordinates, peers, and superiors of the adopter has been found to have a favorable impact on implementation outcome.

In reviewing eleven separate experiments, Rogers and Shoemaker found that these five factors explained between 49% and 87% of the variance in rate of innovation adoption. In the Rogers and Shoemaker framework, the experience, training and expectations of a potential innovation adopter play a key role in determining the likelihood of successful assimilation. This observation is in keeping with more recent findings of Srinivasan and Davis [20] regarding individual managers, and with the findings of Ball, Dambolena, and Hennessey [1].

We chose to adopt the Rogers and Shoemaker framework, viewing an off-the-shelf software package as an innovation, and measuring the above five factors to determine likelihood of acceptance. However, while most of the innovators in the Rogers and Shoemaker study were single individuals, we elected to use the framework in an environment in which the innovator is a large, complex organization. In such a situation, who, if anyone, determines or defines the perspective of the organization? Theory and practice from the field of study of organizational change provide the tools for extending the diffusion framework to the case of complex organizations.

A common practice for identification and evaluation of the impact of a given change upon an organization is the segmentation of that organization into a number of component parts, and the examination of the effects of the change upon each of those components. A key property in this concept is that there is no fixed or “optimum” degree of organizational segmentation; the proper choice is contingent on the specifics of the situation under study. Consequently, in independent studies, Souder et. al. [19] chose two levels of segmentation, organizational and personal, while Coffee et. al. [3] used three levels, organization wide, small group, and individual.

We chose to segment the organization into two groups with distinctly different roles and perspectives on the implementation process:

1. the managers who would actually use the system outputs in carrying out their executive functions, and

2. the information systems technical specialists who would be responsible for running and maintaining the system.

## Field study

To determine whether the application fit framework could be used to understand an actual implementation effort, 19 different organizational units of a multi-billion dollar forest products organization operating in the Pacific Northwest were involved in a field study. Each of these units reports to the same regional superior in Portland, Oregon. Each site was visited prior to, immediately after, and then five years after an effort to implement a major, mainframe based, planning and budgeting software package. The application had been developed by a parallel regional unit operating in California. Thus, none of the Northwestern units had played any role in developing the package. The decision to implement the package in the Northwest area was made by the supervising unit in Portland, and was based on the free availability of the package and its perceived successful implementation in the California operating units. (Interestingly, it might be argued that the visibility of the use of the application in California contributed to its implementation in the Northwest.)

The application being implemented was a major financial and operations planning tool, which allowed unit executives to assemble a yearly plan based on the selection of a large set of activities and projects. The application allowed the planners to compare and contrast both the resources required for and outputs produced by alternative business plans. If the user wished, the application could employ linear programming to select an optimal plan based on resource constraints (e.g. personnel, funding, equipment) and the maximization (or minimization) of some goal (e.g. products produced, revenues garnered, wages expended.) Once site management has settled on a plan for the upcoming year, the application would then be used to transmit the plan to Portland for approval and consolidation. Thus, the application was critical for site planning, and represented a significant advancement from the non standardized, paper-base approach previously in use. However, site management enthusiasm for the application varied substantially: it represented a transition from a more subjective and politically oriented approach towards a more objective and analytical approach. For some, the application was viewed as an opportunity to provide objective analysis in support of their plans and projects. For others, particularly those who were adept with subjective arguments and political maneuvering, the application was viewed as a potential threat.

The ultimate end user of the application consisted of the senior site manager and his or her immediate reports concerned with planning, finance, and operations. Given the size and complexity of the application, the information systems specialists associated with the project consisted of the senior site information systems manager, information systems training personnel, and an information systems person responsible for inputting alternatives and producing reports for management. Information systems staff at each site typically consisted of one manager, who reported to the site administrative or accounting officer, and two or three technicians. The information systems organization in Portland was significantly larger, and was responsible for regional consolidation and providing consulting services to the 19 Northwest units. There was no direct reporting relationship between site information systems personnel and the Portland information systems organization.

Shortly prior to implementation, each of the nineteen units was visited, and interviews were conducted with four to six members of each of the two groups: manager/users and information systems specialists. Each group was asked to evaluate the advantage, complexity, compatibility, trialability, and visibility of the application on a five-point scale. Using the Delphi method, recursive rounds of discussions and votings were conducted until there was agreement on the evaluation of the five factors from the perspective of that specific group. The difference in the number of individuals in each group was a function of minor differences in organization from site to site. As the purpose of each group was to derive a single group evaluation of the five factors, the minor differences in group composition are not viewed as relevant. In summary, prior to implementation, data were collected on each of Rogers' and Shomaker's five factors from both the line and specialist perspectives at nineteen sites.

Several months after the application was put into use, and then again five years later, the parent unit which had required the implementation, passed judgment on the degree of success experienced by each of the nineteen units. Parent unit evaluation of application outcome was straightforward. The power and flexibility of the application allowed for multiple levels of use. At one extreme, users could continue to do their planning in the old, paper and pencil based way, using the application solely for transmitting their plan to Portland. At the other extreme, the users could employ the application to greatly assist their planning effort: it could help them create and compare competing plans, examine tradeoffs between inputs and outputs, perform sensitivity analyses, determine shadow prices for constraints, and perform a range of other powerful analyses and manipulations. The parent unit evaluated each of the 19 user units based on their actual use of the capabilities of the application. Use of the parent unit in determining success ensured that consistency of evaluation would exist across the nineteen units. With outcome data collected at two points in time, with five intervening years, the basis was established for examining implementation dynamics on a longitudinal basis.

Initial data collection immediately simplified the framework to be tested. The implementation of the package was mandated on each of the 19 units, which were told that there was no going back; that the application was the way of the future. Trialability, therefore, was a consistent zero across all units and dropped out of the field test. Additionally, given that each unit reports to the same CEO, and that they were each implementing the same application, the visibility of the effects of the application was, by definition, consistent across all units. Therefore, visibility also was removed from the field test. Thus, the framework of Figure 1 has been reduced to only six cells: the three factors Advantage, Complexity, and Compatibility as measured from the perspectives of both manager/users and information systems technical specialists, as shown in Figure 2.

## Data analysis

The primary objective of the data analysis is to infer which set of factors is important in determining the short run and long run receptivity of the implementation of the applications package. A secondary objective is to develop a structural model which can be used to predict short and long run acceptance. While we had intended to use ordinary least squares regression analysis, a moderately high level of correlation among factors, as presented in Figure 3, suggested that a more sophisticated approach would be suitable.

While a complicating factor, moderate correlation among the “independent variables” in this study is to be expected. In fact, the absence of such correlation would be quite surprising. For example, managers who found the application clearly compatible with their own approach to planning and management should be expected to view the application as advantageous. This situation, where “independent variables” are not truly independent, but have some relationship with each other, is often referred to as “multicollinearity” or more simply “collinearity” and is a common menace for statistical model builders. A common estimation technique to combat this difficulty that has been applied in a variety of settings over the last decade is ridge regression. Readers interested in the knowing more about ridge regression and our use of the technique should refer to the Appendix. ## Discussion

Both models are quite interesting for several reasons. First, the relatively high levels of R-squared indicate that our framework for measuring the fit between an application and a potential implementor does, in fact, capture information which bears significantly on the implementation outcome. Both on a short term and on a long term basis, the fit measured prior to any implementation effort explains, in large part, the outcome to follow. This finding implies that, in cases where the fit is poor, even the most heroic of managerial, technical, and financial intervention may be doomed to failure.

Additionally, the model for describing short term outcome is very interesting in that it implies that the perspective of manager/user is dominant. While the literature has always supported the importance of management buy-in and support, the lack of importance of the information systems specialist for short term success is somewhat surprising. However, an examination of both the application in question and the organization being studied provides strong reasoning behind this finding. First, the application under study is not a low level transaction processing system, rather it is a high-level decision support system for senior management use in planning and budgeting. Thus, to find that the three factors which accounted most for successful adoption were, in order, the relative advantage of the application to the manager/user, its compatibility with that user's view of proper planning, and its lack of complexity from the user's perspective should not be surprising. Second, some explanation for the lack of relevance of the systems specialist's perspective might lie in the strongly hierarchical culture of the organization under study. This was very much a "can do" type of organization: when the senior site management committed to a policy or activity, subordinates had a strong inclination to give vigorous and unquestioning support. In this culture, the information systems specialist who might not have been technically up to the task at hand would have been inclined to do whatever was required for success. Activities which were observed included great amounts of (uncompensated) overtime work, appeals for help from colleagues at other sites, temporary employment of consultants, and direct appeals to the parent unit for more resources.

Over the long run, however, significant and successful use of the application required more than just management support. While the analysis of the five year data still stresses that the manager/user perspective is important, in the long run, the most significant single factor is the relative complexity of the application vis-à-vis the information systems specialists. Therefore, while management buy-in appears to be the fundamental factor in the initial stage of an implementation effort, the continued success of the effort requires that the information systems specialists have the required training and skills necessary to provide continued support for the system and its users. In other words, the Herculean efforts of some information systems specialists to overcome their own inadequacies allowed their organizations to get through with the initial implementation effort, but, in the long term, the inadequacies could not be ignored.

The implications of the above findings for the selection and implementation of software packages seems clear. The short term and long term outcomes of implementing a mainframe software package are quite dependent upon the fit between the chosen application and two sets of players: the people who will actually use the application and the information systems specialists who will support the application. In addition, the short term or initial outcome is particularly sensitive to the fit between the application and its potential end users. This finding clearly implies that mandated implementations without the support of end users have little chance of success. In addition, over the long run, packages which make technical demands which exceed the capabilities of the available information systems support staff have a reduced chance of success.

At the risk of over generalizing based on a small sample, one might use the above findings to develop a strategy for application package selection and implementation. This strategy would have two thrusts: one would be directed toward the potential end-user, and the other would be directed toward the information systems staff who will support the application.

## End-user thrust

The data show the importance of finding a match between the application and the potential end-user, based on high relative advantage and compatibility and low complexity. Identification of applications which would fit well with the end-users will clearly require a very detailed knowledge of the end-users themselves, including their current tasks, procedures, problems, priorities, decision style, and technical experience. The implications for each of these three factors follow.

Relative Advantage: As the most significant factor in the short term model and the second most important factor in the long term model, the user's perception of the relative advantage of the new application over current practices is clearly critical. Central to the identification of applications with potential for high relative advantage is an understanding of the current priorities, in terms of duties and responsibilities, of the individual end users. Applications which support activities which are of a high priority to the users and which show a promise in increasing either the efficiency or effectiveness of the adopter in accomplishing his or her task would be potential candidates for high relative advantage. Identifying applications with high relative advantage will obviously be easier in situations where the user is already dissatisfied with current tools or practices, or, better yet, where the potential user is actually requesting the application. In cases where the end user is relatively satisfied with current practices and is not actively requesting a new application, the agent seeking to encourage implementation will be required to market the new approach, to essentially sell the potential user on the additional merits of the new system. Successful marketing of the new application will clearly require an insightful understanding of the potential user's perspective.

Compatibility: The determination of compatibility between an application and a potential adopter, similar to relative advantage, requires an in-depth understanding of adopter. High compatibility arises when the application supports or conforms to the manner in which the user is accustomed or comfortable in approaching the problem or process which the application purports to support. For example, the application examined in this paper, which supported operational and budgetary planning, required the users to do their planning by developing competing portfolios of projects, with each project possessing a number of inputs and outputs. For some managers, this approach was very compatible with their prior means of planning. However, particularly for managers who viewed planning in a more holistic or programmatic way, the project orientation was quite alien to their norms, thus, not very compatible. Clearly, then, the evaluation of compatibility requires not just knowledge of the potential user's problem and priorities, but also a solid understanding of the way the user wishes to address and solve their problems.

Complexity: While compatibility is fairly attitudinal in nature, and addresses the way a user views a problem and its solution, compatibility is more technical in nature, dealing with the computer and analytic skills possessed by the potential user. In the case under study, a number of managers were familiar with linear programming, and understood concepts of resource constraints and optimization techniques, while, to another group of adopters, the concepts were quite foreign and seemed overly complex. In a similar vein, some of the managers were quite comfortable personally using the application, having had past experience with software packages. On the other hand, some of the subject managers had never personally used a computer. Thus, the evaluation of the complexity of an application requires one to understand, first, the demands which a given application make on its users, and second, the technical and analytic background and skills which the potential users possess.

In summary, the measurement of the fit between an application and its potential users requires an in-depth understanding of both the application and the intended users. In point of fact, this understanding is very similar to the knowledge which is sought in the systems analysis phase of the systems development life cycle, wherein the analysis seeks to develop the system specifications which will meet user needs, expectations, and capabilities.

## Information systems specialist thrust

Over the long term, our study indicated that implementation success could be hindered in cases where the application in question was overly complex or difficult from the perspective of the information specialists charged with systems support. In our field study, we found a number of instances in which the systems personnel assigned to support the application simply did not have the experience or training to adequately perform their duties. In these cases, even when the end users were keenly interested in the application, there were often major setbacks and disappointments.

While it is certainly intuitive that the support staff need to be competent to handle the complexities of their job, the fact remained that many were not, and that application selection and implementation needs to take that fact into account. A straightforward approach to ensuring better fit between applications and information systems support personnel might involve the following two steps. First, the list of potential applications packages could be limited to those which do not significantly stress or exceed the technical capabilities of the staff. Second, to the extent that a selected package does exceed staff capabilities, training and other support resources could be provided to reduce the gap. Both of these steps clearly require an understanding of both the technical demands of a potential package and the technical capabilities of the available staff.

## Concluding comments

While the findings in this study are not particularly surprising, they serve a useful purpose. With the increasing trend toward software packages, this study provides strong support for the notion that successful package choice and implementation still require the detailed analysis usually associated with systems analysis in the development of custom software. In other words, success will depend on obtaining an in depth understanding of the capabilities and requirements of both the package in question and the people who will both use it and support it. In addition, deconstruction of the user perspective into three different factors—advantage, compatibility, and complexity—provides a conceptual basis for both researchers and practitioners to understand the customer base.

While the two factors of trialability and visibility were not tested in this study, the strong support which Rogers and Shoemaker $[17]$ found for these factors in their extensive studies would indicate a need for further study by researchers and their consideration in implementation strategy by practitioners. In addition, while this study was directed at a mainframe-base package, one might anticipate that similar factors would prove relevant to microcomputer-based packages. However, in the case of microcomputer-based packages, the fact that the end-user and information systems functions are often joined in the same individual may well change the balance of factors for success. The authors intend to conduct further studies to explore similarities and differences in both microcomputer-based and client/server-based systems.

The implication is that perhaps the apparent importance of complexity from the staff perspective in the ordinary least squares regression analysis is the result of collinearity, and in fact the management viewpoint of complexity plays a more significant role in short term receptivity.

Fig. 7. Ridge estimates, long term.

Figure 7 contains the ridge estimates for the long term analysis. The least squares results (k = 0) imply that complexity (staff) and relative advantage (management) are the only two factors of importance. However, the ridge analysis implies that complexity from the management perspective is also important. The simple correlation between complexity (management) and long term receptivity is 0.724. However, in the least squares estimation, its coefficient is not significantly different from zero, due to its high correlation with complexity (staff) and advantage (management), 0.550 and 0.635 respectively.

From the above, we infer that the complexity factor from the manager's viewpoint is a significant determinant of the long term success of the implementation. This finding is consistent with our theoretical hypothesis, but it opposes the inference one tends to draw based upon the standard ordinary least squares regression analysis approach. This result is an example of the capability of ridge regression to uncover the significance of one or more lurking variables whose importance may be masked by high correlation with one or more other factors.

Figures 4 and 5 in the main body of the paper contain a summary of the analyses of short and long term implementation using ridge regression. A common problem in ridge regression is selection of the appropriate value of the ridge parameter k to achieve a satisfactory compromise between bias and efficiency [22]. A wide variety of procedures for determining k have been suggested by many writers, including Hoerl and Kennard [11]. Draper and Van Nostrand [5] provide a survey of suggested methods. The value of k that produced the results in Figures 4 and 5 was 0.15. The selection procedure was simple and sensible. The values of the regression coefficients were plotted for k between 0 and 1 (a “ridge trace”) and the lowest value of k for which they appear to have stabilized is 0.15.
