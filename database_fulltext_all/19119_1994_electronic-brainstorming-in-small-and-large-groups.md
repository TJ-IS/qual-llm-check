# Electronic brainstorming in small and large groups

Milam Aiken \*, Jay Krosp, Ashraf Shirani, Jeanette Martin

Department of Management and Marketing School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677, USA

## Abstract

An experiment was conducted with small groups averaging about eight people and large groups averaging about 48 people in size. We compared the group members' perceived production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction with a meeting of each group size using electronic and verbal brainstorming. While there was no significant difference in perceived satisfaction, evaluation apprehension, or production blocking in either group size using electronic brainstorming, there was a significant difference between small verbal groups and large verbal groups. There was also a significant difference in the dependent measures between large verbal groups and large electronic brainstorming groups. Results suggest that the benefits of electronic brainstorming are more pronounced with larger groups than with smaller groups.

Key words: Electronic brainstorming; Group decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

Group Decision Support Systems (GDSSs) are helping many organizations make better, quicker decisions by providing support for the anonymous and simultaneous exchange of ideas and preferences $[28]$ . These computer-based systems are often implemented in face-to-face environments on a local area network and are used by group members for generation of new ideas. This “electronic brainstorming” is one of the most important functions of electronic group meetings $[11]$ .

Much research has investigated the advantages of electronic brainstorming over the more traditional, verbal form. For example, one study investigated the effect of electronic brainstorming on groups with sizes ranging from two to 12 participants [16]. Results of that study indicated that larger groups generated more unique, high-quality ideas, and that members were more satisfied using electronic brainstorming than with its verbal counterpart. Fewer differences were observed between the two brainstorming techniques for smaller groups. The authors of the study concluded that electronic brainstorming reduced the effect of production blocking (communication hindrance such as the inability to talk while another person is talking) and evaluation apprehens sion (participants' fear of what others may think of their remarks) on group performance, particularly for large groups.

The study reported here attempts to replicate these findings on satisfaction, evaluation apprehension, and production blocking while extending the groups to much larger sizes. Comparisons with groups of varying sizes were made between verbal and electronic brainstorming. Small groups with an average size of eight people in verbal and electronic brainstorming sessions were compared against larger groups with an average of 48 people in the same two types of sessions. The largest electronic brainstorming groups contained 41 people and are believed to be some of the largest groups to be studied using GDSS technology in a face-to-face Decision Room environment.

## 2. Background

Brainstorming has been used for many years as a means of generating and sharing ideas in a group. Early studies of brainstorming showed that a group of people can initiate many more new ideas than can an equivalent number of individuals working autonomously if the group participants concentrate on composing the ideas that come to them and avoid trying to evaluate their own or other group members' ideas while brainstorming $[5,29,36]$ . However, studies also showed that the number of ideas generated per participant in a brainstorming group declined as the size of the group was expanded (with a maximum group size of about 12 people) $[3,4,16,18,26,31]$ . Thus, with verbal brainstorming groups, the marginal productivity of individuals declines as the groups get larger, due principally to the restriction of allowing only one person to speak at a time. Such production blocking can nullify the synergistic effects that brainstorming is supposed to create. Another limitation of verbal brainstorming is evaluation apprehension (participants' fear that their ideas will not seem valuable to the group) caused by lack of anonymity in face-to-face communication $[6,25,35]$ .

Several structured procedures, such as the Delphi and the nominal group techniques have been proposed to overcome such limitations $[9,39,40]$ . Although these procedures can improve idea generation in some cases, most studies have found that groups using these techniques do not generate more ideas than groups using traditional brainstorming or unstructured group interaction $[12,20,22,24]$ . In addition, all of these non-automated, verbal techniques suffer from high production blocking and evaluation apprehension due to the communication medium.

Electronic brainstorming is a relatively new technique developed to overcome the problems of production blocking and evaluation apprehension. Using electronic brainstorming, group participants can enter written, anonymous ideas simultaneously over a computer network and quickly view the ideas of the others in the group [37]. With this technique, group members can develop new ideas out of other members' ideas, increasing group synergy. The advantages of electronic brainstorming include:

1. Anonymity. Group members may feel comfortable since they will not be subject to ridicule for asking “foolish” questions or making unpopular comments $[19,41]$ . In some verbal meetings, a few group members may exert undue influence or monopolize the group’s time; electronic brainstorming makes every participant equal $[38,42]$ . Similarly, participants will not be as subject to conformance pressure, the reluctance to criticize the comments of others due to politeness or fear of reprisals $[1,17,33]$ .

2. Parallel Communication. Electronic brainstorming allows participants to enter comments simultaneously and thus permits each to have more time to contribute ideas. In verbal meetings, people must listen to others speak and cannot pause to think, while electronic brainstorming allows everyone to “speak” in parallel. Finally, more information will be presented to the group as more people participate.

3. Automated Recording. All comments are recorded during the electronic brainstorming meeting: participants may therefore concentrate on the meeting instead of taking notes. In a verbal setting, participants have to remember comments until they have a chance to speak. Group members may also forget what has been said before [22]. In verbal meetings, some participants may also not understand what was said or not be able to process the information quickly enough [21,27,34].

The disadvantages of electronic brainstorming include:

1. Slow Communication. Most people type slower than they speak, and in some cases, group participants may be unable to type at all. Most people would rather talk than type. Because talking is faster than typing, it is therefore generally more efficient to use electronic brainstorming for larger groups.

2. Resistance to Change. The most important disadvantage of electronic brainstorming is the human trait of resistance to change. People are sometimes intimidated by computers and feel threatened if forced to use them in a new meeting environment. Also, using electronic brainstorming involves some training in the use of the software.

3. Lack of Media Richness. Because electronic brainstorming relies primarily on written information, other forms of communication are minimized. For example, body language and facial expressions [32] can help group members determine if a comment is meant to be funny or sarcastic in a verbal meeting, but this media richness is reduced or eliminated in an electronic meeting.

The impersonal nature of an electronic meeting is not universally popular. Participants may miss the camaraderie and challenge of face-to-face meetings where dominance and power can be exercised. The computer can make meetings rather impersonal and completely issue-oriented.

4. Possible Increase in Conflict. Another concern with the use of computers is that there could be an increase in conflict and animosity due to anonymity in the meeting. Participants may be unnecessarily contemptuous of some of the ideas and may be overly critical in their comments. Personal attacks may be made easier with the provision of anonymity, resulting in hurt feelings and bitterness.

## 3. Experimental design

The purpose of this study was to investigate the advantages and disadvantages of electronic brainstorming over a wide range of group sizes. It was expected that production blocking and evaluation apprehension will increase as group size increases for verbal brainstorming groups, but will not increase for electronic brainstorming groups and that the superiority of electronic brainstorming should grow as group size expands.

This research builds on the work of several previous studies [e.g., 13, 14]. Some of these were limited by the subject group size or by a lack of baseline groups. For example, one study [16] used relatively small groups (all less than 14), while another study [11] restricted their groups to using electronic brainstorming with no verbal groups for comparison. This study focuses on groups ranging in size from seven to 63 people and uses electronic and verbal brainstorming groups for comparisons.

## 3.1. Subjects

Two hundred and forty-two undergraduate students enrolled in a Management Information System course at the University of Mississippi participated in the experiment for partial course credit. The students had very little or no prior experience with electronic brainstorming or Group Decision Support Systems. The students were randomly assigned to three small, verbal meetings (group sizes: 7, 8, and 8), three small electronic brainstorming meetings (group sizes: 7, 9, and 8), two large verbal meetings (group sizes: 50 and 63), or two large electronic brainstorming meetings (group sizes: 41 and 41). The group sizes were chosen because they allowed a test of the hypotheses and most previous brainstorming studies have used group sizes in the smaller range [e.g., 10].

## 3.2. Tasks

Two questions similar to those used in other studies were asked. One was a variant of the traditional tourism problem: "How can tourism be improved in Oxford, Mississippi?" The second was "How can the campus parking problem be improved at the University of Mississippi?" In addition, we used a practice question: "How can the curriculum in the School of Business Administration be improved?"

## 3.3. Treatments

The two independent variables were group size and technology. The dependent variables were production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction. Each dependent variable was measured on a Likert scale from 1 (very severe production blocking, very severe evaluation apprehension, or very dissatisfied) to 5 (no production blocking, no evaluation apprehension, or very satisfied). Thus, a higher rating was perceived as a favorable result and a lower rating was perceived as an unfavorable result.

## 3.4. Procedures

The subjects brainstormed on a practice problem for five minutes using the technology they would use for the experiments. The subjects then discussed the main problem for 15 minutes. A postsession questionnaire was then administered to assess members' perceptions of production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction.

For those groups assigned to electronic brainstorming, the Brainstorm program provided by GroupForum was used $[30]$ . During electronic brainstorming, group members entered ideas by typing them at individual computer workstations. Every time a group member entered an idea, it was displayed with the set of comments from the other group members to all participants. In contrast, during verbal brainstorming, the ideas were manually written on a blackboard in front of the room.

## 4. Statistical analysis

A two-factor (technology by group size) analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the subjects' responses to production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction. All variables were analyzed using a mixed ANOVA. Group size was a two-level between-group factor, and technology was a two-level within-group factor. There were no significant differences between the two topics in their effect on evaluation apprehension (F = 0.03, p < 0.87), production blocking (F = 0.47, p < 0.50), and satisfaction (F = 2.7, p < 0.11). Thus, there is evidence to support the hypothesis that the non-controversial questions do not significantly affect production blocking and evaluation apprehension. A very controversial question regarding politics, religion, or racial relations may significantly affect the latter, however, and is left for further research. The evidence is less clear on the effect of topic on participant satisfaction, because group members may prefer one topic over another.

Hypothesis 3: There is a significant difference in production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction between large, electronic brainstorming groups and large, verbal groups.

There were significant differences between the two technologies in large groups in their effect on evaluation apprehension (F = 80.5, p < 0.0001), production blocking (F = 224, p < 0.0001), and satisfaction (F = 241, p < 0.0001). Thus, there is evidence to support the hypothesis that electronic brainstorming is superior to verbal communication in large groups for idea-generation types of tasks.

Hypothesis 4: There is a significant difference in production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction between small, electronic brainstorming groups and small, verbal groups.

There were no significant differences between the two technologies in small groups in their effect on evaluation apprehension (F = 1.5, p < 0.23), production blocking (F = 0.01, p < 0.92), and satisfaction (F = 0.12, p < 0.73). Thus, this evidence did not support the hypothesis that electronic brainstorming is superior to verbal communication in small groups (of about eight people) for idea-generation types of tasks.

Hypothesis 5: There is a significant difference in production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction between small, electronic brainstorming groups and large, electronic brainstorming groups.

There were no significant differences between the two group sizes in their effect on evaluation apprehension (F = 0.60, p < 0.44), production blocking (F = 1.58, p < 0.21), and satisfaction (F = 1.76, p < 0.19). Thus, this evidence did not support the hypothesis that large electronic brainstorming groups (with a size of 41 members) is superior to small electronic brainstorming groups (with a size of eight).

## 5. Discussion

This experiment is consistent with earlier studies which have shown that performance in electronic brainstorming groups increases while performance in verbal brainstorming groups decreases as group size increases. In addition, per person output in the verbal brainstorming groups falls steadily as group size increases; however, for the electronic groups it remains relatively constant.

These results may be attributed to differences in production blocking and evaluation apprehension. Production blocking increases as group size increases in verbal brainstorming groups because many group members attempt to state their ideas but cannot because someone else is speaking. In contrast, all members of electronic brainstorming groups can work simultaneously regardless of group size. Thus, production blocking should not differ with the size of electronic groups. Evaluation apprehension in verbal brainstorming groups also increases as group size increases. In contrast, ideas entered by members of the electronic brainstorming groups are anonymous, and evaluation apprehension should not increase. Members of the electronic brainstorming groups reported less apprehension than did members of the verbal brainstorming groups. Perceptions of evaluation apprehension remained constant across group size for members of electronic brainstorming groups but increased with group size for verbal brainstorming groups. This pattern is consistent with the view that apprehension will increase with the number of potentially critical members in verbal groups. We conclude that the technology's effect on production blocking and evaluation apprehension is the most likely explanation for the observed effects on the productivity of larger and smaller electronic brainstorming and verbal brainstorming groups.

In addition, the members of the electronic brainstorming groups were more satisfied than the members of the verbal brainstorming groups. Furthermore, although satisfaction increased with group size for electronic groups, it decreased for the verbal groups. Thus, the technology not only eliminated the per-person productivity decline that is normally found as verbal groups grow in size, but also increased the satisfaction of group members.

One other possible explanation should be noted: novelty. If subjects found the electronic brainstorming technology more engaging because it was novel, they may have worked harder and produced more ideas. The design of the experiments does not provide a strong test of this factor. However, novelty cannot easily account for the significant interaction of technology and group size found in this and earlier experiments.

Based upon the results of this study and other research, we hypothesize that the break-even point (where the advantages of electronic brainstorming begin to outweigh the disadvantages) occurs with a group size of about eight people. Of course, for a task in which anonymity is of primary importance, it may be more advantageous to use electronic brainstorming for smaller groups. Similarly, large groups of more than eight people which need the extra media richness of verbal communication and body language may find that a traditional, verbal brainstorming meeting is superior.

## 6. Implications

The main finding of this study was that the productivity of groups (as measured by self-perceived ratings on production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction) using electronic brainstorming appears to be relatively static above a group size of eight, while the productivity of traditional, verbal brainstorming groups decreases as group size increases.

## 6.1. Implications for research

What factors influence production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction? The advantage of parallel entry and anonymity that electronic brainstorming provides presumably contributed to the improved performance and satisfaction of the electronic brainstorming groups. It is not certain which of these benefits is the more important in improving performance and satisfaction. We must investigate further the comparative effects of production blocking and anonymity [7].

A second implication for researchers is that the benefits of electronic brainstorming need to be assessed over a wider range of group sizes. It seems unlikely that the increase in the per person productivity in electronic brainstorming groups will remain constant over a large increase in size [2].

## 6.2. Implications for practice

One implication of these results is that a large group size should not be considered a constraint on the success of idea-generation meetings. Our study showed that there was very little difference between small verbal groups and small electronic brainstorming groups for a group of eight members in terms of evaluation apprehension, production blocking, and satisfaction for the task of generating ideas about tourism.

Another possible ramification for practitioners is that the anonymity that electronic brainstorming affords diminishes the inhibitory forces of status differences which may reduce the willingness of participants to verbalize their views, particularly if the views raise questions of loyalty and team spirit [23]. With electronic brainstorming, lower-ranking participants may be willing to express their views without feeling that they are being scrutinized by their superiors. The anonymity may also make it easier for participants to assume the role of devil's advocate. Electronic brainstorming will not erase all of the hazards of stating ideas, but it may reduce them, especially in larger, more structured groups. The parallel entry of ideas in electronic brainstorming also precludes any one participant from controlling the idea generation process.

## 7. Conclusion

The research reported here investigated the effects of technology (verbal or electronic brainstorming) and group size (small-about eight people and large-about 48 people) on group satisfaction, production blocking, and evaluation apprehension. Results showed that electronic brainstorming is superior to traditional verbal brainstorming for large groups involved in exchanging ideas and small verbal groups are superior to large verbal groups.
