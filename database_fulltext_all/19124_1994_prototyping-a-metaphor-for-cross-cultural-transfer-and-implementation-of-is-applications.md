# Prototyping: A metaphor for cross-cultural transfer and implementation of IS applications

Barry Shore \*, Venky Venkatachalam

Whittemore School of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire, Durham, NH 03824, USA

## Abstract

While there has been considerable research addressing issues of national culture, and information systems analysis and design, little effort has been directed at how information systems developed in one culture can be transferred to another. This paper introduces the concept of a cultural prototype, a framework to be used in the gradual introduction of IS applications. It considers the culture within which the application was developed and the culture into which the application will be introduced. Prototype stages are defined to provide this gradual and culturally sensitive transfer.

Key words: Cross-cultural; Technology transfer; Information systems; Global information systems

## 1. Introduction

It is becoming clear that “cultural differences must be considered when building and implementing global information systems” [5]. It is less clear, however, what these differences are and how they affect the development and implementation process.

While there has been an increasing level of research directed toward global information systems, this effort has been primarily limited to descriptive cross cultural studies in which comparative analyses are made among information technologies in different national cultures [3,7,4,16, 13,6]. There has been little research directed at building a general framework that focuses on the process of transferring applications from one culture to another. Descriptive studies, then, have suffered because they are not placed in the larger context of a cross-cultural information systems framework.

This paper attempts to build a general framework and to identify the problems and issues associated with cross cultural information system research and information technology transfer. It suggests that one way to take cultural differences into consideration is to introduce an application as a series of carefully designed stages using prototype development methodology as a metaphor.

## 2. Risks inherent in a general framework

There are considerable risks in cross-cultural research. Nasif et al. [1991] suggest that these include criterion problems, methodological simplicity, sampling issues, and problems related to data collection and analysis. While these problems should not be minimized, their focus is on empirical research. Our focus, however, is more conceptually based and as a result needs to address differences among culture if it is to be useful.

The risks we encounter in developing this conceptually based model include:

Defining and measuring national culture;

Measuring the cultural gap and the consequence of the gap; and

Establishing the effect of problem complexity on the transfer.

## 3. Measuring national culture

National culture has been the focus of many studies. One of the more comprehensive was undertaken by Hofstede [1980]. The framework which evolved from this study has served as a reference for cultural research in information systems for more than a decade [10].

In studying national culture Hofstede identified four dimensions by which national cultures differ. From his analysis of 116,000 questionnaires he found they differ by:

Power distance

Uncertainty avoidance

Individualism–Collectivism

Masculinity–Femininity

Power Distance indicates “the extent to which a society accepts the fact that power in institutions is distributed unequally.”

Uncertainty Avoidance is the extent to which a society feels threatened by uncertain situations and avoids these situations through career stability, establishing formal rules, and not tolerating deviant ideas.

Individualism–Collectivism describes a loosely knit social fabric in which people take care of themselves (individualism) contrasted with a social fabric in which groups take care of the individual in exchange for their loyalty (collectivism).

Masculinity–Femininity reflects whether the dominant values are associated with the collection of money and things (masculine) as contrasted with values associated with the caring for others and the quality of life (feminine).

The research data compared beliefs and values of employees from a multinational corporation with facilities in 40 “wealthy” and “more prosperous third world countries.” It concluded that these dimensions are valid measures of cultural differences among these countries.

## 4. National organizational cultural profile

Of the four dimensions described by Hofstede, both power distance and uncertainty avoidance are considered dominant in studying organizations within a particular national culture. Accordingly it is these dimensions that we use to build a framework.

Figure 1 positions countries on a map that plots power distance and uncertainty avoidance. This map we call the Organizational Culture Profile, OCP. Positioned in the second quadrant, for example, the USA is a culture in which both power distance and uncertainty avoidance are low. Organizations in this culture can be expected to exhibit a relatively equal distribution of power and a relatively high willingness to take business risks. Characteristic of these organizations is a tendency toward a decentralization of responsibility and authority. Companies in Mexico, since that culture is positioned in the fourth quadrant, can be expected to display a relatively high propensity to avoid risk as well as a high power distance. To avoid risk, organizations in that culture tend toward a formalization and enforcement of rules and procedures. India is in the first quadrant and Germany in the third.

While these conclusions suffer from the fact that they represent generalizations and other dimensions of culture are not included, they can be used in a framework to suggest the general nature of the problems that can be expected as information technology is transferred across cultures.

## 5. National cultural gap

When an information system is designed in one culture and intended for use in another, a national cultural gap may exist. This gap is a measure of the differences between cultures and does not imply that all dimensions of these differences need be bridged. To simplify measurement problems, this gap will be classified as either high or low. We define a low gap as one in which the application is developed by an organization positioned in one quadrant and transferred to a target organization in the same quadrant. For example the cultural gap associated with an application developed in Great Britain and transferred to an organization in the USA would be classified as low.

The cultural gap for an application developed by an organization in one quadrant and transferred to an organization in another quadrant would be classified as high, for example, an application developed in the US for use in India.

While this classification may oversimplify identification and measurement problems associated with a cultural gap, it is used here to suggest that the gap varies by culture and that it can be measured even if the measurement, at this stage in our research program, is a crude one.

## 6. Position in the information hierarchy

In addition to a national cultural gap, the transfer of information technology must consider the complexity of the application. If two cultures are in the same quadrant, the transfer of a transaction application, such as payroll processing, would produce fewer transfer problems than would the transfer of a decision support system, such as media selection.

Obviously, not all applications are the same. Using the framework developed by Gorry and Scott-Morton (1971), applications can be classified as belonging to one of four levels in the information systems hierarchy. These levels include: transaction processing (TPS), operations planning and control (OPC), management planning and control (MPC) and strategic planning (SP). In addition, applications can also be characterized by the degree of structure in the problem domain. Two extremes are structured and unstructured problems.

Transaction processing application are very structured and represent a class of problems where task usually dominates the problem domain. However, as applications migrate from transaction processing toward strategic planning, these applications become less structured and contextual variables play an increasing role in the development and use of an application.

## 7. National cultural-hierarchical gap

The complexity of the IS technology transfer problem is related to the gap between national cultures and the nature of the application measured by its level in the information hierarchy.

As the gap increases and as the level of the application in the information systems hierarchy increases it is hypothesized that the complexity of the information system transfer problem will increase.

This hypothesis is illustrated in Figure 2. Function A-A' represents the complexity of the transfer problem when the cultural gap is low. Under this condition, complexity of the technology transfer problem increases as the applications migrate from transaction processing to strategic planning. Function B-B' represents the complexity of the transfer problem when the cultural gap is high. Again the complexity of the technology transfer problem increases as the application migrates toward unstructured applications, but for all levels in the hierarchy the transfer problem is more complex than the transfer problem under conditions when the gap is low.

When the cultural gap is high, it may also be reasonable to hypothesize that transaction applications may be dominated by their tasks and that these tasks pervade both low and high cultural gaps. But as applications migrate up the information hierarchy, culture as well as other contextual variables play an increasing role in the technology transfer problem. Accordingly B-B' has a higher slope than A-A'.

## 8. Cultural prototyping: A metaphor

## 8.1. Information technology transfer options

As the cultural-hierarchical gap increases, resistance to new technology may increase. In Hofstede's framework, this resistance will manifest itself through challenges to established power distance and uncertainty avoidance behaviors. How can an effort to negotiate this gap be managed? Information technology transfer strategies can take one of two directions. The first assumes that the task is dominant and that the information system can be overlaid on the organization without cultural adjustments. This approach can be called the “shock approach” and assumes that task is so pervasive that culture must and will change. But, as expressed by Hofstede “Culture...is often difficult to change; if it changes at all, it does so slowly.” The shock approach may therefore lead to problems and the failure of technology transfer.

Janczewski (1992) suggests problems with this approach.

“Computers, the products of western civilization, are adjusted to the culture of their developers. African cultures are quite different and as a result, their implementation may lead to psychological stress. Developers of information technology should be aware of this fact and adjust (emphasis ours) their systems accordingly.”

A second strategy is the gradual implementation of an application in such a way that it is sensitive to the target culture into which the application is to be introduced. This approach will be called “cultural prototyping.” First we contrast prototyping with cultural prototyping.

## 8.2. Prototyping

Traditional prototyping methodologies have been used to reduce the likelihood that an application will fail to meet user needs. They minimize business systems risk. According to Janson and Smith:

“Prototyping addresses the inability of many users to specify their information needs, and the difficulty of systems analysts to understand the user’s environment, by providing the user with a tentative system for experimental purposes at the earliest possible time.” [14].

This view is confirmed in a study of US companies by Hardgrave, Doke, and Swanson [1993]. They found that over 90 percent of the respondents in their survey used prototypes in the requirements planning stage. Prototypes are also used in the design, and implementation stages, but that their use in implementation and post implementation were, according to this study “least important.”

While the use of prototypes during the implementation stage might be “least important” to USA companies, we suggest it can be very important as a metaphor if not as a formal tool in cross-cultural information technology transfer.

## 8.3. Cultural prototyping

Cultural prototyping reverses the emphasis implicit in the conventional definition given for prototyping. First, when an application is transferred to a different national culture, users may be bound in ways that make it unlikely for them to even think about their ‘information needs’. This would be true, for example, when an application is developed in the USA and transferred to India. Second, while conventional prototyping provides users with a ‘system for experimental purposes’, cultural prototyping provides the developers with a system for experimental purposes. Third, cultural prototyping provides the developers with the opportunity to introduce business and cultural changes at the ‘earliest possible time.’

![](/api/attachments/CHRJ45KU/fulltext/images/325071d0103f4db408849d207b58f4dd9bc9f77e027291a1796eabd2edb6d94b.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Cultural prototyping: A metaphor for gradual introduction of software technology into a target organization.

Since the transfer of technology across cultures introduces a cultural dimension, we propose that rather than using a prototype to minimize business risk, a prototype can be used to minimize national cultural risk.

The process of cultural prototyping, illustrated in Figure 3, begins with an evaluation of the more abstract issues related to information hierarchy and national culture. This leads to the development of initial strategies to manage the cultural-hierarchical gap.

In the next stage the analysis becomes more concrete. Here the application software together with the current methods used in the process to be changed and the current levels of IT technology are introduced and woven into the strategic plan. It is at this point that a first prototype would be developed.

When the prototype is introduced, it will be necessary for management or the government to begin a public discourse $[1]$ . This will extol the virtues of the steps to be taken; they may include: greater efficiency; better competitive position; safer working environment; etc. In many environments this discourse must also consider the resistance to labor-saving innovations that may threaten job security. In many national cultures the target audience will not only include management and workers but government agencies as well. Nonetheless the purpose of this discourse is to initiate a process whose objective it is to facilitate incremental changes in the prevailing national and organizational culture.

Once the political/social process of public discourse has been initiated, the prototype can be introduced. This will initiate conflict that occurs as the cultural bias of the application confronts the culture of the target organization. It will be important for those responsible for implementing the application to understand how national and organizational cultural values are affected and how the members of the group are threatened. Sensitivity must therefore be maintained to the socio-psychological variables in the transfer process. Depending upon the magnitude of the national cultural-hierarchical gap a social-psychological process is set in motion to resolve or defend against these differences.

The observation of the more global cultural-hierarchical gap and the more local social-psychological process of the target population will provide the necessary feedback before the next stage in the prototype process can begin. In traditional prototyping methodology the user provides technical feedback. In cultural prototyping methodology, the user provides absorptive feedback...that is, the rate at which the new technology is being absorbed into the target culture. This absorptive rate is related to the ability of the target group's social-psychological process to resolve issues of conflict.

The way in which these conflicts are resolved and the rate at which new information technology is absorbed will play a leading role in the development of the stage $n+1$ prototype. At that point the sequence repeats. Official discourse followed by new a prototype, implementation, and so on.

This process is suggested by a study of a planning system in Ghana $[3]$ where a number of models for budgeting and planning were developed using a spreadsheet package. The models were purposefully kept simple and considerable time was spent in training its users. In addition many of these models followed existing methods. The implication, although not expressed directly in the article, is that the earlier models were prototypes to be enhanced at some later time as culture and training would permit.

Thus early prototypes should be kept simple and they should follow existing methods and procedures where possible. Cultural prototypes are therefore used because they are an evolutionary tool for the incremental change of a social system.

## 8.4. Cultural prototype categories

Cultural prototypes belong to one of three categories. They include:

1. Automation

2B. Individual/Group - Business risk

2C. Individual/Group - Cultural risk

## 3. Management structure

A category 1 prototype focuses on the automation of tasks already performed in the organization. A category 2B prototype focuses on the individual or group changes necessary to accommodate the business risks associated with new software. To a large extent prototypes in this category must deal with uncertainty avoidance that may be expected as groups resist changes to business methods and procedures. A category 2C prototype deals with uncertainty avoidance when cultural standards are threatened by the new application. A category 3 prototype focuses on management processes that must be changed. Prototypes in this category must deal not only with uncertainty avoidance but also the changes in power distance that may be necessary to accommodate the new application.

Fig. 4. Prototyping categories based on culture gap and problem structure.

The choice of an appropriate prototype category will depend on the two factors shown in Figure 4. They include the structure of the problem and the cultural gap. Problem structure may be structured or unstructured, while the cultural gap may be low or high.

## 8.4.1. Quadrant I - structured application, low cultural gap

The interaction between the problem structure and the cultural gap can be illustrated with a manufacturing domain application. Consider the transfer of an inventory record-keeping system from one organization to another where the national cultural gap is low (say from the USA to Great Britain). Assume this system simply maintains inventory balances and does not address other issues such as safety stock, reordering, just-in-time, and manufacturing scheduling interfaces. Since the nature of the application is structured and the gap between the two cultures is low, a prototype need only focus on the issues related to automation. Accordingly its function would be to introduce new business methods, procedures and information technology which users must master. In this scenario it is reasonable to expect that national cultural differences will not obstruct the transfer of technology and that the task will dominate any cultural differences where they exist.

## 8.4.2. Quadrant II - structured application, high cultural gap

When a structured problem is transferred across a high cultural gap, then uncertainty avoidance in Hofstede's framework is implicated because the group may feel threatened by ‘uncertain or ambiguous situations’ … and ‘tries to avoid those situations’ … ‘not tolerating the ideas of others’.

Category 1 and 2C concepts would be appropriate here. The category 1 prototype would contribute to the resistance to automation while the category 2C prototype would contribute toward the reduction of uncertainty avoidance. Returning to our inventory record-keeping example, it might be appropriate to develop an early prototype which simply requires users to check errors in the manual system. In the process of error checking, manually computed balances would be compared to balances computed by the prototype information system. The category 2C component needs to reduce uncertainty avoidance and might provide feedback on the screen that: encourages users to accept the methods and procedures associated with the new application; highlights benefits of the new methods; and assures them of the appropriateness of their responses. Slowly as end users accept the prototype and as their anxiety over uncertainty is reduced, plans for the complete replacement of the old system can be advanced.

## 8.4.3. Quadrant III - unstructured application, low cultural gap

Moving to the quadrant that represents the transfer of unstructured applications to a country with a low cultural gap, the prototyping strategies would include category 1 and 2B. Focus of these prototypes will be on automation and the management of uncertainty avoidance. Uncertainty avoidance in this context, however is different from applications in the high structure/high gap quadrant because high uncertainty avoidance is more closely tied to business risk than cultural risk. In order to deal with the management of uncertainty avoidance with business risk the application can be broken into a series of manageable prototypes with increasing technical complexity. The strategy is to start small and gradually increase the complexity of the prototype towards a full working application. The difference, however, is that the prototype is primarily developed to control business risk not cultural risk.

To minimize business risk, the function of the prototype would be to reduce the size of the problem. Problem reduction strategies would include:

Reduction in the number of variables,

Reduction in the size of the data base, or

Reduction in the time frame or planning horizon.

Continuing with a manufacturing example, consider the transfer of a long range capacity planning system between countries with a low cultural gap. The first prototype might reduce the planning horizon by focusing only on short term forecasting for a few products, this may be extended in a second prototype by increasing the planning horizon to a longer period. A third prototype might extend the system to include a greater number of products.

## 8.4.4. Quadrant IV - unstructured application, high cultural gap

Turning to the quadrant which represents the transfer of an unstructured application to an organization in which the cultural gap is high, prototyping strategy would involve: management of automation (category 1); management of uncertainty avoidance (category 2B and 2C); and management of power distance (category 3).

Unstructured decisions may require that power distance in organizations be reduced if effective decisions are to be made. This can imply a relatively equal distribution of power. Managers must be able to cross functional lines and subordinate/superior exchanges of data and information must take place to plan, organize, and control while in competitive environments.

However, in the management of uncertainty avoidance, both cultural risk and business risk need be considered. This implies that the strategies described earlier for quadrant I and III are applicable. Continuing with our long range capacity planning application, in addition to business risk which motivated the prototypes for quadrant III, we also overlay the cultural issues that can influence the success of the transfer.

Prototypes must address power distance issues by focusing on a select few issues at a time to reduce the size of the cultural risk by addressing a few cultural issues at each prototype level. For example, the prototype requires that an individual is made responsible for compiling a short range forecast with only moderate supervision from superiors. This may involve collecting input data from individuals or groups in the organization that previously were inaccessible due to management structure constraints.

## 9. Conclusion

While a considerable body of work has developed around the management of information technology in multinational corporations and multicultural environments, a review of the literature suggests that no conceptual framework has been developed to help us understand and compare problems of information technology transfer.

Using the work of Hofstede to measure cultural gap and the work of Gorry and Scott-Morton to measure hierarchical gap, this paper develops a conceptual framework that suggests how a cultural prototyping metaphor can be useful in spanning the cultural-hierarchical gap. The objective of a cultural prototype is to travel this gap in reasonable increments so as to facilitate information technology transfer.

While this paper does not propose to present any definitive solutions to the problem of information technology transfer, it has attempted to highlight the issues and develop a metaphor that may be useful in both guiding the transfer of information technology and in initiating a dialogue on an appropriate framework around which research can proceed and be compared.
