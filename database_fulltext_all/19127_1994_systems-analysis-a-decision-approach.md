# Techniques

# Systems analysis: A decision approach

Alberto M. Bento

IQS Department, Merrick School of Business, University of Baltimore 1420 N. Charles Street, Baltimore, Maryland 21201, USA

## Abstract

This work presents a meta-theory for deciding which systems analysis method should be used in a given circumstance. The decisions are to be based upon three criteria: (a) scope, (b) structure, and (c) nature of the process to be supported by information systems. Four development strategies are based on: (a) end-user development, (b) inquiring systems, (c) decision analysis, and (d) data analysis. The paper summarizes the decision and data analysis methods, and discusses the inquiring systems method in more detail.

Key words: Systems analysis; Systems development; Inquiring systems; Decision analysis; Data analysis; Prototyping; Reengineering; End-user development

## 1. Introduction

This paper presents a framework for systems analysis from a decision perspective. It reviews what is understood by information (systems) analysis, the existing approaches to conduct the analysis, and introduces a meta-theory to aid in making decisions about the specific method that should be used in a given environment.

The information analysis process determines the content of the information system (IS): the problems to be solved, the assumptions, and decision rules to be used, the solutions to be considered, the data to be gathered, and the way information should be presented to the managers. Information analysis is the front-end of IS development [3]. Information analysis, therefore, is of paramount importance and “a most critical factor associated with successful MIS implementation” [42]. On average, 21 percent of the MIS development effort has been found to be dedicated to information analysis. User satisfaction with the final products was also found to be positively associated with this – accounting for 34 percent of the total [29].

The information analysis process starts with the idea that an organizational disfunctionality exists, and that information and IS can contribute to reducing or eliminating the unintended consequences of a specific task and/or decision-making process. The problem, as seen by the user(s), is merely the symptom (effect) of the real problems (causes) perceived by the user(s). It is the systems analyst's task to interact with the user(s) to elicit the real problem. The decision whether or not IS are able to contribute to solving the real problem depends on the nature of the real problem.

The identification of information needs has previously been viewed as a decision-making process. Cooper and Swanson [8] proposed a “dissonance” identification as the basis for focusing information analysis. Vitalari and Dickson [44] hypothesized a set of mental behavior and problem solving modes that successful analysts should have to achieve high performance in systems analysis and design. Information needs are complex and unstructured because it is not easy to define a set of information specifications that will support a given decision-making process. Rather, the task is to choose both a cause for the change, or problem, and the data that will aid in solving this problem.

Determination of the information flows necessary to support the information needs is also a complex and semi-structured process. The complexity results from various sources, among others the intrinsic characteristics of managerial data and the turbulent environment that organizations face today.

The data flows problem, however, is more structured than the information needs. Many structured analysis approaches are emerging, e.g., Gane and Sarson [18], DeMarco [13], Davis [12], Ward [45], and King [24], with procedures and tools (including CASE - Computer-Aided Software Engineering) to help by automating the process.

## 2. Traditional information analysis methods

Traditional information analysis methods are described in more detail by Davis and Olson [10], Munro et al [32], and Davis [11], among others. The following is a summary of these methods.

## 2.1. Data analysis

This is the oldest way to determine information needs. It focuses on the organization's existing data and information flows. The existing information system design is not changed but, rather, automated in a more efficient way – a conversion process from manual to computerized tasks. The majority of changes made are primarily due to the characteristics and limitations of computers, e.g., the need for accuracy and standardization of inputs; they are seldom due to an intentional change of the existing information system. The better methods, however, suggest a deeper look - to improve the new system over the current one.

The method consists of the following main steps:

1. examine the existing reports, files, and other sources of information used by the managers;

2. review every piece of information available with the managers and determine the information not in use and information that should be used but is not;

3. identify sources of the information, its path through the organization, the departments and functions involved, and draw data and information flows;

4. determine data volumes and ways to capture data and determine how to convert data into information;

5. define the information systems requirements in terms of a logical system design – a blueprint of the information flows, files, sources, documents, and logic to be used in the system design phase.

## 2.2. Decision analysis

The method focuses on the decision-making process and problem definition. The existing information system is used to provide background to the study, but a new design will evolve from a detailed analysis of the decision making process. The changes may be substantial if the present system is not geared to providing information for decision-making. The method is not task oriented, and therefore, can only be used in situations where decision-making is the heart of the problem to be solved.

The following are the main steps of Decision Analysis:

1. determine the main decisions that must be made; this is achieved through interaction with the manager;

2. identify the organizational policies and objectives related to the manager's needs;

3. identify specific steps necessary to make each of the decisions;

4. develop a model, or a diagram, representing each;

5. examine the model/diagram to identify the information requirements of each step;

6. identify sources and data flows, as well as volumes necessary to produce the information required;

7. define the information systems requirements as a logical system design.

Although the two methods seem to be quite different, Munro and Davis [30], in a field study, did not find significant difference in the final information requirements specifications obtained by them.

## 3. Inquiring systems method

The Inquiring Systems method evolved from three basic concepts or basic thrusts: (a) the wicked problems concept, (b) management measures, and (c) learning systems.

## 3.1. Wicked problems concept

According to Rittel and Webber [38] many social (managerial) problems have no definitive formulation – they are wicked problems. We can only “tame” the problem, then resolve it. Taming the problem depends upon the possible solutions that appear to exist. “In order to describe a wicked problem in sufficient detail one has to develop a comprehensive inventory of all conceivable solutions ahead of time”.

The implications of this to the IS development process are quite obvious, and this could account for the cyclical maintenance and re-design of IS, where the typical IS department commits three-fourths of its resources to maintenance. Whenever the managers are familiar with a given system design, either their knowledge of the problem has changed, or the environmental conditions have changed, or both; consequently the need for information has changed, and maintenance and/or re-design will be required.

Therefore, we should think of the IS development process as an evolutionary, dynamic process – instead of a static process with a final product. The MIS implementations are temporary resolutions to management problems, and the development methods should take this phenomenon into consideration.

## 3.2. Management measures

According to Mason and Swanson [27] the data and information used in MIS are managerial not scientific measures. Thus, they include assumptions and judgments as filters and lenses to real world facts. For example, “hard” accounting data is produced from applying a chart-of-accounts to transactions. Depending upon how the descriptions are written, some transactions may become “personnel”, or “travel” expenditures, etc.

The implications for IS are also quite direct, and they could account for the conflict between results obtained before and after the IS is implemented. Whenever we define new sets of specifications, we are changing, or at least dealing with, the way management measures will be obtained. Therefore, the assumptions and judgments associated with data gathering and processing should also be specified to avoid distortions in the management measures – the raw material of IS.

## 3.3. Learning systems

According to Churchman [9], but not necessarily using his terminology, the treatment of the IS development process should be similar to inquiring, learning systems, where knowledge is obtained through successive approximations to the problem and its solutions, rather than assuming that we are looking for the final solution to the definitive problem. An organization, or management team, learns in the same way its basic components (human beings) learn – through trial and error, use of scientific methods, analytical tools and intuition. Therefore, the IS development process should be understood as one of the basic learning processes management utilizes to gain knowledge of the business, the business structure, and the environment.

The Inquiring Systems method starts with an assumption that the management problem is already identified and a resolution already in effect. It also assumes that data related to the problem is available, and that there is a known structure to deal with the problem. In general, these data and structures are not enough to solve the problem, both from the user's and the analyst's perspective.

These assumptions are less restrictive than they seem. A preliminary problem definition is always available: the existing system. It contains the data and the structure to deal with a given problem, corresponding to the problem's existing level of knowledge. This will be the starting point.

The main steps of the method are:

1. formalize the existing problem definition and resolution;

2. review and analyze the measures, assumptions, and judgments contained in the current problem formulation;

3. un-tame the existing problem formulation;

4. search for new problem solutions; and

5. choose a new resolution and formalization of the problem.

In the first step, the existing system is documented and an overall description of the current problem definition and its solutions are obtained. This initial description is instrumental in showing the reasons why the users are not satisfied with the existing formulation of the problem.

Next, assumptions, judgments, and measures are related to the existing formulation. Gaps, inconsistencies, and contradictions are identified. This analysis is instrumental in identifying possible causes, or sources, of the user's dissatisfaction with the existing system.

In the un-taming step, the existing formulation is examined to find the parts of the problem resolution that no longer suffice. The problem resolution is intentionally made non-structured. That is, the sequence that leads to its resolution is made meaningless by spotting the contradictions and inadequacies identified in the previous steps.

In the next step - the search for new solutions - possible alternatives to solve the problem, and to provide a new order to the decision steps are searched by all interested parties. These alternatives are suggested by the previous analysis and are obtained from the cooperation of users alone and with analysts.

Last, a new formulation is chosen through coalitions, majority, and/or line authority. Based on this new formulation the new data and structure for the problem are defined, in terms of a logical systems design.

Unlike the traditional methods, the Inquiring Systems method assumes that no design is definitive and, therefore, it is not appropriate to call “maintenance” the successive resolutions of the problem. The concept of IS development is much broader than one design and implementation; it involves all other future formulations of the problem. The method implies a step-function format for the information systems life cycle. At a given time, structures and data are generated to deal with a managerial problem. For a while the system is adapted to adjust for variations from the current solution. At a certain point in time, a qualitative breakthrough in the understanding of the problem occurs, and a new system is defined and implemented with a higher level of knowledge and effectiveness, and the process repeats itself, as shown in Fig. 1. Prototyping [2,5,34] is one of the ways that the Inquiring Systems method has been implemented. More recently Reengineering [22] is another way of implementing the Inquiring Systems method.

## 4. Systems analysis: A decision approach

The decision approach concentrates on what needs to be done to identify the information and to specify information flows. It is a meta-theory (or method) compared to the previous methods.

The approach draws upon previous meta-methods. Naumann, Davis and McKeen [31] proposed a meta-method based on a contingency theory regarding the knowledge of the problem. Three different strategies for systems development were recommended given the level of structure of the problem: the experimental, iterative and linear assurance processes.

Gremillion and Pyburn [20] also proposed a meta-method for systems development based on commonality (uniqueness), impact (scopc) and structure (knowledge) of the problem. Four alternatives to systems development were considered:

(a) in-house (traditional) systems development, (b) prototyping, (c) packaging, and (d) end-user systems development.

These, however, assume that the problem is known to the user and/or analyst: the proposed criteria can be applied to the problem and a development strategy selected. The decision approach assumes no prior knowledge of the problem, but rather, the problem's symptoms. It then goes through a series of decision points (scope, structure and nature) to identify the problem and to select a development method, as shown in Fig. 2.

## 4.1. Problem scope

Problem scope is the first of the decision points. If the number of users or the number of organizational units is small, then the problem scope is narrow, and end-user development is recommended. Since 1985, the average DP department [43] has been spending more in PC hardware than in mainframe CPUs and more in microcomputer software than in mainframe software. This increasing introduction of microcomputers in organizations points out that it is no longer far-fetched to think of an information systems development process where there are no traditional system design and implementation phases. That is, after identifying the user needs, the user will design and implement the systems on the personal computer. Rockart and Flannery [37] have estimated this growth of end-user computing as approximately 50 percent per year. Furthermore, more than 80 percent of all systems implemented in the end-user environment were found to be developed and coded by end-users. Recent studies [20,32] have confirmed this trend, indicating “... it has developed from a curiosity to a force of such magnitude that strategic planning for EUC [end-user development] is a common item on the agenda of IS managers”.

## 4.2. Problem structure

If the scope is broad (many users and/or organizational units are affected by the problem), then the next decision point is the problem structure. If the symptoms indicate that the problem has the characteristics of a wicked problem, then inquiring systems are to be adopted. Based on knowledge theory, a broader explanation of the problem structure can be hypothesized. Fig. 3 depicts the general form of the knowledge function. In phase I, the rate of growth in knowledge is very fast, with the understanding of the problem changing drastically in a short time. In phase II, the problem and its corresponding knowledge becomes semi-structured, with significant changes in knowledge occurring, but at decreasing rates. In phase III, the curve reaches a plateau, where some changes are still taking place, but these are no longer significant to the existing knowledge base. Also, the problem becomes well structured, or a tame problem, and quasi-optimal solutions exist.

The knowledge-change process is not only dependent on the knowledge management accumulation, but also on the environmental conditions. That is, if the environment changes, the part of the problem yet to be known is increased. In this sense, some decisions may not be programmable or defined, i.e., may not reach stage III, if the situation is complex and the rate of change in the environment is high, probably originating wicked problems.

The most common implementation of inquiring systems, as indicated previously, has been prototyping $[2,34]$ . The advent of fourth generation languages and their penetration in the DP/MIS landscape $[16,35,41]$ have made the large scale use of prototyping for systems development possible. However, as Plinskin and Shoval $[36]$ point out, “prototyping is controversial with conflicting reports on its effectiveness and efficiency.” Hybrid models combining traditional systems analysis methods and prototyping have been proposed $[14,15,26]$ to overcome its possible pitfalls.

There is enough evidence $[21,25]$ to say that careful consideration of the structure of the problem and identification of wicked problems are prerequisites to obtaining benefit from prototyping. Tame problems can be treated by prototyping in order to gain speed in implementation only at the expense of efficiency (lack of systems assurance) and effectiveness (lack of integration and narrower scope).

Finally, reengineering [22] is an implementation of inquiring systems suitable for situations where the old ways of doing business are not enough to cope with environmental changes and competitive forces, and a radical change (a higher magnitude of shift-point in knowledge) is required. In this case an apparently tame problem is made wicked to obtain substantial increase in efficiency and effectiveness over the current system.

## 4.3. Problem nature

If the problem structure is tame, the next decision point involves the problem nature. This refers to the intrinsic characteristics of the problem. Tame problems can be either task or decision oriented and are to be treated respectively, by data and decision analysis.

The transaction processing problems, for example, are typically task oriented. The systems are supposed to automate, or replace, part of the manually performed tasks of acquiring, verifying, organizing, and maintaining records, and reporting on transactions. Data analysis using modern structured methodologies and CASE technologies $[6,7]$ is suitable for this. Please note that these tame problems (transaction processing) can be made wicked and treated using inquiring systems methods, particularly reengineering, as discussed previously.

Decision oriented problems have received extensive treatment in the literature (notably Anthony [1], Simon [38], Forrester [16], Gorry and Scott-Morton [19], Sprague and Carlson [40], and Bodily [4]). Decision oriented problems have the same basic nature: obtain intelligence from the environment, design alternatives to cope with environment changes, and provide a choice between the alternatives designed. They vary, however, in the location of the knowledge base: in the system or in the user [28].

The courses in systems analysis and design should be changed to reflect this plurality of methods and to introduce the student to all methods, including how to choose among them. End-user development probably should be treated separately in another course, such as Decision Support Systems or End-User Systems Development. Practicing systems analysts should be exposed to this same material, emphasizing prototyping, decision analysis and end-user development. They are probably quite familiar with data analysis. Finally, research is needed to evaluate the fit between the method used to develop systems and the type of target problem. Research would illustrate how effective these systems are in solving the target problem, and when, and if, suitable methods were used.

## 5. Conclusion

Information analysis, and its importance for systems development was discussed. Then the traditional methods of information analysis (data and decision analysis) were introduced.

Prototyping and its potential pitfalls were discussed in the context of inquiring systems. Re-engineering was introduced in the same context. The origins of the inquiring systems method was presented, with a step by step description of it, as well as a discussion of the change the method introduces to the systems life cycle concept.

Finally, the information analysis was presented as a complex, interpersonal and quasi-logical decision making process. From this, the choice of a method can guarantee or void the chances of a successful managerial learning process and IS implementation. Significantly different IS development strategies are to be followed based on the problem scope, structure and nature: (a) end-user development, (b) inquiring systems, (c) decision analysis, and (d) data analysis.
