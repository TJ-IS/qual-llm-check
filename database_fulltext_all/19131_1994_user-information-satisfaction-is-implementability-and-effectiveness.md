# User information satisfaction: IS implementability and effectiveness

Juhani livari $^{a,*}$ , Irja Ervasti $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyväskylä, P.O. Box 35, SF-40351 Jyväskylä, Finland $^{b}$ Oulu City Council, Box 237, SF-90101 Oulu, Finland

## Abstract

This paper presents an analysis of the relationship between user information satisfaction (UIS), information systems (IS) implementability, and IS effectiveness, considering UIS at the level of individual IS applications. Implementability describes the ease of the system's implementation in terms of the users' and implementors' effort required for successful institutionalization of the system. IS effectiveness is the change in organizational effectiveness resulting from the introduction and use of the IS. Based on a field study of 21 information systems in one public sector organization, the results suggest that UIS may be a usable indicator of IS implementability and effectiveness.

Keywords: User information satisfaction; Information system; Implementation; Evaluation; Assessment; Effectiveness

## 1. Introduction

The general aim of IS development is to contribute to organizational effectiveness, to lead to higher productivity, better customer service, better quality of working life, etc. Unfortunately, it is usually very difficult to assess IS effectiveness in terms of the these factors. This has led to attempts to find various measures of IS success that could serve as surrogates for IS effectiveness. User Information Satisfaction (UIS) is potentially such a measure. Ives et al. [25], for example, propose it as “a meaningful ‘surrogate’ for the critical but unmeasurable result of an information system, namely changes in organizational effectiveness". In fact, UIS is frequently used to measure IS effectiveness in IS research ([19,38]). This use is normally based on a hypothetical relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness, an assumption that has increasingly been criticised ([8,35,42,49]). To the authors' knowledge, there are only a few empirical works studying the relationship between UIS and some independent measure of IS effectiveness, especially if IS use as a measure of effectiveness is excluded on account of its obvious deficiencies [14].

The recent taxonomy of IS success $[10]$ consists of six categories of measure: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. Most UIS constructs (e.g. $[2]$ and $[11]$ ), are hybrids in their framework, spanning system quality, information quality and user satisfaction. DeLone and

McLean [10] also outline a descriptive model of IS success in which system quality and information quality separately and jointly influence both use and user satisfaction. Use and user satisfaction are assumed to be highly interdependent, and they are further assumed to be direct antecedents of individual impact. Finally, individual impact is assumed to have some organizational impact.

The purpose of our exploratory work is to analyse the potential applicability of UIS as an indicator of IS implementability and IS effectiveness. The paper is based on a field study of 21 information systems implemented in one public sector organization, Oulu City Council. The results suggest that UIS may have some applicability as a predictor of IS implementability and an indicator of IS effectiveness.

## 2. UIS, IS implementability and IS effectiveness

## 2.1. Introduction

The concept of User Information Satisfaction (UIS) is plagued with problems, in spite of almost twenty years of active research (e.g. [1,3,12,28,39, 44,46,51]). The problems can broadly be divided into three categories: the concept of UIS, its measurement, and its role in the web of variables describing IS phenomena. UIS or its equivalents may be applied to the assessment of quite different phenomena, ranging from the whole IS function in an organization (e.g. [2,5,25]), to an individual application information system ([11,41]) or to individual reports and queries (e.g. channel disposition in [45]). The psychological nature of the concept is also far from clear [35]. The measurement of UIS is still an open question, varying from quite direct features of the system (e.g. [2,11,45]) to various beliefs concerning the effects and value of the system (e.g. [41]). Finally, the theoretical basis for UIS in terms of its causal antecedents and consequents is also deficient ([3,16,26]), especially the relationship between UIS and the actual use of the system (e.g. [4,52]) has been of considerable interest.

Measures of UIS have recently been criticised (e.g. [8,13,22,35,47]). This paper reports on some positive results in applying UIS as an indicator of IS implementability and IS effectiveness. Since an individual application forms a much more coherent unit of IS development and use than an organization-wide information system or the whole IS function, the paper analyses the relationship between UIS, IS implementability and IS effectiveness at the level of individual IS applications.

The applicability of UIS as an indicator of IS implementability and IS effectiveness is based on two considerations of IS development, ease of implementation and long-run organizational effectiveness $[33]$ . Markus and Robey state that increasing organizational validity (the degree of change implied by the system) may produce a desirable change in the first dimension and an undesirable change in the second. This implies a fundamental conflict between IS implementation and IS adoption (development) success $[20]$ . The latter involves the desirability of the consequences of the whole IS adoption process from a viewpoint of the host organization, the users, and other stakeholder groups, whereas the former refers to the success of the institutionalization of the information system in its host organization. The conflict occurs because IS implementation success is a necessary but not sufficient condition for IS development success. Indeed maximization of the probability of IS implementation success may detract from IS development success in the longer run.

IS implementation success can be defined as a decreasing function of the effort (cost) of transforming the designed model of an IS into an effective working system. Correspondingly, IS adoption success can be defined as the impact of the information system on the effectiveness of the adopting organization. IS implementability is used to describe the ease of implementation. IS effectiveness is interpreted as the change in organizational effectiveness resulting from the introduction and use of the IS.

## 2.2. UIS and IS effectiveness

There are only a few empirical research papers that address the relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness. The review by DeLone and McLean [10] identifies only three studies, [30,37,40], that cover IS effectiveness in terms organizational impact and UIS in terms of system quality, information quality and/or user satisfaction. Among the three, only [37] analyses the relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness. In a field study of 21 organizations, they found a high positive correlation (0.91) between the composite success measure calculated as the arithmetic mean of the performance ratings of the 38 items of their instrument and a single item measure of the success/failure of the organization's overall computer-based information system. In a later paper, Miller [36] proposed a model for IS effectiveness and tested it in nine companies. He found that the overall performance of the IS function, assessed by users with a single scale, correlated significantly with the fit between the users' performance and importance ratings and with the fit of the IS staff's performance and importance ratings. The performance and importance ratings of both respondent groups were measured using the Miller–Doyle instrument.

More recently, Hufnagel [18], drawing upon attribution theory, examined the effects of performance outcomes on users' judgements about the IS in the experimental use of a computer-based business game. Her findings lead her to conclude that "user satisfaction may be a less than adequate surrogate for system effectiveness" and that the normally assumed direction of causation may be reversed – performance outcomes may lead to feelings of satisfaction or dissatisfaction. She used two single-item measures to gauge the participant's attitudes toward the system.

One may conclude that empirical support for the assumed positive relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness is very limited. The results of existing research cannot be regarded as very definite, for several reasons. The operationalizations of UIS or corresponding constructs may be criticised (e.g. [18]). Most of the effectiveness measures are problematic as measures of the impact of an information system upon organizational effectiveness (e.g. [36,37]). A considerable problem is the potential measurement bias brought about by the fact that the same respondents estimate both UIS and IS effectiveness, which can be expected to inflate the relationship between the two variables. One can identify two explanations for the measurement bias [17]: common method variance and self-serving bias. The former “occurs when the characteristics of the measurement method elicit a systematic variance in the assessments of the variables”. They further identify two major sources of common method variance, the halo effect, “the tendency to rate an object in the constant direction of a general impression of the object” [27], and response set, “any tendency causing a person consistently to give different responses to test items than he would when the same content is presented in different form” [9]. This corresponds to the idea of a self-serving bias.

Our work pays attention to appropriate measurement of the relevant variables and to elimination of the risk of measurement bias. The measure of UIS is applied to individual IS applications, selecting a subset of the Bailey–Pearson instrument. The measure of IS effectiveness is adapted from the literature on organizational assessment $[50]$ . Finally, our work makes it possible to analyze the relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness, where the two variables are assessed by different groups.

## 2.3. IS implementability: a neglected aspect of IS implementation

Reviews of existing implementation literature show that IS implementability is a largely neglected field. There are several explanations for this. Firstly, implementation is not always recognized as a distinct phase of IS development. Lucas [29], for example, defines implementation as “an on-going process which includes the entire development of the system from the original suggestion through the feasibility study, systems analysis and design, programming, training, conversion, and installation of the system”. This naturally obscures the role of implementation as a critical stage in the IS adoption process.

Secondly, most of the existing IS implementation research has involved custom-designed systems [32]. This explains the extension of the IS implementation process to cover the whole IS development process, because in in-house development the analysis and design process is often very critical in terms of resource consumption and risks. It is also crucial from the viewpoint of IS implementability, since the system to be implemented is essentially determined by the analysis and design process. Many central implementation factors, such as user participation, management support, etc., also involve the whole IS development process. The increasing adoption of application package-based information systems can be expected to alter this, since a package reduces the extent of the analysis, requirements specification and technical design, leading directly to implementation after a short analysis of requirements, a survey of existing packages, and a decision on the package to be acquired. This can be expected to lead to a situation in which the problems of IS adoption are encountered in the implementation phase $[21]$ .

Thirdly, those who identify implementation as a separate activity tend to view it primarily in terms of acceptance. Lucas et al. [31], for example, define implementation as a change in information processing or decision-making behaviour and, noting that this is often difficult to measure, propose acceptance and use as two variables closely related to it. This leads to the basic form of their structural model of IS implementation, in which manager and user acceptance are assumed to be influenced by several variables while user acceptance is taken to influence use, performance, and satisfaction with the system. Their view of the IS implementation process is highly simplified, being implicit in three aspects of the model. First, variables such as involvement and knowledge of the system indicate how well the implementation process was handled; second, they view implementation as a two-stage process that includes a manager model and a user model; and third, the sequence of relationships among the variables and intermediate outcomes implies a causal process.

We wish to emphasize more explicitly the actions of the user and implementer in the IS implementation process. Successful implementation normally requires an effort on the part of the users to learn how to use the system. This can partly be supported by training provided by the implementer. IS implementation may also include modifications to the system.

## 2.4. The hypotheses

The restriction of UIS to the level of individual IS applications can be expected to make the relationship between UIS, IS implementability, and IS effectiveness more tractable than the application of UIS at the level of the organization-wide IS or IS function. Our general hypotheses are: H1: UIS is positively associated with IS implementability H2: UIS is positively associated with IS effectiveness

## UIS and IS implementability

Hypothesis H1 is based on the assumption that the implementation (institutionalization) of a system in the normal working activity of the adopting organization means that the user's work, involving IS use, and the actual system must be aligned. In the case of a discrepancy, either the user must adapt his work to the system or vice versa, or both must be changed. Interpreting UIS as “the extent to which users believe the information systems available to them meet their information requirements” [25], a low UIS value implies a mismatch between the user's requirements and perception of the system characteristics. Therefore it can be expected to indicate the implementation effort required to align the user's work and the system with each other. Assuming that the mismatch is because of the user's limited knowledge of the system, the user may investigate the system in order to become acquainted with it and/or he may be provided with various forms of training. Assuming that the discrepancy is more “real”, there are only the two options: to modify the system or to adapt the user's work to fit the system.

## UIS and IS effectiveness

Hypothesis H2 is problematic in several respects. IS effectiveness is defined as the contribution of an information system to organizational effectiveness; this links it to the problems of organizational effectiveness. Cameron and Whetten characterize the assessment of organizational effectiveness through seven critical questions [6]:

1. What is the purpose of assessing effectiveness?

2. What level of analysis is being used?

3. From whose perspective is effectiveness assessed?

4. What domain of activity is the assessment focused on?

5. What time frame is being employed?

6. What types of data are being used in the assessments?

7. What is the reference against which effectiveness is being judged?

Our purpose is to evaluate IS effectiveness, the level of analysis will be the organizational unit adopting the information system, the perspective will be that of unit managers, the effectiveness will be assessed using a number of criteria for unit performance suggested by Van de Ven and Ferry [50], the time frame will concern the past, the data will be perceptual, and the reference will be improvement with regard to past performance.

There is a considerable problem entailed in the relationship between UIS and organizational effectiveness. Most authors addressing the issue assume UIS to affect IS use and IS use to affect organizational effectiveness. Miller [36] presumes that the economic and process outcomes of an information system result from specific IS-related behaviour, especially use of the system. He does not explicitly incorporate UIS in his model, but it can be construed as corresponding to the fit between the specific task needs and perceived IS capabilities. This fit is presumed to affect IS use through a number of intervening variables. Overall, existing empirical research does not yet provide much support for the assumed role of IS use as an intervening variable between UIS and IS effectiveness measured as the improvement in organizational effectiveness. The results concerning the relationship between UIS and IS usage have been inconclusive (eg., [34,42]), and Udo [49] reports that system usage had a significant relationship with only one of the eight measures of DSS effectiveness, namely competitive edge.

One can imagine several explanations for these results, starting from the inconsistent definitions of the relevant variables and their poor operationalizations (cf. [15,48]). Goodhue [16] defines “IS satisfactoriness” as the individual’s belief in the correspondence of fit between job requirements and IS functionality and “IS satisfaction” as the correspondence between the information system’s intrinsic benefits of use, such as providing a sense of accomplishment due to crisp, attractive output, and the needs of the individual. Our proposal is that when UIS is interpreted in the sense of “IS satisfactoriness” rather than “IS satisfaction”, the link between UIS and IS effectiveness can be established directly without the intervening variable of IS use. Even though UIS is a user’s personal assessment, the requirements for the system normally reflect an interpretation of the requirements imposed by the user’s task or work. Depending on the user’s organizational position and the nature of his task, e.g. whether it is well structured, the interpretation may include more or less subjective elements.

Assuming that UIS is the user's best estimate of the match between the requirements imposed on the system by his work and the system capabilities, the positive correlation between UIS and IS effectiveness is quite understandable. If the user's interpretation of those requirements and estimate of the match between the requirements and the system capabilities are correct, increased UIS should be positively associated with task performance. One should observe, however, that we do not assume the association to be causal, but it is "cognitive" in nature: UIS is based on the user's estimate of the effectiveness of the system in his work.

Can UIS can be expected to be associated with effectiveness at the level of organizational units and the whole organization? This depends on the extent to which the tasks of individual users are aligned with the whole organization and to what extent the incentives related to individual tasks are aligned with the goals of the whole organization. Assuming that the organizational design is reasonably consistent, one could expect a higher task performance at the individual level to lead to higher effectiveness at higher organizational levels.

## 3. Research design and setting

The empirical part of our research was carried out at Oulu City Council, which is a municipal organization of about 8000 employees. It involved a field study of 21 major IS applications implemented between 1985 and 1991. These systems included all the major ones, except for three that were simultaneously subject to another investigation. Each system was assessed separately, and consequently all the measures concern individual applications. Data collection consisted of guided sessions in which 93 users and 44 managers of the adopting units answered questionnaires. The set of managers included 22 user managers who used the system “hands-on”. Senior systems analysts on the IS staff of the Oulu City Council also assessed selected aspects of UIS. Because the analysts were organized according to application areas such as finance, health care, social services, construction, etc., they were highly knowledgeable about their systems.

The idea was to use existing validated instruments as far as possible. Table 1 lists the variables, the groups which answered each question, and the Cronbach alpha coefficients showing their reliability.

User information satisfaction with the system was assessed using a subset of the Bailey–Pearson instrument. It was primarily selected because it is one of the most frequently applied measures of UIS and its use, even as a subset, is at least implicitly advocated by DeLone and McLean [10]. Since the Bailey–Pearson instrument concerns the whole IS function rather than an individual IS application, a subset of scales was selected. The selection was mainly based on the correspondence between the Bailey–Pearson instrument and the Iivari–Koskela construct ([23,24]). The subset consisted of factors 13, 15–16, 18–24, 26 and 38–39. The Doll-Torkzadeh [11] instrument would have been an alternative way to measure UIS at the individual IS applications. It takes the quality of user interface (ease of use) into account more explicitly. The major reason for its rejection was its specific way of measuring satisfaction in terms of frequency of satisfaction. This has a strong tendency to lead to an experiential interpretation of UIS in which the user assesses the system in terms of his use history (cf. [22]), making it difficult to apply UIS as an ex ante measure and as a predictor of IS implementability.

The three variables describing the implementability of an information system were measured using a semantic differential technique developed by the authors, each variable consisting of four items.

IS effectiveness was measured as the application system's impact upon the effectiveness of the adopting unit using five criteria of perceived unit performance described in Van de Ven and Ferry [50]: unit rating of quantity of output, quality of output, innovativeness, reputation for excellence, and morale. "Output" here refers to the outputs of the whole adopting unit, not of the information system. The reason for excluding such criteria as the percentage of the performance target attained and unit rating of efficiency is that the units in question are not particularly target-oriented and that the human, financial, and physical resources allocated to units are quite independent of the IS developed. One should furthermore note that Oulu City Council does not have any uniform practice for financing systems. For example, when buying application packages, some systems may be financed directly by the adopting organizations while others are financed by the ADP center, which nominally charges out its costs. Consequently, changes in unit rating of efficiency can be expected to be determined by changes in the quantity and quality of outputs rather than by changes in inputs. Note also innovativeness and reputation for excellence as measures of effectiveness can be expected to cover the impact of “more symbolic” use of information systems, in addition to the impact of their instrumental use covered mainly by the first two effectiveness measures.

The reliability coefficients of all the UIS variables were high, except that of UIS1, which will be dropped from subsequent analysis. The reliability of the implementability variables can also be considered acceptable.

## 4. Results

Since the results are primarily based on perceptual data, the following analysis considers the consistency of findings in and between different respondent groups. If the results are consistent, this increases their credibility. Since the present study is exploratory in nature, being limited to one organization in which the number of systems analysed is quite small (21), the reporting is not strictly limited to statistically significant results. For example, in the correlation analysis coefficients exceeding 0.3 in absolute value are reported as potentially interesting, even though not statistically significant.

## 4.1. User information satisfaction

The construct validity of the UIS variables was assessed by subjecting them to factor analysis. Principal component analysis using orthogonal varimax rotation gave a clear structure of three factors, as depicted in Table 2. The first factor, “information satisfaction”, included the variables UIS2–UIS7, the second factor, “ease of use”, the variables UIS8–UIS12, and the third factor included only the variable UIS13 (= integration of systems).

One aspect of the multitrait–multimethod (MTMM) was applied in order to analyse convergent and discriminant validity. Varying methods for measuring the same construct were not used. Table 3 presents the correlation matrix for the UIS variables. Convergent validity presupposes that the correlations between variables of the same trait are different from zero and large enough to warrant further examination [43]. The smallest correlation between the UIS variables was 0.63 in factor 1 and 0.56 in factor 2. Both are statistically significant (p = 0.0001). Discriminant validity means that the correlations between variables of the same trait are higher than those between variables of that trait and other traits. Discriminant validity can be tested by counting the number of times each variable correlates more highly with variables of another factor than with variables of its own factor. There are no precise statistical criteria for the MTMM, but Campbell and Fiske [7] suggest determining whether this count is higher than one-half of the potential comparisons, presupposing that there is no common method variance. In our case, there were 4 violations among the 36 correlations between the UIS variables of factor 1 and the remaining UIS variables and 10 violations among the 35 correlations between the UIS variables of factor 2 and the remaining UIS variables. In the latter case, the violations especially concern the variable UIS8, Response / turnaround time, which has a lower correlation with the variable UIS11, Language (= the set of vocabulary, syntax and grammatical rules used to interact with the computer system) than with variables UIS2, UIS3, and UIS4–UIS6.

## 4.2. UIS and IS implementability

Table 5 presents the statistically significant correlations between the three UIS factors and the implementability (I) variables at the level of individual respondents (n = 115). It indicates that factor 2, ease of use, has statistically significant correlations with all the I variables, whereas factor 1, information satisfaction, is significantly associated with all the I variables except I2. Furthermore the correlations between UIS factor 2 and I1, I2 and I are relatively high.

Table 6 depicts the correlations between the three UIS factors and implementability at the level of the systems, distinguishing the respondent groups. In order to keep the table readable, only correlations with an absolute value $\geq 0.40$ are included. $^{2}$ The lowest cell on the right, based on all respondents' answers, summarizes the major findings of the analysis. It indicates that ease of use was critical from a viewpoint of implementability. A more detailed analysis indicates quite consistently significant correlations between users' assessments of the ease of use and users' and adp experts' assessments of IS implementability. The user-managers' corresponding correlations are also moderately high, even though not statistically significant in the case of adp experts (because of the smaller number of respondents). Information satisfaction based on the user-managers' assessments also has significant correlations, especially with their own assessments of implementability. The correlations between UIS factor 3, integration of systems, and implementability seem more random.

## 4.3. UIS and IS effectiveness

Table 7 shows the correlations between the three UIS factors assessed by the three respondent groups (users, user-managers and adp experts) and the impact of the system upon the effectiveness of the adopting units as assessed by unit managers, keeping an absolute value 0.30 as a threshold. Overall, the results indicate statistically significant correlations between ease of use (UIS factor 2) and the impact on the overall unit effectiveness in all respondent groups except the ADP Centre. A more detailed analysis reveals that, in the case of users, the only significant correlation is between ease of use and the impact on quantity of output. Furthermore, the correlation between ease of use and the impact on quality of output is higher than 0.30, though not statistically significant.

The correlation between the ease of use and the first two effectiveness measures in the assessments by user-managers are consistent with those of users. There are some additional correlations worth recognizing. Information satisfaction has a moderately high correlation with the impact on quality of output (0.39), even though not significant because of the low number of systems having user-managers (n = 16). The correlation between ease of use and the impact upon reputation for the excellence is also moderately high (0.45), even though not statistically significant. Finally, UIS factor 3, integration of systems, was found to have correlations greater than the threshold value with innovativeness and reputation for excellence, the latter being statistically significant.

In the case of the ADP Centre, an interesting result is the focus on integration of systems, which was found to have a statistically significant correlation with the impact upon quantity of output, quality of output and reputation for excellence, and a moderately high correlation (0.38) with the impact upon innovativeness.

## 5. Discussion

There are two general hypotheses for our work: H1) UIS is positively associated with IS implementability, and H2) UIS is positively associated with IS effectiveness. The results of the study provide partial support for these hypotheses. Among the three UIS factors, ease of use was quite consistently found to have significant correlation with the implementability of information systems and was also found to be associated with the impact on unit effectiveness, especially quantity of output and quality of output.

The dominance of the ease of use is quite understandable, because the 21 systems analysed are used mainly at the operational level. The correlations do not, of course, indicate the direction of causality, especially because the relevant factors were measured retrospectively. One could imagine that the implementation problems experienced by users influenced their assessment of the ease of use or that the latter influenced the implementability, or that there was a mutual relationship. We believe however, that the direction is from UIS to implementability rather than vice versa, in the sense that UIS could serve as a useful predictor of IS implementability. $^{3}$ First, we wish to point out the relatively high correlations between ease of use as assessed by users, user-managers and adp experts. If we assume that it is mainly influenced by the implementability of the system, one could expect equally consistent assessment of the I variables in the three respondent groups. Table 8 depicts the correlations between the means of the I variables for the different respondent groups for the systems analysed, and indicates that the correlations between the users and adp experts are quite high, those between the users and user-managers mixed and those between the user-managers and adp experts relatively low. Assuming that the I variables describe the implementability of the systems as perceived by the three groups, it seems more natural to assume that the ease of use of each system is assessed quite consistently by the three groups, and that the ease of use experienced by the users has influenced their perception of the implementability of the system. The potential implementation problems are further noticed by adp experts in the form of complaints, requests for modifications, etc., (cf. the relatively high correlations between users and adp experts).

The results concerning user-managers include an interesting aspect. While the ease of use as assessed by them correlates significantly with all the implementability variables assessed by the users, it correlates significantly only with one variable among their own assessments of implementability. Instead, information satisfaction was discovered to dominate user-managers' assessments of implementability. This is understandable, since they are more interested in the reporting possibilities of the systems, etc. In particular, the high correlation between information satisfaction and the inverse of modifications (0.81) is natural.

To summarize the discussion relative to Hypothesis 1: we propose that UIS can be interpreted to precede or parallel the implementation process, so it could serve as a useful predictor of IS implementability. This relationship requires additional research, however.

Returning to the relationship between UIS and IS effectiveness, a distinctive feature of the present work is that UIS and effectiveness were assessed by separate groups, allowing inter-group analysis; this can be expected to alleviate the problem of measurement bias. The moderately high correlation between ease of use and quantity of output found consistently in the inter-group analysis is easy to understand, since operational systems that are easy and convenient to use can be expected to influence the quantity of output. Part of the increased efficiency may also be directed towards increasing the quality of output. The relationship between UIS factor 3, integration of systems, and the effectiveness measures for the adopting units are also quite easy to understand. A higher integration of systems may be seen as innovative and it may increase the unit's rating on reputation for excellence.

We do not expect UIS to influence effectiveness causally through usage of the system, but we argue that the association is cognitive. UIS, as the users' assessment of the match between the requirements for the system and the actual system, implies an estimate of the effectiveness of the system. The fact that the measurements took place retrospectively leads to a question: to what extent did the “realized impact” of the system upon organizational effectiveness influence UIS assessments rather than UIS being an estimate of effectiveness? The “realized impact” on organizational (unit) effectiveness was based on the unit managers’ perceptual assessment of it. This can be expected to reduce the risk of attributing UIS to the impact on unit effectiveness in the case of non-manager respondents, but this cannot be totally ignored, since the perceptions of “realized impact”, including those of unit managers, may shared in the units. Independently of the possibility of the above attribution, one should also note that the ex post measurement of UIS and effectiveness can be expected to make the correlations between UIS and effectiveness higher than in the case of ex ante assessment, since users probably have a better understanding of the “true” requirements for the system.

To summarize, in spite of the above reservations, the results give some promise that UIS may serve as a surrogate for IS effectiveness, the latter being measured in terms of the system's impact on organizational effectiveness. The considerable differences in correlations between the three UIS factors and effectiveness measures suggest that UIS should be interpreted as a vector of the three factors rather than as a single aggregated measure.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

UIS has recently been subjected to increasing criticism. This paper reports some positive results on UIS based on a field study of 21 application information systems in one public sector organization.

Firstly, factor analysis applied to a subset of the Bailey–Pearson instrument gave an elegant and easily interpretable factor structure that has acceptable convergent and discriminant validity. Secondly, the results suggest that UIS may have some applicability as a predictor of IS implementability and as an indicator of IS effectiveness. Because UIS can be assessed quite soon after the requirements analysis and functional specification of the system, these results should be of value to both IS research and practice.

Generally speaking, the results of the analysis are understandable, and lend credence to the validity of the operationalizations and the reliability of the measurements, even though they were perceptual and the measurement took place retrospectively, several years after the implementation of the first system. The field study nevertheless has its limitations. It is based on experiences gathered in one organization in the public sector, and the number of systems analysed was quite limited. Consequently, the results are only exploratory and it is difficult to assess to what extent the findings can be generalized. It is our hope that the present work will provide motivation and a starting point for further research into the important issue of the relationship between UIS, IS implementability and IS effectiveness.

Juhani Iivari is a Professor of Information Systems at the University of Jyväskylä. He received his M.Sc. and Ph.D. degrees from the University of Oulu in 1974 and 1983, respectively. His research has broadly focused on IS development approaches and methods, including their comparative analysis, object-oriented analysis and design, organizational analysis, design and implementation of information systems and IS assessment. Iivari has

published in journals such as Data Base, European Journal of Information Systems, Information and Management, Information and Software Technology, Information Systems, International Journal of Information Management, Journal of Information Systems and MIS Quarterly, as well as in a number of conference proceedings.
