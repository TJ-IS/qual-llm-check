Research

# Traditionally-moderated versus computer supported brainstorming: a comparative study

Otto Petrovic \*, Otto Krickl

Institute for Business Administration, Karl-Franzens-University Graz, Babenbergerstrasse 10, A-8020 Graz, Austria

## Abstract

The work reported here investigated the use of the computer in an Electronic Meeting Room (EMR). It can help by supporting creative tasks. We compared results using the EMR with traditional brainstorming, particularly with respect to efficiency and effectiveness. The latter was measured by assessing the quality and the quantity of contributions and the degree of satisfaction of the participants. Since moderated groups were used as the basis of comparison, the results diverge from those published by others.

Keywords: Computer-supported co-operative work; Groupware; Electronic meeting systems; Group decision support systems; Office automation; Computer aided team

## 1. Introduction

For some time now, effectiveness of Electronic Meeting Rooms (EMRs) has been studied. Such rooms are equipped with special hard- and software and are meant to improve the meetings. This study investigated EMRs by comparing the use of computer support with traditional brainstorming groups which are co-ordinated by a moderator; isolating technology as an independent variable; and comparing their efficiency and effectiveness.

The EMR - due to the length of meetings, the presence of an external moderator, and the necessity to leave the normal environment - represents an alternative particularly to meetings which are co-ordinated by a moderator rather than to those where the group uses only self control. Furthermore, attention has been paid to the external conditions due to the type of individuals, the group, the task, the context, and the technology used. The first four factors are unchanged in our study and technology represents the independent variable.

## 2. A model of variables to study technological support of meetings

The starting point for our investigation is the model shown in Fig. 1, which determines the effectiveness of a meeting by the ratio value to cost [27] [4] [31] [1]. The ratio depends on five factors, one being the technology. We define effectiveness as the degree to which the results of a meeting correspond with the original expectations and goals, while efficiency shows the extent to which resources have been used best to achieve those goals [34]. Obviously both should be made as close to 100% as possible.

Effectiveness can result from either task-related results, the satisfaction of participants, or group development. Task-related results are, for instance, the quality of the results, the number of generated alternatives and comments, the potential to implement the results and the satisfaction of those who commissioned the task. Of equal importance is the attitude of participants towards the results, the procedure at the meetings and the technology used. Group dynamics are involved when the meeting leads participants to adapt to new conditions, improves co-operation, facilitates learning, and increases the feeling of group cohesiveness.

Process gains improves results, while losses reduce them. Fig. 1 shows important process gains and losses that can occur in meetings: the list is not complete.

The kinds of and the ratio between process gains and losses are in turn determined by the environment of the meeting. It depends on the kinds of participants, the characteristics of the group, the specifications of the task, the context, and the technology used. Different contexts are needed to allow conclusions to be drawn on the impact of technologies on the process gains and losses. Thus, when one talks about the effects of using different technologies in meetings, the external conditions must be considered and the kind of results must be presented in detail. In order to be able to explain these effects, changes of process gains and losses must be analysed.

Effective management of a meeting should improve both the ratio of process gains to losses and improve the use of the resources, thus increasing efficiency. The highest costs of a meeting are the salaries of the participants. Opportunity costs are incurred by the loss of time by the participants. Another cost factor is travel expenses incurred because participants may need to meet at the same location, if teleconferencing is not available. In addition, infrastructure (conference rooms, technical and other equipment essential for running the meeting) needs to be considered. Efforts in organising the meeting must also not be underestimated: these involve co-ordinating dates among participants, an essential for traditional meetings [29].

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Environment

Kinds of participants, the group, the context and the task

The participants in the meetings were groups of students of business administration, who are in their final years at Karl-Franzens-University Graz. To finish their studies, a project seminar requires the participants to solve a concrete task taken from practise in an organisation. Project groups are formed at random at the beginning of the seminar.

When the data for this study were collected, students had already been working for three months with employees in organisations. The seminar was concluded by project presentations with an audience of business people who assessed the results. The project group judged to have the best results was awarded a prize donated by the organisations. The goal of the meetings in the EMR was to generate ideas to obtain the best assessment by the judges.

## Technology used

Technological variation 1: Electronic meeting rooms and electronic brainstorming. An EMR is a conference room equipped with information technology. One basic variation provides each participant with a personal computer that can be linked both with those of other participants via a local network and with a Public Screen, which is a giant screen visible to everyone; through its use, selected information may be made accessible to participants. The equipment is supplemented by flip charts, pin boards, overhead projectors and other supporting tools used in a traditional mode [18]. An EMR attempts to create an additional communication channel through which everyone can “talk” simultaneously, can use the advantages of anonymity when contributions are made, and structures the process by using special, software supported methods. See for example Petrovic [28].

An EMR can be used not only for Electronic Brainstorming (EBS) but also for producing joint documents, co-ordinating and fine-tuning of projects, as well as grouping and evaluating alternatives $[19]$ . At the beginning of the EBS meeting, participants are introduced to the task by the moderator. After putting the question to be considered into the system, it is distributed to all participants and displayed permanently on the screen. Each participant can enter ideas that will be passed on to others randomly; others create further ideas or comments which, together with the original idea, will again be passed on to participants. Before passing a contribution on, it can be allocated to a theme. If participants cannot find an appropriate theme or group of themes, they can create them. In this way chains of ideas and comments are developed.

Technological variation 2: Traditional moderated meeting. As early as in the late 50s, it was recognised that controlling the group process by a moderator who is content neutral can positively affect the results of meetings [21]. Generally speaking the moderator plays several roles by: a) guiding the flow of information; b) influencing the sequence of group activities; and c) assisting in forming relations between group members [15]. For a detailed description of tasks and activities performed by moderators see Dubs and Hayne [7].

Traditional brainstorming can be performed in different ways. However the moderator must not criticise ideas nor check them for viability. The fact that all participants can see the generated ideas supports participants in finding associations and analogies; this promotes creativity [23]. Individual variations of brainstorming, such as brainwriting and the 6–3–5 method differ in the way ideas are made visible [13].

## Reasons for the selected design of the study

The advantages of this procedure are: a controlled experiment allows exact isolation of the variables; the participants are a natural group with a high level of interest in the solution; the group constituency and the duration of the effort roughly correspond to project teams in organisations; and a task is taken from organisational practice.

This design attempts to make use of these principles [c.f. 9] to improve the quality of the results.

## 3.2. Variables

## Effectiveness

Quantity of results. The total quantity of contributions can be counted. The number of different contributions requires that a team of experts checks duplicates before counting. Contributions that were developed in chains are considered different if the development signifies substantial progress. In order to offset bias of the experts, assessments were carried out individually at first and the results were then discussed until agreement was reached. Interpersonal reliability of results was measured by the extent of the assessed duplicates and different contributions that had been recognised by all experts without discussion $[9,10,5]$ .

Quality of results. The quality was assessed by the same team of experts. Each contribution was attributed a quality value on a seven-step Likert scale. The procedure was the same as used in evaluation of quantity, with discussion. As a measure for interpersonal reliability in individual evaluations Cronbach's test of reliability of expert team opinions was used. A “good” contribution obtained quality values of at least 4 on the scale of 7 allocated by the team of experts.

Attitudes towards results, process and technologies used. The attitudes of participants towards the results, the process, and the technology used were obtained both in a computer supported and traditionally moderated group setting by means of an electronic questionnaire. This facilitated anonymous replies to questions about individual goals of the investigation. On a seven-step Likert scale, participants were able to communicate their agreement with 5 statements on the results achieved, 6 statements relating to their satisfaction with the meeting, and 5 statements characterising used technologies. The levels on the scale range from total rejection (1) to total agreement (7). The selection of questions was based on similar experiments with EMRs [6,3].

## Efficiency

Efficiency of meetings was expressed by the time spent by participants resulting in wages or opportunity costs, travel costs incurred and necessary expenses for organising and making the appropriate infrastructure available. There are no absolute figures for time requirements to achieve the “correct” solution. Thus we use the average time required for a “good” contribution. The remaining factors mentioned above remain unchanged throughout the experiment.

## 3.3. Procedure

Technological variation 1: Computer supported session

Seven groups of five or six people were involved in Electronic Brainstorming using the Software GroupSystems [19] and the Graz Electronic Meeting Room [29,30]. At the beginning of each session the participants were introduced to the basic functions of an EMR and Electronic brainstorming in particular for about ten minutes. In order to preempt misleading results through technical problems in handling the system, a ten minutes' pretest session was carried out.

Then, following the presentation of the questions for brainstorming, the real session was started. This session lasted as long as ideas continued to be generated and participants expressed their desire to conclude the meeting. The results of this stage were then printed out and handed to the participants. In conclusion the survey investigating the level of satisfaction was completed using the electronic questionnaire. For further study, all sessions were recorded on video and audio tape and all input was saved (Fig. 2).

Technological variation 2: Traditional (with a moderator)

Traditional brainstorming was carried out with the same number of groups in the same location. Each session was introduced by a brief presentation of the rules of brainstorming. This step is essential, since the participants' anonymity cannot be maintained; thus, the rules of brainstorming has to be explained by the moderator. Subsequently the brainstorming question was presented and written on a flip chart. During the session, participants wrote down ideas on small cards that were pinned in chronological order onto a board by the moderator so that everyone could see them. The participants were also allowed to ask for cards to be positioned where they wished.

The board thus fulfils the function of the generally visible public screen in the EMR and the optional positioning of cards corresponds to the chain of ideas in the computer supported variation. The end of the session was determined in the same way as before and the survey of attitudes used the same electronic questionnaire. To check the results, participants were allowed to look at the cards; they were also given a written version of the minutes of the meeting.

## 4. Hypotheses

In the second half of the eighties research into EMS produced widely diverging and contradictory results. Only recent studies have investigated more closely and attempted to explain and integrate the results $[31,14,33]$ . We constructed our hypotheses on the basis of research carried out in similar environments. The hypotheses relate to external factors, viz. to the task of creating ideas and using technological support.

Several studies confirmed a positive correlation between the use of information technology to support meetings and the number of generated ideas $[37,36,1,24,25,35]$ . These led to the first hypothesis:

\- Hypothesis 1: The degree of participation is higher in computer supported groups than in those that have a traditional moderator.

We assume that in computer supported brainstorming, due to the greater number of contributions, the number of different contributions increases, too:

\- Hypothesis 2: Computer supported groups generate more different ideas.

Most published studies show that technological support improves or does not negatively affect the quality of results $[36,8,11,12,16,37]$ . These fairly consistent results lead to another hypothesis:

\- Hypothesis 3: Computer supported groups generate in total more “good” contributions than traditional groups.

This leads to:

\- Hypothesis 4: Computer supported groups generate contributions of higher quality per item than traditional groups.

Inconsistent results are obtained in the studies with regards to attitudes of participants – expressed in confidence and satisfaction. Due to very similar external factors in those studies that showed positive correlation between technological support and attitudes to the achievements [24,36], we formulated the following hypothesis:

\- Hypothesis 5: Computer supported groups show a more positive attitude towards the results than traditionally moderated groups.

Inconsistent reports are found on the satisfaction with the running of the meeting: we assume a positive correlation between technological support and satisfaction [36,17,24–26], thus:

\- Hypothesis 6: Computer supported groups show a greater degree of satisfaction with the process of brainstorming than traditional groups.

We also postulate that the participants have a more positive attitude towards computer supported brainstorming than towards the traditional method.

\- Hypothesis 7: Computer supported groups show a more positive attitude towards the technology used.

In conclusion we would focused on efficiency and assumed that the use of information technology reduces the time requirements for meetings $[2,25,26]$ :

## 5. Results

5.1. The quantity and the quality of the results achieved and the efficiency of meetings

To check the quantity and quality of the contributions and to compare efficiency, a t-test was carried out. Results are shown in Table 1. The degree of participation expressed by the total number of contributions generated in a meeting is very significantly higher (p < 0.01) in the computer supported group than in the traditional one $^{1}$ . Computer supported groups made 104 contributions compared to 61. This is in line with hypothesis H1. Hypothesis H2 concerning the number of different contributions is also supported.

The absolute number of generated “good” contributions in computer-supported groups is significantly higher (p < 0.05). While they generated on average 74 “good” contributions, traditionally moderated groups came up with only 52.

This supports hypothesis H3. The average measure of quality per contribution generated, however, is highly significantly lower in the computer supported groups. While the former achieved a mean value of 5.75 on a scale of seven, the team of experts allocated a mean value of only 4.66 to the latter. Hence, hypothesis H4 is not supported.

The time requirement per “good” contribution does not indicate any significant differences between groups. Hence hypothesis H8 has to be rejected, and there is no difference in time requirement per “good” contribution generated. The difference of the length of meetings is also highly significant: we reject hypothesis H9, and conclude that computer supported meetings last longer.

## 5.2. The attitudes of participants

For each of the 16 questions on attitudes about process, results, and technology a t-test was performed in order to show any significant differences. The results are shown in Table 2. The text in brackets in the section on technology was used in the questionnaire of the traditional group. A highly significant difference $p < 0.01$ is shown in the assessment of the role of the moderator. As expected, the traditional groups show higher results than computer supported groups. Significant differences $p < 0.05$ can be observed in three areas: Computer supported groups get more fun out of this form of co-operation than traditionally moderated groups. However, traditional groups perceive this form of co-operation to be fairer and they feel more personal involvement and responsibility for the results achieved than the computer supported groups. Low significance $p < 0.10$ can be attributed to the difference of attitudes. Traditional groups feel that the result corresponds largely to the individual contributions.

Table 3 combines single questions to check hypotheses concerning attitudes towards contributions, the process, and technology. To test reliability, Cronbach's alpha was used. High values (except for one case) indicate that the combinations of questions are permissible as they show a common trend. A weakly significant difference $p < 0.10$ was detected in attitudes of participants. As can be seen, traditional groups show a more positive attitude towards contributions than computer supported groups. Hence hypothesis H5 is rejected. Hypotheses H6 and H7 are not supported since neither satisfaction with the process nor attitudes towards technologies differ.

## 6. Discussion of results and issues of further research

The significantly higher number of generated contributions in the computer supported groups is due to several reasons. The higher degree of anonymity reduces group pressure as well as social control, which promotes unusual, creative behaviour. Communication via an electronic medium also leads to de-personification of contributions; this reduces orientation towards a group but also towards personal ideas $[27]$ . Electronic brainstorming leads to higher media speed; obviously writing and reading electronically transmitted contributions is faster than writing cards and having them collected by a moderator [38]. The number of duplicates of generated ideas is nearly identical, due to the similarity of displaying ideas.

The greater number of contributions results in a greater number of “good” contributions – as can be seen in the support of hypothesis H3. The advantage of computer supported groups is, however, reduced because of the lower average quality of contributions. Anonymity, de-personification, and higher media speed not only lead to more contributions but also to lower quality on average. This is indicated by a greater number of humorous and irrelevant remarks.

Further process gains in EMRs found in other studies were due to process structuring, parallel communication that reduces interference (occurs when two ore more participants try to speak at the same time) and the development of a group memory (a collection of contributed ideas instantly accessible [27]). These were also achieved by the moderator without computer support.

The extended length of computer supported meetings is due to better use of the creative potential of participants, as manifested in the higher degree of participation.

The experiment had a more prominent role of the moderator in the traditional method. The higher level of fun experienced by the computer supported groups is probably due to the novelty of using an EMR and of using personal computers in creating ideas. The fact that this way of co-operating has been felt to be less fair may be due to varying degrees of keyboard skills. However, the danger of dominance due to positions of power or better rhetorical skills is reduced in both variations. Less personal responsibility for the results achieved in the computer supported groups can be traced to the de-personification of the electronic medium.

The traditional groups felt that the results depend more on the personal contributions made and felt a higher degree of responsibility for the achieved results because of the increased transparency over the computerized room. Participants immediately see how contributions are generated and passed on. These two factors may also be responsible for the fact that the general attitude towards results in traditional groups is more positive than that expressed by the computer supported groups. Of particular note are the equal high degree of satisfaction that participants had with the process and the technology.

In conclusion it can be said that computer supported brainstorming leads to more contributions, more different contributions and more “good” contributions than traditional brainstorming. In total, individual meetings last longer when computer supported with possible better use of the creative potential of participants. The time requirement per “good” contribution is identical. Concerning the attitudes of participants, it can be stated that no difference in process and technology can be detected; however, traditional groups show a slightly more positive attitude towards the results.

Probably, groups should have been observed over a longer period of time in order to eliminate effects like beginner's fears and euphoria and the effects of the learning curve. Thus research which has found time savings of between 50 and 90 per cent shows that these may be due to a reduction in the number of meetings until the problem is solved and a shorter time span between meetings [22,32]. More research is also needed on the process of convergent thinking, i.e. organising ideas. First findings [20] and experience with business people in the Graz Electronic Meeting Room show, for this stage of process, a number of potential areas for development of more useful software. A third area for further research is the interdependency between organisational culture and usefulness of an EMR. For instance, some preliminary findings lead to the suggestion, that traditional groups work better in ‘information-based’ organizations, where a free flow of information is a cultural given. On the other hand, the anonymity of computer supported groups is very useful in traditional hierarchies.
