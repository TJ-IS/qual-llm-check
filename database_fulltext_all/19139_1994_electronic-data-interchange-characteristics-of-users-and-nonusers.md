# Electronic data interchange: Characteristics of users and nonusers

Snehamay Banerjee

Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA, USA

Damodar Y. Golhar

Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, MI, USA

Information exchange between a firm and its customers and suppliers is vital for the business. Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) is the new frontier of communication technology that facilitates information exchange. This study is aimed at defining key issues of EDI from both users' and nonusers' perspectives. We examine the positive and negative impact of various factors on the EDI selection decision, and the impact of EDI on the firm's employees. To this end, we surveyed both users and nonusers to get the proper perspective of underlying issues in adopting EDI. Our study finds that the acceptance of this technology is growing steadily. Customer and peer pressure and the desire for speedier and better communications are major motivators for using EDI. We find that its users are generally satisfied with performance. Also, EDI calls for a better educated work force. We also discuss the significance of our findings.

Keywords: Electronic data interchange; Data transmission; Computer communication

## 1. Introduction

Development of systems to support information flow between firms has been the focus of developers for some time. Such systems are commonly known as inter-organization systems (IOS) [14]: their implementation is expected to make the user firm competitive and enlarge its strategic options by identifying: (a) focused market, (b) supporting product differentiation, and (c) reducing overall cost [4, 21]. There are different types of IOS. Benjamin et al. [2] presents a classification scheme for different IOS systems, as presented in Figure 1. Expected benefits from an IOS depend on the type of system implemented.

We focus on an IOS that automates routine transactions by integrating tasks and functions across a predetermined set of organizational boundaries. Such an IOS, commonly known as Electronic Data Interchange (EDI), has received widespread attention in recent years. EDI is the new frontier of communication technology. It involves an automated exchange of business documents. According to Hansen and Hill [12], “EDI

is the movement of business documents electronically between or within firms in a structured, machine-retrievable, data format that permits data to be transferred, without rekeying, from a business application in one location to a business application in another location." In this environment, a computer can directly use the data sent in electronic form. Other communication technologies, such as facsimile (FAX) and E-mail, are not considered a part of EDI, because they do not support automation facilities and the information sent using such communication technologies has to be rearranged or rekeyed for further use. A schematic of the basic EDI functions is given in Figure 2 [22]. The three basic functions of EDI include: (a) data transfer between trading partners (actual transmission), (b) data transformation or translation between proprietary format (as used by sender or receiver) and the standard format (as required to meet transmission protocol), and (c) directing the data to and gathering it from different computer applications. It should be noted that data can be transmitted between trading partners either by establishing a dedicated link between them or by using one of the existing communication networks.

It is predicted that, by changing the organization structure and work processes, EDI will change the way firms do business and that EDI will become an essential. Keen $[15]$ argues its economics are so compelling that no firm can ignore the competitive opportunity. However, the growth of EDI usage since its inception has been limited in the industrial world. Only about five percent of the total business transactions in the U.S. has been carried out in 1990 using EDI $[13]$ . Also, a sizable percentage of these firms are not considering its implementation in the near future $[7,24]$ . It is also reported that less than half of the U.S. firms are planning to use EDI in the near future. Given its well publicized benefits this is surprising. It is therefore important to find out why. Firms that actively considered the adoption of EDI and decided not to use it can provide valuable information about their reasons. We define these firms as the nonuser firms. Similar studies are being conducted at other countries to identify the nature and type of problems associated with EDI use and to develop ways to address those problems $[25]$ .

Expected benefits of EDI include faster communication between trading partners, reduced overhead cost, increased data accuracy, improved information about suppliers, and reduced inventory $[2,8,12]$ . Difficulties associated with using EDI include a general lack of signature or authorization for transactions, audit trails, and a universally acceptable standard for data transmission. However, the impact of these factors on the EDI adoption decision of different organizations have not been tested empirically. Examination of these issues are important to EDI vendors and to firms considering EDI implementation. A potential EDI user firm can compare its own profile with that of the user and nonuser groups to judge the suitability of EDI for the firm. Critical examination of the benefits and problems will also help in any EDI selection decision.

This empirical research addresses these issues. In particular, objectives of the study are to: (a) develop EDI user and nonuser profiles,

(b) examine the influence of various factors on a firms' EDI selection decision,

(c) evaluate EDI performance,

(d) discuss realized benefits of EDI, and

(e) examine effects of EDI on human resource requirements.

## 2. Research methodology

EDI is expected to increase the speed and accuracy of communication. Direct computer to computer linkage eliminates the need for rekeying the data thus reducing potential errors $[19]$ . This can reduce labor cost and costs due to data errors $[6]$ . Faster communication reduces purchasing lead times enabling buyers to purchase more frequently in smaller lot sizes, thus reducing inventory costs $[3]$ . Quick response to customers creates competitive advantage $[18]$ . Successful EDI depends on its adoption by the firm's peers $[5]$ and the firm's customers $[10]$ . However, a recent study on the insurance industry determined that such external forces have little influence on an insurance firm's EDI adoption decision $[20]$ . Such studies help us focus on the factors influencing EDI adoption. Success or failure of computer based information systems depends on user satisfaction and their perceived benefits of the system $[11]$ . We therefore considered user satisfaction with current EDI systems and the perceived benefits of EDI systems to both user and nonuser firms. A review of the existing literature and discussion with EDI consultants resulted in our developing two separate questionnaires: one for EDI users and the other for nonusers. The questionnaires contained common items that are measured using the same scale. The questionnaires were validated by two consultants who are involved in EDI implementation. They were asked to identify ambiguous and irrelevant questions, and any for which answers may not be easily available. The questionnaire modification process continued until the external reviewers were fully satisfied. Both were then mailed in the same envelope to computer professionals of 330 firms in the U.S. The person was chosen at random, one per company, from the mailing list of Data Processing Management Association (DPMA). The firms were chosen randomly from the DPMA lists of four states in the U.S. The respondents were asked to complete: (i) the user version if they are using EDI, (ii) the nonuser version if they had evaluated EDI in the past and chose not to use it, or (iii) ignore the questionnaire if the firm does not belong to either category. For ease of identification, the user and nonuser questionnaires were printed in different colours.

A total of 62 usable responses were received. Twenty six (42%) of the responding firms are EDI user firms; 17 of these are manufacturing firms. The remaining 36 of the responding firms are nonusers; 12 are manufacturing firms. The responding firms represented diverse industries, such as: merchandising, banking, manufacturing and health care. To assess reliability of the responses, some people were contacted at random from each group by telephone. These confirmed the reliability of the responses. The profile items (number of employees, annual revenues, etc.) were measured directly. For all other items, a 5-point scale was developed. Any statistical significance is identified in the tables with one, two and three asterisks to indicate a 5%, 1% and 0.1% significance level, respectively.

## 3. Survey results

## 3.1. Profiles of EDI user and nonuser firms

EDI implementation requires capital investment and may involve other expenditure (e.g., upgrading the computer and integrating some systems). It seems that large firms should be able to such investments more easily than smaller ones and, therefore, EDI user firms are expected to be the larger ones. To examine this, we compared employee size and annual revenue of responding firms and categorized this information into manufacturing and nonmanufacturing in Tables 1 and 2. 50% of the EDI user firms employ more than 2500 workers, while only 5% of the nonusers are so large. Also, no EDI user firm employs less than 251 workers, while 47% nonuser firms do. 46% percent of EDI user firms have an annual revenue of more than \$500 million, whereas only 10% of the nonusers generate that much. At the lower end, none of the EDI users generate less than \$10 million, while 50% of the nonuser firms are in this category. The revenue profile is comparable to that of a national survey [7].

The volume of EDI transactions is one of the factors that affects EDI usage. Growth in the volume of transactions is a function of the number of trading partners that an EDI user firm will add in future. The user firms were asked to indicate the number of EDI trading partners they added between 1988 and 1990, and expect to add in 1991 and 1992. Their responses are summarized in Table 3. To identify a trend in the EDI system acquisition, the user firms were queried about the year in which they installed EDI. However, many of these firms are at the early phase of EDI implementation. As technology matures, the user firms are expected to increase their EDI usage and add more trading partners. This will ensure a steady growth of EDI users and may change the current demographics. On the other hand, nonusers are smaller firms. However, in the next three years, a considerable percentage (42%) of the nonuser firms will reconsider their decision to use EDI. Nonuser firms with some EDI experience are more likely to reexamine their use of EDI. These results suggest that many firms will implement EDI in the 1990s.

## 3.2. Factors affecting the EDI implementation decision

A literature review and discussions with EDI consultants helped us identify 23 factors that may influence the EDI implementation decision. To measure the level of influence of each factor on the decision, a five-point scale was developed (1 = no influence, 2 = minor influence, 3 = moderate influence, 4 = major influence and 5 = a great influence). To examine the difference in motivation for implementing EDI in the manufacturing and nonmanufacturing firms we compared responses by both user and nonuser groups. We find no difference in the motivation for implementing EDI between manufacturing and nonmanufacturing EDI user firms (p = 0.05). The same is also true for EDI nonuser firms (p = 0.05). Each of the 23 factors has some influence on the EDI implementation decision. However, some of these factors have more influence than others. We select those factors whose means are significantly higher than 3.0. These are marked in Table 4 with asterisks: 12 factors are more influential among the user firms and 11 are more influential for nonuser firms. We asked five EDI systems managers from five user firms to group the 23 factors and, because of this, they are grouped into five categories: (a) customer, (b) communication, (c) peer pressure, (d) cost, and (e) productivity.

## 3.2.1. Customer related factors

EDI user firms report that customer request for EDI and better customer service have most influence on the EDI implementation decision. EDI's potential to increase sales has less influence on EDI implementation decision. For nonusers, better customer service is the only significant customer related factor. We also find that customer request has significantly higher influence on user firms than on nonusers. For other factors, no significant differences between the two groups are observed. Other recent studies also found customer request a significant factor for EDI implementation among user firms.

## 3.2.2. Communication related factors

Of the six factors in this category, there is agreement among both EDI users and nonusers on the significant influence of four (better and speedier communication with trading partners, improved data control, and reduced paper work). However, there is disagreement among these groups regarding the significance of the remaining two: quick response is more important to user firms, while increased data accuracy is important to nonuser firms. When the mean responses are compared, we find better and speedier communication with trading partners having significantly greater influence on user firms than on nonuser firms (better communication reflects the overall quality while speedier communication only refers to reduction in time lag).

## 3.2.3. Peer pressure related factors

Peer pressure (i.e., adoption of technology by competing firms) is expected to influence the adoption decision of any new technology. We find all peer pressure factors have significant influence on the EDI implementation decision of user firms. There is clearly a perception among user firms that EDI systems make them competitive, help them meet industry standards, and improve product service. However, for nonusers firms only improvement of product/service has major influence on the EDI implementation decision. Possibly some of the nonusers belong to a part of the industries where no firms use EDI and, therefore, firms do not have pressure to meet industry standards.

## 3.2.4. Productivity related factors

Increasing productivity and internal efficiency have significantly motivated user firms to adopt EDI, but the ease of processing orders, aid in accounting and billing, and increased internal efficiency have more influence on nonusers. When mean scores of the two group are compared, use of EDI as an aid in accounting and billing have significantly less influence on EDI users. There are no significant differences in the influence of other six factors on user and nonuser firms.

## 3.2.5. Cost related factors

Some analysts believe that EDI is a potential tool for cost reduction. However, we find that potential is not an influential factor in the EDI implementation decision of the users. In particular, it is interesting to note that the potential to reduce the number of employees has the least influence. For nonusers, reduction in administrative cost is a significant factor. A between-group comparison of factors indicates that two [reduction in the number of employees and reduction in inventory cost] influence user firms less than nonusers. Since these factors do not have significant influence on both groups, any further intergroup comparison will be of little value.

While each of the factors has some influence on EDI implementation, about 50% of these have significantly more influence than others. Among these more influential factors, there are seven that are common to both groups. These factors are: better customer service, better communication with trading partners, speedier communication with trading partners, improved data control, reduced paper work, improved product/service quality, and increased internal efficiency. The cost saving opportunities have the least influence on the implementation decision. This is not surprising, because successful diffusion of a new technology requires resources that add to the initial cost $[16,23]$ . Controls are introduced later.

## 3.3. Difficulties with EDI implementation

There seem to be some difficulties in using EDI. Practitioners are concerned about shortcomings such as: lack of audit trail, lack of signature etc. To test the influence of the shortcomings, both groups are asked to indicate, on a five-point scale (1 = no influence, 2 = minor influence, 3 = moderate influence, 4 = major influence and 5 = great influence), the extent to which these affected the EDI implementation decision. Table 5 summarizes the results. None of these factors have high influence (i.e., mean score $\geqslant$ 3.0) on users. Lack of data integrity and lack of control have the least influence. These two have significantly more influence on nonusers. Lack of signature (authorization) and lack of data integrity are the two most influential factors for nonusers in their decision not to use EDI. When the mean scores are compared, user firms show significantly less concern about lack of signature, lack of data integrity, lack of hard copies of documents and lack of control than nonusers.

## 3.4. EDI performance issues

Successful performance is critical for EDI acceptance in the business world. We used nine factors to measure its performance. Using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = extremely dissatisfied, 2 = dissatisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = satisfied, and 5 = extremely satisfied), the user firms were asked to evaluate performance. The results are shown in Table 6. Except for flexibility, all characteristics have an average score above 3. A significant level of user satisfaction stems from speed of communication, interface capability, growth potential (to accommodate increased volume of data), confidence (reliability), compatibility, and control of the system. However, users are neutral on flexibility (ease of handling different formats), documentation, and security procedures. Overall, user firms express satisfaction but there is a scope for further improvement.

## 3.5. Benefits of EDI

Some literature gives a list of potential benefits of EDI. The EDI user firms were asked to indicate, using a 5-point scale (1 = not at all, 2 = minor extent, 3 = moderate extent, 4 = major extent, and 5 = great extent), whether such benefits are realized. Results are reported in Table 7. Only five have been at least moderately realized (mean score $\geqslant$ 3.0). Comparing the responses of manufacturing and nonmanufacturing firms we find no differences in realized benefits. This shows that the benefits of EDI may not be dependent on the type of business. It is interesting to note that EDI significantly improved customer relations but failed to improve vendor relations.

The propagation and effectiveness of any technology depends, to a great extent, on the time and effort required to learn and use the system. Since EDI is a software driven technology (that may use specialized hardware) time spent by employees in learning it use can be critical. Respondents were queried about the time needed and the level of difficulty to learn EDI. The users reported that the concept is not difficult, but it takes a long time to learn its detailed use. Therefore, the EDI user firms should plan for early training for employees who directly use EDI.

Error handling capabilities of a software, user interface, and system users' skills play a dominant role in designing user documentation. Good system documentation requires compatibility between system sophistication and user skill levels. Hence it is important for EDI developers to know the educational level of employees who will use EDI. When asked about the average level of education of the employees involved in using EDI, 35%, 46%, and 19% of the user firms report some college, undergraduate degree, and graduate degree, respectively. These findings suggest that EDI software, the user interface, and system documentation should be developed for fairly well educated users.

## 4. Conclusions

There are authors who state that investment in information technology and specifically in EDI is essential for firms in the 1990s and that the use of EDI will increase dramatically. However, the actual use of EDI is still much less than predicted. Our study focuses on the difference between EDI users and nonusers to understand why some firms decided not to implement EDI. It also identifies issues that can help EDI developers in improving their products and potential users in preparing for EDI implementation. Given the sample size, the findings cannot be considered conclusive, but the study examines trends in EDI usage and identifies critical EDI related issues.

We found that EDI users are normally found in large corporations. Many are at the early stage of EDI implementation and are adding EDI trading partners at a steady pace. Some have implemented EDI at the customer's request. This fact has significant marketing implications for EDI vendors. If a large firm with a strong supplier base becomes an EDI user, it will probably add some of its suppliers to the EDInet each year. Thus, EDI vendors should identify a few important suppliers of an EDI user firm and target them for future sales. On the other hand, nonuser firms are smaller, but 42% will reexamine the question of EDI implementation in the next three years. The nonuser firms with some EDI experience are most likely to reconsider. Hence, EDI vendors should pursue their marketing effort with these firms.

Customer request to use EDI, its potential for a better and speedier communication with trading partners, remaining competitive, and meeting industry standards significantly influence the EDI acquisition decision. It should be noted that customers play a dominant role in creating peer pressure. For nonusers, lack of customer influence and absence of peer pressure are the major reasons for not implementing EDI. The use of EDI is more of an industry specific phenomenon rather than that of a particular firm. Factors that are detrimental to EDI implementation (e.g., lack of audit trail, data integrity) have low influence on both groups. However, user firms are significantly less concerned about such problems than are nonusers. This suggests that either the user firms have effectively dealt with the EDI implementation problems or that such problems are mythical, or that these customers take a calculated risk. A further investigation may help EDI vendors to alleviate nonusers' concerns or suggest a real need for additions to the software.

EDI users express satisfaction with nearly all the performance characteristics of the EDI system. Users are most satisfied with communication speed, reliability, growth potential, interface capability, compatibility, and control characteristics. User are less satisfied with flexibility, documentation and security. Therefore, the EDI system developers should probably pay closer attention to these three characteristics.

Improved customer service and improved communication speed are two commonly realized benefits. Other moderately realized benefits include reduced clerical error, gaining competitive advantage, and improved customer loyalty. Through improved communication with suppliers and carriers, an EDI user firm should be able to reduce inventory. Our findings do not show this to be true (even for manufacturing firms). However, JIT firms get some benefits from EDI.

Successful EDI operation requires a skilled work force. Probably EDI imposes a rigid work environment and demands more from employees, e.g., greater skill and attention. Learning time for using EDI is long. Therefore, firms will have to select skilled employees and start EDI training programs well in advance of its start-up. Since trained employees represent a valuable asset, user firms also need to develop programs to retain them.

Overall, the study finds a steady growth of EDI usage. Larger firms in a competitive market with customer and peer pressure are more likely to implement EDI. It appears that EDI is currently being viewed as an efficient communication tool rather than a means for strategic competitive advantage. EDI improves customer relations but it is not viewed as a tool to enhance vendor relationships. EDI is also not viewed as a cost savings tool.
