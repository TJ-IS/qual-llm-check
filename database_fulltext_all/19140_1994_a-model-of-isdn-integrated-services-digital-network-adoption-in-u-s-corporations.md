# A model of ISDN (integrated services digital network) adoption in U.S. corporations

Vincent S. Lai \*

The University of Alabama in Huntsville, Huntsville, AL, USA

Jan L. Guynes

The University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX, USA

Although ISDN technology has often been touted as the networking technique of the future, its adoption rate has not seen as high as expected. This study develops and tests a model that determines the significant ISDN and organizational characteristics that influence the adoption decision. Business Week 1,000 corporations were surveyed to determine their views on ISDN. The results indicate that this technology is most likely to be adopted by open, dynamic organizations that are conducive to change. However, most of these organizations still fail to utilize ISDN effectively.

Keywords: ISDN; Integrated services digital network; Information technology; Innovation adoption and diffusion; ISDN adoption; Data communication

## 1. Introduction

The objective of Integrated Services Digital Network (ISDN) technology is to provide data communications with voice, video, and data on a single system. The intent is to control the increasingly diversified, complex data communications technologies while reducing the installation and maintenance costs in their facilities.

The future of ISDN remains unclear. Why is ISDN taking so long to reach the market? What are the critical factors that need to be considered when organizations are considering the adoption of ISDN? What characteristics of an organization contribute to its propensity to adopt ISDN? So far, little empirical research has been conducted for exploring its adoption factors.

## 3. Organizational adoption of ISDN

The objective of our research was to provide a research model (Figure 1) for understanding the process of adopting ISDN and to offer a foundation for empirical testing. This model synthesizes the myriad of adoption factors from innovation adoption research, general research on technology implementation, and research on strategic information systems, into a testable model that depicts a more complete view of option for ISDN.

## 3.1. Innovation

Many research studies have been conducted to investigate the influence of innovation characteristics on adoption decision. However, there has been a wide variation in characteristic variables employed for each study. Brown [3] and Rogers [11] summarized them to determine the characteristic variables that were commonly found in most of them. The characteristics include compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, and availability.

Compatibility is the degree to which a new innovation is perceived as being consistent with the potential adopter's current task environment.

Relative advantage is the degree to which a new innovation is perceived by the potential adopters as being superior to the practice(s) it will supersede and any other solution that might be possible. These include the ability to solve pressing problems, generation of higher profits, and enhancement of social status of adopters. Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being difficult to understand. Availability is the existence of the technology in a given location. Here, availability is excluded from the study as ISDN adoption is definitely controlled by its service availability.

## 3.2. Management characteristics

When studying the adoption of an innovation, it is important to know which particular managers or executives are involved. Obviously, the evaluation process, the adoption process, and the implementation success are likely to depend on those directly concerned: their background, qualifications, experience, and orientation. In the past, many management characteristics variables have been used to examine their relevancy in the adoption process. More expressive variables come from the research of Huff and Munro [9] and Alexander [1] who have shown they have a positive influence on the adoption decision. Champions and technology awareness are the underlying factors in the information technology assessment and adoption process.

Curley and Gremillion [5] define champions as “individuals in the organization who make decisive contributions to the innovation by actively and enthusiastically promoting its progress through the critical stages of its development and adoption.” The roles of champions in the innovation adoption decision appear in several forms. They can be the opinion leader, the change agent, or the top management surrogate. No matter what roles the champions play, their existence will affect the outcome of the adoption decision.

Technology awareness by management determines the relative timing of an organization's decision to adopt an innovation, compared to the other organizations. Management with high technology awareness normally has a high tolerance for uncertainty and is eager to try out new ideas. Technology awareness is apparently a significant dimension of the adoption process and this factor probably contributes positively to the adoption decision.

## 3.3. Organizational context

Many research studies in organizations indicate that contextual factors have a significant impact on innovation. Burns and Stalker [4] suggested two different types of organizational structure: mechanistic and organic. A mechanistic structure is similar to Weber's ideal type of bureaucracy, while an organic structure is more dynamic, decentralized, flexible, and informal. Daft [6] states that organic organizations are more susceptible to innovation. He believes that more organic organizations tend to adopt new technology more readily.

The size of an organization is also a factor. It has consistently been found to be positively related to innovativeness, which determines how soon an organization is likely to adopt new ideas. Rogers [11] stated that there are two reasons why size and innovativeness are found to be consistently correlated. First, size is a variable that is easily measured and with a relatively high degree of precision. Second, size is a surrogate measure of several dimensions, such as total resources, slack resources, and organizational structure, that lead to innovation. Stoneman [13] reiterated this observation and indicated that the innovativeness of an organization is determined, primarily by its size.

Brancheau [2] conducted further investigations to identify potential organizational factors that would influence the adoption decisions; these included its openness and interconnectedness, the existence of norms that encourage change, and the degree of slack resources. Our study only considers the openness of the organization and prevalence of norms encouraging change.

In the context of ISDN adoption, openness becomes the degree to which an organization is willing to infuse innovation while changing norms are the positive attitudes toward change among all employees. There should be positive impacts of these on the adoption decision.

## 4. Research methodology

A questionnaire concerning ISDN adoption was developed, based on prior studies [1,2] and field testing. Preliminary interviews identified questions of practical importance. The initial questionnaire was pilot tested in twelve organizations that had already adopted ISDN, and then revised to deal with issues of content and construct validity before being used.

The questionnaire was mailed to the Directors of MIS of the Business Week 1,000 largest industrial companies. Six weeks later, a follow-up mailing was sent to those companies that had not responded. Ninety-nine companies responded to the first mailing and seventy-four responded to the second. Of the 1,000 questionnaires, sixteen were non-deliverable, producing an overall response rate of 17.6 percent. This was good, considering the length, difficulty, and confidential nature of the subject matter of the questionnaire. After reviewing the responses, it was found that some questionnaires were invalid. These were removed, resulting in 153 usable questionnaires.

## 5.2. Decision makers of ISDN adoption

Successful technological innovation requires a competent management system. Effective managers are needed to set the right technological policy for the creation of an environment conducive to innovation and to prioritize available resources to facilitate its development and implementation. In companies like the Business Week 1,000, the MIS management system is normally headed by a Chief Information Officer (CIO), Vice President of Information Systems, or Director of Information Systems. These positions have a continuing responsibility to monitor advances in information technology (IT) and to keep the company informed. But who makes the actual decision to acquire new IT? Our survey results were quite diverse, as shown in Table 2.

The majority (93.5%) of the change agents of IT were senior MIS executives. They decide the acceptance or rejection of IT innovations. Others who make such decisions include the President, VP of Finance, VP of Engineering, and Chief Financial Officer. If the adoption decision was made by a committee, the VP of MIS, Director of MIS, and/or CIO were normally represented.

## 5.4. Reasons for ISDN rejection

Although the problems of ISDN have been cited frequently in the literature, no empirical studies have been conducted to investigate their criticality. In our survey, the high rejection rate of ISDN indicates a great degree of dissatisfaction among the companies who responded. We therefore asked respondents why they rejected ISDN; we then ranked the top three in their order of significance. The following algorithm was used: four points were assigned to the most significant problem, three points to the second, two points to the third, and one point to an unranked problem. The results are shown in Table 4.

The companies that have evaluated, but rejected, ISDN pointed out that ISDN is not revolutionary. This, plus the immaturity and unavailability of ISDN, has prevented its nationwide adoption; e.g. eleven respondents stated that “other networks can serve their communication needs equally well" was most significant, while eleven others believed it was either the second or third most significant reason. Thus potential users are not convinced that ISDN will provide any important service capabilities at present.

The concern with nationwide ISDN is the second most frequently cited rejection reason. ISDN adoption depends on the size and content of the set of existing subscribers. From an economic perspective, the utility of a network service is slight if the subscriber's base is small, but significant when a “critical mass” occurs [8]. A standard for nationwide ISDN-1 is just under way and the subscription base is still small. When the critical mass is not cumulated and ISDN service is isolated in “islands”, it is no wonder that ten respondents cited this reason as the second most significant rejection reason.

## 5.5. Driving forces for ISDN adoption

Issues and technology are two driving forces behind IT assessment and adoption; they can be classified into a $2 \times 2$ matrix to form four distinct models of IT assessment and adoption (see Table 5). The normative model posits that technology adoption is a result of organizational needs and technology opportunities. The opportunistic model, however, suggests technology adoption based on the chance connection between needs and technology. The technology driven model, on the other hand, is interested only in the technological opportunities. The issue driven model suggests that technology should be adopted as a result of a corporate planning process.

## 6.3. Complexity

Respondents were also asked to compare the complexity of ISDN with either their prior networks (if currently using ISDN) or their existing network (if ISDN was not used) by answering three questions about understanding, using, and introducing ISDN. With a scale from “1 = very difficult” to “7 = very easy”, the mean scores are given in Table 10. We did not expect scores to be so low. Maybe the difficulty of understanding and using ISDN have stopped users from implementing advanced communication applications. But respondents indicated that introducing ISDN to users after its implementation was slightly easier than for other networks. Possibly the basic ISDN telephony features (like displaying incoming caller's number and voice mail) aid users and capture their interest. On the whole, our respondents perceived ISDN to be more complex than existing networks.

## 6.4. Champion

Of the fifty-seven companies that had evaluated ISDN (excluding the six companies that did not respond to this question), fifty-one of them had identified at least one champion who was a real advocate of ISDN. In fact, there were five companies that identified themselves as having more than ten champions. On the whole, there are about four champions per company, and they are located within the senior general management and MIS management, plus a few non-MIS department managers and users.

When it comes to making the decision to adopt ISDN, the respondents said that almost all (mean score of 5.6) had champions. For the companies that had adopted ISDN, the respondents stated that champions were no longer as highly involved (mean score of 4.7) as at the decision making stage.

ISDN installation is a technical procedure; therefore, champions do not contribute to the implementation activities: only MIS executives can assist at this stage.

## 6.5. Technology awareness

Technology awareness of MIS management was measured by the number of technical journals and books subscribed, professional meetings attended, and external connections made by the respondents. The results exposed some interesting findings. On average, the respondents subscribed to “1 to 2” books and “3 to 4” journals annually, attended “1 or 2 meetings” annually, and talked to “3 to 5” colleagues outside of the organization about IT on a monthly basis. Further analysis indicated that the ten technology driven companies (45.5%) were well endowed with technical gatekeepers. They have higher technology awareness and more structured procedures for new IT assessment. They subscribed to more books and journals than the others and their MIS executives attended more professional conferences regularly.

## 6.6. Organizational structure

For those companies which had evaluated ISDN, nine questions were included to determine whether the company structure was mechanistic or organic. The results (Table 12) were used to determine which types of companies would be more likely to adopt ISDN.

The overall mean score of the nine questions was 5.7 (where 1 = a perfect mechanistic company and 7 = a perfect organic company), indicating that most respondents felt that they were working in an organic environment. In the organic company, the working environment is usually more open, dynamic, and informal. This is a condition that will encourage computer professionals to scan for new IT to improve their productivity.

## 7. Summary

ISDN services are used by organizations that have sophisticated communication demands. Adopters showed either great enthusiasm or great unhappiness with ISDN. It appears to have been successful in meeting some organizations' communication needs. The extent of ISDN implementation among the adopters is still low. We also found that many of the alleged benefits claimed by the ISDN vendors/ developers remain unfulfilled. However, many adopters agreed that ISDN could enhance communication performance and reduce the company's telecommunication expenses.

The survey indicated that the technology awareness of our respondents, who were the key personnel in the MIS department, was not high. Possibly their busy schedules and work-loads prohibit them from meeting external colleagues, attending professional conferences, and subscribing to professional journals, etc. Hopefully, they have “technologists” on their staff who possess this technical awareness.

It was interesting to note that though the ISDN adopter profile consists of an open, dynamic organization that promotes change, these adopters have only tapped the surface of ISDN's capabilities. This could possibly be attributed to the imminent arrival of broadband ISDN.
