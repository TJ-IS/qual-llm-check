# Knowledge acquisition skills and traits: A self-assessment of knowledge engineers

Peter P. Mykytyn, Jr. \*, Kathleen Mykytyn \* and M.K. Raja

University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX 76019, USA

The academic and practitioner literature has suggested that knowledge engineers are critical members of the expert systems development process, which includes knowledge acquisition. In fact, knowledge acquisition has often been cited as being the bottleneck to successful development efforts. Unfortunately, other than normative viewpoints, little attention has been paid to the behavioral and interpersonal skill set and related training of knowledge engineers, especially as pertains to the knowledge acquisition process. This study surveyed 101 persons who are, or have been, actively engaged in knowledge acquisition activities; the intent was to determine their interpersonal skills training, to ascertain their perceptions of how important many behavioral and interpersonal skills are for knowledge acquisition, and to determine how proficient knowledge engineers believe they are in terms of these skills and traits. Results indicate only minimal training in most interpersonal skills. In addition, the results showed that knowledge engineers generally feel less qualified in many of the more important skills, possibly as a result of the lack of effective education and training.

Keywords: Expert systems; Knowledge engineer; Knowledge acquisition; Interpersonal skills; Training; Self-assessment

## 1. Introduction

A critical member of most expert systems (ES) development projects is the knowledge engineer (KE). Like traditional information systems development projects, the KE must possess a high degree of technical expertise. Unlike these, through knowledge acquisition they must acquire, refine, and structure factual and heuristic knowledge by applying efforts involving significant interaction with domain experts to elicit the knowledge base for the ES.

In addition, KEs may be regarded by organizations as essential components of ES development projects, but, given their recent emergence, organizations may simply be uncertain as to what skills, training, etc. are important for KEs. Much could be gained if data regarding the background, skills, and training of KEs were made available to organizations that develop ES and to institutions that provide much of the higher education to prospective KEs. Such information would enable ES developers to reconcile any apparent differences and uncertainty that may exist between what is needed of KEs and what skills and training KEs actually possess. Further, this information may provide a barometer for ES projects that have already been implemented by allowing ES developers the opportunity to correlate the success or failure of the projects with the skills and training that are either not currently considered important, but which should be. Alternatively, it may allow modification of those skills that may be deemed to impede ES development. In addition, educational institutions that offer courses involving knowledge engineering could use this data to examine their curricula to ascertain if changes or improvements need to be made. Unfortunately, there has been little, if any, empirical research conducted to determine the expected or actual background, skills, and traits of KEs.

This study begins to provide answers to many of these questions. Specifically, a survey was conducted that examined the background, skills, and training of KEs who are actively engaged in knowledge acquisition, a bottleneck to effective ES development [11].

## 2. Definition and importance of knowledge engineers

The uncertainty surrounding the job of KEs is perhaps best expressed in a report by Zunin and Zunin. Their study, based on a survey of recruiting firms across the United States, reported that respondents consistently questioned “just what a knowledge engineer is” [18]. Martin and Oxman [9] define KEs as individuals who design and build knowledge-based systems, such as ES, by working with domain experts to map information into a suitable form for building such systems. Regarding skills and training, they only say that KEs “will often have to rely on a multiplicity of skills from a variety of academic areas including computer science and cognitive psychology”.

McGraw and Harbison–Briggs [10] state that KEs are responsible for structuring ES. Necessary skills include being a fast learner, an effective communicator, organized, able to conceptualize well, and having knowledge in many diverse areas. They also feel that KEs are: managers who must actively lead and control the knowledge acquisition process involving domain experts and themselves; facilitators who use verbal and non-verbal behaviors to ensure that knowledge acquisition goals are achieved; and are persons who must possess good leadership skills relating to goal achievement and interpersonal relations. Activities include contributing and evaluating ideas, gaining consensus, synthesizing ideas of others, resolving conflicts, setting the communications climate, and promoting interaction.

A more recent study by Couger and McIntyre [1] was based on a survey of 238 persons selected from the membership roles of the American Association for Artificial Intelligence. It was primarily concerned with perceptual differences about the work environment of KEs and other computer professionals, especially software engineers. Although this work provides ES developers with specific guidelines on job motivation, measures of satisfaction, social need strength, etc., it does not address the issues of background, skills and training of KEs.

A comprehensive review of the entire scope of issues dealing with KEs, knowledge acquisition, etc. was provided by Fellers [4]. Regarding the skill set of KEs, he noted that numerous non-technical skills and traits are essential for successful ES development. These include good communications and conceptual ability, patience, and organizational and domain knowledge. Although Feller's conclusions are based on a review of existing literature, it is almost all of a prescriptive nature.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1 Subjects

Preliminary discussions with organizations located in a large metropolitan area in the southwestern United States revealed that KEs are often very difficult to identify. In practice, executives in the organizations surveyed stated that many individuals performed KE duties without possessing that specific title. Knowing this, telephonic contacts were made with organizations throughout the United States and Canada who were known to be actively engaged in ES development, either for in-house use or for resale. A total of 32 private organizations and two governmental agencies were contacted. Also, six individuals identified as performing ES development on a consulting-type basis participated.

During conversations with prospective participants, particular attention was paid to identifying only individuals who had actively engaged in ES development projects and also participated in traditional knowledge acquisition activities. For our purposes, knowledge acquisition was defined as “acquiring information directly from domain experts”.

In most cases one of the researchers spoke directly with the participant. In some cases the same researcher spoke with a manager who, in turn, identified several of their employees. In all cases the participant's and the organization's name were removed. Participants and managers were informed of the purposes of the study and were also told that they would receive a copy of the Executive Summary of the results.

A total of 152 persons were initially contacted, and 126 persons were identified as willing to participate. One hundred and one questionnaires were actually returned, giving a response rate of 66%.

## 3.2 Instrumentation

The instrument utilized in this study was based on a questionnaire concerning behavioral skills and traits of or for systems analysts developed by Green [5]. Much of that instrument was concerned with behavioral skills and traits of and for systems analysts. However, it was deemed useful as a starting point since it has been suggested that many similarities exist between ES and traditional information systems development [17].

Part I of the questionnaire dealt with background information: job title, length of time with current employer and in current position, educational background, type and intensity of training in subject areas (e.g., interpersonal communication, listening behavior, interviewing, etc.), and primary type of business of the participant's organization.

Part IIA of the questionnaire identified 26 traits and behavioral skills that have been cited, either specifically or in broader terms, as being important for KEs to be effective. In developing the questionnaire, particular attention was paid to the references cited previously. In some instances, many skills/traits are precisely named, e.g., conceptualizing, patience, leadership, domain knowledge, etc. In other cases, skills/traits such as listening, interviewing, speaking, tolerance, etc. can be broadly categorized as communication and/or interpersonal skills/traits. For that reason, additional skills/traits not specifically identified in the literature were included in the questionnaire. Each was identified in bold print and was followed by a brief definition. Participants were asked to rate each in terms of its importance for a KE to perform effective knowledge acquisition. Responses were measured using a seven point Likert-type scale with 1 identified as Very Unimportant and 7 as Very Important.

Part IIB identified the same 26 traits and behavioral skills as Part IIA, but participants were asked to rate their own competence in each skill or trait (i.e., how qualified they believed they were in each). The order of the 26 skills and traits was randomized for Part IIB in order to minimize a response bias. Again, responses were measured using a seven point scale with 1 meaning Very Unqualified and 7 for Very Qualified. As in Part IIA, each skill or trait was emphasized and defined. Table 1 contains the definitions.

One aspect of the skills and traits is the issue of self-reporting of perceptions of proficiency by the KEs. Although perceptions lack complete objectivity, they can provide AI researchers and ES developers a first step in determining a more realistic status for knowledge acquisition skills. Instead of relying merely on normative statements provided in textbooks, this study addresses this issue by surveying only those KEs who have significant experience with the knowledge acquisition activity. Further, Nelson [12] indicates that self-report measures dealing with individual ability are often chosen as the preferred measure. A number of studies in the psychology literature pertaining to self-evaluation and/or task usefulness are noted. These include Ekpo-Ufot [3], Mabe and West [8], and Terborg et al. [16].

## 3.3 Instrument validity and reliability

Content validity considers whether instrument questions/measures have been drawn from a universal pool $[2]$ $[7]$ . In this regard, the literature pertaining to knowledge acquisition was examined extensively in order to determine the behavioral skills and traits most often cited as essential for successful knowledge acquisition. In addition, discussions with pilot study participants, who were all experienced KEs with extensive knowledge acquisition backgrounds, revealed that the skills and traits identified were appropriate.

A second aspect of instrument validation is construct validity, which asks what the instrument is really measuring. One of the more powerful methods used is factor analysis. It, using varimax rotation, produced six factors with 23 of the 26 items loading successfully at 0.50 and above; these factors accounted for 62% of the variance. Table 2 contains the loadings for the first five factors. Factor 6 contained two items (liberal arts knowledge and nonverbal communication). Although these are unrelated and only one could be considered a behavioral skill or trait, each was retained in the instrument because the literature deems them as being important.

Regarding reliability, Cronbach alphas were computed. The alphas were 0.86, 0.77, 0.71, 0.72, 0.73, and 0.51. Except for the last factor, the alphas do appear to offer sufficiently high reliability.

Care was taken to ensure that pilot participants were drawn from the same population as the subjects for the full study. None of the pilot participants participated in the final study.

Some minor changes in definitions were made as a result of post-pilot study interviews. These simply clarified some definitions, and all participants agreed that those listed should be included in the final version, based on their experience with knowledge acquisition activities and normative statements in academic and practitioner literature.

As a result of discussions with participants from the first two pilots, changes in the scales of importance and proficiency were made. Participants felt that the five and four point scales used in pilot studies did not sufficiently discriminate measures. The seven point Likert-type scale in the third pilot was favorably received by participants; it achieved higher internal consistency and was therefore used in the survey.

## 3.5 Procedures

Once the participants were identified, a questionnaire was mailed to either each participant or a manager responsible for several participants. Participants were provided with a stamped, self-addressed return envelope for the completed questionnaire.

## 4. Results

4.1 Type of business, formal job title, and employment data

The 101 participants identified the primary type of business of their company or organization. These included manufacturing (10), software development (52), government (16), and service (23).

One of the more interesting findings concerned the formal job title of the respondents. Only 29 of the participants gave their formal job title as Knowledge Engineer. This would suggest that organizations wishing to hire KEs would do well to scrutinize applicants who appear qualified but who do not have a current job title of KE.

Two persons indicated that their title was Programmer while two others stated that they were Systems Analysts. Ten persons stated that they were Engineers, while an astoundingly large number, 57, responded with something else. Representative responses to the “Other” category included AI Specialist, Manager, Researcher, and Systems Engineer. One person did not provide a formal job title.

The average length of time the respondents served in their current position was in excess of 30 months, ranging from 1 month to 15 years. In fact, 41% have been in their current position for five years or longer. Many organizations may not be searching for or hiring KEs from the outside. This may also indicate that organizations may be unsure of the criteria to use in searching for and hiring KEs.

## 4.2 Level and type of education

Taylor [15] has suggested that the best KEs should come from diverse backgrounds such as English, Philosophy, and the arts. Sviokla [14] feels that computer scientists, especially those with computer science Ph.D.s, may not always make the best KEs.

From the perspective of level of education, all respondents had completed undergraduate degrees, 19 had completed some graduate work, and 65 possessed one or more graduate degrees, including 19 who possessed a Ph.D. At the undergraduate level, most participants did possess technical degrees. These included Computer Science/Mathematics (33), Electrical Engineering (10), and other engineering disciplines. Many other backgrounds were noted. These included Computer Information Systems and Data Processing, typically provided by business schools, Economics, Education, English, Foreign Languages, History, Philosophy, and Psychology.

The diversity of educational backgrounds was also evident among those with graduate degrees. Computer Science, various engineering degrees, MBAs, Anthropology, Behavioral Studies, Philosophy, and Psychology were the more common.

For all degree levels, Computer Science is the most prevalent. The range of non-technical degrees may suggest support for the literature which advocates that KEs should be broadly educated and well informed persons [13]. On the other hand, persons from diverse fields may be performing these duties because the KE concept is still so new and somewhat less defined.

## 4.3 Training involving interpersonal skills

This study asked respondents to identify the type of training they had received in: interpersonal communication, listening behavior, public speaking, small group communication, oral interpretation, interviewing, nonverbal communication, persuasion, leadership, argumentation, semantics, individual decision making, and group decision making. Respondents were then asked to state whether this was:

(1) Self taught, e.g., by reading, observation

(2) One to two day seminar and/or workshop

(3) One to two weeks, full time, eight hours a day

(4) A few hours each week for one to two months

(5) Quarter or semester coursework from a college or university

They were told not to include any training that was part of a non-related course, such as equating public speaking with a presentation of a report in a college class: the intent was to ensure a strong focus of attention on specific training in a given subject area, not a course or seminar with incidental training.

The data shows that the KEs involved in this study have not participated in any type of extensive formal training or coursework in any of the subject areas. The most common vehicle for instruction was “self taught”, by reading books, observing others, or discussion. The next most popular training vehicle was a one to two day seminar and/or workshop.

4.4 Perceived importance of behavioral traits and other skills

Since the importance ratings of the skills and traits investigated in this study were perceptual in nature, similar to studies by Joshi [6] and Nelson [12], the participants said that they were very experienced with knowledge acquisition and other KE duties. For example, the average time spent by the participants exclusively on knowledge acquisition was nearly 20% of the normal work day, with many exceeding 40%. This would suggest that these people may possess some degree of experience to know what skills/traits are important for effective knowledge acquisition.

The overall mean score for the 26 skills/traits for all respondents was 5.41 with a standard deviation of 0.68; fourteen skills/traits were rated above the mean. Only two skills/traits, liberal arts knowledge and salesmanship, scored below 4. Apparently, the respondents felt that almost all of these skills/traits are at least somewhat important in knowledge acquisition.

A closer examination of the importance rankings revealed that five of the first six highest ranked skills/traits are concerned with essential elements of the knowledge acquisition process: listening, conceptualize, interviewing, probing, and open-minded. These involve communication and problem understanding, as discussed previously. In general, there was greater consensus among the more important rated skills/traits with somewhat more variation among the less important.

It was also surprising to find that KEs feel that domain knowledge is not a very important skill/ trait for effective knowledge acquisition; 27 respondents cited this skill as least important. Researchers state emphatically that domain familiarization is critical for effective knowledge acquisition; in fact, they identify numerous steps, including developing a knowledge dictionary and planning seminars, lectures, or other training opportunities. This issue deserves additional attention.

## 4.6 Relationship between perceived importance and qualification of skills / traits and educational background

We examined the relationships between importance and qualification and type of undergraduate degree, either technical or non-technical. Technical degrees were defined as computer science, all engineering and science, and mathematics.

First, those with technical and non-technical degrees were split evenly in terms of skills/traits rated more important, i.e., there were 13 skills rated more important by both, but in a different order. Those with non-technical degrees rated the skills considered more important overall, listening and interviewing, higher than those with technical degrees. Correspondingly, the majority of skills/traits rated more important by those with technical degrees were those ranked lower in importance. Of the five skills rated most important overall, the mean score for those with non-technical degrees was 5.92 while the score for those with technical degrees was 5.80.

Regarding perceived qualification in the skills/traits, only eight out of the 26 were rated higher in qualification by those with technical degrees. Of those eight, only conceptualize and rational thinker were among those rated most important overall. Finally, the average score for perceived qualification was higher for those with non-technical degrees.

## 5. Recommendations

More than 75% of the KEs possessed technical undergraduate degrees, most in Computer Science. Although these programs serve a very useful purpose in providing the necessary “tool-oriented” classes, they may lack the requisite breadth in people and organizational skills.

In order to meet the needs of organizations that employ KEs, computer science programs might investigate including elective courses that are interpersonal in nature, e.g., from the Departments of Communication or Psychology.

Organizations that develop ES, either for resale or in-house use, should strive to improve the education and training of KEs. As with many professions, academia is unable to provide specific training, especially that which is unique to each organization. Accordingly, organizations should investigate formal, on-going, in-house programs, special one to two week seminars and workshops provided by in-house trainers, etc.

## 6. Conclusions

Although the KE may be a new job category, the formal title of KE is not common in industry. There is little evidence to suggest movement by KEs from one company to another, as over 40% of the participants have been with their present organization for five or more years. Organizations may be creating KE positions as necessary from the ranks of their own employees.

As would be expected, KEs are highly educated with many possessing advanced degrees in diverse fields of study. Although computer science is the most popular undergraduate and masters degree, this is not the only educational background that organizations should pursue when looking for KEs.

The extent of formal training in many behavioral and interpersonal skills was found to be minimal. Many participants had not received any training in some of the subject areas examined, and those indicating some type of familiarity achieved it on their own. This clearly contradicts the academic and practitioner literature that advocates knowledge and proficiency in these skills. There is apparently little effort by organizations to provide internal education in these areas. As ES development continues to expand, organizations must not make the same mistakes as were made with traditional systems development efforts: failure to recognize the importance of communication and interpersonal skills.

Notwithstanding the lack of formal training in these types of skills/traits, KEs do recognize their importance for effective knowledge acquisition. However, KEs generally feel less proficient in these skills/traits. There would therefore appear to be a correlation between the degree of qualification and extent of formal training in them, especially that type of training which would be provided by a KE's organization. Additional research might also investigate whether KEs who have greater “on the job” experience outperform those less experienced, irrespective of any formal skills training.

Organizations should not consider technical educational backgrounds, especially computer science, as the premier requirement for KEs. What is more important is to identify those individuals who possess a broad range of technical and non-technical skills/traits, including the ability to conceptualize, interview, listen properly, and probe effectively. Additionally, since the non-technical skills/traits rated most important are not unlike those possessed by traditional systems analysts, might not the best KEs come from the ranks of experienced systems analysts who have had some ES technical training? Further research should examine this issue.
