# An investigation of the relative presentation efficiency of computer-displayed graphs

E. Vance Wilson \*

University of Colorado at Boulder, Boulder, CO, USA

Theophilus B.A. Addo

San Diego State University, San Diego, CA, USA

Guides in the selection and use of graphs draw heavily on opinion and anecdotal information. Presentation research has provided little assistance to users of modern computer graphing software, due to the ambiguous and contradictory findings that characterize this literature. Furthermore, the majority of the presentation studies that address differences between basic graph types were conducted prior to widespread use of computers, and are therefore of questionable value in predicting efficiency of computer-displayed graphs. This paper presents the findings of an experimental investigation that ranked the efficiency of five computer-displayed graphs across a range of question types and question complexity levels. The study concludes that, in several key areas, the advice from guides for use of graphs do not match the observed efficiency rankings of the graphs that were investigated. Based on these findings, guidelines are proposed for selection and use of graphs for computer-display.

Keywords: Management information systems; Decision support systems; Computer graphics; Graphical presentations; Graphs; Tables; Human information processing

## Introduction

During the past decade, business use of graphs for presentation of numeric data has increased dramatically, driven by the ready availability of desktop computers and graph-generating spreadsheets, accounting packages, and decision support systems. We question the guidance that is available to the “new generation” of graph users. Do existing guides to selection and use of graphs promote presentations that are accurate and fast to use, especially via the modern medium of high-resolution color computer displays? Or do they simply repeat unproven assumptions of the past?

These are not trivial questions. Walter Eells [13] stated that guidelines found in the graphing literature of the 1920s (e.g., [22]) “seem to be purely matters of opinion – none show any evidence of an experimental basis of fact.” Over sixty years later, guides to selection and use of graphs are still based on intuition, opinion, and anecdotal information [27].

Unfortunately, presentation research has not provided much alternative guidance. As DeSanctis observed, “It is apparent that, despite the presence of a large collection of studies that deal with graphics and other visual displays, we really

3. Researchers often failed to integrate findings from previous studies, especially studies from outside their own disciplines, into their research designs.

Our primary purpose in this study is to develop guidelines for selection and use of computer-displayed graphs through theory-based experimentation. We anticipate that this action will help business practitioners, who will benefit from the reduced uncertainty and higher efficiency provided by empirically supported guidelines. Researchers, in addition, will benefit from using the comprehensive framework in support of future graph comparison research.

## Review of the literature

Presentation research comparing graphs falls into two distinct areas. In the first, performance is compared among a comprehensive range of graphs across a range of tasks. In the second area, performance is compared between two classes of graphs: component graphs and whole graphs. Component graphs are those in which the component parts indicate numeric values against a constant scale and are not fundamentally constrained in overall size, e.g., vertical bar graphs, horizontal bar graphs, and line graphs (see Figure 1). Whole graphs are those in which the overall graph is a fixed size and its component parts indicate proportions of the whole, e.g., pie graphs and divided bar graphs.

## Comprehensive graph research

In 1927, Washburne [40] conducted a comprehensive investigation of a range of presentation variables, including bar graphs, line graphs, tables, and pictographs. These were evaluated across a range of distinct tasks in which subjects answered questions requiring specific amount answers, static comparisons, and dynamic comparisons. From this, Washburne developed the following prescriptions: (1) bar graphs are best for complex static comparisons, (2) pictographs are best for very simple static comparisons, (3) line graphs are best for dynamic comparisons, and (4) tables are best for finding specific amounts.

His choice of tasks anticipated current theory in representation/task dependency, and his study is still widely cited. However, Washburne's work was sufficiently comprehensive to discourage replication. Succeeding studies, such as those by Carter [5], Vernon [36], Schutz [29], and Feliciano [14], focused on comparing graphs to tables, and used experimental designs that had substantially fewer graph variables and/or task variables than were used in the Washburne study. To date, no investigation similarly comprehensive has been published. Extrapolation from this study to modern computer graph use is questionable, however, due both to Washburne's reliance on school children as subjects and to the differences in context that exist between the 1927 public school environment and modern business computing environments.

## Research comparing whole graphs to component graphs

The value of whole graphs has long been disputed by authors of graphing guides. Karsten wrote that the divided bar graph should only be used to compare its parts to “100% of itself.” In more recent times Edward Tufte stated, “The only worse design than a pie chart is several of them... Given their low data-density and failure to order numbers along a visual dimension, pie charts should never be used" [35].

Results from research have been less consistent in faulting whole graphs. A 1926 study by Eells reported that subjects performed with similar accuracy in answering questions from pie graphs as from bar graphs. Continuing to the present time, findings have been split between studies that found pie graphs to be inferior [7,9,39] and those that found them to be equivalent or superior to bar graphs [8,32].

## Need for research

In both research areas, the bulk of the literature is substantially dated. In addition, the research that provides comprehensive comparisons of graphs has no reported replication, and the research that compares whole graphs to component graphs lacks resolution. Existing research gives little direction for general selection and use of graphs, and it specifically fails to guide the proliferating users of graphing computer software.

## Theoretical background

Two recent lines of theory and research have shown promise in explaining aspects of presentation efficiency. These comprise studies of dependency between representation and task-culminating in the cognitive fit paradigm – and studies in task complexity.

## The cognitive fit paradigm

Current thought in presentation research holds that the efficiency of a given form of information representation depends on the task that is to be accomplished $[3,19,20,34]$ . The cognitive fit paradigm adds to this concept of task dependency by predicting the circumstances under which different representations of information will outperform one another. Where symbolic (number answer) tasks are matched with tabular representations, or spatial (comparison answer) tasks are matched with graphical representations, the paradigm predicts increased efficiency of task performance, resulting from cognitive fit. Where the representations and tasks are mismatched, the paradigm states that cognitive fit does not exist, and predicts task performance to be less efficient.

Empirical testing of symbolic and spatial task types has been applied only to comparisons of tables vs. graphs [6], however, the cognitive fit paradigm defines an important distinction between task types which can be adopted in the design of research for comparing graphs. In addition, the spatial task type can be expanded into part-to-part and part-to-whole comparisons, allowing investigations of efficiency to be undertaken within these two distinct frames of comparison.

## Task complexity

Task complexity refers to “the behavioral responses a person should emit in order to achieve some specified level of performance” in completing a task $[41]$ . Task complexity has been reported to be an influential variable to presentation efficiency $[10,42]$ . In research comparing tables vs. graphs, high task complexity level has been linked to superior performance of the graphical presentation forms $[30]$ , however, studies employing this variable in the comparison of different graph types have not been reported.

## Research propositions

The research purpose is translated here into two specific propositions to be tested:

Proposition 1. Different graph types will exhibit significant deviations from one another in efficiency ranking when tested across a range of tasks.

Numerous researchers studying graphical presentations have elected to use a single graph type as a generic representative of all graph types (e.g., see [15,43]). Guides to the use of graphs recommend certain graph types, such as bar graphs and line graphs, interchangeably for broad applications such as time-series analysis. Beginning with Washburne, however, researchers have noted that variations in performance exist between different graph types under different conditions. This suggests that certain graph types may be especially well or poorly suited for specific task applications. The research provides little guidance for hypothesis development concerning the performance of the various graph-task combinations, so Proposition 1 is necessarily exploratory. It investigates the efficiency of five computer-displayed graphs across six question type and complexity treatments.

Proposition 2. Whole graph types will exhibit efficiency levels that are comparable to those of component graph types across all spatial tasks.

This seeks to determine whether the bias against whole graphs which has recurred in graphing guides (e.g., [28]) is justified. This extends the work of Eells and of Spence and Lewandowsky, who reported that whole graphs provide similar performance to component graphs when used in combination with certain spatial tasks. In this study the efficiency of whole graphs – represented by pie and divided bar graphs – is compared to that of component graphs – represented by vertical bar, horizontal bar, and line graphs. This comparison is conducted across four spatial questions, comprising part-to-part and part-to-whole frames of comparison at two levels of question complexity.

## Methodology

A laboratory experiment was used for the investigation of relative efficiency of the graphs. The experimental design employed was a $5 \times 3 \times 2$ factorial design with repeated measures for control of between-subject variations, which can otherwise confound the results [23,26]. Each experimental treatment comprised one of five graph types and one of three question types at one of two complexity levels. The dependent variable was efficiency, a joint measure of performance accuracy and speed.

Experimental rigor was enhanced by random assignment of subjects to treatment sets. Conditioning effects were eliminated by systematic cycling of treatment sets and randomized treatment administration order. Experimenter reactivity and related threats to external validity were minimized through the use of self-administering test software.

## Research variables

## Graph type

The five graphs selected for the present study are a representative sample of those found in spreadsheets such as Microsoft Excel and Lotus 1-2-3. The selected graphs are vertical bar, horizontal bar, line, pie, and divided bar graphs. For this study, the default image for each graph from the Excel spreadsheet application was used.

## Question type

There are three question types derived from the cognitive fit paradigm (see Table 1): (1) symbolic questions, (2) spatial questions with part-to-part comparisons, and (3) spatial questions with part-to-whole comparisons. The spatial dimension was subdivided into two types of spatial questions to accommodate the whole graphs vs. component graphs investigation of Proposition 2.

## Efficiency

As recommended by previous researchers [18], joint measurement of accuracy and speed of information acquisition was used as the dependent variable in this study, defined herein as efficiency. The employed measurement technique was based on a theoretical definition of graphical efficiency by Jacques Bertin, who writes, “If, in order to obtain a correct and complete answer to a given question, all other things being equal, one construction requires a shorter observation time than another construction, we can say that it is more efficient for this question” [4].

In our study, accuracy was recorded as dichotomous data and speed as ratio level data. For each experimental treatment the five graphs were ranked primarily on accuracy and secondarily on speed, creating rankings from 1 to 5 which were used for ensuing data analysis. For this reason, the term efficiency, rather than accuracy or speed, is used to report and discuss all findings.

## Controlled variables

Other variables that have been reported to affect presentation efficiency include information complexity $[21,24,25]$ and color $[16]$ . In our study, information complexity was controlled at a low level, using data points in a single time-series of twelve periods for each graph. The points were implemented with randomly generated numeric values in the range of 1 to 5000. Color was controlled in this experiment to represent current business practices.

## Subjects

The experiment was administered to 36 volunteer subjects from a population of senior and graduate business students attending San Diego State University. The mean employment experience for these subjects was over eight years, and mean managerial experience was over three years.

## Test hardware and software

The experiment was administered using a 13 inch color monitor. The test software application was developed to be generic in appearance and self-contained. It introduced and explained the experiment, provided sample experimental treatments for practice purposes, administered the actual experiment, and collected speed and accuracy data. Prior to actual administration, the application was pilot tested for robustness and clarity.

## Experimental procedure

Each subject was welcomed by the researcher and seated at the test computer. The researcher obtained the subject's written consent to the experiment and then left the room - during the experiment, the subject was alone. Subjects progressed sequentially through the software application by entering data as requested, and by pressing the return key to move to the next screen (see sample test screen in Figure 2). Instructions emphasized that the subject's goal was to "answer each experimental question as accurately and quickly as possible." No researcher intervention was required during the course of the experiment, and post-test subject comments indicated that the subjects were comfortable with the level of instructions and training administered by the software application.

## Data analysis

The efficiency data generated from the experiment is of ordinal level. Therefore, distributionfree statistics were chosen for data analysis. An initial two-way analysis of variance was performed across the five graph types for each of the six experimental treatments, using the Friedman test [31,33]. Paired tests between the highest ranking component and whole graphs were conducted using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-ranks test.

## Results

Proposition 1. Different graph types will exhibit significant deviations from one another in efficiency ranking when tested across a range of tasks.

The results indicate that most graph types vary substantially in efficiency ranking when measured across a range of question type and question complexity levels, providing support for Proposition 1 (see Table 2). However, some graph types show overall consistency in ranking. Specifically, the following results are noted:

1. The vertical and horizontal bar graphs exhibit intermediate to top efficiency rankings, regardless of question type or complexity level.

2. Pie graphs and divided bar graphs are the least efficient for symbolic questions, regardless of complexity level.

3. Pie graphs and divided bar graphs have unique performance characteristics, as indicated by the wide deviation in efficiency rankings between these two graphs on the spatial part-to-part questions.

4. Despite their widespread use in business graphing applications and their popularity in graphics experiments, line graphs were found to be erratic performers, with intermediate to low efficiency rankings on spatial questions.

Proposition 2. Whole graph types will exhibit efficiency levels that are comparable to those of component graph types across all spatial tasks.

Efficiency of the best whole graph was found to be similar to the efficiency of the best component graph for all spatial questions at low and high complexity levels (see Table 3). The findings support Proposition 2.

## Discussion and implications

Several aspects of the reported findings have value in guiding users of graph-generating software and future presentation researchers. These can be incorporated into future guidelines for selection and use of graphs in general, and especially for computer display.

Particularly troublesome are the findings concerning line graphs, which are frequently recommended for general purpose use. Results from our study suggest that line graphs are often a poor choice for spatial tasks. This contradicts the general practice of using line graphs for spatially oriented time-series analysis. As time-series analysis is the most common category of business graphs, these findings carry special weight for their users. Caution is recommended in use of line graphs.

The poor showing of line graphs carries additional emphasis for researchers. Specifically, it advises against the commonly reported practice of using a single graph in an experiment and then generalizing the results to all graph types.

The class of whole graphs was vindicated by our study. Although these graph types do not carry sufficient numeric information to perform well in conjunction with symbolic tasks, the best of them proved to be as efficient as the best alternative graph in all tested spatial tasks. This finding supports earlier studies and refutes practitioner guides. However, a note of caution concerning whole graphs is suggested by the finding that the pie and divided bar graph types do not exhibit similar efficiency for all tasks. Divided bar graphs are significantly better choices for low complexity part-to-part spatial tasks, for example, than are pic graphs (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon). In general, however, these findings suggest that whole graphs deserve unbiased consideration for computer display of numeric data.

If universally useful graphs can be said to exist, this study indicates that the bar graphs hold that distinction. Moreover, the findings show that there is little to choose between vertical and horizontal bar graphs, as the two types exhibit nearly identical efficiency rankings across all tasks. The vertical and horizontal bar graphs are therefore recommended for general use, especially in those instances where the task is likely to be symbolic in nature, where the nature of the task varies, or where the task is indeterminate.

## Conclusions

Presentation is an essential component of all information systems and, as an increasingly common presentation form, computer-displayed graphs must be considered an important area of study within the Information Systems field. Many business computer users now have the ability to produce and view graphs on their computer systems, but they have little empirically derived guidance for doing so.

Our study provides users with several useful guidelines. Vertical and horizontal bar graphs perform similarly well for a wide range of tasks. Line graphs, on the other hand, should be used with caution. Despite the wide use and acceptance of this graph type, its efficiency is erratic and often low when viewed on a computer monitor. Finally, users can feel that they are not necessarily giving up efficiency when they choose pie graphs and divided bar graphs to aid in accomplishing spatial tasks.

Designers should note the following:

1. Graph types are not interchangeable. It is evident from the study that efficiency can be significantly affected by choice of graph.

2. Tasks are not interchangeable. Efficiency can be affected by choice of task.

3. Graphs are not the exclusive province of graphic designers. It is a reasonable assumption that far more graphs are currently being generated by computer-using business practitioners than by graphic designers. As overall business efficiency is thereby affected, the question of graph efficiency should be considered a valid and important Information Systems concern.

4. Presentation research on graphs has not been concluded. Although comprehensive work by Washburne has served well as a foundation for investigating different presentation formats, this and other studies have limited generalizability to business use of computer-displayed graphs.

5. Presentation research on graphs is not inherently inconclusive. Despite the contradictory studies, research that is well-designed and grounded in theory can avoid problems of the past and produce meaningful contributions.

## Limitations of the study

Three limitations in the study should not be overlooked. The first concerns information complexity, which was controlled at a low level in this study. This design was considered appropriate in meeting the objectives of the research. However, other studies have reported that information complexity can influence presentation efficiency. Caution should therefore be exercised in extrapolating the results to graphs incorporating high levels of information complexity.

The second limitation recognizes that alternative forms of presentation were not studied here. As reported by Washburne, Vernon, and Feliciano, in some circumstances such presentation forms as pictographs, tables, text, and alternative graph types may prove to be more efficient than any of the graphs in our study. However, the graph types we used are representative of the typical choices available to users of graph-generating computer software. The primary purpose of the study was to address and advise this real world choice process.

The third limitation concerns the research sample. While this subject group had substantial mean length of work and management experience, it is not clear how well their performance is generalizable to that of business managers and other real-world graph users.
