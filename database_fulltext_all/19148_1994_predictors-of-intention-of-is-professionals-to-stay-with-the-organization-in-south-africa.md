# Predictors of intention of IS professionals to stay with the organization in South Africa

Magid Igbaria $^{a,1}$ , Guy Meredith $^{b}$ and Derek C. Smith $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, 32nd and Chestnut Streets, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

$^{b}$ Applied Systems Engineering, 15 Shirley Road, Claremont 7700, South Africa

$^{c}$ Department of Accounting, University of Cape Town, Private Bag, Rondebosch 7700, South Africa

The study examines the determinants of intention to stay with their organization among 112 IS employees in South Africa. Results show that job satisfaction is the most important factor. Organizational commitment also played a very important role in affecting a person's decision. Indeed, the impact of job characteristics operates through these two variables. Results show that both task-based and organizational-based activities affect commitment and satisfaction. Implications for management are identified.

Keywords: Intention to stay; Satisfaction; Commitment; Job characteristics; Promotion; Challenge; International; South Africa

Further, IS employees have historically displayed a high rate of turnover that reached nearly 20% in 1987 (Connolly, 1988), and is about double the average for business managers and professionals (Lucas, 1989). Such turnover is costly, not only in terms of replacing staff and the high cost of training new employees (Pierce, 1991), but also in terms of systems development productivity (Butler Cox, 1991).

The high IS staff turnover is equally prevalent in the South African IS industry. White (1989) reports that the average IS staff turnover for 1988 was 26% as opposed to 15% in 1987. Furthermore, he believes the skills shortage to be worse in this country than experienced elsewhere, as there is a weak foundation of skills due to the historical deficiencies in education experienced by much of the work force. Smith et al (1991), in a South African study of system analysts, estimate turnover to be between 20% and 30%, undersupply to be greater than 20% and 15% to be below-average performers. Hamlyn (1992) estimates the current turnover to be approximately 20%, and suggests that this poses a serious problem for South African companies as only those employees who remain with a company for at least three to four years provide a company with any return on investment. Couger and Smith (1992), in a major study of motivation norms of IS staff in South Africa, reported that 74% of a sample of 2700 IS professionals did not have a bachelor's degree. Therefore, the need to retain skilled IS staff in South Africa is of perhaps even greater importance than elsewhere.

The increased importance of information to society and the rapid changes being made in technology imply that the skills shortage is unlikely to improve. Indeed, an acute shortage of IS talent is forecasted for the year 2000 (Niederman et al, 1991), making the ability to attract and retain skilled IS professionals even more important over the next decade. The shortage will be exacerbated by the sizable labor shortages expected in the 1990s due to diminished birthrates (Naisbitt, 1985). It is becoming increasingly important, therefore, for IS and general management to understand the reasons behind the turnover. To the extent that specific aspects of jobs held by these IS employees contribute to high levels of turnover, and are within the control of the IS managers, retention levels could be increased through appropriate actions by IS management designed to minimize these problems.

The study examines the factors affecting IS employees' decision to stay with the organization. Specifically, four related research questions are addressed: (1) What is the impact of job characteristics and salary on the IS employees' decision to stay with the organization?; (2) What is the impact of satisfaction and commitment on IS employees' intentions to stay with the organization?; (3) Do satisfaction and commitment mediate the effects of job characteristics and salary on the intentions to stay?; and (4) Does organizational commitment mediate the effect of satisfaction on the intentions to stay?

Current turnover models and related empirical research provide strong support for Fishbein and Ajzen's (1975) proposition that behavioral intentions constitute the most immediate determinant of actual behavior, in this case turnover (Bluedorn, 1982; Cotton and Tuttle, 1986; Hom, Katerberg and Hulin, 1979; Michaels and Spector, 1982;

## The conceptual framework

Figure 1 presents the model of turnover intentions examined in this study. The model includes six sets of variables: (1) job characteristics; (2) salary; (3) satisfaction (both job and career satisfaction); (4) organizational commitment; and (5) intention to stay. It predicts direct effects of salary and job characteristics on satisfaction, organizational commitment and intention to stay. The model also predicts direct effects of satisfaction on organizational commitment and intention to stay, and a direct effect of organizational commitment on intention to stay. It is important to note that in addition to these direct effects, the model also postulates indirect effects of salary and job characteristics through satisfaction, and organizational commitment. The model further postulates that satisfaction has indirect effects on intention to stay through organizational commitment. Since such effects are composites of direct effects, only direct effects are presented as hypotheses. The rationale for each is presented below.

## Job characteristics

Job characteristics have been found to be potential determinants of turnover among IS personnel (Couger, 1988; 1990; Couger and Zawacki, 1980; Garden, 1989; Goldstein and Rockart 1984). Job characteristics have been found to influence intention to stay through their relationships with job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Furthermore, the IS literature has focused extensively on the importance of a job which permits IS people to work on challenging and interesting tasks. It was suggested that if the organization provides technical professionals the opportunity to engage in challenging and exciting jobs, they will be more involved and satisfied in their organizations, more committed to their organizations, and finally, less likely to leave. The job design literature suggests that motivators (e.g., job challenge, autonomy, responsibility, and achievement) lead to satisfaction and commitment and eventually reduce an employee's intention to leave (Hackman and Oldham, 1980). Therefore, Hypothesis 1 predicts that task characteristics are positively related to satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to stay.

## Salary

Salary has been found to be positively related to satisfaction and organizational commitment (Brooke, et al., 1988; Liou, et al., 1990; Mottaz, 1988), and negatively related to turnover (Compton, 1987; Mobley, et al., 1979). Therefore, Hypothesis 2 predicts that salary has both direct and indirect effects on intention to stay through satisfaction and organizational commitment. It is expected that salary is positively related.

## Satisfaction and commitment

Job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment reflecting a positive evaluation of the job and/or of the employing organization are assumed to influence individuals' intention to stay. Satisfaction represents an affective response to specific aspects of the job or career and denotes the pleasurable or positive emotional state resulting from an appraisal of one's job or career (Locke, 1976; Porter, Steers, Williams and Hazer, 1986), whereas organizational commitment is an affective response to the whole organization and the degree of attachment or loyalty employees feel toward the organization.

The findings provide evidence that satisfaction has a direct effect on intention to stay as well as an indirect effect through organizational commitment. It is reasonable to expect that high levels of satisfaction would enhance organizational commitment since employees who are satisfied with their jobs and careers should perceive greater benefits in retaining membership in their organizations than employees whose jobs and careers have been less gratifying. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 predicts that job satisfaction and career satisfaction are positively related to organizational commitment and intention to stay.

It is reasonable to believe that job satisfaction should also promote feelings of career satisfaction. Since a career can be viewed as a series of work-related experiences over time (Greenhaus and Callanan, 1994), feelings about one's career should be determined, in part, by one's attitudes toward current job experience (Romzek, 1989). Therefore, Hypothesis 4 predicts that job satisfaction has a direct effect on career satisfaction.

A number of empirical studies confirm the important role of organizational commitment in influencing turnover intentions (Baroudi, 1985; Bartol, 1983; Gupta, Guimaraes and Raghunathan, 1992; Steers, 1977). IS employees who are highly committed to their organization are more likely to stay than employees who are not. It has also been reported that organizational commitment is more strongly related to turnover intentions than job satisfaction (Peters, et al., 1981; Shore and Martin, 1989). Therefore, Hypothesis 5 predicts that organizational commitment is positively related to intention to stay.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

A representative sample from the Computer Society of South Africa (CSSA) was selected. As this study intended to measure the determinants of intention to stay of individuals who have made a firm decision to follow a career in IS, the CSSA was considered an appropriate body for sampling. The CSSA is a body whose members are actively employed in, or associated with, the IS field. Strict rules govern full membership, and these stipulate that an applicant should have a computer related qualification (ranging from a certificate in computing to a Master's Degree) together with a minimum number of years experience (from

3 to 8, depending on qualification). Individuals may apply for Associate Membership provided they are employed, or studying on a full time basis, in the computer field.

The sample was limited, furthermore, to members between the ages of 30 and 44 inclusive. Firstly, a career is developed through one's interaction with the work environment, and may take ten years or more to manifest itself clearly (Schein, 1985). It is probable, therefore, that individuals under the age of 30 are in an exploratory stage of career development, while those over 30 have developed a clear and stable career orientation. Secondly, limiting the sample to those below the age of 45 was expected to reduce the likelihood of extraneous variance caused by the “midcareer crisis” (Schein, 1987; Burke, 1989; Stout et al, 1988), which is a period during which individuals undergo self-confrontation and can be expected to reassess their attainments, goals, values, initial career choice and future.

Finally, the choice of sample is supported by demographic trends taking place in the labor force. Naisbitt (1985) reports that the number of entry-level workers (ages 18–24) has dropped substantially in the 1980s, with the figure expected to drop even further in the 1990s. As a result, the average worker of the 1990s will be between 35 and 44 years old. As this study sought to examine the average IS professional, the age limit imposed seemed appropriate. Indeed, this decision is borne out by the membership figures of the CSSA, which suggest that the majority (54%) are in the 30–44 age group.

Sampling and mailing procedure and respondent characteristics

In similar research, conducted in the USA, Igbaria, et al. (1991) had a response rate of 20.3%, while Kaplan's (1990) was 44%. Based on these figures, a response rate of 33% was assumed, which implied that the sample size chosen had to be three times greater than that dictated by statistical considerations. Thus, 300 members of the Western Cape Chapter of the CSSA, between the ages 30 to 44, were extracted from the full membership list.

## Measures

Salary consisted of six tiers from (1) below R40,000 to (6) R120,000 or above.

## Perceived job characteristics

Thirteen items were used to assess the extent to which employees' current job provided them with various opportunities and rewards. The response options were anchored on a five-point scale ranging from (1) not at all to (5) to a great extent. Igbaria, et al. (1991) identified two factors; one which represented task-based rewards and the other organizational-based rewards. The task-based rewards, included pursue your ideas, build a professional reputation, work with competent colleagues, work on technically challenging tasks, work on professionally important projects, have freedom to be creative and original, and be highly respected by your peers. Responses to the seven items were averaged to create a scale tapping the task-based component of the job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was.87.

Factor 2, labeled organization-based rewards, included work on organizationally important projects, work on projects leading to advancement, be highly respected by top management, receive substantial annual salary increases, have a great deal of power and influence on the job, and receive a promotion within the next year or two. Responses to the six items were averaged to create a scale tapping the organizational component of the job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was.83.

## Career satisfaction

This was measured by a six-item scale adapted from prior research (Greenhaus, et al. 1990), with appropriate changes to make the items more relevant to the present study. Individuals were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with each statement on a five point-scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. Sample items include: “I am satisfied with the success I have achieved in my career,” and “I am satisfied with the progress I have made toward achieving my overall career goals”. This measure also included satisfaction with the rate of promotion, the pay level, and with the status that had been achieved during their careers. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution with an eigenvalue 4.09 which accounted for 68.2 percent of the explained variance. Responses to the six items were averaged to create a career satisfaction score (alpha = 0.91).

## Job satisfaction

This was operationalized by a three-item scale developed by Hackman and Oldham (1975) reflecting overall satisfaction with the job. Each item required the respondents to indicate their agreement or disagreement on a five-point scale from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution with an eigenvalue of 2.05 which accounted for 68.2% of the explained variance. Responses to the three items were averaged to produce a total job satisfaction score (alpha = 0.76). A factor analysis of the six-item scale of career satisfaction and the three-item scale of job satisfaction was used to examine the existence of the two measures previously specified. In this case, factor analysis was used to confirm rather than to discover factors (Nunnally, 1978). The analysis produced two factors with eigenvalues $\geqslant$ 1.0 that accounted for 71.1 percent of the total variance. The six items that loaded highly on the first factor are identical to the items measuring career satisfaction. The remaining three items that loaded highly on the second factor are identical to the three items measuring job satisfaction.

## Organizational commitment

This variable – defined as the identification with a particular organization and the desire to maintain membership in the organization – was measured by an abbreviated version of the Organizational Commitment Questionnaire (OCQ) developed by Porter, Crampon, and Smith (1976). The nine items used to construct the scale tap two of the three dimensions of commitment included in the longer version of the OCQ: (1) a strong belief in and acceptance of the organization's goals and values; and (2) a willingness to exert considerable effort on behalf of the organization. In order to avoid concept redundancy (Morrow, 1983), the six items reflecting a strong desire to maintain membership in the organization were excluded from the measure of commitment because they overlapped with the measure of turnover intentions. Thus the shorter version of the scale used in this study represents a more “pure” measure of the affective dimensions of commitment to the employing organization. Sample items are “I really care about the fate of my organization,” and “I feel very little loyalty to my organization.” Since the reliability and validity of the nine-item version have been found to be acceptable, the shorter version of OCQ was used (Price and Mueller, 1981). The responses to the nine items were coded such that high scores reflected greater commitment to the organization. They were averaged to obtain an overall index of organizational commitment. The 9-item scale had an internal consistency reliability of .86.

## Intention to stay

This was assessed by a single item used previously by us: it asked individuals to indicate how long they intended to continue working in their current organization. The response options were anchored on a time-linked 5-point scale ranging from (1) “one year or less” to (5) “eleven years or more, or until retirement.” The item was recoded so that a response of “one year or less” would indicate the weakest intention to stay. Kraut (1975), Parasuraman (1982), and others have attested to the efficacy of a single-item measure of turnover intention.

## Data analysis

The technique of path analysis using least squares multiple regression was used to determine whether the observed pattern of relationships among the variables was consistent with the causal model. The choice of path analysis over structural equation modeling techniques such as LISREL is based on the small sample size and the well established measures for all the variables (for more details, see Fornell (1982)). However, it was necessary to first take several steps to check for possible violations of the assumptions underlying the use of path analysis (Billings and Wroten, 1978; Heise, 1969). An examination of the alpha coefficients indicated satisfactory levels of internal consistency reliability among all the multi-item scales (alpha coefficients ranged from 0.71 to 0.90).

## Results

Table 2 presents the means, standard deviations, and intercorrelations among the variables examined in this study. ## Discussion and implications

The results of this study indicate that the intention to stay with the organization among is employees in South Africa is a product of linkages among job characteristics (task-based and organizational-based activities), salary, satisfaction (career and job), and organizational commitment. These findings highlight the importance of organizational commitment and job satisfaction as the most immediate predictors of intention to stay. They underscore the key mediating role of organizational commitment and job satisfaction in the intention to stay model. Indeed, the impact of job characteristics operates through these two variables.

Therefore, IS management must monitor the level of IS employee attitudes toward their job and organization on an ongoing basis and attempt to understand the factors that contribute to the development of positive work attitudes. Managers should try to ensure IS employees' satisfaction with their job. This study shows that while salary and organizational-based activities impact career satisfaction of IS employees, task-based activities had a strong impact on job satisfaction. This suggests IS management should develop creative ways to increase the internal mobility opportunities of IS employees. Organizations could develop a multi-career path system for IS employees, where a technical path can be followed that parallels a managerial path in rank and salary. Individuals who wish to transfer to non-IS function should also be evaluated and these individuals should be able to advance within the non-IS business functions. A multi-career path approach can provide important incentives and career advancement opportunities to technical and non-technical employees that might encourage them to stay with their organizations. Organizations should also provide IS employees, who wish to be managers, with career planning opportunities and training and development experiences so they can make an effective transition to management. These organizational-based activities are critical to increase the career satisfaction level of IS employees.

Furthermore, organizations should also focus on the task-based activities. The results of this study support the notion that if jobs provide autonomy and challenge and other task-related rewards and activities, IS employees will be more satisfied with their job, more committed to their organization, and eventually more likely to stay with the organization. This suggests that IS managers should also focus attention on three factors. First, they must provide sufficient challenge and autonomy on the job. Second, they should try to assign interesting tasks, make sure that IS employees work with competent colleagues and work on professionally important projects. Third, they must, whenever possible, provide jobs for IS employees which permit freedom and autonomy to be creative and pursue their own ideas. If the organization provides all these features which are found to be important to IS employees, they will create an environment which can be expected to increase satisfaction and commitment and eventually increase the likelihood of staying with the organization.

Inconsistent with our expectation, salary had no effects on commitment and intention to stay. This may suggest that individuals may be concerned about intangible rewards and promotion than salary.

Furthermore, note that job characteristics had mainly indirect effects on intention to stay through satisfaction and commitment. This emphasizes the importance of job satisfaction in determining organizational commitment and consequently the decision to stay with the organization. The results show that the most immediate determinants of intention to stay are job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Job satisfaction and commitment are the most substantial and the most direct influences on the intention to stay of IS employees. Indeed, the impact of other variables on intention to stay operates through job satisfaction and commitment. It is suggested that employers of IS personnel must monitor the level of employee attitudes on an ongoing basis and attempt to understand the factors that contribute to the development of positive work attitudes, mainly job satisfaction and commitment.

Overall, the results of this study are meaningful and interesting. However, it would be prudent to cross-validate the findings in other countries. Although the respondents in this study were employed in many different organizations and functional areas within IS, they were all members of a single professional association, and they are likely to be more highly educated and more technically oriented than the general population of IS employees (CSSA, 1990). In this sample, 64% of respondents held degrees compared to the larger sample (measuring motivational norms) that had only 26% (Couger and Smith, 1992). However, our study limited the age of its sample.

Organizational interventions should also be introduced to determine whether actual changes in job duties and characteristics have an impact on the IS employees' decision whether to stay with the organization. For example, the results of this study would suggest that employees whose jobs are redesigned to provide more autonomy, challenge and freedom should display higher intentions to stay with the organization. Indeed, the ultimate practical test of any model is its ability to produce desired changes in an applied setting.
