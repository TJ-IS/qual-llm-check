# Interorganizational information systems: New approaches to global economic development

Nanette S. Levinson

School of International Service-8071, The American University, 4400 Massachusetts Ave., N.W., Washington, DC 20016, USA

The emergence of cross-national interorganizational configurations (IOCs) has important implications for information resource structuring, planning, and strategy. Findings reported in this paper stem from a review of the literature, a database of cross-national interorganizational configurations formed during the last two years, and a five-year study of information systems resource management in primarily U.S.-based major organizations. This study involves eight subsets of approximately twelve to fifteen organizations examined once each during this period. Within each organization, a field researcher conducted open-ended, semi-structured interviews with the top information systems manager. Findings indicate the need for a paradigm shift to incorporate the IOC as a level of analysis to match the emerging new world economic order and the needs for cross-national information resources structures and strategies.

Keywords: Strategic information systems; Strategic alliances; Strategic planning; Organizational learning; Information resource management; Organization structure; Global systems; Economic development; Interorganizational planning.

## Introduction

During the last decade, the number of strategic alliances within the U.S., as well as those involving organizations from different countries, has increased dramatically $[20]$ . New information and telecommunication technologies have made these increases possible. Cross-national configurations are emerging as structures of note in the new global economy $[12,13,27]$ . While much has been written in an anecdotal manner about the emergence of such configurations and concomitant technologies, there has been relatively little analysis of either interorganizational configurations (IOCs) that cross national boundaries as significant forms of the middle 90s or the implications of these forms for information resource management.

## Brief overview of literature

There is a growing literature that studies formal interorganizational arrangements. (See, for example, [28]; [7]; [15]; [10]; [26]). This work focuses primarily on the structure and initiation of formal arrangements involving more than one organization. It also examines the flow of resources (including information) that cross organizational boundaries. Rarely does this work deal with international interorganizational arrangements.

A second body of literature is that focusing on strategic alliances; this is the literature that is primarily descriptive and sometimes anecdotal $[2,25,8,16,17,19]$ . The third body of literature is the information resources management work that looks primarily at IRM in the context of an inter-organizational perspective [24,29,30].

Taking these three bodies of literature together paves the way for a systems-based examination of today's cross-national IOCs and for crafting recommendations on information resources structure, planning, and strategy for such configurations. There is a need now for new work that ties together the theory bases of these three bodies of literature and forges research questions that match both the paradigm shift described here and the challenges to information systems structure, planning, and strategy for the future of global economic development.

## Significance

A data-base of cross-national IOCs compiled during the last two years indicates a major increase in the number of international interorganizational arrangements. An excellent example can be seen in the Boeing Company's production of the new Boeing 777. Boeing itself manufactures only the wings, nose structure and engine nacelles. The remainder of the airplane is produced through strategic alliances with companies in North America, Japan, and Europe [9,21].

IOCs similar to the Boeing configuration are increasing. Airbus Industrie, headquartered in Paris, is considering forging an interorganizational configuration with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Fuji Heavy Industries, and Kawasaki Heavy Industries to build a 600-seat jet airplane.

The reasons for the formation of these IOCs are myriad and, often complex. In the case of Boeing, participation allows Boeing to sell in Asian and European markets. By manufacturing parts in locales where cost is low, Boeing reduces the tremendous expense of producing a sophisticated major airplane. Thus, their strategy for maintaining competitive advantage is participation in an IOC, despite the potential for unwanted knowledge transfer. Boeing keeps wing manufacturing, final assembly, and integration of electronic hydraulic and mechanical systems at home in order to protect their own interests against losing competitive advantage. The know-how involved in systems integration is extremely complex and not easily transferable. There is, however, some concern that Asian competitors might gain access to critical technology currently through leakage from one of the team.

Another example comes from recent work by Badaracco. His examination of strategic alliances indicates that the strategies of both General Motors and IBM include the formation of formal IOCs in order to gain access, not only to markets, but also to what he terms embedded knowledge. Indeed, gaining access to embedded knowledge provides impetus to the creation of new knowledge; i.e., the new patterns of information flow forged by the initiation of an IOC increase individual organizational learning and begin the configuration-wide process of inter-organizational learning. Thus, the cross-national IOC amplifies access to embedded knowledge and creates and shapes new embedded knowledge that provides strategic advantage.

While these configurations raise a plethora of general policy issues, they also pose challenges for information resource management. There is excellent work that focuses on large multinational organizations $[1]$ . This highlights boundary spanning roles; it is extremely useful for examining IRM challenges in interorganizational configurations. However, information resources management traditionally uses the organization, department, group, or project as the focal unit. Rarely does the interorganizational level of analysis come into play.

## IRM structure in IOCs: An emerging priority

During the most recent phase of our study, a subset of twelve major organizations (seven of which are international in scope) indicate that there is no formal structure for managing cross-organizational information systems (IS). Six of the organizations report that they have a separate group for monitoring IS technologies. But there appears to be no group of individuals who scan the environment for potential partners in IOCs; nor is there any formal management structure for dealing with interorganizational systems that cross organizational boundaries. One organization reports that it uses a major computer manufacturing organization to assist with its planning and environmental scanning.

Although this response applies specifically to technology planning, the potential here for IS corporations to play roles in the formation and maintenance of IOCs is a fascinating possibility. What are the potential and actual roles of IS manufacturing or systems integration organizations, not only in servicing client needs, but in playing roles in the initiation and maintenance of IOCs? As Mintzberg has pointed out, there is a need to analyze the specific environmental setting of single organizations. Similarly, the environmental niche of IOCs needs to be examined to determine appropriate IRM structures not only within specific participating organizations but within the configuration as a whole [18].

Today it is usually chief information officers and their staffs who provide the impetus for interorganizational IS. Here, a focus on standards and on the role of third party providers, particularly EDI, comes into play. While there appears to be no single pattern of IRM structures in IOCs that cross national boundaries, there does appear to be a mechanism that could work where there is a more balanced and widespread exchange of resources including information: the steering committee with representatives from the IS departments of participating organizations. This encourages flexibility. There is a need for high-level involvement in such steering committees in cross-national IOC's. Finally, the steering committee mechanism also matches the need for dealing with different IRM structures within participating organizations (whether they be centralized, decentralized or any combination thereof), as well as the challenges of cross-cultural communication.

The steering committee concept does presuppose some equality among the participating members and organizations. There are cases that indicate the advantage of single major organizations taking responsibility for IRM in IOCs; for example, those that participate with suppliers in an IOC and dictate the standards and implementation of specific interorganizational systems $[14]$ .

Another research question emerges regarding the nature of structures for managing IS in IOCs where there is an unequal power balance among participating organizations of different countries. Preliminary data indicate that, in these cases, especially in the pharmaceutical and automotive sectors, the dominant participating organization sets the standards and defines the amount of participation in shared interorganizational IS. The dominant participant then controls and constrains information transfer within the organization. For example, a major U.S.-based pharmaceutical company participates in IOCs of varying types. In those that can be called limited (where the focus is on development of one particular product or one particular market), there are strict delineations of information transfer that are negotiated at the outset. In the same company, there also is more broad interorganizational participation in other joint arrangements. It is in such broad configurations that the challenge for IRM structuring is greatest.

Another factor influencing the nature of interorganizational IRM in cross-national IOCs is the purpose(s) of an IOC. When top information managers indicate their two or three most important problems, a majority state the problem to be the relationship between information technology and the business of the organization. This stems from the lack of parallelism between the IRM success factors and the business, planning, and IT strategies within the same organization. The challenge is, of course, compounded in the case of cross-national IOCs. If IOCs utilize the information steering committee mechanism but only involve their IT organizations, the power and potential of the mechanism is limited. Including representatives of the major business divisions as members of the steering committee helps to ensure the needed parallelism between the IOC's technology and its business. Thus, there needs to be additional research on the presence and operation of coordinating structures.

There is a final element that is too often overlooked: training for those involved in international interorganizational IRM (IIRM). The long established concept of an information center (IC) housed in a single locale in a single organization, presents a model for training related to IIRM goals. Here, the notion of a “traveling” IC with strong training and possibly demonstration functions emerges. The technology today, of course, exists to support this concept. Those working in such a structure, should not only be adept in the technical and business aspects of the IOC, but also should possess strong cross-cultural communication skills. An effective byproduct of this floating IC is its inter-organizational learning and information sharing potential.

## IRM structure and global economic development

As globalization continues and we begin to view economic development not just as a national or regional activity, IRM structures need to match this complexity and challenge. There are those in the international affairs field who predict that we should develop new institutions to deal with global as opposed to nation-state based economic development. If, indeed, these predictions are correct, IRM structures that are flexible, match the complexity of their environment and also match the complexity of new institutions developed to deal with global economic development will be effective.

It is interesting to observe that the development of IIRM seems to parallel the development of information resource management itself in single organizations. At first, there was little attention paid to the nature of structure. There was no link between structure and strategy; and there was not even a link between the technical and business sides of the organization. Based on the limited data available today, the same developmental patterns are evident in IIRM.

In addition, IRM structures developed as technology itself began to allow for connectivity (moving from a lower level, technically focused structure with almost no links to the environment to a higher level structure with both centralized and decentralized aspects and with multiple links not only to technology suppliers but to key elements of the environment). This pattern also appears to be emerging in terms of IIRM structures. It is now time to address consciously the development of these patterns, as well as the present and potential options for structuring and planning information systems in cross-national configurations.

## IRM planning: Implications for IOCs

The field of information resources planning within organizations has grown rapidly. There is much literature, both anecdotal and theoretical, that identifies actual planning mechanisms, planning failures, and planning successes. For example, the work of Boynton and Zmud, 1987 [4] provides an excellent overview of approaches to information resource planning in organizations.

Additionally, major consulting organizations as well as the federal government have documented varying approaches. The recent increase in the number of cross-national IOCs indicates that these are, for the most part, young phenomena; therefore, there is no history of either planning efforts or planning mechanisms. Oftentimes, participation in an IOC whether cross-national or even within one nation, has been reactive. Devoid of planning, a small supplier, for example, has responded to a major manufacturing organization's calls for a specific system as a part of a formal IOC. This is a reactive mode and a survival mode for, at least, the less dominant participants.

Data from the most recent study of the set of twelve organizations in this subset $[13]$ indicates that two of the twelve organizations only have budget-based planning systems; another five have an annual planning process; an additional five have what they term a “long-range planning process;” and two of these five organizations report what truly is a strategic (both long-term and environmentally focused) IS planning process. An additional two organizations with a long-range planning process are in the midst of moving to a more strategic focus in their planning. Many of the interviewees indicate this direction.

These organizations also report on their business (as opposed to IS) planning. Two of these organizations have only budget focused business planning; four have what they term long-range business planning; and three have strategic business planning; with another three indicating that they are moving toward a strategic business plan. Thus, change is afoot in terms of both IS planning and business planning. The range and variety of approaches to planning is great. But is there a linkage between IS planning and business focused planning in these organizations? Currently, only two out of the sample of twelve highlight such a linkage between the organization's strategic plan and the IS planning process. Here the top IS manager is a full participant in the organization-wide planning processes: it is not an after-the-fact participation.

In addition, an examination of IS planning methods in single organizations does highlight some elements key for successful IS planning in cross-national IOCs $[6,11]$ . These elements are: high-level involvement of both technical and business people; strong linkages between the IS planning processes and the organization-wide business planning processes of participating organizations. But here a problem emerges. IS planning processes have been developed over the years with a focus on either a single project, work group, division, or organization as a whole as the focal unit of analysis. Even if an interorganizational focus is involved (and this is very rare today!), the starting point is always the vantage point of a single participating organization.

This dilemma presents tremendous opportunity for both research and practice. Using the IOC as both the unit of analysis and the foundation unit for planning changes, participants, horizons, and perspectives constitutes a major change. For the first time, the environmental niche of the IOC as a whole, its emerging strategic vision, and its strategic potential can become the focus of the IS planning process.

Consulting organizations, systems integration organizations, and major technology manufacturing organizations might begin to fill the gap and propose planning methodologies. It is clear that an IS planning process that adopts the IOC as the foundation point for strategic planning as a whole sets up new planning perspectives, visions, scenarios and potentials. It also sets up the need for an IIRM structure to match the planning process. Here the structure advocated earlier in this paper, that of an IIRM steering committee at the highest level, allows for this novel and powerful planning scenario.

One method useful in IIRM can be found in the work of Boynton and Zmud [5] which focuses on the concept of critical success factors (CSF). This method is still rooted in a single organizational perspective but does have an external orientation. Using the IIRM structure advocated here allows for the IOC-wide identification of CSF and utilization of these factors in IS planning. At the same time, it also allows for the use of what I call an interorganizational value chain (IVC approach) based on the work of Porter [22,23] and Porter and Millar [24]. This approach looks at the IOC value chain activities, existing technologies, and potential technologies. But the IVC approach adds the following dimensions:

\- Identification of potential linkages across value chain and organizational boundaries.

\- Identification of potential linkages with organizations in the unique environmental niche of the IOC as a whole.

Thus, this IVC approach replaces the single organization vantage point and amplifies the power of the interconnections among value chains in the complex cross-national environment niche of the IOC. This IVC planning process can match those emerging institutions that will be involved in the new global economic development. The new unit of analysis, the IOC as opposed to a single organization, parallels developments in the global economy as well as in the new international relations and sets the scene for IOC strategy development.

## IOC strategy: Comparative advantage and long-term stability

As Bakos and Treacy point out in their classic article $[3]$ “there is little disagreement about the strategic importance of information technology.” Focusing on technology-based competitive opportunities, they highlight three perspectives: the internal, the competitive, and the business portfolio strategy. The focus is on how information technology itself can provide a competitive strategy. This, of course, is very critical and provides a solid basis for examining strategy in IOCs from the unique vantage point of the unit as a whole.

Today there is a major need for an examination of IOC strategy, especially at the interorganizational wide level. This could add a fourth element to Bakos and Treacy's three: IOC scenarios. (Naturally, talking about the strategy of an IOC from the vantage point of the IOC depends upon the nature and purpose of the IOC itself.) For short-lived and limited in scope IOCs, the amount of resources needed for development of IOC scenarios is too great to make sense. But, in the case of those IOCs with a more permanent life cycle and with a breadth of purposes, using IOC strategic scenarios can make a difference. As of yet, the territory is uncharted. The challenge is great. But the potential for sustained comparative advantage from such an approach is impressive. While this step is probably several years out for most IOCs, it is clear that tomorrow's planning and strategy formulation methods will include such approaches.

A final dimension is the key role of boundary spanning in the interorganizational configuration and indeed, the key role of those individuals – those boundary spanners – who connect the business and IRM functions of the participating organizations. Anecdotal evidence and theoretical work provide the basis for the significance of this dimension. Building relationships among the chief information officers of organizations in the IOC as well as key managers from its operating divisions not only strengthens the identification of appropriate strategies but also the long-term stability of the IOC. The concept of “attachment” – the informal relationship developed over time among boundary spanners in IOC’s – provides stability and indeed, interestingly enough, cements exchange relationships. This “attachment” works even in times when the configuration might not be providing the resources or benefits for any one organization or subsystem that it might have envisioned at the initiation of the arrangement. The structures and processes advocated here promote attachments which contribute to the stability of the IOC from a life cycle perspective.

## Conclusion

What is needed now is a truly systems based approach. Viewing the IOC as a whole system with many complex interacting parts and with potential for suboptimization as well as for strategic advantage is the approach of the future. Examining the current interactions among participating organizational systems with their own subsystems as well as among the IRM systems positions the cross national IOC as a key contributor in the new global economic development. This approach matches the complexity in the world environment and developments in global economic approaches and concomitant global institutions.

The number of IOCs is increasing dramatically as is the number of industries involved in such configurations. Even cross-industry linkages appear to be increasing. IRM has a key role to play. It can adopt a systems based perspective – one that uses not only the single organization as a vantage point but the IOC as a whole as its starting point for structuring, planning, and strategizing.

The preliminary approaches to structure, planning, and strategy advocated here are rooted in theory as well as in practice. There is a strong foundation in both interorganizational theory, international economic theory, and in IS management theory of recent years. We are parallel to the point in our IS theorizing and practical history where IS began to be seen as an organization-wide resource linked to key business purposes. Now there is a need for a paradigm shift – a shift to an interorganizational systems based paradigm to match the emerging new world economic order.

Compelling data to support this new paradigmatic shift come from cross-national configurations in the automotive industry that include Japanese-based automobile manufacturers. The recent work of Florida and Kenney $[8]$ provides clear evidence of the need for this paradigmatic shift and for successful IOCs to have planning mechanisms to ensure effective strategy implementation. But even this work is still rooted in a single dominant organization based metaphor for analysis. In this case, of course, it is the vantage point of the Japanese automobile manufacturer involved in formal cross-national IOCs. The shift to the cross national IOC as the focal unit with its challenges and opportunities marks the beginning of this next era in linking global economic development with its own processes, structures, and strategies.
