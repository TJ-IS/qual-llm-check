# Impacts of problem structure and computerized decision aids on decision attitudes and behaviors $^{1}$

Daniel J. Power $^{a,*}$ , Susan L. Meyeraan $^{a}$ , Ramon J. Aldag $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business Administration, University of Northern Iowa, Cedar Falls, IA 50614-0125, USA

$^{b}$ Graduate School of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin, Madison, WI 53706, USA

Little attention has been given to the fit of specific types of computerized decision aids with various decision problem situations. Information regarding this potential contingency relationship is needed both for theoretical development and for guiding practical applications. This paper reports a laboratory study of the relationships between use of an ad hoc, personal, computerized decision aid, problem structure, and various dependent variables. Results indicate computerized decision aid users had positive attitudes toward the aid and, compared to a group of non-users, (1) considered fewer alternatives, (2) took more time making decisions, and (3) used more analytical tools. Identifying alternatives for decision makers seemed to seriously limit search behavior and alter choice behavior. Judges' assessments indicated that decision quality of the computerized decision aid users was generally worse than that of nonusers. There were few significant interaction effects between problem structure and use of the computerized aid.

Keywords: Problem structure; Computerized decision aids; Decision making; DSS; Decision support systems; Management support; Decision software; Decision behavior; Behavioral decision theory

Hypothesis 1B: Individuals in less structured decision situations will consider more alternative courses of action than will individuals in more structured decision situations.

These issues have received relatively little empirical attention. Nevertheless, it seems reasonable that the added uncertainty of a less structured situation will require more time for establishing and applying a problem-solving approach. Also, a more structured situation probably constrains search and hence limits the number of alternatives considered.

Hypothesis 1C: Individuals in less structured decision situations will be less likely to choose readily available alternatives than will individuals in more structured decision situations.

In less structured situations, a search for alternatives is necessary. While some of the alternatives ultimately identified in the less structured situation may be similar to those presented in the structured situation, others probably will not. The key issue here is whether the presentation of alternatives to individuals will cause them to focus specifically on them, thus constraining search. If so, the danger exists that decisions may be manipulable through the simple provision, or suppression, of alternatives.

Hypothesis 2: Individuals using a multi-tool computerized decision aid will report using more decision aiding tools to analyze a decision situation than will unaided decision makers.

A variety of decision tools can be made available to users of a computerized decision aid. Ease of access should result in reported use of more tools. However, while it may seem obvious that those presented with tools will use more than others, contrary arguments can be presented. It is conceivable that those with access to a set of tools will rely solely on those tools while others will sample more broadly.

Hypothesis 3A: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will take more time to reach a decision than will unaided decision makers.

Hypothesis 3B: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will consider more alternative courses of action than will unaided decision makers.

Hypothesis 3C: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will be less likely to choose readily available and identified alternatives than will unaided decision makers.

Benbasat and Schroeder [4] found DSS users to take longer to make decisions than did non-users. Sharda et al. [27] found that DSS users took significantly more time to reach decisions than non-users in the first three sessions of their study with no significant differences in the remaining five. They also found no significant differences in the number of alternatives considered between DSS and non-DSS groups. Sainfort et al. [25] found users of a computerized conflict-resolution decision aid to generate more alternative solutions than those assigned to a videotape technology condition or a no-aid condition.

However, Goslar et al. [13] found that subjects who did not have a DSS took no longer to make decisions. Also, they found that subjects without DSS training or DSS availability considered more alternatives. They found no significant differences in the amount of data considered. Additionally, Coll et al. [6] found that computerized decision support system use did not reduce the time needed to come to a decision. In fact, users of the DSS took significantly longer.

Hypothesis 4A: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will make higher quality decisions than will non-users.

Hypothesis 4B: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will report greater confidence in the quality and acceptability of their recommendations than will non-users.

Hypothesis 4C: Individuals using a computerized decision aid will express more satisfaction with their decision process than will non-users.

The support in experimental studies for the assumption of increased effectiveness and/or a more efficient decision process from using DSS is mixed. A seminal study of computerized decision aids by Scott-Morton [26] introduced the issues in hypotheses 4A and 4B to the literature. An excellent summary of the results of studies on the effects of DSS use is provided by Benbasat and Nault [3]. That summary clearly indicates the contradictory nature of results. In a seminal study, Joyner and Tunstall [16] found no significant increase in decision quality from use of a computerized decision aid. Benbasat and Schroeder reported that DSS users had lower cost solutions. Cats-Baril and Huber [5] found that subjects in a computerized delivery of heuristic condition did not significantly outperform those using paper and pencil heuristics. Further, there were no sigsignificant differences in subject confidence level or satisfaction between the treatments. King and Rodriguez [19] found no significant increase in decision quality with use of a DSS. Eckel [9] reported that DSS users showed increased profits and greater information acquisition than non-users. Goslar et al. found that DSS availability was not related to performance.

Aldag and Power [1] found users of a computerized decision aid reported greater enhancement of problem-solving ability, greater process structure, and greater adequacy of the problem solving process than non-users, but did not find significant differences in confidence in decision quality, satisfaction with resource expenditure, perceived acceptability of solution, or affect toward the process between users and non-users. They also found no differences in rated quality between decisions of users and non-users. Dos Santos and Bariff [8] found that those subjects with guidance provided by a computerized aid exhibited superior performance relative to those with no system guidance. King et al. [18] found decision accuracy and decision quality to be higher for users of a computerized decision aid than for nonusers.

Morris [22] compared users of one of two computerized decision aids to nonusers on a case analysis task. Decision report quality, as rated by three judges, was higher for those using an aid leading students through traditional strategy formulation steps than for nonusers; users of another aid based on an incremental approach did not significantly outperform nonusers. Nonusers were more confident in the appropriateness of their recommended strategy than users of either decision aid. Finally, Coll et al. [6] found that use of a computerized DSS did not improve decision quality. In that study, quality of the decision was evaluated by comparing the chosen alternative with a predetermined “best” solution.

While these findings are mixed, it is probably true that users of a computerized decision aid anticipate increased satisfaction, confidence, and decision quality from use of the aid. If the hypothesized consequences of use of such tools are not supported in the face of these favorable expectations, such use becomes problematic and perhaps unnecessary or even counterproductive. However, the mixed results of prior research may have been due in part to a poor fit between the decision aid and decision problem.

Hypothesis 5A: Use of a computerized decision aid will improve decision quality more in a structured decision situation than in a less structured decision situation.

Hypothesis 5B: Use of a computerized decision aid will improve confidence in the quality and acceptability of recommendations more in a structured decision situation than in a less structured decision situation.

Hypothesis 5C: Use of a computerized decision aid will improve satisfaction with the decision process more in a structured decision situation than in a less structured decision situation.

Cats-Baril and Huber asked rhetorically “for what types of problems are DSSs more effective, and what design characteristics make them more effective?” They concluded that widespread user acceptance of computerized decision aids in the context of moderate to well-structured problems indicates their usefulness in those situations, but they felt it was not clear that computerized aids are helpful for ill-structured problems. Morris found an interaction effect (p < 0.10) of computerized decision aid use and turbulence of the environment reflected in the decision case. With high environmental turbulence, a computerized aid based on traditional strategy formulation steps outperformed both a computerized aid based on an incremental approach and no aid. In a case reflecting low environmental turbulence, there was slightly higher rated quality for the incremental aid than for the strategy formulation-based aid and the no-aid condition. However, it is not clear from the Morris paper whether turbulence may be taken as a proxy for lack of structure.

If it is found that computerized decision aids are of little help in addressing ill-structured/unstructured decision problems, then decision aid developers should not market or target their aids to unstructured problems. At a minimum, they should specifically test their decision aids to assess their effectiveness in unstructured situations.

## 3. Methodology

In our opinion, computerized decision aids are appropriately tested in controlled, laboratory settings. Ilgen [15] has argued that laboratory research is appropriate when one or more of the following conditions exist: (1) high fidelity between the laboratory and the field can be established, (2) laboratory conditions are to be recreated in the field, (3) field conditions limit the feasibility of field research, and (4) the hypothesis of interest is one demanding simply the demonstration of an effect rather than direct generalization of that effect to a particular setting. Applying these conditions suggests that computerized decision aids are appropriately tested in controlled, laboratory settings because of the similarities to actual field settings in which computers are used. Further, we argue that, while field testing is desirable, it should follow laboratory experimentation. It has been demonstrated elsewhere that student subjects may serve as surrogates for managers in experiments on business decision making. For instance, Remus [24] found no differences in decision-making behavior and performance on an experimental production scheduling task between MBA students with no work experience and experienced managers. For these reasons, the hypotheses were examined in a laboratory experiment using student subjects at a midwestern university in the USA.

## 3.1. Subjects

Eighty-two students in three sections of a class titled Management Decision Making, a senior-level course required of Business Management majors, participated in the study. The course had covered the topics of rational decision-making processes and behavioral decision theory prior to the study. On average, the students taking part were 22.4 years old. All were planning to graduate within two semesters. 37 (45 percent) of the subjects were male, while 45 (55 percent) were female. Ninety percent of subjects agreed with the statement, “I am experienced with general use of a computer.” No subjects reported strong disagreement with that statement. Subjects reported an average of 4.2 years of experience with computers (s.d. = 2.6) and 5.4 years of work experience (s.d. = 3.5).

## 3.2. Research design

A $2 \times 2$ experimental design that manipulated both availability of computer support and amount of decision structure was used in this study. Onehalf of the students involved in the study solved a decision problem with the assistance of a computerized decision analysis tool called the Management Decision Assistant (MDA) (Power [23]). The others did not use an automated aid. The MDA has 10 tools, including Multi-Attribute Scoring, Cost-Benefit Analysis, Decision Process Selection, Cost/Volume/Profit Analysis, Strategic Business Analysis, Economic Order Quantity, Threat-Opportunity Matrix, Goal Analysis, Project Planning Chart, and Pro-Con Choice Analysis. Students could choose to use any or all to help them with the decision task. All the capabilities can be accessed through the use of pull-down menus or command keys. An option is selected by moving the cursor on the menu bar to highlight one of four categories: TOOLS, FILE, HELP, or OUTPUT. Once the menu is displayed, the arrow keys may be used to highlight the specific option. Pressing the ENTER key activates the student's choice.

Students were also provided with either a structured case that identified three alternatives under consideration or a less structured version where no alternatives were identified. This manipulation provides a conservative test of the role of problem structure. The problem structure manipulation took place at the end of a short business case called Yancey Pools, Inc. In the less structured condition, students were asked for their recommendations and given no additional information. In the more structured condition, students were additionally told that Yancey had been considering: (1) discontinuing the franchise agreement and operating independently, (2) stressing repair and maintenance service during off-season, or (3) piggybacking on homebuilders by including a pool in new home construction. Subjects were assigned randomly to one of the four conditions.

Not all tools in the computerized decision aid were appropriate for application to the case. While some tools (Multi-Attribute Scoring, Cost-Benefit Analysis, Threat-Opportunity Matrix, Goal Analysis, Pro-Con Choice, and Cost/Volume/Profit Analysis) may have been useful, others were less appropriate. Thus, as in an actual situation, subjects were required to decide which, if any, of the tools they should use. In addition, students in the computerized-aid condition were not precluded from using non-computerized tools.

## 3.3. Research procedure

The week prior to the experiment, all students participating in the study were taken into the computer lab and introduced to the Management Decision Assistant (MDA) and its capabilities as part of a course section titled “Computerized Decision Aids.” Students were told that, at some point in the semester, they would all have the opportunity to apply the MDA to an actual decision task. Student volunteers signed an informed consent form a week prior to the experiment. For the experiment, approximately one-half of the randomly assigned volunteers were taken to the lab and then used the MDA, to the degree they deemed appropriate, as they solved the Yancey Pool case. The other half of the participating students remained in the classroom and solved the Yancey Pool case as a paper and pencil task. While participation in this study was voluntary, it should be noted that 87% of students in the class participated.

In both the computer lab and the classroom, one of the researchers greeted subjects using a prepared script and gave them a packet of experimental materials. This included a short introduction to the task, the appropriate form of the decision case, and a decision report sheet. All relevant data were included in this materials packet. Subjects in the computerized decision aid condition were also given the MDA on a disk. The experimenters then answered questions.

The Yancey Pools decision problem was presented in a short one-page case format. On a decision report sheet, students were asked to answer four questions: (1) What decision issues is Yancey facing? (2) What course of action do you recommend? (3) What are the reasons for your recommendation? and (4) What decision tools did you use to analyze the Yancey Pools decision situation?

All participating students were given ten minutes to read and review the problem, a maximum of 30 minutes to analyze the case, and a maximum of 20 minutes to complete their responses to the decision report questions. They then turned in their experimental packet and picked up the appropriate follow-up questionnaire(s). They were given 15 minutes to complete either one or two questionnaires, as appropriate, based on their treatment. Following completion of the task, the experimenters explained the intent of the experiment to the participants and answered questions.

## 3.4. Measures

After completing their decision report sheets, all participants were given a questionnaire to assess their attitudes toward the decision process and solution. A Likert-type questionnaire (1 = completely disagree to 7 = strongly agree) was used to evaluate: (1) confidence in decision quality, (2) perceived acceptability of solution, and (3) perceived process adequacy. Other questions were included to measure students' feelings about their approaches to the case and their understanding of the task. Embedded within the questionnaire was a manipulation check with respect to problem structure. Subjects were asked to indicate their degree of agreement with the statement, "Alternative courses of action were clearly specified." General demographic information was also requested. Students who were assigned to use the Management Decision Assistant were given an additional questionnaire to measure their attitudes toward the computer program. Questions were included to assess (1) positive affect toward the use of computers in making decisions, (2) warmth of interaction, and (3) the degree of challenge and accomplishment. These two questionnaires based on Aldag and Power are included in the appendix.

## 4. Results

The manipulation check for problem structure was significant in the hypothesized direction (Chi $^{2}$ = 18.4; p < 0.01). Also, users of the computerized decision aid reported favorable attitudes toward using the aid. Three scales, challenge and accomplishment, warmth of interaction, and positive affect, assessed user attitudes toward the software. The means for these scales indicate that users did, in fact, report favorable attitudes toward using the computerized decision aid. On a 7-point scale (1 – completely disagree; 7 – completely agree), the mean for warmth of interaction was 5.1. The mean response for positive affect was 5.4. However, there was some ambivalence in the results for challenge and accomplishment associated with using the program. The mean on that scale was 4.1. This examination of user feeling about the decision aid is critical since, whatever its effectiveness, an aid which is disliked by decision makers is unlikely to be used.

The first set of hypotheses looked at problem structure. Hypothesis 1A focused on time to reach a decision; Hypothesis 1B examined how many courses of action were considered; and Hypothesis 1C considered the likelihood of choice of an identified course of action. Hypotheses 1A and 1B were tested using separate ANOVAs. As shown in Table 2, neither the time to reach a decision nor the number of alternatives considered differed significantly between the high and low structure conditions.

Hypothesis 1C was assessed by considering the percentage of people in the more structured and less structured conditions who chose one or more of the given alternatives as opposed to creating a new alternative. This comparison was simplified by the finding that, while many individuals recommended more than one alternative, no individual combined an identified alternative with a new alternative. In fact, 78 percent of subjects in the more structured condition chose experimenter-identified alternatives. Conversely, only 34 percent of subjects in the less structured situation chose alternatives which the experimenters had identified. An overall Chi-Square of 102 shows this difference is significant at the 0.001 level, confirming hypothesis 1C.

The second, third, and fourth hypotheses focused on the impact of the use of the computerized decision aid on the decision making process. Hypothesis 2 stated that users of a multi-tool computerized decision aid would employ more tools than would non-users. This hypothesis was tested using analysis of variance. The results supported Hypothesis 2 at the 0.01 significance level.

Hypothesis 3A stated that users of computerized decision aids would take more time to reach their decisions than would non-users. Analysis of variance results, reported in Table 2, supported the hypothesis (p < 0.001). Hypothesis 3B stated that those using a computerized decision aid would consider more possible alternatives than would non-users. Results of the ANOVA testing this hypothesis are also presented in Table 2. The results were significant (p < 0.10) but opposite those predicted. That is, non-users considered significantly more alternatives than did users. Hypothesis 3C focused on the number of individuals who selected experimenter-identified or other alternatives. It was hypothesized that users would be less likely to choose an experimenter-identified alternative than would non-users. Results of the Chi-Square test for this hypothesis indicate that the hypothesis was not confirmed.

Hypothesis 4A stated that users of a computerized decision aid would make higher quality decisions than would non-users. As noted above, quality was assessed by five judges. The patterns of their responses, shown in Table 3, indicate that users of computerized decision aids tended to make lower quality decisions than did non-users. Thus, Hypothesis 4A was not supported.

Hypothesis 4B stated that users would report greater confidence in the quality and acceptability of their recommendations than would non-users. These hypotheses were tested using ANOVAs which are reported in Table 4. Findings were not significant for either confidence in decision quality or perceived acceptability of solution. Hypothesis 4C stated that users of computerized decision aids would express more satisfaction with their decision process than would non-users. The ANOVA results relating to perceived process adequacy were significant (p < 0.001) but opposite the direction hypothesized. That is, users of computerized decision aids were less satisfied with their decision process.

Hypothesis 5A stated that use of a computerized decision aid would have a greater influence on decision quality in a more structured situation than in a less structured situation. Only two of 25 interactions of aid use and quality were significant (p < 0.05). Both of those interactions were as hypothesized. That is, aid use improved rated decision quality more in the more structured condition than in the less structured condition. In fact, in each case aid use slightly reduced decision quality in the less structured condition. However, in view of the total number of interactions considered, these findings are probably due to chance. Hypothesis 5B stated that use of a computerized decision aid would have a greater influence on confidence in decision quality and acceptability in a more structured situation than in a less structured situation. The ANOVA results indicate a significant interaction (p < 0.10) for perceived acceptability of solution, but not for confidence in decision quality. Thus, Hypothesis 5B received mixed support. Hypothesis 5C stated that use of a computerized decision aid would have a greater influence on satisfaction with the decision process in a more structured situation than in a less structured situation. The ANOVA results also showed no such interaction for the measure of satisfaction with the decision process, perceived process adequacy.

## 5. Discussion and suggestions

We have stressed the need to develop empirically-based theory which contingently links decision problems with computerized decision aids. Such theory is needed both to provide direction for future research and to guide managers and other decision makers who are considering using a computerized decision aid. The results of this study were generally not as anticipated, in the sense that the computerized aid did not generate expected benefits. Also, problem structure had only limited effects on our dependent variables. Problem structure remains, however, an important and complex variable relevant to DSS use.

One can draw a number of conclusions from the study. First, identifying alternatives for a decision maker seems to seriously constrain search behavior and alter choice behavior. The negative consequences of this finding depend upon the initial quality of the identified alternatives. Sadly, this attribute of a problem's structure is much easier to judge retrospectively than prospectively. If this finding is supported in subsequent studies, it seems reasonable to caution managers about this phenomenon. Senior managers should especially be cautious about requesting recommendations and delegating decisions when they have needlessly constrained the set of available alternatives. Even the provision of computerized decision support did not overcome the constraints created by an identified alternative set.

Second, it appears that using a computerized decision aid requires an increase in the time spent making a decision, but this increase may have been used in manipulating the computerized tools and technology rather than for increased analysis. This seems unlikely in this study because the students were familiar with the tools provided by the aid. The complexity of a decision may influence the balance of this trade-off between the time costs of using the technology and the motivation to spend time on a more complete analysis. The decision incident used in this study was reasonably short (1 single-spaced page in length) and focused on one primary, reasonably straightforward issue. This relatively low level of problem complexity may have needed very little analysis. A complex decision might be evaluated and considered for a few hours and in some cases weeks or months. The time spent on the manipulation of computerized tools may under such circumstances have less significance as a percentage of total decision time. Also, at some low level of problem complexity, computerized decision aids may be more of a hindrance than an advantage.

This study is one step toward the development of an adequate contingency theory of computerized decision aid use. Additional effort must be devoted to the development of taxonomies of decision problems and of computerized decision aids. In view of the central role that decision problem structure has had in previous theory and of the rather compelling logic underlying the relevance of that dimension, we explored it here. Nevertheless, other problem dimensions, such as the importance of the problem to the decision maker, the complexity of the problem and/or the crisis versus noncrisis nature of the problem, may also be important. Further, it may be the case that the problem structure construct is itself overly broad. That is, it may be preferable, at least for some purposes, to consider the separate roles of feedback, uncertainty, repetitiveness, and other dimensions subsumed in this classification scheme. In a limited way, our study took this approach, focusing on the specification of alternatives.

More attention must also be devoted to the classification of computerized decision aids. While we have emphasized the distinction between ad hoc, personal DSSs and DSSs developed for specific, recurring problems, many other bases for classification may be appropriate. For example, Kottemann and Remus [20] recently examined the role of decision model “naturalness.” Further, there is considerable variety even within the subcategory of ad hoc, personal, computerized decision aids. For example, such an aid may include a set of distinct decision tools or it may attempt to replicate a specific problem solving process.

Following such taxonomy development, more adequate interpretation of past research should be possible. Without such a taxonomy it is difficult to determine whether negative results are due to fundamental inadequacies of computerized decision aids for specific classes of problems or to an aid-problem mismatch.

Completing these important preliminary steps will facilitate additional empirical research examining computerized decision aid efficacy within a contingency framework. Further, it has been argued elsewhere that user individual differences may influence reactions to computerized decision aids. Such differences may also influence the nature and consequences of aid-problem fit. As one example, fit sensitivity may be a function of field dependence-independence $[21,31]$ .

Ultimately, of course, the goal of this sequence of theoretical and empirical steps should be to provide concrete guidance to practicing managers in their selection of appropriate computerized decision aids. Unfortunately, it appears that any such prescriptions on the basis of research conducted to date would be premature.
