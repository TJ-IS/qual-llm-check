# Firms that choose outsourcing: A profile

Kirk P. Arnett and Mary C. Jones \*

Drawer MG, College of Business and Industry, Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS 39762, USA

A nationwide survey of senior Information Systems (IS) managers in U.S. organizations reveals several structural and managerial characteristics of organizations that outsource one or more IS activities. The characteristics include organizational position of the IS manager, CEO involvement in IS (e.g., presence on an IS steering committee and personal use of computers), and IS performance. Outsourcing activities examined are hardware (e.g., network, PC, workstation, mini-computer, and mainframe maintenance and support); software (e.g., contract programming and software support/training) and comprehensive management activities (e.g., facility management and systems integration). Apparently CEOs who are heavily involved in a steering committee are the least likely to outsource. CEOs that actively use computers are more likely to outsource specific hardware and software activities, whereas CEOs who do not personally use a computer are more likely to outsource comprehensive management activities. In addition, the distance between the CEO and the IS manager is a factor: further distance makes it more likely that IS functions are outsourced. Industry leaders are among the smallest proportion of outsourcing firms, whereas close followers are the largest.

Keywords: Outsourcing; Hardware maintenance; Network management; Software support; Contract programming; Facility management; Systems integration; CEO involvement; Steering committee; Organizational position; IS performance; CIO; Personal use of computers; Organizational characteristics; Managerial characteristics

U.S. organizations spend heavily on outsourcing; the spending is estimated to grow to \$15.2 billion by 1995 [12]. Outsourcing contracts that have attracted media attention are long-term, multimillion dollar agreements: they are not made in isolation from the rest of the firm, but rather must have CEO approval. Basic tactical and operational level functions are often a larger part of IS than are unique, non-routine strategic level functions $[21]$ , and outsourcing these basic functions provides firms the freedom to pursue strategic IS opportunities internally; these are often otherwise ignored. Increasingly, executives who “want more time to focus on business issues and strategic planning are taking the initiative in exploring the possibility of outsourcing” $[25]$ . In contrast to many studies that examine why companies choose to outsource, this study examines structural and managerial characteristics of organizations that make this choice.

## 2. Conceptual background

Managerial and organizational characteristics of firms have long been examined for their effect on various IS activities. Because the characteristics may also affect the decision to seek external IS support, a profile of firms that have made such a decision was developed using several managerial and organizational traits. There are not many research results on which to base the choice of the characteristics to be examined, therefore this study provides a preliminary framework. Characteristics examined are those that are believed to impact the decision to seek external IS support: CEO personal use of computers [11], CEO role in an IS steering committee [10], reporting level of the senior IS manager, and IS performance.

## 2.1. CEO involvement

2.1.1. Personal use of computers. In the past, top level executives were not expected to demonstrate much computer literacy, but today, at least a minimum is needed. However, a little knowledge may be dangerous: executives who think they know more than they do may actually impede IS by encouraging inappropriate applications. At the other extreme are executives who are quite computer literate, but prefer not to get involved. Somewhere between the extremes is the CEO that knows enough to be involved and set appropriate IS direction, but has sufficient confidence in the IS manager's abilities to contribute to the firm's strategic mission.

The perfect match between CEO, CIO, and highly effective IS operations can be attributed to a number of personal, organizational, and industry factors. One of these is the CEO's personal use of computers [2,27].

This measure is a relevant component of a profile of firms that outsource. CEOs who are heavy users of information technologies may be more inclined to maintain IS functions in-house in order to maintain control over the use of information for strategic purposes. On the other hand, CEOs with minimum computer literacy may prefer to maintain an arm's length and therefore outsource.

2.1.1. Steering committees. Steering Committees (SCs) are composed of a broad range of organizational representatives, including functional area specialists, mid-level managers, and top executives. Regardless of the composition, the SC provides leadership and direction in several ways including resource allocation, project selection, technology acquisition, priority planning, and strategic MIS planning.

SCs contribute to IS success through a wide variety of roles, ranging from bridging the gap between user expectations and IS capabilities to charting a course for the firm's use of information technology. Firms can benefit from the use of an SC even if they rely heavily on the CIO for direction: working in partnership with the CIO, the steering committee can effectively integrate IS into every aspect of the organization. In addition, for firms in which there is a high level of top management concern for IS management, procedural formalization of IS development is less of an issue than when concern is lower [13]. Thus, when top management concern translates to active steering committee involvement, SCs help to improve the IS process.

Current industry trends make the role of the steering committee more important today. Outsourcing changes the way a firm manages a critical resource; thus, in turn, affects the way the firm operates. Therefore, because of the strategic nature of SCs, they should be involved in major, long range outsourcing decisions. The a priori assumption is that a high level of outsourcing is associated with a firm whose SC tends to handle more routine problems because some portion of the allocation decision has been given to outside sources.

Five types of CEO to SC involvement are assessed: no committee, CEO fairly inactive, CEO assigns someone else as a representative, CEO active, and CEO chairs. The particular role of a single CEO is primarily dictated by two factors: CEO comfort with information technology and historical organizational arrangements. For example, if the committee tends to discuss routine topics, executives generally assign a representative to replace them $[6]$ .

## 2.2. Reporting position

Reporting position of the IS manager is also related to the extent to which the CEO is involved [8]. Some CEOs have moved the IS manager position closer to them in order to better utilize the corporate information technology resource [LaPlante, 1992]. The higher the reporting position of the IS manager, the better the communication between that manager and the CEO [24]. Not only must the CEO have some IS knowledge, but the IS manager must understand the needs of the top executives [18].

Beginning in the late 1970s the CIO concept emerged to remove the historical tie-in of IS to a functional area and to hold a single executive directly accountable for organizational information processing needs. Several factors supported this movement: among them recognition of the value of information and the need to manage this resource.

Although few firms have adopted the CIO concept, it is clear that many support an organizational position such as Data Administrator in the organizational hierarchy. Some empirical evidence from the USA indicates that when the IS manager is only one level removed from the top, firms pursue less outsourcing than when there are more levels between $[1]$ . However, there is no evidence that there is any relationship between reporting position and specific activity outsourced.

We decided to limit our investigation to two reporting positions: one and greater than one level from the CEO. It is believed that the higher ranking IS managers will exert more influence on the decision to outsource. An inverse relationship between IS reporting level and extent of comprehensive outsourcing is expected. In addition, it is expected that when IS is one level removed, there are fewer specific hardware and software activities outsourced.

## 2.3.IS performance

Outsourcing affects IS performance through the quality of work provided by the outsourcer and reduction in IS staff to support non-outsourced activities. Many organizations use outsourcing to downsize the IS area, often leaving only an IS skeleton in place. Often most of the organization's IS employees go to work for the outsourcer, and many others leave to seek work elsewhere; e.g., of the 100 employees at Revlon Inc., fifteen became employees of the outsourcer, and most of the remaining employees were phased out, leaving an IS staff of twenty [16]. The loss of key IS employees can have a devastating effect on an organization's ability to use IS to support the activities that are not outsourced.

Performance may also be affected by the quality of services provided by the outsourcer. One insurance executive has said “in this day and age with everything driven by the bottom line … can an outsider give us what we need on a more competitive basis than our own staff can?” [17]. All aspects of the work must be specified in order to achieve good performance from the outsourcer. Firms must examine prospective outsourcers’ performance records and potential longevity: without proper planning, if the outsourcer goes out of business, IS performance is likely to be diminished.

On the other hand, if the right outsourcer is chosen, if the appropriate activities are outsourced, and if the internal IS staff is effectively refocused, then outsourcing can boost performance. In our study, performance is measured as the relative IS standing in the organization's industry. Respondents are asked to indicate which of five categories best describes their firm's IS performance: industry leader, close follower, middle-of-the-pack, somewhat behind, and industry laggard.

## 2.4. Outsourcing activities

An assessment of a sample of the larger outsourcing contracts of the past two years and the services offered by others provides the basis for classifying outsourcing activities. Table 1 indicates activities supported by outsourcers that serve a variety of industries, and Table 2 profiles activities supported by outsourcers that primarily service firms within their own industry. However, there is little difference between the two types with respect to their offerings.

Outsourced activities include facility management, hardware maintenance, contract programming, and software support. Of these, hardware maintenance activities show major differences between platforms. For instance, today's PCs are much more reliable than the mainframes that have long supported all data processing functions. Today's movement from minicomputers and mainframes poses different maintenance and support requirements than were previously required. Thus, hardware maintenance is divided into three categories; networks, workstations/PCs, and minicomputers/mainframes.

An additional activity that requires external support is systems integration. The use of outside integrators has been common in government organizations for many years, yet has only become common in the private sector since the late 1980s [20]. With the proliferation of new technological advances and new types of applications, there is increasing pressure to deliver strategic applications faster. Systems integrators with comprehensive capabilities may be vendors, such as IBM and Digital Equipment Corporation, or consultants, such as Andersen Consulting. These companies provide a combination of packaged integration services that, if properly managed, can make the installation of new systems easier and less costly.

Thus, in our study, seven types of outsourcing activities were examined: systems integration, facility management, contract programming, software support, network maintenance, minicomputer/mainframe maintenance, and workstation/PC maintenance. The activities examined primarily relate to the operational life of the system rather than to planning and development activities. This does not suggest that the latter are less important in outsourcing decisions – instead they are assumed to be part of the more comprehensive activities of systems integration and facility management rather than being explicitly addressed. There is no suggestion that any single activity is more or less important to organizations than any other, even though a single activity may be critical in a specific organizational location. For example, hardware maintenance of a twin machine that spooled print-out during the night shift is less critical than maintenance on the hardware used to control the life support equipment in a hospital.

For clarity and ease of discussion, the seven activities are classified into: hardware, software, and comprehensive management activities. Hardware includes maintenance of the minicomputer/mainframe, workstation/PC, and networks. Although the last might include some software installation and tuning, it seems likely that wiring or optical connections, LAN-cards, PCs and peripherals will be the bulk of the activities in the network category.

Software activities include software support, training, and contract programming. Comprehensive management activities include facility management and systems integration, although each of them may have components that overlap with other categories.

All are primarily what Gorry and Scott Morton [9] call “organization independent” activities whose general structure is consistent across firms; they differ only in implementation details. This is consistent with an observation of Max Hopper (architect of American Airlines’ Sabre reservation system) that much of

the IS role today is like plumbing and electricity. It's the basic running of the computers, the communications lines, ensuring the terminals stay up and operational in service. There's every reason to believe that over time, those functions can better be performed by outsiders because of the economies of scale [4].

For example, National Car Rental has contracted the data management of the reservation system that has given them a competitive advantage in the marketplace to EDS. However, the reservation system was in place prior to the decision to outsource, thus EDS is primarily responsible for maintaining and upgrading the system and ensuring that it functions properly.

## 3. Methodology

A mail survey of a systematic random sample of 252 CIOs listed in Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives yielded forty usable questionnaires for an adjusted response rate of 17%. A variety of industries was represented: manufacturing (38 percent), finance, banking, and insurance (35 percent), wholesale and retail trade (12 percent), and utilities (12 percent). The relatively low response rate may be due, in part, to the use of CIOs to report on a sensitive aspect of their relationship with their CEOs. Findings should be interpreted in light of the limitation imposed by the small response rate.

## 4. Results

Contract programming and maintenance of the mainframe and minicomputers are the major types of outsourcing: two-thirds of the respondents reported using outsourcers for these activities (Table 3).

Ninety-seven percent of the organizations surveyed use outsourcing for at least one activity and many pick and choose more than one of the candidate activities (Table 4).

However, outsourcing is not an all or nothing proposition. Only 3% of firms do not outsource any activity, and only 6% use outsourcers for all of these activities.

CEOs who are heavy users of computers employ technology for electronic mail, word processing, spreadsheets, and data base queries. The largest percentage of CEOs who outsource are heavy computer users. CEOs who have no personal involvement with computers (non-users) are the largest proportion who outsource the more comprehensive management activities of systems integration services and facility management, and also the hardware activity of network maintenance. CEOs who make symbolic use of the computer and those who insist that staff use computers, but do not personally use one, fall between these two extremes.

The only anomaly is network maintenance: non-users are larger outsourcers of this activity than heavy users. Perhaps heavy users of networks are reluctant to relinquish control of something they find personally useful. CEOs who are not personally involved in computer use may not understand the critical nature of IS or the implications (e.g., loss of control) that can accompany the use of an external provider. If they are not actively involved in in-house IS activities, then the loss of control will have little effect on their management activities.

## 4.2. CEO steering committee involvement

Table 6 profiles outsourcing with respect to CEO involvement in an IS steering committee is examined.

Organizations in which there is no steering committee are the largest users of outsourcing, and the firms with actively involved CEOs are the least likely to use outsourcing. Perhaps actively involved CEOs want to maintain control over IS activities, or they have enough confidence in the internal IS staff that they see no benefit to outsourcing.

When there is no committee, outsourcers are frequently used. Perhaps this is a “chicken and egg” phenomenon: lack of a committee could indicate a lack of interest in internal IS management, or that outsourcing could eliminate the need for a steering committee. The latter seems unlikely unless the entire IS operation has been outsourced. It is more likely that the more actively involved the CEO, the more careful that executive is in deciding to seek external providers of IS services.

An interesting anomaly is that fairly inactive CEOs are among the smallest proportion of those who outsource: none outsource systems integration, contract programming, network management, or workstation/PC maintenance. 20 percent of organizations that outsource facility management have CEOs who are fairly inactive SC members.

## 4.3. Reporting position

The next question involves the reporting position of the IS manager (Table 7). The assumption is that higher level IS managers have more control over outsourcing decisions and attempt to keep most activities in-house.

Approximately half the respondents are only one level removed from the CEO. The largest proportion of outsourcing is done by organizations where the IS manager is more than one level away from the top. This relationship holds for all activities except systems integration and mainframe and minicomputer maintenance.

## 4.4. IS performance

The last assessment involves the relationship between outsourcing activities and performance (Table 8). Respondents were asked to indicate their firms' IS performance relative to other organizations in their industry.

Although performance choices range from industry leader to industry laggard, no one reported being an industry laggard, and few reported being somewhat behind. Therefore, firms that are somewhat behind were combined with the middle-of-the-pack firm and are referred to as trailers. Thus, three categories of performance are used: industry leaders, close followers, and trailers.

Close followers are the largest proportion of firms that outsource. These firms rank second behind trailers in outsourcing. Leaders are the smallest proportion of outsourcing firms in each category. Industry leaders are firms that actively and aggressively pursue competitive advantage in their industries. They have the resources and the willingness to take risks. For example, American Airlines is a leader in airline reservation systems because of its SABRE system. It has the critical mass of resources, knowledge, and skills to acquire, develop, and implement advanced technologies long before the competition. Although outsourcing may alleviate some of the IS overload, many companies find that “IS in the 1990s will probably be too critical to their primary mission and too integral to their corporate strategy to be managed by outsiders” [22]. Thus, those that can afford to support internal IS activities are doing so.

## 5. Summary and conclusions

A limitation of this study is the small response rate and the small sample size – thus findings should be interpreted as providing a basis on which further research can build, rather than as predictive of the effects of organizational traits on outsourcing. However, despite the limitations of the study, the findings do provide a unique profile of outsourcing companies with regard to structural and managerial characteristics. Findings are summarized in Table 9.

Two factors make this study distinctive. First, outsourcing is examined with respect to several characteristics of CEOs. Because CEOs exert a powerful influence on major IS decisions, the extent to which they understand technology and become involved in IS management affects the decision to outsource.

Second, outsourcing is thought to affect IS performance, yet little attention is given to whether IS performance affects the decision to use external IS providers. If the desire to improve performance prompts outsourcing, then it is reasonable that poor performers are more likely to outsource than high performers.

Results indicate that this is not entirely true. As expected, leaders are the smallest outsourcers. However, close followers are the largest outsourcers, whereas trailers fall somewhere in-between. Close followers continually try to maintain their industry position, but trailers often react too late to position themselves higher in the industry. Thus, firms that traditionally seek to improve performance try outsourcing, whereas trailers have not yet joined the outsourcing movement.

Outsourcing is classified into hardware, software, and comprehensive management activities: however, there is little difference among categories with regard to the characteristics of firms. In general, the largest outsourcers are firms whose CEOs do not make extensive personal use of computers, whose IS managers do not report directly to the CEO, who have no steering committee, and who closely follow IS leaders in their industry. This suggests that firms may pursue extensive outsourcing primarily for two reasons: cost and performance.

First CEOs who view IS as a necessary evil to be dealt with as cheaply and effortlessly as possible may outsource many activities. This partially explains the use of outsourcers by firms in which the CEO is inactive in IS, where there is no steering committee, or where IS managers are removed from the CEO. CEOs in these firms may use external providers because they do not fully understand the critical nature of IS.

Extensive outsourcing may also reflect the desire to stay on top of the dynamic movements of the IS environment. Because of the costly nature of the frequent technological changes in IS, firms must have either extensive internal resources or access to resources outside the firm. This explains why firms in which CEOs are active in IS or in which IS performance is a close follower in the industry use external providers to maintain their competitive position in an cost effective manner.

It is important to note that although there are many benefits to outsourcing, there are pitfalls as well. Functions related to the operational life of an IS, such as maintenance, are among the most commonly outsourced; however, even outsourcing something as basic as hardware maintenance has posed problems for some companies. For example, one company was penalized for breaking a mainframe maintenance outsourcing contract when the company moved from mainframes to client/server systems [Horwitt, 1993]. Thus, it seems even more crucial that CEOs understand IS so that they can better foresee strategic changes in IS prior to committing the firm to an outsourcing contract that may preclude such changes. This understanding is facilitated by CEO involvement in an IS steering committee; moving the CIO closer to the CEO to increase interaction between the two; and greater personal use of computers. IS leaders also seem less likely to outsource something they will later regret because of their commitment to and experience with using information technology to their competitive advantage.

Even if outsourcing does not interfere with strategic IS decisions, firms have encountered other problems with outsourcers; one of the most common is workforce management. For example, the employees of Eastman Kodak Co. filed suit against their network outsourcer because they did not believe they received the benefits and job security they were promised as part of the outsourcing contract. Other firms have cited dissatisfaction among their own employees who work alongside the outsourcer's employees who receive more pay and benefits than the internal employees.

Outsourcing changes the way organizations manage the IS function, regardless of the reasons underlying the decision to outsource. Most firms use external sources to provide at least one IS activity, and many use them for several activities. IS managers who want to remain competitive in today's environment must consider outsourcing. If the CEO decides to outsource, the CIO may have little influence over the extent of activities that become part of the contract. If CIOs view outsourcing as an additional option for managing basic functions and freeing up internal resources for more strategic, organization critical activities, then outsourcers may be used to the advantage of the IS area.
