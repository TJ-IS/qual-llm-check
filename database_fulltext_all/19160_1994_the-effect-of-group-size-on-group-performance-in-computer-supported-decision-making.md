# The effect of group size on group performance in computer-supported decision making

Hsin-Ginn Hwang $^{a,*}$ and Jan Guynes $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer and Office Information Systems, Northeast Louisiana University, Monroe, LA 71209-0120, USA $^{b}$ Department of Information Systems and Management Sciences, University of Texas at Arlington, UTA Box 19437, Arlington, TX 76019, USA

This experimental study investigates the effects of computer-supported systems in groups of nine persons and the effects of group size (three versus nine persons) in the computer-supported environment. A two-by-two completely randomized factorial design was employed, and 192 subjects participated in this study. Group efficiency is assessed by decision time and the number of alternatives generated by group members. Group effectiveness is measured by satisfaction with the decision making processes, decision satisfaction, and decision quality. The main findings of this study were: (1) decision quality can be improved in large computer-supported groups, and (2) large groups generate more alternatives and take longer to reach a final decision than small ones. Implications are discussed.

Keywords: Electronic brainstorming; Electronic meeting systems; Group decision making; EMS; Group size; Group decision support systems; GDSS

## 1. Introduction

Interest in the use of computer-supported group decision making has grown in recent years. These systems are often termed Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS). They facilitate group decision making by managing the information pools generated in the group interaction process. With advances in information technology and increased interest in the both efficient and effective utilization of computers in organizations, the use of GDSS is likely to increase.

Some research was aimed at comparing small groups using GDSS with those not using it in face-to-face decision making $[2,3,13,28,44]$ . The group size of previous GDSS experiments was often controlled as a fixed variable (either three or four persons). Some results of these studies

In order to prove the assumption, several empirical GDSS studies have been conducted to investigate the effects of group size in a computer-supported environment $[9,10,37]$ . It should be noted that these researchers employed an electronic meeting system (EMS), focused on brainstorming type meetings. In such an environment, the members of the group do not have to meet face-to-face and may not even participate in the interaction at the same time. All interactions are facilitated through the system. Additionally, these studies concentrated on option generation, rather than the entire decision making process. Their findings indicate that larger groups have achieved better performance with the EMS. These findings are significant because the so called “process loss” in group interaction may be favorably affected by the presence of computer assistance.

Analysis of previous GDSS studies $[12,33,38]$ indicates that the performance of group decision making is highly dependent on the types of decision tasks and computer-supported systems used. More studies related to different types of computer-supported systems and decision tasks are needed for generalizing the results across the effects of group size in a computer-supported environment. A judgmental decision task was used in this study, which was intended to investigate the effects of computer-supported systems in large groups and the effects of group size. Since there is no definite cutting point between small and large groups, “small” or “large” can be treated as a relative, rather than absolute.

## 2. Previous research

The relationship between group size and group performance has been broadly studied in the non-computer-supported decision making environment $[4,23]$ . The results of these studies indicated that group decision making performance can be measured in terms of two aspects: efficiency and effectiveness.

Decision time and the number of alternatives generated by group members have been used by many small group researchers to measure efficiency. Two non-computer related studies $[19]$ indicated that while larger groups generate more output, ideas, and alternatives, reaching consensus was more difficult. South $[43]$ reported that smaller groups reached decisions (compromise) faster than larger groups for judgmental tasks. However, other studies showed that group size did not influence decision speed for some task types $[48]$ .

Similarly, the relationship between group size and number of alternatives generated is inconclusive in the non-computer-supported environment $[18,27,52]$ . Under some conditions, there is a positive correlation between these two variables, but under other conditions, there is no difference in the number of alternatives generated by members of large and small groups.

Satisfaction with decision making processes, decision satisfaction, and decision quality have been used to measure group effectiveness for many small group studies $[22,30]$ . Both Hare $[21]$ and Slater $[42]$ reported that as the group size increased the level of group satisfaction decreased. Schellenberg $[39]$ and Hackman and Vidmar $[20]$ also reported similar findings. Ziller $[55]$ concluded that a positive relationship existed between group size and decision quality. On the other hand, Bouchard and Hare $[4]$ reported that there was no major increase in performance when group size increased from five to nine.

For many GDSS researchers, group size, decision task, and proximity are very important variables in understanding the effects of GDSS. Many empirical studies have been conducted in small GDSS groups due to the limitations of the features of computer-supported systems, types of experimental settings, and the number of subjects available $[6,14,16,34]$ . Although the results of these studies are not exactly consistent, their findings still suggest that GDSS can improve group performance in terms of increasing the number of alternatives generated, reducing individual dominance, and improving decision quality.

Few Software Aided Meeting Management (SAMM) studies have been conducted to investigate the effects of group size. Watson [53] compared groups of three and four members and concluded that there was no significant difference

Several EMS studies have been conducted on large groups. Dennis, Valacich, and Nunamaker $[9,10]$ conducted electronic brainstorming experiments to test the effect of group size on anonymity using groups of size three and nine. Larger groups generated more options of a greater quality than the smaller groups. In a follow-up experiment that concentrated on option generation rather than the entire decision-making process, they added a set of 18-member groups to the first. This change resulted in a comparison of three group sizes. They found that the number of options, quality of options, and satisfaction increased with group size. In another experiment, they studied nominal groups (constructed by pooling outputs of individuals who worked separately) against freely interacting groups in an electronic brainstorming environment. Their results showed that interacting electronic brainstorming groups generated more options of higher quality.

Nunamaker et al. [35] reported the results of another experiment in an electronic brainstorming environment that compared group size with the proximity of the members of the group. They found that larger groups were more effective than smaller ones and remote groups were more effective than proximate groups in option generation.

Gallupe et al. [15] conducted two experiments on electronic brainstorming and group size. The results indicated that electronic groups outperformed others. The larger groups produced more ideas than smaller groups. The electronic and the larger groups generated more high-quality ideas. The members of the electronic groups were also more satisfied with the outcome. Satisfaction increased with group size for electronic groups, but decreased for nonelectronic groups.

## 3. Experimental design

## 3.1. Methodology

Our study employed a two-by-two completely randomized factorial design. The independent variables manipulated were group size (three and nine persons) and computer-supported systems (presence or absence). In all, a series of eight replications was performed, with four groups in each replication.

## 3.2. Subjects

One-hundred-and-ninety-two undergraduate and graduate students served as subjects. At a minimum, all subjects had completed an introductory course in MIS and none had previously participated in any GDSS experiments. Subjects were randomly assigned to groups. These groups, in turn, were randomly assigned to be computer-supported or manual. The average age of subjects for this study was twenty-eight years. The ages ranged from twenty-one to fifty. Subjects included 86 females and 106 males. 48% of the subjects had managerial experience, having been at least lower-level managers. 89% of the subjects had worked full time for at least one year.

## 3.3. Computer-supported system

The computer-supported system used here was a menu-driven multi-user system that supports group interaction in a face-to-face setting. This system was designed, coded, and tested in-house; it was similar to the “Decision Room” of DeSanctis and Gallupe [11]. The major functions and features are similar to SAMM.

The system was intended to structure the decision-making process and improve group interaction. The main menu for our system is provided in Table 1. This system could gather data, randomize alternatives generated by group members, and display them on group members' monitors automatically. In addition, the system could gather, organize, and tally the ranking and voting results from the group interaction.

## 3.4. Dependent variables

The performance in group decision making was evaluated by five dependent variables: decision time, alternatives generated, satisfaction with process, decision satisfaction, and decision quality. The first two focused on group efficiency, and the last three on group effectiveness.

Decision time was defined as the time between the start of reading the case and arriving at the final decision. This did not include a thirty minute training period. The time was measured and recorded for each group, either by the system or by a facilitator.

The total number of alternatives generated by each group was determined; two independent examiners counted the number of alternatives. Duplicate alternatives were not included. In the case of a conflicting count, an arbitrator was used to resolve issues of interpretation of the wording of alternatives.

A post-experimental questionnaire (Appendix A) was administered to all subjects. An average score for each element in the group was used to measure the overall satisfaction.

The final decisions were evaluated by three independent “experts.” These full time faculty members had expertise in both group and decision making research and were knowledgeable about the case study used in the experiment. They also used as their reference the characteristics of a good solution to the decision task proposed by Stumpf [46]. These experts did not know anything about the group size or available support of the material they rated.

## 3.5. Decision task

The decision task was an unstructured case involving an uncertain and complex environment. Its objective was to propose a way to resolve the conflict in a small aircraft manufacturing company. This was a judgmental decision task; it required substantial interaction and exchange between group members.

## 3.6. Experimental procedure

Each experimental session consisted of two parts. In the first, each group was given a thirty minute training session. The facilitator answered any questions. The second part involved the actual decision making, including three major phases: (1) alternative generation and discussion to eliminate redundant alternatives, (2) alternative ranking and display of the order, and (3) alternative voting and display of the final decision.

In phase one, subjects are told to read and analyze the case individually. They then begin generating alternatives individually (computer-supported groups key in, or manual groups write down their alternatives). These are collected and displayed. The final activity of phase one is to discuss the displayed alternatives orally in both cases. The discussion only eliminates redundant alternatives.

In phase two, subjects are told to rank the alternatives displayed in alphabetical order, either on the screen or the blackboard. Computer-supported groups key in the ranks, while manual groups write them on paper. The overall ranks of each alternative are then displayed.

In phase three, subjects are told to vote on the final decision. Only one alternative is selected by each member. The results are then displayed, either on screen or the blackboard. The final decision is that alternative approved by more than half the group members. Subjects are asked to rank and vote again if they have failed to obtain more than 50% approval on a decision.

After reaching the final decision, subjects are asked to answer the post-experimental questionnaire. Finally, the facilitator collects all data and forms, and thanks the subjects. The facilitator also serves as a technician supporter to answer questions about computer use.

## 4. Results

Ten hypotheses relating computer-supported systems and group size to the performance of computer-supported systems were tested. Contrasts were evaluated with a modified t-test designed to correct for unequal variances between treatments. The performance of group decision making was measured in terms of group efficiency and group effectiveness.

## 4.1. Group efficiency

Four hypotheses were tested on group size and the efficiency of group decision making:

Hypothesis 1: For large groups, decision time is shorter for the computer-supported groups than for the manual groups.

Hypothesis 2: For the computer-supported groups, decision time is longer for the large groups than for the small groups.

Hypothesis 3: For large groups, the number of alternatives generated is greater for the computer-supported groups than for the manual groups.

Hypothesis 4: For computer-supported groups, large groups generate more alternatives than small groups.

The data relevant to Hypotheses 1 through 4 are presented in Table 2. It indicates that large computer-supported groups required, on the average, 8 minutes less time to reach a decision than large manual groups. This time difference, however, was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.257). This finding is similar to that of previous GDSS research in small groups. While computer-supported systems are able to facilitate the decision making processes, several other factors may reduce the positive direction. For instance, learning may cause subjects to spend more time making a final decision because they were either unfamiliar with the computer-supported systems or had more interest in using the system for a longer period of time.

There was no significant difference in the number of alternatives generated between large computer-supported groups and large manual groups (p-value = 0.491). This is contrary to the findings of small GDSS research and of electronic brainstorming studies in large groups. Other factors, such as personal educational background, may have influenced the number of alternatives.

The larger the group, the more resources are available. Therefore, more interactions among group members was expected. Large groups generated more alternatives than small groups (p-value = 0.0001) and required more time to reach a final decision (p-value = 0.0002). These results are consistent with previous research.

## 4.2. Group effectiveness

Six hypotheses were tested on group size and the effectiveness of group decision making:

Hypothesis 5: For large groups, overall satisfaction with the decision-making processes is greater for computer-supported groups than for manual groups.

Hypothesis 6: For computer-supported groups, overall satisfaction with the decision-making processes is greater for large groups than for small groups.

Hypothesis 7: For large groups, overall decision satisfaction is greater for the computer-supported groups than for the manual groups.

Hypothesis 8: For computer-supported groups, overall decision satisfaction is greater in the large groups than in the small groups.

Hypothesis 9: For large groups, the decision quality is higher for computer-supported groups than for manual groups.

Hypothesis 10: For the computer-supported groups, the decision quality is greater for large groups than for small groups.

As shown in Table 3 only Hypothesis 9 was found to be statistically significant. Large computer-supported groups have better decision quality than large manual groups (p-value = 0.039). This is consistent with results of small GDSS research and electronic brainstorming studies in large groups. The large computer-supported groups also generated decisions of higher overall quality than small computer-supported groups (15.5 versus 14.4). However, the difference in the quality level was not significant (p-value = 0.197).

## 5. Conclusions and discussion

Our results can be summarized as follows. First, large computer-supported groups generated more alternatives than those of small computer-supported groups. However, large computer-supported groups required more time to make a final decision. These findings are consistent with earlier research. Second, the results of previous GDSS studies in small groups indicated that group efficiency was improved in terms of the number of alternatives generated, but the GDSS groups never took less time. The Burr-Brown experience $[8]$ and other studies indicated that the EMS has the potential for improving group decision making in large groups. However, our findings failed to support the previous small GDSS research and EMS studies. Although the computer-supported system did reduce decision time and increase the number of alternatives generated for large computer-supported groups compared to large manual groups, the results were not statistically significant. Third, previous research in group decision making without computer-support suggests that there is a negative correlation between the level of satisfaction and group size. Our study shows that computer-support does not seem to have any positive effect on the satisfaction of large groups with either the decision process or the final decision. That is, the use of computer-support did not overcome the negative effects of larger group size. This finding is consistent with some of the previous small GDSS research and non-computer-support studies. Fourth, prior research on decision quality in small GDSS groups has been mixed. The results of the majority of small GDSS studies and observations from other large GDSS groups studies have indicated computer-supported systems can improve decision quality. The results of our study indicated that decision quality improved significantly in large computer-supported groups.

These findings demonstrate both the limitations and strengths of utilization of computer-supported systems in business society. While many researchers and practitioners imply that computer-supported systems may greatly assist in improving the group interaction and performance, this study found only a limited set of positive factors. These results, by themselves, do not suggest that computer-supported systems should be avoided altogether for large group interactions. They do, however, suggest that one should not expect improvements in all areas of group dynamics. If those areas that showed significant improvement are very important in a particular situation, such as increased number of alternatives generated, and decision quality, computer-supported group decision making can be justifiable. In other areas, computer-support for large groups may not be effective.

The results of previous studies using the electronic brainstorming environment indicated that performance increased substantially with group size in terms of the number of ideas generated, satisfaction, and total quality of ideas. However, The results of this study do not support these arguments completely. The previous arguments that the performance of computer-supported systems varies among different decision types of decision tasks, experimental settings is enhanced in this study. The results of this study also indicate that the features of the computer-supported system might be a critical factor. The features of the computer-supported system used in this study

were limited. It is possible that this is why some of the results did not reach expectations. The effects of computer-supported systems might be stronger if decision makers could communicate with one another anonymously, access both internal and external databases, fetch decision models, and access expert opinions during the decision-making process. Since there are many limitations of using students as subjects, future research on group size should proceed in the field study area. More SAMM type of research regarding group size is also needed for generalizing findings.
