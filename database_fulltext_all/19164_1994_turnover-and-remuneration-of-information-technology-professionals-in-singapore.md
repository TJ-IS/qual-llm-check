# Turnover and remuneration of information technology professionals in Singapore

Margaret Tan $^{1,*}$

Department of Decision Sciences, Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 0511

Magid Igbaria

Department of Management, 32nd and Chestnut Streets, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

The need for increased research in human resource management issues as they pertain to the information technology (IT) industry has been emphasized in a number of recent studies (Crook, Crepeau and McMurtey, 1991; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988; 1992; Igbaria and Greenhaus, 1992; Igbaria and Siegel, 1992). The importance of gaining a better understanding of the factors related to recruitment, satisfaction, and retention

The rapid growth of technology in Singapore and the shortage of IT professionals had an impact on the country's competitiveness. The study shows that the turnover rate among IT professionals is still high, particularly among software professionals. There are large differences in the salaries of IT professionals. Salaries vary by job title (category), sector, working experience, nationality, education, and in some jobs by gender. The turnover rate and the number of job openings fell in 1990 and is expected to fall more in the near future. Apparently, the main reasons for turnover are competition from other employers in remuneration and limited advancement opportunities. The managerial implications of these findings are discussed.

Keywords: Personnel management; IT professionals; Turnover; Salary; Job openings; Singapore; International

Such turnover is costly, not only in terms of replacing staff and the high cost of training new employees, but also in terms of systems development productivity and quality.

The rapid economic development of the South East Asian countries have forced entrepreneurs to emphasize the role of IT in their organizations. The number of professionals in these newly industrialized countries has increased tremendously, particularly in the IT field. Among these countries, Singapore has “special educational and infrastructure advantages which undergird goals for expansion of computer services. The principal goal of this area is expansion of software services” (Couger, 1986). Singapore’s investment in IT has rapidly increased in the last ten years as it positions itself as a communications, business, and economic hub for Asian and the Pacific rim countries.

Because Singapore does not possess any natural resources and historically is known as a trading port, IT has been given special emphasis. Singapore intends to raise the productivity level of both public and private sectors. Relying on its human resources, IT is to play a significant role in boosting its economic well being. The country has attracted investments from multinational reputable computer technology companies by intensive promotion and tax incentives. Since 1985, 12 multinational companies have opened software development offices in Singapore. It has also developed widespread training and education programs in IT.

In 1990, IT Households Survey, reported that one in four households in Singapore has a computer and the percentage of companies with more than 10 employees using computers increased from 13% in 1982 to 75% in 1990 (Survey Report on Usage of IT in Singapore, 1990). The supply of IT professionals increased from about 850 in 1980 to about 14,000 in 1991 with an average growth rate of 20% from 8,300 in 1988 to 14,300 at the end of 1991 (The IT2000 report, 1992). The NCB has projected continued strong growth of between 10% to 15% annually between 1992 and the end of the decade. It also plans to enlarge the IT workforce to between 33,000 and 35,000 by the year 2000 (Straits Times, 1992). The 1990 World Competitiveness Report ranked Singapore top in the world in the effective use of IT. These achievements can be attributed to the government commitment to technology.

One key problem that Singapore faces is manpower. Singapore has a population of 2.7 million and a work force of 1.6 million, with unemployment under 2%. Singapore relies on more than 200,000 foreign workers. Because of the shortage of labor, wages are rising inexorably. The Financial Times (1991) reported that in 1990, there was a 6.4% real increase in wages and yet the productivity growth was only 3.4%. The report also stated that labor is a major constraint on Singapore's expansion and a shortage of labor is Singapore's over-riding problem. Economically Singapore is positioned for growth. Singapore's GNP in 1989 was US\$10,752 rising to US\$12,765 in 1990 (the second highest in Asia after Japan). It also has an average growth rate of about 6% to 7% from 1986 onward, with a record high of 8.3% in 1989. Singapore could sustain a growth rate of about 6% without getting overheated, yet may find it difficult to cope with its increasing labor shortage. Therefore, the Singapore government, through different ministries and statutory boards, developed a plan to make Singapore more competitive in the export market.

The National Computer Board (NCB) was formed in 1981 to coordinate computer education and training, to implement the computerization of the civil services, and to develop and promote the computer services industry. The board recognized skilled IT manpower as its first important hindrance to the development of the software industry and the use of IT. This study is based on the 1990 NCB survey conducted to address some of the concerns related to the human resources issues of IT professionals in Singapore.

Although much research has been performed on managing IT employees, most of these studies have been restricted to examining North America (Baroudi, 1985; Bartol, 1983; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988; 1992). Further, none of these studies examined the actual turnover among IT employees. A few studies have also focused on the motivational factors of IT employees in Singapore. To our knowledge, however, no study has focused on the actual turnover among IT employees, particularly in Singapore.

Further, most of the MIS career literature focused mainly on the effect of salary as a determinant of turnover intentions or one of the motivators rather than on the distribution of salary among different groups by age, gender, education, nationality, sector, job category, etc. Given the importance of salary as a significant factor affecting an individual's decision to leave and as one of the top five motivators of IT professionals, we emphasize the importance of gaining a more complete understanding of the salary distribution. This study attempts to examine the salary by demographic and organizational factors. Moreover, given the high demand for IT professionals in Singapore in recent years, this study investigates the projected demand and number of openings for IT professionals as well as the reasons for the inability to fill opening jobs.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

The NCB carried out this survey focusing on the remuneration of IT professionals in Singapore. It was designed to find out the compensation they received in Singapore and to gather information on their turnover and remuneration. The survey comprises three profiles: (1) corporate; (2) turnover; and (3) employee. NCB decided to survey organizations known to be significant employers of IT people using three sources; The Singapore Federation of Computer Industry (SFCI), the Data Processing Managers' Association (DPMA), and the Stock Exchange of Singapore. In October 1990, participation was invited from 155 companies that employ a relatively large number of IT employees. By mid-January 1991, the closing date of the survey, sixty four surveys (41.3%) were completed and returned.

Of the 4,362 IT professionals, 2,332 were males (57.3%) and 1,864 females (42.7%). Figure 1 shows that approximately 6% of the sample had postgraduate degrees (Masters/Ph.D.), 57% had IT degrees or diplomas, and about 18% non-IT education. There were also 4,140 (94.9%) Singaporean (including permanent residents) IT professionals and 222 (5.1%) non-Singaporean IT professionals. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the sample by sector.

## Analysis of results

Turnover Rate among IT Professionals. The overall turnover rate in 1990 (from January 1, 1990 to September 30, 1990) is 17.2%. This rate fell from 18.4% in 1989. As the turnover rate reflects labor market conditions, this decline suggests a slight easing in the general market for IT professionals. However, the turnover rate varies among IT professionals. Figure 2 shows that software professionals had the highest turnover rate while IT managers had the lowest. In the absence of comparable data from other countries, particularly the U.S., and other professional groups in Singapore, we can not say whether it is high or low. But, it appears to us that the turnover rate among some groups, particularly software specialists, appears to be high and firms should address this problem.

We also examined the reasons for turnover among IT professionals. This study shows that IT professionals leave or stay for a variety of reasons. Table 2 reports reasons given by employers for the turnover of IT professionals. This may be a result of inadequate compensation and the high demand for IT professionals in Singapore. Note that respondents provided several reasons for turnover, and therefore the figures do not add up to 100%.

Job Openings. Companies were asked to indicate the number of job openings in the IT field. They reported a total of 303. The job vacancy rate, i.e., the number of job openings divided by the number of employed IT professionals, is 6% for private companies and 13% for statutory boards. The largest number of vacancies (52.4%) are for software professionals. All key sectors, including banking, management consulting, manufacturing, and transportation, report numerous openings for software professionals. Among them, the largest number are application programmers (78), systems programmers and specialists (52), and systems analysts (37). Note that the turnover rate of software professionals is the highest among all IT professionals and it is more likely that they leave because, due to high demand, companies are willing to accommodate them. The data also show that there are 88 (29%) positions for IT professionals in services and support and 28 (9.2%) new IT jobs for marketing and sales. It was noted that 37 of the 88 (42%) opening jobs for service and support specialists are in the public sector (statutory boards) and 24 of the 28 (85.7%) opening jobs for marketing and sales are in IT supplier sector.

We also examined the reasons for the inability to fill these job opening. The results show that the two main reasons for a firms inability to fill these job openings are the tight labor market (43.2%) and a shortage of suitable candidates (35.1%). Note that high salary expectations (8.1%) and competition from other employers in remuneration (8.1%) are not a main reason for their inability to fill these positions. This suggests that there is a high demand and a shortage in the supply of qualified IT professionals, mainly software professionals, in Singapore.

Salary. Companies were asked to report the base salary for its IT employees. The average monthly base salary for IT professionals is S\$2712. Figure 3 shows that the average monthly base salary varies among IT groups and ranges widely among IT professionals. The highest salary reported by the companies, paid for some of the IT consultants (S\$16,667), is more than 30 times that of the lowest paid to some of the computer operators (S\$496). These differences may be due to personal, job and industry factors.

Table 3 shows that the IT professionals holding managerial jobs are, on average, the highest paid while the hardware and software professionals are, on average, the lowest paid. The data also show that IT professionals holding managerial positions in the management consulting sector made, on average, the highest salary, followed by managers in the public sector – statutory boards. The second highest paid group are the IT professionals holding marketing and sales positions, mainly in the IT supplier and transportation sectors.

We also examined the average monthly base salary by the number of years in the IT field (IT tenure). Table 4 shows that, for all job categories of IT professionals, the average monthly base salary and IT tenure are positively correlated. It seems that there is a premium for experience as those marketing and sales professionals with eight or more years of experience receive salaries that are nearly three times those of new entrants into the profession. For hardware professionals, the average monthly base salary also rises with experience.

We also examined the average monthly base salary by education (qualification). Table 5 shows that IT professionals holding managerial jobs with postgraduate degrees earn more than those with basic degrees, and those with basic degrees earn more than those with diplomas. However, these differences are not great.

The salary differences between software professionals with different educational qualifications are even smaller. Software professionals with postgraduate degrees earn about S\$550 more than similar professionals with “O” and “A” level qualifications. These small differences may be due to the fact that some IT professionals with lower formal education have more years of IT experience than similar IT professionals with postgraduate degrees.

On average, females earn about S\$400 less than their male counterparts (S\$2,479 vs. S\$2,867). However, females in three groups, software, hardware, and marketing and sales, earned almost as much as men. On the other hand, other females earn significantly less than men (see Figure 4).

Figure 5 shows that overall there is about S\$700 difference between the salary of Singaporean (Singapore citizens and permanent residents) and non-Singaporean IT professionals (S\$2,665 vs. S\$3,407). The largest gap is between Singaporean IT managers and non-Singaporean IT managers. Another large gap is found among Singaporean and non-Singaporean IT professionals providing services and support. The most significant group with the services and support is among IT consultants.

Salary Increase in 1990. Companies were asked to indicate the salary increase in 1990. Fifty three of the sixty four responding firms provided these figures. The average salary increase of IT professionals was 8.8%. Figure 6 shows that the salary increase of IT professionals in two sectors – management consulting firms and IT suppliers – were more than those of IT professionals in other areas while salary increases were lowest in transportation firms. Finally, we examined the average cost per head of IT professionals. The data show that the average cost of employing an IT professional rose from S\$41,993 in 1989 to S\$43,199 in 1990, an increase of 2.8%. ## Summary and conclusions

The results of the NCB survey provide information about three related areas of IT personnel in Singapore. This study reports the status of IT professionals in Singapore: we examined their turnover rate and salary. Other issues related to the management of IT professionals were also addressed; this provides the basis for several conclusions and recommendations for the management of IT personnel worldwide and particularly in Singapore. Reviewing the current projections for shortages of IT professionals and the increasing demand in this field, as well as economic concerns associated with high turnover, it would seem that most organizations should willingly try to understand the reasons for turnover, including answering what should be done to reduce it, and address the needs for more effective human resource management of IT professionals.

Turnover Rate. This study shows that the turnover rate among IT professionals in 1990 is 17.2%. This is down from 1989 (18.4%) and this may suggest a slight easing in the general employment market for IT professionals. Software professionals and IT professionals in marketing and sales faced higher turnover rates of 20.6% and 17.9%, respectively, compared to the 9.8% for management. Employee turnover, although not the only contributor, plays a significant role in cost escalation. Beyond the obvious implications of direct costs associated with it, there are secondary ramifications to be considered. For example, the constant replacement of personnel could produce a disproportionate ratio of employees in the “introductory” stages of the learning curve instead of the more productive “maturity” stages. Those employees remaining within the IT group could become somewhat demoralized as a result of an organizational malaise that occurs when turnover becomes excessive. Depending on the physical isolation of the IT group, there could be even spin-off morale problems in other groups. Thus, there is a growing need to control the increasing volume of voluntary turnover actions of IT professionals.

The main reason for turnover, as reported by the participating companies, is salary competition followed by limited advancement opportunities. Companies believed that their IT professionals left for higher paid jobs. Another important job related factor is the availability of advancement opportunities. Possibly IT professionals who perceive limited tangible benefits and limited opportunities for career advancement tend to hold strong intentions to leave their companies. Therefore, organizations should develop creative ways to increase the internal mobility opportunities of IT professionals and provide them both tangible and intangible rewards. Organizations should recognize that there is a need to take professional development more seriously and to combat manpower shortage and skills deficiencies. Companies should examine their career progression path, provide faster promotions and develop a dual career path system for professionals. A dual career path approach can provide important incentives and career advancement that might encourage professionals to stay with their organization. At the same time, IT professionals who wish to enter a managerial path must be provided with career planning opportunities and training and development experience. Moreover, since lateral moves can provide feelings of challenge and growth, organizations should provide IT professionals with flexible alternatives to the traditional emphasis on vertical mobility. Organizations should also allow cross-functional mobility within the company.

If management is not able to compete in remuneration, then focus on the intangible benefits and rewards, including providing ample recognition for achievement and assigning challenging and interesting projects that allow for autonomy and creativity. We should also emphasize that meeting the needs of IT professionals may compromise higher organizational goals. Increasing salary, autonomy in decision making, interesting work, challenging jobs and advancement may reduce turnover, but could also detract from organizational effectiveness in maintaining rapid systems development cycles. McClelland's (1978) work in the field of achievement motivation cautions that highly achievement oriented professionals sometimes view the organization as existing primarily to serve their needs. To off set this problem, many firms employ personnel who not only remove uninteresting work from the professional's job, but also allows the professional to spend a greater percentage of time engaged in higher income producing activities.

Job Openings. Job vacancies were running at 6% for private companies and 13% for statutory boards. Companies reported a total of 303 vacancies. Of these, most were found in the software job category. Note that the two related reasons firms were not be able to fill these vacancies are the tight labor market and a shortage of suitable candidates. Companies facing these problems need to develop strategies to address them. The common practice used by companies to address the manpower shortage has been to increase salary to attract experienced IT professionals. Generally, companies have been reluctant to invest in training, because experienced IT professionals have been hired by their competitors. Since technology changes rapidly, experience may no longer be an asset unless the employees are continuously retrained. Productivity may also increase subsequently.

Remuneration. There are large differences in the salaries of IT professionals. Salary among some groups vary by sector; while the industry salary variation among software professionals is small compared to the other groups, the average monthly base salary of IT managers and IT professionals in services and support varies across industries. The study also shows that management consulting firms and IT suppliers on the average pay more than other firms and that they increased the salary of their IT professionals by 11.7% and 10.3%, respectively. However, consulting firms say their average cost of employing IT professionals jumped by almost 43%, which suggests that the non-salary costs also jumped. Some of these companies had to pay fringe benefits to improve the status of their employees. It is interesting to see that on one hand companies are willing to pay experienced employees a high salary but on the other hand they are unwilling to develop training and management programs for them.

Gender differences were observed in only two groups, IT professionals in management and in services and support. It has been stated that women who entered the technology field in the

80's were lacking IT experience. As more women gain experience with technology and move up the corporate ladder, this salary gap may narrow. It was found that women may start out with comparable salary, yet their salaries lag 25% below that of their male counterparts within ten years. This may be a result of the “gender gap” within the IT field which is most evident at the senior management and executive levels of organizations. This also may be a result of a “glass ceiling” or invisible barrier encountered by women which restricts their advancement to top executive positions: a worldwide phenomenon indeed. For example, confirming the presence of such a barrier in IT, reports in trade journals on computing (Johnson, 1990; Laberis, 1992; Marenghi, 1992) have highlighted the achievements of a few exceptional women in the U.S.A. who have broken the barrier to become the chief information officer. Future studies should identify the origin and determinants of salary discrepancies between females and males in similar positions, particularly in high levels, to check for possible gender bias.

The Singapore government is reluctant to offset the labor shortage by stepping up permits for low-paid foreign workers. The study shows that the average salary of Singaporeans and non-Singaporeans who perform low level functions is similar. Overall, Non-Singaporeans earn S\$700 a month more than Singaporeans. It seems that many companies bring foreigners to perform all the consulting managerial, and services and support jobs.

It is acknowledged that there is a need for increased research on cross-cultural differences in the IT field. This interest reflects the recognition that international information systems, in general, and cross-cultural differences, in particular, will take on greater importance in many organizations. Information and communication technology have made the world much smaller and the applications are globally adopted. Despite the increasing importance of international information systems, international issues are discussed only occasionally in the IT journals (Eindor and Segev, 1992; Ghani and Al-Sakran 1988; Igbaria, 1992; Lu, Qiu and Guimaraes, 1988). All of these studies focused on microcomputer usage in different countries. More research is needed to examine the human resources aspects of IT in different countries and conduct cross-cultural studies focusing specifically on differences and similarities between countries and cultures with regard to the management of IT personnel. The present study provides important insights into the management of IT personnel in Singapore and represents an important step for additional research for examining the human resources aspects of IT in other countries.
