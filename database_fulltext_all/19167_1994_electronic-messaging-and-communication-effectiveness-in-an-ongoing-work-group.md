# Electronic messaging and communication effectiveness in an ongoing work group \*

Michael H. Zack

Northeastern University, College of Business Administration, 214 Hayden Hall, Boston, MA 02115, USA

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) technologies are transforming the way work is performed within groups. Exploratory research on managers' use of CMC in natural settings suggests that shared cognitive and social context may be the most important factors influencing the communication. The research reported here examined the use of electronic messaging (EM) vs traditional modes of communication (i.e., face-to-face (FTF), telephone, and written memo) in an ongoing work group performing a cooperative task. By means of an in-depth multi-method field study, the research explored the context surrounding when and why a particular communication mode was chosen, and how those choices related to communication effectiveness. Communication was more effective when FTF was used to build or share interpretive context where deficient, when leaner EM was used to communicate within an existing shared interpretive context, and when communicators complied with communication procedure by choosing the expected mode of communication.

Keywords: Computer-mediated communication; Computer-supported cooperative work; Work group effectiveness; Electronic mail; Group communication

## 1. Introduction

A fundamental issue surrounding computer-mediated communication (CMC) technology is how it differs from more traditional modes of communication. Much research has been done on how different media affect the collaborative decision making process for group decision support systems (GDSS) and computer-supported cooperative work (cf, [10], [11], [16], [17], [22], [27], [29]). This work suggests that the use and impact of electronic decision support is a function of the group structure, task, characteristics of the particular information technology, and the context [21]. GDSS research has generally focused on the effects of group structure and process to show that use of the technology to structure a particular task has positive value (i.e., process gains outweigh losses). Less emphasis has been placed on examining the effects of information processing characteristics of the task, and little on the social and cognitive context in which it is performed. The day-to-day work of ongoing groups is much less structurally constrained and much more contextually constrained. Further, these groups generally perform a range of tasks with blurred boundaries and varying in degree of structure and clarity. Thus while all four factors remain important, the emphasis of the research changes.

The experiments have typically used groups whose members share no organizational or group history, context, or knowledge. They often perform artificial tasks. More importantly, there has been no free choice of communication medium. While wholly appropriate for studying the structured GDSS environment, such efforts may have limited generalizability. Only by systematically observing patterns of unrestricted choices of communication mode within naturally occurring groups can we begin to understand their pragmatic differences.

Almost no CMC research has observed ongoing work groups in natural settings where history, routine, norms, social relationships, and deeply shared interpretive and behavioral context may play a large role in determining choice of communication mode. Exploratory research on managers' use of CMC in natural settings $[19,20,24,25]$ suggests that shared cognitive and social context may be the most important factors influencing communication behavior in work groups.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Media richness

One important line of research on computer-mediated communication channels has focused on media richness, suggesting that it should match the equivocality or richness of the information being conveyed. Daft and associates $[1,2,3,4,5]$ have suggested that communication about ambiguous or equivocal events and situations requires rich media, defined as those providing (1) multiple channels having the capacity to transmit high-variety languages, (2) personalization, and (3) capacity for immediate feedback. Thus FTF would be very rich, as it provides multiple high-bandwidth channels operating simultaneously, takes place in the presence of all parties to the communication, and enables mutual and immediate response at the level of a thought or utterance. EM, in contrast, would be less rich, as it is usually restricted to a single text-based channel, reduces or eliminates social presence [30], and constrains communicators to respond to or interrupt whole messages.

## 2.2.Interpretive context

Effective communication requires participants to share enough background knowledge or context to render messages meaningful $[18,26]$ . Ambiguity and equivocality arise when this is lacking $[12]$ . Ambiguity refers to a lack of meaning or means to interpret a message, while equivocality refers to multiple, possibly conflicting meanings $[32]$ . Although not synonymous, here these terms are used interchangeably to represent a lack of shared interpretive context. To build a shared context, interactive exchange via rich channels providing continuous, instantaneous, multichannel feedback is needed to enable response to unanticipated remarks, repair communication breakdowns, and resolve divergence. These characteristics are vital to the dynamic and adaptive evolution of discourse.

## 2.3. Behavioral context

Recurring behaviors may be anticipated, and those expectations provide an additional context for interaction $[13,28]$ . Communication processes are governed by rules of procedure, usually tacit, and effective communication requires compliance with them $[8]$ . Thus, choosing an expected communication mode may contribute more to effective communication than choosing the mode most appropriate in terms of richness.

Hackman [15] proposed that groups operate more effectively when they discuss their performance strategies, and Guetzkow and Simon [14] provided experimental evidence that this applied explicitly to communication processes in small task-oriented groups. It is reasonable to conclude that groups which either discuss their communication rules and procedures, or at least adhere to some tacit but well-known and shared set of communication procedures will communicate more reliably and effectively.

Communication also will be more reliable and meaningful when both parties comply with expected communication procedure. ## 4. Research method and design

The objective of this exploratory study was to refine the research framework rather than to generate or test hypotheses. As a real-world context was essential, a field study was required. Data were collected from several sources and by several methods, including interviews, observation, questionnaire, and electronic mail capture, and analyzed to identify convergent findings.

This research studied the core editorial group of “The Morning Times” (pseudonym), a large daily newspaper (circulation 450,000). It was considered to be an innovative and high-quality newspaper, based on a ranking by a national news magazine, performance in news competitions, the number of industry awards received, and the opinions of industry vendors and editors at other newspapers. The work group had eighteen members who were the senior and middle-level newsroom editors responsible for producing the first (“news”) section of the newspaper. They operated within a newsroom roughly the size of a football field, and although most of them were located within the same general area, many interactions occurred over large distances.

Stories were written, transmitted, edited, and the process managed using primarily EM and FTF, therefore the study focused on these communication modes. EM, as used at the Morning Times, included standard electronic mail, the most used method; a “notes” function to allow insertion of comments into a story, viewable by anyone browsing the story text but ignored by the typesetting computer; and the ability to compose, store, and send a message as though it were a story-in-process (to allow the user to create and save templates for repetitive, structured, and institutionalized messages). Written and typed memos played little or no observed role in the day-to-day production of the paper and were reserved solely for infrequent, formal administrative communication among the top executives. Telephone accounted for only 3.7% of the more than 1400 explicitly recorded communication events, primarily to communicate with people outside the newsroom (e.g., to talk to a reporter at home or on location). The group conducted daily scheduled meetings to coordinate and negotiate newspaper content and story placement, and communicated on an ad hoc basis throughout the publishing cycle using both EM and FTF.

Eighteen primary interviews of one-half to two hours each with numerous follow-on interviews were conducted. All phases of the publishing process were observed during a total of 110 hours. The publishing cycle consisted of three phases: planning, decision-making, and execution. The first was observed four times, the second eleven times, and the third seven times. During each, a central actor was shadowed and the time, mode, participants, and content of all observed interactions were recorded, as was an ongoing record of the context of exchange. Twelve news meetings were attended. Electronic messages were obtained directly from participants at the end of each observation period. A questionnaire was administered to the eighteen managers, and fourteen questionnaires (78%) were returned. The questionnaire, used as part of a larger study, was created and tested at a pilot site, reviewed by several editors at the Morning Times for clarity, and was modified slightly to reflect their particular jargon and publishing process before being administered.

## 5. Data analysis

5.1. Shared interpretive context and communication mode

The data suggested that the greater the shared interpretive context among communicators, the less ambiguous the exchange and the less channel richness required to support it. FTF provided the rich, interactive channel used to enact an early consensus about newsworthiness, importance, angle, etc., for upcoming stories. As the editing cycle progressed and editors began to talk about and read particular stories, shared context grew. As the process moved from editing to layout, a greater volume of terse, simple logistics and status-oriented messages were sent via EM (Figure 2), reflecting the large amount of tacitly shared background knowledge and implied meaning built earlier in the cycle.

In general, I talk face-to-face early and send messages late. For example, I keep the slots appraised about what [stories] will be moving to the A section so they can balance that with their own work. This is usually done early on using lots of face-to-face followed later on by [electronic] messaging for updates, rewrites, timing, length, and so forth. [Associate News Editor]

I use [electronic] messaging for sending simple notes and questions about facts or particular situations. For major things, I use face-to-face so you can get feedback, reasons, and explanations. [Executive editor]

I use the [electronic] message system either to impart information or to ask routine questions. I much prefer faceto-face when I don't want any room for misunderstanding. And I like the personal touch. [AME/news]

FTF was used whenever the sender wanted to employ nuance and emotion, or to influence the outcome of the interaction, for example, selling others on stories and projects.

I use face-to-face when I think that the response might not be what I want. I want to be able to argue in my behalf or to get an explanation. [AME/news]

If I want to exert influence, and I think as a means of strategy it will be better to confront the person face-to-face because my personality matches up well against his or hers, then I will do it face-to-face. [National editor]

If I want you to do something you don't want to do, I will use face-to-face. [Day national slot editor]

Electronic messaging is cold. People can take something the wrong way without the nuances of face-to-face. [State editor]

Electronic mail has no inflection or sense of humor. With electronic mail, people misinterpret you and you lose a lot of the personal aspect. [Night national slot editor]

Editors usually communicated via FTF when they needed highly interactive discourse or they anticipated requiring more than a simple one-way message or a message-response pair; e.g., the news editor negotiating the length or placement of a story with a layout editor. Simple messages tended to be factual or had a rich, shared context, and those could be sent electronically; e.g., questions about story length and status. When many back-and-forth messages were required, FTF enabled the parties to quickly converge to a consensus on meaning, decision, or action. A similar exchange would be slower with EM, and convergence, if it occurred at all, might take an exceedingly large number of messages.

[Electronic] messaging saves time, but you can get into a back-and-forth situation electronically, where one face-to-face conversation would have been faster and addressed all the issues. [Associate News Editor]

Editors often chose a communication mode to control the extent of interaction. They used rich FTF to obtain a diversity of opinion; e.g., to agree on the angle of a story or on the planned content of the paper. The inherent interactivity of FTF tended to encourage divergence, exchange of opinion, and discussion, avoiding premature closure. EM, on the other hand, would usually preclude discussion.

## 5.2. Shared procedural expectations and communication mode

It became apparent during the research that the behavioral context provided by shared routines, habits, and expectations of ongoing groups proved to be as important as shared interpretive context in enabling effective communication. These expectations ranged from explicit, particular, ad hoc agreements between two editors, to implicit enduring habits for the entire group.

Effective execution of communication procedure could be influenced in two ways: through (tacit) existing agreements on how to communicate and through discussion about how to communicate.

Communication Agreements and Rules. The factors influencing the choice of communication mode were so strongly shared that they functioned as implicit communication rules. What rendered these group rules was that all editors were aware of and agreed with them, though they fell short of being group norms because there were no sanctions for noncompliance [9]. Thus they tended to be followed, but not always consistently. Although a few editors held a personal preference for a particular mode of communication, the rules appeared to be appropriate enough and sufficiently complied with that serious breakdowns were infrequent.

Questionnaire data (Table 2) provided additional evidence for the existence of strong shared expectations about communication procedure. The editors' general agreement with the stated rules corroborated observation and interview data and the low standard deviations indicated strong agreement among themselves. Interestingly, the group agreed a bit more strongly about when to use FTF than when to use EM. EM was introduced to the newsroom seven years prior to the study and its role never explicitly planned or discussed. Rules for its use emerged and evolved over time and by trial and error, however, there still existed a margin of uncertainty and disagreement concerning its appropriate use.

Discussion of communication procedure. Although most editors individually agreed that effective communication was perhaps the most important component in producing a high quality product, communication procedure was not explicitly regarded or treated by the group as an important part of their work process. All editors interviewed stated that group communication procedure was rarely discussed, and then only when a breakdown occurred with a wide enough range of editors or an important story or problem; e.g., two desks accidentally running the same story in the paper.

Questionnaire responses (Table 3) suggest that shared communication procedures and expectations existed, were accurate and effective, and rarely discussed. The small standard deviation suggested that the group members agreed.

Communication routines. In the absence of explicit discussion or management of communication procedure, shared routine communication habits and practices were the key to effective communication. Often the routine nature of the work replaced the need to negotiate communication rules and strategies. Over time, many different situations were encountered, and expectations and procedures became well-defined and deeply ingrained – even for dealing with the unexpected, and for changing the procedures.

Routine communication occurred both via FTF and EM. Where ambiguity and equivocality were routinely encountered, FTF meetings and exchanges became institutionalized (e.g., the daily news meetings, daily informal conversations and meetings prior to the news meetings, and the routine discussions among particular editors as part of their shift transitions). EM was used as an efficient means for distributing these routine and highly context-based messages, and its use became embedded within the routines and context of the group.

## 5.3. Virtual communication

Dretske [7] suggested that a communication channel was a dependency relationship between sender and receiver that did not necessarily require an actual physical link or direct transfer of information. If person A sends the same information simultaneously to B and C, then, because of the common dependence of B and C on A, it will be possible to learn something about C by looking at B, and vice versa [23]. According to information theory, there is a channel from B to C: a “ghost” channel.

The findings suggested that “virtual” communication was used to provide an efficient communication channel among the group without having to realize each link.

When editors know that they all process the same information from the same source within a similar context (mode, timing, etc.), they are able to operate as though they had directly communicated among themselves. For example, a message routinely sent by a news editor each night to the senior day editors was read by all four early in their day; subsequent communication occurred with the tacit, shared knowledge that all had read it.

## 5.4. Communication effectiveness

Although miscommunication occasionally occurred, the editors believed that communication was generally effective. Communication breakdowns occurred several times per week, but this was not considered excessive. The editors considered the publishing process to be well-coordinated. Miscommunication Due to Lack of Shared Context. Questionnaire responses indicated that most miscommunication occurred across shifts and departments, with less when communicating within the same shift and department (Table 5). This provides additional evidence to support the proposed relationship between shared context, choice of communication mode, and communication effectiveness. According to the editors, the highest frequency of miscommunication occurred with another department on another shift.

Context was shared to a greater extent with people in the same department than with those in others, and the incidence of miscommunication varied accordingly.

Sometimes, because of the specialized nature of desks, the person being communicated to at one desk by another desk may not fully understand the import of what's being communicated, because person A, the attempted communicator, has tremendous background knowledge and person B does not – and vice versa. [National editor]

However, miscommunication might occur any time where interpretive context is not shared or well-established.

For example, I tell the news editor that I will [electronically] message him if I decide to use a particular story inside. Now I send him a message saying “You got [story]”, meaning “I’m not going to use it inside, so you can use it for page one”. However, he gets the message “I’m using it inside, I’m done with it, and I’m sending it to you to [be laid out and have specifications added]. Then he kills his plan to use it outside, I kill my plan to use it inside, and it doesn’t get used”. [National editor]

Miscommunication Due to Lack of Shared Procedural Expectations. Miscommunication related to procedural expectations was also observed. Having multiple communication modes available increases the potential for miscommunication by using an unexpected mode.

The complexity of the organization and the need for coordinating the communication process created a great dependence on complying with communication procedure. Communication and coordination were essential to insure that stories were budgeted by the appropriate desk for the appropriate section of the paper and of a length compatible with the available space. Copy flow had to be coordinated between the originating desk, the editing desk, and the news (layout) desk. All parties had to be told of changes.

There are so many editions. A story might run on page one in one edition and in the local section on another, and if the word does not get out or it is assumed to be page one in all editions, it might get left out altogether. [Associate news editor]

Not communicating what was expected or communicating to someone in an unexpected way (e.g., mode, timing, etc.) opened the possibility for miscommunication.

Some nights it seems like there is no communication. For example, I'll hear a lot of people saying "Well, you didn't tell me that", or "I didn't know that". Overall the quality of communication is good, I guess, but there are a lot of times when one of us will not tell the other what we need to know. [County bureau news editor]

The problem is we are a big organization with lots of departments. There is a lot of communication but often not all the people hear the information. [Night editor]

Similarly, editors who did not need a good story might refer it to another desk so that the story might run somewhere else in the paper. For example, a story came over the wires about the actress Kathleen Turner's involvement with a New York City social club that burned down and, based on the wire service-assigned category, was automatically routed to the editor of the People column. He alerted the night editor via EM and an FTF discussion ensued to determine whether the story should run in the People column, on page one, inside the A section as a national desk story, or in the Living Arts section.

Miscommunication by not complying with expected behavior could be as simple as forgetting to tell someone something.

If we didn't tell the city desk that Hondurans were preparing to come to Miami, they might not be prepared for the story. [Foreign editor].

As another example of miscommunication, a substitute night editor instructed a news editor to place a story in the A section. Simultaneously, the associate news editor was negotiating to place the same story in the People column, therefore the news editor decided not to run it in the A section. The People editor had no room for the story, could not run it, and neglected to inform the associate news editor. The substitute night editor neglected to follow-up on his directive. Because there was no formal procedure for coordinating these ad hoc placement decisions, the story fell through the cracks and did not run.

The above example illustrated one of the greatest weakness in communication “strategy”. Lack of formally established procedure resulted in everyone watching out for themselves, trying to stay in contact with as many people as possible, hoping to gather information from impromptu and ad hoc conversations and encounters, hoping and expecting to overhear important conversations, and monitoring as many news wires and story budgets as possible. Informal networking led to routine “procedure.”

The newsroom is too compartmentalized desk-to-desk. Communication through the grapevine is better than the formal channels. I know just from listening what is going on. [Day national slot]

While this might work in a small group or simple organization, because of the complexity of the Morning Times, it was impossible to engage in the number of exchanges required to remain fully informed. There was a tradeoff, however, because this approach continually established and renewed a strong shared interpretive context, while increasing the difficulty of living up to procedural expectations and requirements to keep in contact with all others. Thus communication tended to be meaningful and unambiguous, but occasionally unreliable.

The foreign editor described how a lack of clear and established communication procedure affected the quality of the editing process.

Bad quality may result from a lack of communication between the slot [editors] and the news desk. The [slots] do not learn what will run until about 6:30 to 7 p.m. This is a problem as they will work and plan and execute and the story may not even run. And the slots are always reacting because they never talk to the layout editors before the page is laid out. Also if they disagree on length or placement there is currently no structured process to negotiate or communicate this. For example, [the day foreign slot] may negotiate with them, or he may tell me to monitor the news desk and then I'll check in with them, or he may go to the layout editor directly and try to negotiate better placement. The news desk might respond with hostility – seeing this as interference or lack of confidence in their ability. And there is no formal mechanism for the slot to see the page before it goes down to composing.

Despite occasional miscommunication, the group's communication processes were generally effective, and most editors believed that their communication led to a high-quality paper.

## 6. Conclusion

This field study identified shared interpretive context and expectations about communication procedure as important factors which influenced how use of EM affected the performance of an ongoing work group. EM provided a means for efficiently and meaningfully exchanging information when messages were factual or placed within a well-shared interpretive context and patterns of interaction were not complex. These messages might range from simple facts to moderately rich messages depending on the extent and richness of that shared context. Many of the electronic messages were routine exchanges, and therefore took place within a shared and institutionalized behavioral context. Typically, anything requiring more than a simple exchange within a shared context would be FTF.

EM complements, rather than substitutes, for FTF interaction. The editorial group was generally aware of the different roles of EM and FTF and chose a communication mode appropriate to their need for richness and for compliance with expectations. This led to an effective communication process, enabling the group to produce a high quality paper (Figure 3).

Communication mode choices should be of central concern to work groups. EM is effective and efficient within a shared context, either pre-existing or built specifically around the communication event. To sustain effective communication, groups must be aware of their communication options and their characteristics, and accordingly choose the appropriate mode. Further, they must become aware of their entrenched communication habits and practices and be willing to discuss and thereby improve them.

This study has limitations, of course. Generalizability has been identified as a problem with case-based research. Yin [31], however, suggests that a case study be treated as an experiment. Experiments generalize to a theory and not to other experiments. Cases, likewise, should be seen as generalizing to a theory, not to other sites.

The results reported here are similar to in-depth studies of management groups performing within both ad hoc cycles and periodic cycles varying in duration (e.g., [20]). However, in all cases, computer use formed an integral part of the managers' work. Only an effective group was studied. However, studying groups which are effective but which occasionally do ineffective things may be the most efficient means to building normative theory. The analysis of miscommunication provided the exceptions that help to prove the rule, and those data generalized to the same framework.

The results provide a foundation on which to build more focused and controlled (e.g., social survey or behavioral laboratory) studies of communication context and its relationship to communication mode characteristics and communication effectiveness. Knowledge of how best to use EM and similar group communication technologies is becoming increasingly important as teams become more widely adopted as an organizing method.
