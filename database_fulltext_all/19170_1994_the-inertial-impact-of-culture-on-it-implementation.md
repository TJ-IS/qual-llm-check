# Research

# The inertial impact of culture on IT implementation

Randolph B. Cooper \*

College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, Texas 77204-6282, USA

## Abstract

Many researchers suggest that information technology (IT) implementation can be used to facilitate organizational reengineering. However, there are several factors that foster organizational inertia and inhibit such change. When an IT conflicts with an organization's culture, the implementation of that IT is resisted in two ways. The first may result in implementation failure by undermining the analysis and design process, underutilizing the system once implemented, and/or sabotaging the implemented system. The second adapts the IT during implementation or use so that conflicts with the existing culture are reduced. Both of these reduce the potential of IT implementation to engender organizational change. The impact of culture on organizational change is well known and documented in the organizational literature. This paper demonstrates some theoretical linkages among various cultures and different ITs. A theoretical case is therefore made concerning the inertial impact of culture on IT implementation.

Key words: Culture; Implementation; Socialization; User resistance; Organizational change; Reengineering; Implementation failure; Implementation success; Diffusion

## 1. Introduction

With the increasing competitiveness and hostility of organizational environments, significant organizational changes are becoming necessary for survival. The concurrent increase in computer capability has resulted in a call for implementing information technology (IT) to facilitate these changes. However, such change is difficult and risky $[30]$ because of the inertia built into organizational processes and structures $[28,49]$ . In order to take advantage of the potential of IT implementation, this inertia must be better understood.

The impact of culture as a significant source of organizational inertia is well known in the organizational development and change literature [10,66]. However, this impact of culture has largely been ignored by IT implementation researchers. This paper illustrates the potential importance of cultural inertia in both IT research and practice by:

– providing a description of organizational culture with an emphasis on issues that are important for IT implementation,

\- illustrating how culture is theoretically linked to implementation inertia through existing work in IT user resistance,

\- providing examples of culture's impact on IT implementation using an increasingly popular cultural framework that focuses on competing values.

## 2. Organizational culture

The concept of organizational culture has been borrowed from anthropology, where there is little consensus on its meaning. In accord with much of the organizational literature, we define culture as the social or normative glue that holds an organization together and that expresses the values or social ideals and beliefs which organization members come to share [68]. Some important concepts are [64,66]:

\- Shared cultural assumptions are preconscious (taken-for-granted) and are powerful, because they are less debatable and confrontable than espoused values. A person questioning cultural assumptions (e.g., business should be profitable, schools should educate) may be thought ignorant or insane. Therefore culture is hard to identify and change.

\- Culture is a group phenomenon; it is the shared assumptions that make up a culture. Though individual assumptions may lead to individuals resisting an IT, this paper focuses on the impact of assumptions shared by the group.

\- Culture is developed by a group as it learns to cope with external adaptation and internal integration. Stronger cultures develop when the same people have been coping together for a long time, when the environment has been relatively hostile, and when strong leaders have consistently emphasized the same beliefs and values. Culture therefore becomes a more important inertial agent in contexts which result in stronger cultures.

\- Though a single culture may pervade a relatively small firm, as the firm grows and becomes more geographically and functionally diverse, the potential for the existence of subcultures increases [54,75]. A cultural examination at the firm level might therefore result in the conclusion that culturally-based resistance to an IT implementation is not an important issue. This would be misleading since pockets of resistance (subcultures) may exist at the department or work group level. (To simplify further discussion, “culture” will refer to both the culture of the organization as a whole and of subcultures within the organization.)

## 3. Culture, resistance, and IT implementation

The organizational development and change literature recognizes that most significant organizational changes (in structure, job design, reward systems, etc.) will foster resistance from organizational members and fail if not accompanied by cultural changes. For example, organizational groups typically build their culture and its manifestations around their underlying technology [66]. A realignment of status, power, and working habits which can accompany the implementation of new technologies may violate some of a group's shared values and meanings, and result in culture-based resistance.

Resistance to change leading to implementation failure as identified in the IT literature includes (1) uncertainty concerning jobs, skills, etc., (2) lack of felt need, (3) potential redistribution of power and resources, (4) lack of organizational validity, and (5) lack of management support (see [31] for a review). Little mention is made of the potential of culture to promote IT implementation resistance.

However, there are some indications from IT research that culture may be important in this regard. For example, it has been found that different cultures:

\- require different kinds of information [73],

\- process information differently [71],

\- play a role in the degree of user satisfaction with decision support systems [37].

If different cultures require, process, and are satisfied with different kinds of information, then felt need and organizational validity of a specific IT may differ for different cultures. Based on the IT literature, this would lead to resistance of that IT in cultures for which there was no felt need or for which there was low organizational validity. In addition, it is argued in $[62]$ that the degree to which IT conflicts with the organizational culture can increase the likelihood of implementation failure. These authors provide examples of cultural factors of potential importance during IT implementation.

In summary, based on the organizational change and the IT literature, IT which conflicts with an organization's culture can foster resistance of organizational members to a degree that implementation fails or results in less than the desired organizational change. From a management perspective, this culture–IT implementation link is an important factor that may need to be controlled during implementation. For example, to assure implementation success, affected cultural aspects (specific assumptions) may need to be changed prior to or concurrent with IT implementation.

However, organizational culture is difficult to change because [52,66]:

\- Much of organizational culture is taken for granted and it is difficult to change things that are implicitly part of people's thinking and behavior.

\- Much organizational culture has deep historical roots.

\- Certain power groups have vested interests in the beliefs and assumptions and are unlikely to be willing to discard them.

\- Cultural changes lead to increased anxiety for group members.

Because changing an organization's culture may challenge the dominating ideology, power relationships, systems of meaning, and comfort levels, such change can be tortuous and long. Therefore, culture can act as an inertial agent.

An important implementation issue, then, involves identifying ITs which conflict with some cultures and are therefore prone to implementation failure.

## 4. A competing values view of organizational culture

Though many researchers believe that culture and its manifestations are idiosyncratic to each organization [25], there are many others who believe that there are important consistencies across cultures [20,32,44]. For example, the nature of a group's shared assumptions has been the focus of a number of psychologists who assert that individuals' interpretations of reality are limited to a small number of archetypes such as those developed by Jung. Cameron and Freeman [10] suggest that these individual-level psychological archetypes can also be used to identify types of cultures because they are based on and defined by these values, assumptions, and interpretations.

One of the better known psychological frameworks was developed by Jung [35]; it has been used to determine personality types and is valuable for identifying organizational cultures [45,46]. Quinn and Rohrbaugh [57,60] have developed a competing values framework which is an organizational analog to Jung's framework and is increasingly being used to analyze organizational culture [59,61,74,76]. This framework is used here to demonstrate the inertial impact of culture on IT implementation.

This framework emphasizes the competing tensions and conflicts inherent in human systems. Of primary importance are two major conflicts: (1) order versus flexibility and (2) demands of the internal socio-technical systems versus demands of the external environment [21]. Applying the framework to culture, Quinn and Kimberly [58] note that the framework's value orientations can be used to “explore the deep structures of organizational culture, the basic assumptions that are made about such things as the means to compliance, motives, leadership, decision making, effectiveness values, and organizational forms”.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, one dimension depicts the desired amount of organizational order versus flexibility:

\- Order: Organizational order is considered paramount, typically leading to an integration of activities and centralization of control.

\- Flexibility: Organizational flexibility is considered paramount, typically leading to a differentiating of activities and an ability to respond to change.

The other dimension depicts the focus as a desire for internal versus external view:

\- Internal: An internal focus emphasizes the maintenance of an organization's social and technical systems. The condition of these systems is considered critical to organizational performance.

\- External: An external focus emphasizes the importance of the organization's linkage to its environment. The environment is a source of threats, opportunities and resources. An appropriate linkage to the environment is a key to organizational survival.

The four quadrants (which are very similar to those proposed by Scott [67]) represent four different cultural archetypes that provide competing views regarding organizational issues, such as effectiveness, leadership, and motivation. ## 4.1. Survival

The survival culture represents an open systems view of organizations. The environment plays a significant role, impacting the organization's behavior, and is perceived as the source of materials, energy, and information vital to the organization's continuation. Organizational effectiveness is defined in terms of organizational survival, with criteria including growth, new market development, and resource acquisition. This orientation may lead to neglect or distortion of the organization's explicit goals or lead to its continued existence after such goals have been successfully attained. The environment is perceived as complex, turbulent, and politicized. To overcome and take advantage of it, leaders tend to be entrepreneurial and idealistic, willing to take risks, and able to develop a vision of the future. Key motivating factors include growth, stimulation, creativity, and variety.

Start-up firms typically have this kind of culture. Organization members are in a state of flux, with their products, financing, potential competition, etc. relatively unknown and frequently changing. Control systems are not in place and are not considered important, job descriptions are relatively unformed and are changing. In this kind of an environment the organizational culture values flexibility and adaptability, in order to react to or take advantage of opportunities.

## 4.2. Productivity

The productivity culture is based upon the rational goal model. From this viewpoint, the organization affects or reacts to its environment as a cohesive unit in an intentional optimizing manner. Effectiveness is defined in terms of production or economic goals such as profit maximization. Organizations exist because they are more efficient than the market system alone. Managers and organizational designers create formal organization structures – with varying degrees of formalization, differentiation, centralization, specialization, etc. – to deal with contextual factors such as organizational size, firm strategy, technology, environmental uncertainty, supplier power, and competition. Leaders tend to be directive, goal-oriented, instrumental, and functional. Key motivating factors include competition and the successful achievement of predetermined ends.

A firm exemplifying a productivity culture is PepsiCo. In competing with Coca-Cola, PepsiCo managers know that their jobs are on the line; there is continuous pressure to show improvement in product volume and profits. Jobs are won or lost over small differences in bottom line indicators. Nearly all decisions are driven by rational-economic considerations.

## 4.3. Stability

The stability culture is based upon the internal process model. From this viewpoint, the environment is largely ignored, with management interest focused inward upon the organization. An organization is effective to the extent that it is in control, and stable, and internally efficient. It is assumed that goals are known, tasks are repetitive, output of the production process somehow disappears, and resources in uniform qualities are available. There is “one best way” of organizing, with specialization, departmentalization, and control determined based upon some overall strategy; the creation of this strategy by policy makers is outside the model. The influence of human characteristics is reduced by focusing upon the office (rules, salary, career) rather than the individual’s behavioral characteristics. Leaders tend to be conservative and cautious, emphasizing technical issues. Key motivating factors include security, stability, and order.

Examples of stability cultures can be often found in utility companies. Operating in a regulated environments, most utilities are very hierarchical. The dominant mindset is oriented to engineering and technology. Change is often met with suspicion and resistance.

## 4.4. Human relations

This culture represents a human relations view of organizations. An organization's informal behavioral structure (interpersonal systems of power, status, communication, and friendship) rather than the formal structure (rules, positions, procedures) is emphasized. Participants have multiple interests and motives, and their individual characteristics make the organization complex. Maintenance of the organization and its human resources is the focus. Effectiveness from this orientation emphasizes the development of human potential and member commitment. Leaders tend to be participative, considerate, and supportive, and they facilitate interaction through teamwork. Key motivating factors include attachment, cohesiveness, and membership.

A firm exemplifying human relations culture is

J.C. Penney. The focus is on fairness and long-term loyalty. Employees know that if their ability to perform a job deteriorates, they will be given an appropriate alternative position rather than being fired. Profit maximization is not pursued. In fact, a manager was once warned about making too much money and thus being unfair to customers.

This competing values framework provides categories of organizational culture which combine individual-level assumptions about reality with their associated organizational counterparts. Though not the only way to view culture, these categories provide theoretically important cultural archetypes.

## 5. MIS in support of competing values cultural archetypes

Since people tend to embrace MIS that are in accord with their culture and resist MIS which conflict with their culture, a mapping of MIS to competing values is needed to further our argument. Distinctions among different kinds of MIS are made based on their capabilities, such as modeling, record keeping, interpersonal communicating, and environmental scanning. Descriptions and prescriptions in the information systems literature are therefore employed to link MIS examples with cultural archetypes, as summarized in Fig. 2.

Survival. MIS are in accord with the survival culture to the extent that they support organizational survival in a complex, turbulent, and politicized environment. MIS capabilities that are useful in this sense include the ability to scan the environment looking for problems and opportunities and filtering out extraneous information $[18,24]$ . Such scanning systems aid in handling uncertainty if there is a relatively clear notion of problems and opportunities; in contrast, these systems address equivocality if they help define previously unknown/unconsidered problems and opportunities. Inter-organization linking systems can promote organizational alliances and thus reduce environmental complexity and its associated equivocality and uncertainty $[14,34]$ . The ability to evoke doubt and argument may also be useful in that it can help destabilize organizations. This “semi-confusing” ability addresses the need to deal with equivocality and can enhance organizational adaptability and sensitivity to environmental changes $[29]$ .

Productivity. MIS are in accord with the productivity culture to the extent that they support management in facilitating organizational planning, directing, and goal setting. The primary emphasis is on uncertainty reduction: management questions are believed to be well understood and the overriding concern is with gathering information to answer those questions and reduce uncertainty. MIS capabilities that are useful include those associated with operations research, such as modeling, sensitivity analyzing, optimizing (revenue maximization, cost minimization, etc.), and forecasting. As noted by Kendall and Kriebel [36], such capabilities can be found in operations management MIS (e.g., [2,5,7,38]), in planning and control MIS (e.g., [41,48,69]), and in strategic planning MIS (e.g., [27]).

Stability. MIS are in accord with the stability culture to the extent that they support organizational measurement and control. Here, both uncertainty and equivocality are low indicating that the questions which need to be answered (and where to find the answers) are relatively well known. MIS capabilities useful here are typically accounting-oriented [15], such as internal monitoring (e.g., cost variance reporting), internal controlling (e.g., budgeting), and record keeping (e.g., general ledger) [9].

Human Relations. MIS are in accord with the human relations culture to the extent that they support member dialogue, participation, and development. Equivocality is high and uncertainty is relatively low; management must deal with multiple and diverse goals, values, etc. in an attempt to achieve cohesion and commitment. In this sense, MIS capabilities should enhance interpersonal communication and cooperation through systems such as teleconferencing, computer conferencing, electronic mail, and group decision support $[39]$ . Such technological support facilitates group processes by removing barriers to interaction, improving the group in its task, and building or reinforcing the social values of its members through successful task performance $[55]$ . These system capabilities can increase employee commitment by enabling them to participate in the organization $[7,8]$ and having them feel satisfied with the processes $[33]$ . In addition, training can be enhanced through the use of MIS with capabilities such as educating, instructing, and developing individuals and groups $[4]$ .

## 6. Support for this interpretation

The theoretical mapping is based on the author's interpretation of both the competing values framework and the information systems literature. But is this correct? In order to assess the accuracy, the MIS mapping is compared with the current thinking of MIS experts. If a cognitive mapping of MIS capabilities by MIS experts is in accord with the theoretical mapping, there is then support for the relationships.

A survey instrument was developed and first pretested with eight MIS researchers. Since the correct interpretation of short phrases is paramount, pretest respondents were chosen to provide the type of diversity currently present in the MIS academic community; respondent Ph.D. degrees were earned from five different institutions in the following diverse academic areas: Information Studies, Management Science, Industrial Engineering, Computer Science, Management Information Systems, Engineering, Psychology, and Communication Science.

Pretest data were gathered and analyzed using a multidimensional scaling technique described below. Outlier MIS capabilities were noted and respondents were interviewed in order to develop more representative phrases. The final survey instrument (see Appendix) was then used to gather experts' perceptions.

Relying on the research community to identify MIS experts, potential survey respondents were chosen based upon their membership on the executive committee of the International Conference on Information Systems, on the editorial board of MIS Quarterly, or on their position as information systems departmental editors for Management Science or Communications of the ACM. A total of 47 questionnaires were sent out. Of these, 29 were returned and usable, representing a response rate of over 60 percent. With such a high proportion of MIS experts included, the resulting data provide a highly representative MIS mapping.

A multidimensional scaling technique is used to derive the MIS experts' mapping. Though the number of respondents is relatively small, this number, combined with the number of cues, are large enough to demonstrate statistical consensus [40]. As input to this technique, perceived similarities among the MIS capabilities must be obtained. The similarity correlations are used by SPSS's multidimensional scaling procedure employing the INDSCAL algorithm to derive a model of the experts' MIS cognitive mapping. INDSCAL, developed by Carroll and Chang [11], assumes that respondents' judgments of similarity depend on the Euclidean distances between stimuli in an underlying psychological space common to all participants. The models' average correlation of .84 compares very favorably with those found in other applications of the INDSCAL procedure to complex stimuli. Comparing this to the theoretical mapping, we find considerable agreement. The non-parametric sign test [17] supports this accord with a p value of less than .001 (N = 15 and Z = 3.33). Only one capability (optimizing) is not in agreement. This similarity between the theoretical mapping and the experts' cognitive structure supports the theoretical association of MIS capabilities with cultural archetypes.

## 7. Inertial impact of culture on IT implementation

Resistance to IT (capabilities) leads to two forms of inertia. The first can result in implementation failure by undermining the analysis and design process, underutilizing the system once implemented, and/or sabotaging the implemented system by, for example, inputting incorrect data $[6,22,43]$ . The second involves adapting the IT during implementation or use such that conflicts with the existing culture are reduced.

## 7.1. Resistance leading to failure

IT that conflicts with a culture will tend to be resisted by organizational members causing implementation problems or failure. The structure of the competing values framework enables an analysis of this conflict. For example, survival and productivity share the same end of the internal-external dimension. As a result, their cultural assumptions have more in common (interest with the environment) than survival and stability, which are on opposite ends of both dimensions. Therefore, an IT which is in accord with one culture tends to conflict more with a culture at the opposite ends of both dimensions than a culture sharing the same end of a dimension.

The potential for culture-MIS conflict can be determined using the theoretical mapping. For example, an implementation of control-oriented systems such as cost variance and budgeting MIS would be welcomed by managers from a stability culture because of the emphasis on internal order and control. In contrast, these systems would be seen as a threat to managers from a survival culture because of the attendant reduction in flexibility that they feel is necessary for organizational adaptability and creativity. In addition, the internal focus of these MIS would tend to be viewed negatively by survival culture managers due to the resources wasted on internal matters when they know that the environment is key to survival. This relationship is symmetric in that MIS in accord with the survival culture would tend to be resisted by managers in a stability culture. For example, the implementation of doubt and argument promoting systems that are in accord with a survival culture would be resisted by stability culture managers.

Less severe conflicts occur between quadrants which share the same end on one dimension. For example, doubt and argument promoting systems designed to foster environmental sensitivity are in accord with survival cultures. This emphasis on promoting argument (participation, discussion) among organizational members would be viewed positively by managers from a human relations culture while the emphasis on the environment would be viewed positively by managers in a productivity culture. Thus, managers from these two cultures would be less resistant to such MIS than managers from a stability culture.

Although MIS have been used as examples, the competing values framework is generalizable to other types of IT. For example, when mapping transactions systems, decision support systems, or strategic information systems into the competing values framework, attention should be paid to the impact of the IT on organizational order versus flexibility and whether it focuses on internal or external issues. This mapping should then be confirmed with an accord between the IT and the associated quadrant's notion of effectiveness, leadership, and motivation. However, it may not be sufficient to map a general classification of IT (e.g., decision support systems). Rather, attention should be paid to the social and electronic constraints that will accompany its implementation.

## 7.2. IT adaptation

A major feature distinguishing IT from other types of technology is its adaptability, which can result in the “same system” being implemented with very different forms and functions in different organizations $[12,13,47,51]$ . An organization’s culture can play an important role in defining this form and function $[56]$ . This can be viewed in a positive light in that an IT’s adaptation to the existing culture increases the chances that the IT will be implemented and used $[72]$ . However, when viewing IT implementation as a facilitator of organizational change, this adaptation takes on a negative cast.

As an example of IT's potential for cultural adaptation, take the implementation of an electronic mail system. The typical placement of such a system would be in a human resource culture: it would facilitate member participation and free flowing dialogue by breaking down communication barriers due to its asynchronous nature (members do not have to make appointments to communicate), its lack of geographic constraints (members do not have to be in the same place to communicate), the ease with which many people can be addressed using mailing lists, etc. However, the same system can be adapted through electronic and social controls resulting in conflict with the human resource culture and in an accord with any of the other three cultures. For example (uncited examples are based on [1]),

\- Stability: Communication can be constrained in order to control and enforce the organizational hierarchy through social controls. For example, the president of a large manufacturing company received messages from an employee a couple of levels below him. The president responded with an open electronic mail message to all employees indicating that such communication is against company policy and that the chain of command should be followed. Communication constraints can also be instituted electronically. For example, electronic mail systems allow control of: (1) communication direction, so that information can flow down but not up the hierarchy, (2) replies to messages, so that some people cannot reply while others can, (3) the flow of messages, so that only certain people receive messages, and (4) certain mail addresses, making them inaccessible.

\- Productivity: Communication can be constrained in order to enhance organizational productivity. For example, Citibank and United Parcel Service use such social controls by informing their employees that electronic mail communication will be monitored for non-business communication [50]. Electronic constraints can be instituted to reduce information overload (and increase productivity) through, for example, the employment of intelligent filters. A large oil company uses electronic mail to send filtered information obtained from external databases to its employees. Such filtering can be done at the firm level (as with the oil company) or at the employee level [42].

\- Survival: Communication can be used to enhance adaptability and sensitivity to the environment. For example, a large oil company has a system in which variations in the price of oil instantaneously trigger electronic mail messages to appropriate employees.

Note that such adaptation does not have to result from overt electronic or social controls. All technologies are knowledge-based; part of the knowledge required to use a technology is built into it by its creator and part of the knowledge resides in the mind of the user (e.g., a stick used as a club or a screwdriver used as a lever). Thus, a group's culturally-based values and norms can condition an IT's usage without any added social or electronic constraints.

## 8. Concluding remarks

There are many factors that can engender user resistance to IT implementation, resulting in implementation failure or in the IT's adaptation to the existing social and technical context. Neither result bodes well for the significant IT-facilitated changes being proposed to reengineer organizations into more competitive entities. Cultural conflict is a resistance factor which is hard to remedy. Though culture can be changed, it typically is a long term evolutionary project or is precipitated by internal or external crisis. In the short term, without such perceived crises, culture should be viewed as a stable structure that constrains organizational change. Note, however, that cultural conflict does not arise when changes occur without violating the basic assumptions held by a group. It is only when these basic assumptions are violated that culturally-based resistance becomes important.

The purpose of this paper is to raise culture as an important implementation issue by forging the link between culture and information technology. To this end theoretical evidence is provided that different MIS capabilities are more in accord with values and assumptions underlying some cultures rather than others. Based on cultural research in the organizational literature and user resistance work in the information systems literature, this theoretical accord and conflict of different MIS with different cultures provides significant support for the potential inertial impact of culture on information technology implementation.

With this cultural intransigence, it is important for managers contemplating IT-facilitated change to evaluate the chances of cultural conflict. This evaluation can be accomplished through in-depth case studies and quantitative instruments. The primary requirement for the use of quantitative instruments is the development of theoretically well-grounded constructs that have been shown empirically to be generalizable or generic across situations or organizational settings $[63]$ .

The competing values framework fulfils this primary requirement. It is firmly based in both psychological and organizational theory, and provides contrasting views of organizational reality. In addition, the validity and reliability of competing values instruments have been repeatedly shown to be valid and reliable across a variety of organizational contexts.

The simplicity of the competing values instruments adds to the theoretical and empirical reasons favoring the competing values approach. Cultural evaluations can be obtained from a sixteen question survey (four questions per archetype) administered to members of an organization. For example, the instrument developed by Quinn determines the relative emphasis on human relations assumptions by asking about the degree of: (1) participation and open discussion, (2) empowerment of employees to act, (3) assessment of employee concerns and ideas, and (4) teamwork and cohesion.

The fact that the competing values framework has a strong theoretical foundation and has a simple, valid, and reliable instrument does not imply that its four archetypes are conceptually exhaustive. There are many other conceptual frameworks which are based in theory (e.g., Maslow's need hierarchy) and which attend to different cultural concepts, such as notions of time, security, and satisfaction. In addition, the four competing values archetypes may not be fine enough to distinguish among an organization's subcultures; the competing values approach is important and useful and may be employed in conjunction with other approaches to determine the potential for cultural conflict during IT implementation.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the ethical issues surrounding managing cultural change. The position of this paper has been that someone (presumably in power) has decided that significant IT-facilitated organizational change is required (e.g., to meet the demands of competition). If that change does not conflict with existing cultural assumptions, then culturally-based resistance will not be an implementation issue. However, if such culturally-based resistance is expected, then ethical issues may arise concerning, for example, who's culture should be changed, who should determine what cultural changes to make, whether it is ethical to make such changes, and how the changes should be implemented.

## MIS types

A. Survival – The organization is confronted with a complex, turbulent, and politicized environment. In order to increase the chances for organizational survival and growth, management promotes organizational insight, innovation, and adaptation. The MIS supports this management focus.

B. Productivity – The organization affects or reacts to its environment as a cohesive unit in an intentional optimizing manner. Key management activities in pursuit of profit maximization include planning, directing, and goal setting. The MIS supports this management focus.

C. Stability – The environment is largely ignored, with management interest focused inward attempting to maintain the stability and equilibrium of the organization. In order to attain these goals, managers focus on measurement, documentation, and information management. The MIS supports this management focus.

D. Human Relations – Maintenance of the organization and its human resources is key, emphasizing cohesive relationships and individual commitment and contribution. In pursuit of these goals, managers promote employee dialogue, participation, and training. The MIS supports this management focus.
