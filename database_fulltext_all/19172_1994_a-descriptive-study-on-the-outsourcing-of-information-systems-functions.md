# A descriptive study on the outsourcing of information systems functions

Varun Grover \*, Myun Joong Cheon, James T.C. Teng

College of Business Administration University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC 29212, USA

## Abstract

A few years ago some major outsourcing contracts were signed by large corporations like Eastman Kodak and General Dynamics. Since then the trade literature in particular has examined the outsourcing of Information Systems (IS) functions as an important and growing trend. This paper attempts to provide a systematic overview of the outsourcing phenomenon. IS outsourcing is viewed from a definitional and an evolutionary perspective. It is argued that outsourcing is not new, but its nature has evolved over decades. An exploratory study of 188 firms is reported; it provides insight into the types of IS functions being outsourced and the relationship between outsourcing and certain demographics: size, industry and information intensity. The results suggest that while system-operations remains the predominant function outsourced, other functions are also being performed by external service providers. Further, industry and information intensity have some influence on the extent of outsourcing of certain functions.

Key words: Information systems outsourcing; Demographics; Application development; Systems operations; Telecommunications; Systems planning; End user support; Technology management

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, an increasing amount of attention has been paid to outsourcing of information systems (IS) functions. A recent survey of IS senior executives highlights outside services management as one of the six strategic management issues confronting organizations in their management of corporate systems [6]. Over the last few years, the new strategic role of outsourcing in business firms has been given much coverage in trade publications; e.g., Computerworld, Datamation, Network World, and Information Week. Trying to remain competitive and up-to-date is becoming a financial burden to many organizations. This is true particularly in fields such as banking and financial services, health care, and manufacturing. Hiring outsiders to handle part or even all of its IS often helps an organization provide better services and maintain a competitive advantage.

Given the increasing pervasiveness of IS outsourcing, this study seeks to examine the outsourcing phenomenon in a systemic manner. Specifically, the study explores the nature and evolution of outsourcing, the extent of IS functions being outsourced, and whether this profile is related to size, industry type, and information intensity.

(2) Facilities Management Outsourcing is selected when there is a shortage of specialized and competent IS staff necessary to operate complex IS data centers. (3) Systems Integration Outsourcing involves medium levels of internalization of technological and human resources. In many cases, a single service provider – the systems integrator – is contracted to manage the installation and operation of the integrated systems and assumes the overall responsibility for the quality and performance of the multi-vendor environment. (4) Time-Sharing Outsourcing was practised in the late 1950s with mainframe computers. Time-sharing was popular for many companies (e.g., banks and other financial services firms); most of these were small and could not afford mainframe computers to run occasional batch data processing jobs. Ownership of physical IS resources by the service receiver is kept to a minimum. The receiver's IS personnel simply buy computer time from the service provider in order to perform the relevant tasks. GEICO was a company that primarily provided this kind of service.

(5) Other Types of Outsourcing. Rental contracts may involve low to medium levels of the internalization of technological resources and are selected when there is a shortage of specialized and advanced technological resources required to perform IS activities. Installation/procurement contracts involve medium levels of internalization and are selected when there is a necessity to install and procure the information systems. Maintenance/programming contracts involve a low level of internalization of human resources and are used on a one-time basis to meet a particular system development or maintenance need that cannot be suitably met by the internal IS human resources. These types of contracts are used extensively by US Government organizations.

## 2.2. Outsourcing trends

Outsourcing is really not a new phenomenon; its roots can be traced to the traditional timesharing and professional services of the late 1950s. Today outsourcing has become a valid option in all areas of IS service. For example, certain industries – like thrift institutions – have long purchased timesharing and facilities management services. Other companies have dismantled their data center and have now contracted out all data processing and programming.

However, the nature of outsourcing has evolved. Compared to the 1970's, current outsourcing practices differ [3,32]. While smaller companies have often turned to outsourcing as a way to obtain IS services not available or feasible internally [22], larger companies with mature IS departments are also outsourcing. Larger companies may then improve their IS, because they are able to accept the separation of data ownership from data processing, acknowledge the technical sophistication of service providers, and concentrate their resources on efforts to produce high added value. Also, as indicated in Fig. 2, applications packages, contract programming, and specific processing services comprised the major portion of all IS services outsourced in 1970s, while systems integration, application management, and systems operation are the major IS services outsourced in 1990s. Current outsourcing practice allows a firm to outsource proactively and thereby choose specific IS functions to be outsourced based on need.

Traditionally, outsourcing service providers assumed no management responsibility, even when they took over a part of the business. Today growing ranks of service providers are willing and eager to take on such responsibility $[35]$ and the market is becoming intensely competitive. Further, the business relationship between the outsourcing service receiver and the service provider is increasingly that of a partnership rather than merely that of customer and vendor [31]. It is considered as part of the broader context of “information partnership”, as described by Konsynski and McFarlan [23].

The key trend of today's outsourcing strategy is the shift in focus from technology to information utilization and management. Technology intensity makes it harder and harder for companies to acquire the employees they need. The high costs of putting together large, perhaps worldwide, networks make it harder for them to acquire capital. From this perspective, outsourcing can be seen as the evolution of basic data processing services from independent and unique department to a utility-like service, similar to telephone or electrical services. Rather than spend time and resources building an internal infrastructure, an organization concentrates its efforts on the effective use of information and the creation of new analytical data with which they can improve management's responsiveness to organizational needs [8].

## 3. Making outsourcing decisions

Since outsourcing presents an alternative way of providing IS services, it can be more or less attractive, depending on the company's unique needs and circumstances [17,32]. A rational decision to outsource is therefore contingent upon careful examination of needs. Both positive and negative aspects of outsourcing must be carefully considered.

## 3.1. Advantages of outsourcing

The factors contributing to the attractiveness and growth of outsourcing are classified into three major categories: strategic, economic, and technological [9,16,20,21,28].

## (1) Strategic Factors

(a) Focus on the Core Business. Since companies are often distracted from their fundamental strategic thrusts in the marketplace by the ongoing impediments associated with an increasingly complex IS infrastructure, outsourcing allows companies to refocus their business efforts towards higher level issues.

(b) Improved Focus on Strategic Use of IT. Since IS departments have a group of experienced and efficient development professionals, outsourcing allows management to focus available IS talent on IT activities that promote competitiveness rather than routine activities of systems maintenance or operations. Furthermore, outsourcing can improve flexibility and responsiveness in meeting market needs and internal requirements. Quick response in this area is becoming a strategic necessity of time-based competition.

(c) Enhanced IT Competence. Since IT as a source of competitive advantage pervades the entire value chain [30], outsourcing allows the service receiver to leverage the competence of one or more service providers without internalizing all the required competence.

(d) Enhanced IS Staff Expertise. Because of the specialized nature of IT, the staffing of qualified personnel is critical, especially in times of significant technological change. Outsourcing allows the service receiver to transfer this problem to the service provider, who is in a better position to select, train, and manage IT personnel.

## (2) Economic Factors

(a) Economies of Scale and Scope. The challenge for IS management today is to accomplish more with less resources. This pressure is seen as the most significant factor driving today's corporate interest. An outsourcing service provider might be in a position to exploit economies of scale in areas of hardware, software, and staff, since it pools projects from many service receivers. Similarly, the service provider is able to exploit economies of scope, since a single service provider carries out a variety of IT tasks, a capability that may not be possible for any one service receiver. (b) Cost Containment and Predictability. With the escalating levels of IT investments [33,36], there are increasing pressures to move away from a fixed, corporate overhead towards a more direct variable cost approach to controlling the IT operations. Outsourcing provides an unambiguous approach to arriving at a detailed cost structure of IT operations. The costs become predictable for the service receiver, since the responsibility of cost overruns is often placed on the service provider [7].

## (3) Technological Factors

(a) Access to Leading-Edge Technology. Outsourcing allows the service receiver to gain immediate access to otherwise unavailable state-of-the-art technology. This increases the receiver's competitiveness in delivering products or services.

(b) Avoidance of Obsolescence Risk. Due to the accelerated changes in the nature of the IT infrastructure, the risk of obsolescence is high [5]. Outsourcing allows the service receiver to move the obsolescence risk to the service provider: the service provider has the ability to diversify these risks across a broad range of service receivers. However, long term contracts might, in fact, spread the risk back to the receiver!

## 3.2. Disadvantages of outsourcing

There are some drawbacks to outsourcing. These may make outsourcing unattractive to potential receivers and lead to user resistance $[19,26]$ .

## (1) Managerial Factors

(a) Loss of Flexibility or Control. From the point of view of IS executives, outsourcing reduces real or perceived control over both the quality of software and the timetable of a project since the work is now being carried out by people not under direct supervision.

(b) Threat to Long Term Career Prospects. With outsourcing, the service receiver often finds that many IS professionals do not find suitable jobs or promising career paths in other areas of the corporation. In fact, addressing the personnel displacement caused by outsourcing discourages many IS executives from evaluating the option objectively. In practice, however, there are several alternatives, including personnel layoff, retraining, and absorption of displaced personnel into the payroll of the service provider.

## (2) Cost Related Factors

(a) Increased coordination costs. Outsourcing may require increasing time to communicate and coordinate with the service provider: typically, in formal meetings. The costs of negotiating and monitoring the outsourcing contract are potentially wide ranging, indirect, and substantial.

(b) Hidden or Potential Costs. Additional costs may be incurred in releasing or transferring employees, in license transfer by software vendors, and in re-negotiating contracts [13,14].

## (3) Conflicting Interest Factors

(a) Profit-Motivated Service Provider. In some cases, the profit motive of the service provider might not be in the best interests of the service receiver. Some service providers are in the business of maximizing their profit at any cost; this could run counter to a service receiver's interest. (b) Systems and Data Confidentiality. Since the service provider may offer a customized but similar system to several service receivers, with some direct competitors, concern arises over data security.

The advantages and disadvantages suggest that outsourcing might not necessarily be a panacea for all organizations. With the trend toward functional outsourcing, it is possible that different organizations might find it beneficial to outsource selected IS functions.

## 4. A descriptive study of functional outsourcing

Our study assesses the extent to which various IS functions are being outsourced and whether this differs across demographic factors such as size, industry, and information intensity. While multi-million dollar contracts pioneered by companies like Eastman Kodak and General Dynamics involve complete outsourcing of all IS functions, many companies are choosing the functions they wish to outsource. Therefore, it is appropriate to examine the components of outsourcing. Five functions have potential for outsourcing: function 1: applications development and maintenance; function 2: systems operations; function 3: telecommunications and networks management; function 4: end user support; and function 5: systems planning and management [4,27,29]. Fig. 3 shows the relationships being examined.

## 4.1. Instrument

Data were collected via a mail survey questionnaire. It should be noted that this was a part of a larger study on outsourcing. Only items relevant to this study are described here. Three demographic type variables were measured in the instrument. Size was assessed using several measures: sales revenue, number of employees, and number of IS employees. Industry was assessed using a categorical scale. Information Intensity was measured by the IS Budget/Sales indicator, originally proposed by Nolan as a measure of IS maturity and subsequently used in other studies [e.g., 12,18].

A continuous measure (degree of outsourcing) is a superior indicator to a dichotomous measure. Outsourcing of IS functions, the dependent variable in the research model, refers to the difference between the current outsourcing budget and that three years earlier for overall outsourcing and for each constituent IS function. By using a differential measure, we can focus on the proliferation of contemporary outsourcing that occurred after Eastman Kodak and General Dynamics signed mega-contracts rather than the older facilities management that was prevalent in the 1970s.

The outsourcing budget in each case is expressed as a percentage of the firm's total IS budget. Budgets were assessed for the five IS functions. Both the present and earlier outsourcing budgets are calculated as follows: the percentage of the total IS budget allocated for a function is multiplied by the percentage of that function's budget allocated for outsourcing. The sum for all five functions becomes the measure of the degree of outsourcing. Subtracting the sum of three years ago from the present sum yields the change in overall outsourcing budget (COB). The difference was also computed in the case of each IS function; this represents the change in outsourcing for each function.

## 4.2. Data collection

The questionnaire instrument was first reviewed in personal interviews with thirteen practising professionals in the IS area; seven were top IS executives. Feedback was evaluated and changes were made after each meeting. In order to maximize the response rate for the final mailing, the steps suggested by researchers $[10,34]$ were followed. These included addressing respondents by name (names were obtained from recent directories), immediate follow-up on undelivered questionnaires by calling companies for correct addresses or names, and a follow-up mailing three weeks after the first mailing. The unit of analysis for this research is an organization, which may be a corporation, a business unit, a subsidiary, or a division served by an IS department.

One thousand companies received the survey questionnaire. It was addressed to the top executive in charge of Information Systems. The names were obtained from the Spring 1992 edition of the Directory of Top Computer Executives published by Applied Computer Research, Inc. One hundred and eighty eight usable responses were

## 5.1. Responding sample characteristics

The industry representation of respondent companies, as shown in Table 1, indicates that a large proportion of these companies are manufacturers, financial institutions (banking, insurance, etc.) and healthcare. ## 5.2. Validity of the outsourcing measures

Variables with computational measures are evaluated for their internal consistency through correlation with alternative measures. Since the change in degree of outsourcing is measured by personal assessment of the respondent, alternative measures were added to the instrument. These measures are single questions asking for the outsourcing budget, expressed as a percentage of the firm's total IS budget, both currently and three years ago. The resulting correlations between the computed measures (i.e., estimates summed over IS functions) and the overall assessments range from 0.85 to 0.90 (p < 0.001) indicating validity of the measures.

It is apparent that applications development and maintenance and systems operations are the two most outsourced IS functions. Systems operations, which represents 36% of the increase in outsourcing, includes mainframe and mini computer operations for daily processing runs, backup and recovery procedures, and systems software maintenance. This type of outsourcing is more reflective of the earlier “facilities management” days and represents a generic type of outsourcing. Companies are realizing that “reinventing the wheel” through in-house development may not always be appropriate. Numerous service providers specializing in certain software by industry or application facilitate application development through outsourcing.

We suspect that the outsourcing of end-user support and especially telecommunications (increases of 16% and 17% respectively) is a growing phenomenon. The proliferation of networks in the past decade, the shortage of experienced personnel in telecommunications and the availability of large common carrier services in the US (like AT&T, MCI, Sprint, etc.) makes it economical and sensible to outsource. The variety of network solutions offered by third party providers should dramatically increase. For instance, solutions to meet specific industry needs and network integration services to integrate disparate technology platforms should be highly valued. Also, higher bandwidth digital networks for data, voice, and video applications will require specialized services for network design, implementation, and ongoing management. Similarly, while end user computing is growing, outsourcing of end user support might increase among IS departments who would rather not deal with the “non-conventional" services involved in user education, training, and consulting.

## 5.4. Demographic variables and outsourcing

The three industries with the highest representation in our sample were manufacturing, finance, and healthcare. To observe whether the degree of outsourcing differs across industries, Tukey's tests were conducted for mean differences for each pair of industries. The results are illustrated in Table 3.

Within the parameters of the existing sample, healthcare seems to be outsourcing more of its budget for every IS function. The differences are significant between healthcare and manufacturing for outsourcing of telecommunications, end user support, and overall outsourcing. This would suggest that the healthcare industry (which consists here mainly of hospitals) are relying more on external vendors for their telecommunications infrastructure and information center operations than the average manufacturing concern. In fact, end user support for hospitals outsourced at a significantly higher level than both manufacturing and finance firms. Hospitals, while not traditionally strong in the IS area seem to be looking outward for two of the more progressive areas of IS rather than developing expertise in-house. In contrast, both manufacturing and especially finance firms have a stronger tradition in IS and can rely more on in-sourcing. These trends may change as outsourcing decisions become more closely aligned with corporate need, and the vendor environment becomes more specialized and competitive.

Table 4 provides Pearson's correlations between the various size and information intensity measures and the degree of functional IS outsourcing. What seems remarkable is the absolute lack of correlation (magnitudes close to 0) between size measures and any kind of outsourcing. This strongly confirms our prior assertions that unlike a decade ago, outsourcing is not the providence of smaller firms. Larger firms are also looking toward external service providers, even though IS resources might be available in-house. The mega contracts signed by Eastman Kodak and General Dynamics are testimony to this.

Interestingly, information intensity (measured as the ratio of IS Budget to Sales) exhibits two significant relationships between outsourcing of end user support and system planning and management. However, the relationship between the number of IS employees, which presumably comprise a significant portion of the IS budget, and these IS functions is close to zero. Perhaps the IS budget/sales is really referring to the ratio of infrastructural expenses (i.e., hardware and software) to sales. If this is true, the first correlation indicates that companies spending more on IS infrastructure relative to sales tend to outsource their end user support function. The newer infrastructures tend to be microcomputer (e.g., client server) oriented. ## 6. Conclusion

The objective of this paper was to: (1) provide an evolutionary perspective of IS outsourcing, (2) review the outsourcing decision in terms of its advantages and disadvantages, and (3) provide preliminary empirical data on IS functional outsourcing and the effects of certain demographic factors.

IS outsourcing is not a new phenomenon; however its nature has evolved over the years. The results confirm our assertions that size is no longer a factor in determining whether to outsource. There is also support for our contention that a variety of IS functions are being outsourced as opposed to only traditional IS operations. While the predominant function remains operations, applications, end-user support, and telecommunications are also being performed by external service providers. Such findings might indicate some degree of maturity in making outsourcing decisions where every decision is examined on its contingent merits rather than as a result of a prevailing trend. With the proliferation of vendors and their increasing specialization by industry, function, and application, the parameters surrounding this decision might change. Different industries may then pursue different levels of outsourcing. Our results indicate a stronger trend toward outsourcing in the healthcare industry, particularly in telecommunications and end-user support.

The decision to outsource should be evaluated strategically based on technological, economic and strategic advantages. Concurrently, management problems, potential hidden costs and motives of the service provider should be evaluated. It might be advantageous to turn over information center operations to an external provider (which seems to be occurring more in companies with larger IT investments), however, if these applications are critical to the future of the firm then viewing them as a “nuisance” that should be outsourced might be a mistake.

Finally, it should be noted, that the analysis was based on a 19% response rate. Although non-response bias was estimated, it should be recognized that the potential of sample frame error exists. Also, the scope of this study was restricted to the demographic variables of size, industry and information intensity. To determine guidelines for outsourcing decisions would require analysis of a number of rich contextual variables and their effect on outsourcing (e.g., management risk taking propensity, industry practices, vendor aggressiveness, perceptions of in-house IS, role of IT in the organization) as well as a measure of the “effectiveness of outsourcing”.
