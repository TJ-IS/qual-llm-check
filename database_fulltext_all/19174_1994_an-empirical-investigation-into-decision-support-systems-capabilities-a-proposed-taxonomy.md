# An empirical investigation into decision support systems capabilities: A proposed taxonomy \*\*

J. Michael Pearson $^{*}$ , J.P. Shim $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, Kansas State University, Manhattan, KS 66506, USA

$^{b}$ Department of Management and Information Systems, Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS 39762, USA

## Abstract

The capabilities provided by decision support systems (DSS) can have a significant impact on the usefulness and satisfaction provided by a DSS. A questionnaire was sent to nonacademic members of the Institute of Management Sciences in an effort to identify characteristics and capabilities common to DSS. Analysis indicated that five different categories of DSS could be identified. Each of these categories provided the DSS user with a unique set of capabilities ranging from simple data retrieval to a fully integrated DSS. Analysis also indicated that the five DSS categories identified in this study mapped easily onto Alter's DSS taxonomy. By utilizing Alter's taxonomy and the results obtained in this study, the DSS builder is provided a starting point for the development of future DSS.

Key words: Decision Support Systems; Taxonomy; DSS development

## 1. Introduction

The development of decision support systems (DSS) is a complex process that has been investigated by many researchers $[2,3,4,6,7,8,13,18,21,29,33]$ . Much of this DSS research can be classified into one of three distinct areas. The first group of studies $[1,9,15,20,23,24]$ has primarily focused on whether DSS have actually improved decision quality and/or decision performance.

Sharda, Barr, and McDonnell [31] found that most of the studies in this area support the premise that DSS improve decision quality and/or decision effectiveness.

The second area of DSS research $[9,11,17,26,28]$ has been directed towards identifying specific design characteristics and the impact they have on DSS development. Topics investigated have included the influence of different presentation formats, the use of color, the impact of different graphic capabilities, and the influence of different user interfaces. Analysis has generally provided mixed results as to the impact these factors have on DSS and decision-making effectiveness.

The third group of studies [5,14,22,27,30,34] has addressed the role of the decision-maker and how differences between individuals can influence the effectiveness of DSS. Specific characteristics investigated include cognitive biases, novice/expert effects, and cognitive process. While studies generally have suggested these factors are important, the results have been contradictory as to the direction of the significance.

Each of these areas of research has contributed to the general understanding of DSS and the factors that influence how decisions are made. However, partly as a result of this focus on decision-making effectiveness and design parameters, there has been very little empirical research as to the types of DSS being developed. (1) File drawer systems - allows simple inquiries about data in a data file.

(2) Data analysis systems - allows the manipulation of data by means of commands tailored to the task and setting or commands of a general nature.

(3) Analysis information systems - provides access to a series of databases and small models.

They found that their respondents were able to classify their particular DSS into the seven categories proposed by Alter [2]. Huff et al. [16] concluded that the taxonomy proposed by Alter provided an exhaustive classification scheme for DSS.

These and other studies have provided insights into the different tasks that DSS perform. They have not, however, identified whether specific combinations of DSS capabilities have developed to meet the requirements of these tasks. The primary objectives of this study are to (1) determine if specific combinations of DSS capabilities can be identified, (2) present a taxonomy based on capabilities provided by DSS, and (3) compare the proposed taxonomy with the DSS taxonomy developed by Alter [2]. The taxonomy presented in this study has practical application for the DSS builder, since the different categories should provide a starting point in the design of future DSS.

## 2. Study background

During the early 1970's, DSS emerged as a practical approach for applying computers and information to the decision problems faced by management. These early DSS were different from previous computerized systems in that emphasis was on decision-making effectiveness rather than operational efficiency. The late 1970's saw the DSS movement begin to emphasize interactive computer-based systems which helped decision-makers utilize databases and models to solve semi-structured and unstructured problems. The 1980's saw computer-based systems employing a variety of new technologies to improve the effectiveness of managerial and professional decision-makers. Emphasis within DSS development shifted towards providing the decision-maker with balanced support in the areas of decision-making, design, and implementation of DSS [19].

During the past two decades, there has been considerable disagreement as to what specifically constitutes DSS $[2,6,12,19,21,28,33]$ . Currently, there seems to be a consensus that DSS is composed of three interrelated components: the data management, the model management, and the dialogue management components. Each component provides specific capabilities to the decision-maker and improves the effectiveness with which he/she works. For example, capabilities provided by the data management component should include:

\- the capture and/or extraction of data into a DSS database

\- the storage, retrieval, and control of data handled by a database management system (DBMS)

\- the interaction with data from several sources

\- the ability to perform ad hoc queries

\- the tracking of the use of data within the DSS

\- the management of data through a data dictionary.

The model management component of DSS provides the user with a set of capabilities that differentiate it from other traditional computer-based systems. These capabilities include:

\- the use of multiple models to support diverse problems

\- the support of semi-structured and unstructured problems

\- the ability to build models easily and quickly

\- the ability to track models through a model directory

\- the integration of models within the DSS

\- the creation, retrieval, and storage of models handled by a model base management system.

The dialogue management component provides the mode of interaction between the user and DSS. A great deal of research has focused on the interaction process between the user and the computer. The results suggest that a well-developed interface should include the following capabilities:

\- the support of multiple dialogue styles

\- the capture, storage, and analysis of dialogue usage through a dialogue management system

\- the tracking of dialogue usage by DSS users

\- the interaction with the model and data components of the DSS

\- the support of multiple methods of presenting output.

## 3. Method of study

The study used a questionnaire developed from previous surveys and DSS literature $[2,4,6,9,12,16,25,33]$ . The questionnaire was mailed to 1613 randomly selected nonacademic members of The Institute of Management Science (TIMS). This group was selected because of their interdisciplinary nature and their expressed interest in computers and the decision-making process. Each questionnaire included a cover letter, definitions page, and the questionnaire. The definitions page contained words (including the term “DSS”) that could possibly be misunderstood by the respondent. The questionnaire was composed of three parts [see Appendix A]. The first part contained 24 items designed to identify the operating environment in which the DSS exists and 7 items used to classify the respondent’s DSS into the taxonomy proposed by Alter [2]. The second part of the questionnaire contained 20 items that solicited information about the specific capabilities of the DSS used by the respondent. Each item in the first two parts of the questionnaire had a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The third part of the questionnaire contained 13 questions concerning demographics of the respondent.

## 4. Results of data analysis

The sample base that resulted was 273 respondents (16.9% response rate). Not all returned questionnaires were usable since many were from individuals not current/past users of DSS. The mailing provided 158 usable questionnaires. Non-response bias was examined by comparing results obtained from the survey with known values of the population.

Table 1 provides the demographics associated with the respondents who participated in this study. The majority of the individuals are well-educated males who work as “middle” managers. The respondents are employed in functional areas such as management science/operations research (MS/OR), marketing, research and development, finance, and management information systems (MIS).

The respondents varied significantly in the frequency with which they used DSS (daily, weekly, or monthly) and were typically satisfied with the DSS performance. Most of the respondents also took part in the design, construction, and/or implementation of their DSS. It was interesting to note that individuals who did not participate in one or more stages of the development process were generally less satisfied than those who had participated.

## 4.1. Tests of validity and reliability

Both parts of the questionnaire were checked for content and construct validity. Individuals familiar with the DSS concept were given a draft of the questionnaire and asked their opinion as to coverage and clarity. This evaluation process continued until these individuals and the researchers were satisfied the questionnaire provided a fair representation of dimensions relevant to the DSS area. Factor analysis and inter-item correlations were also used to determine how well the research instrument measured the intended dimensions. The results of this analysis indicated the questions factored as anticipated and that inter-item correlations performed as expected.

Cronbach's coefficient alpha was calculated for the three factors identified in the second part of the questionnaire (database, model, and dialogue management components). The three factors proved to be reliable measures of the intended dimensions [see Table 2]. An overall alpha was calculated for this part of the questionnaire and was .8463.

## 5. A DSS taxonomy

A correlation-based clustering procedure (Ward's minimum variance algorithm) was used to classify the respondents' DSS according to capabilities provided by the database, model, and dialogue management components. Analysis indicated that a five-cluster solution was most appropriate. The selection of this solution was based on a three-step process which included (1) comparing the initial cluster solution with clusters obtained using the complete linkage algorithm, (2) verifying the internal validity of the selected cluster solution, and (3) validating the solution on external criteria [32]. An analysis of the coefficients obtained using both Ward's minimum variance algorithm and the complete linkage algorithm provided support that the five cluster solution was the most appropriate.

## 5.1. A model oriented DSS

The model oriented DSS generally supports mid-level managers who use the DSS to evaluate and choose between relatively structured alternatives. As a result, the model oriented DSS provides strong modeling capabilities, while supporting less developed database and dialogue management capabilities. This DSS typically provides access to multiple models through a well-developed model management system. The model management system supports the development of new models via subroutines and model building tools, integration of existing models, and access to a model directory. Users of the model oriented DSS indicated the models within this particular type of DSS supported operational, tactical, and strategic decisions.

The database component of the model oriented DSS typically provides access to a limited number of databases, and these generally are exclusive to the DSS. Although the DSS allows access to a limited number of databases, there typically is no formal database management system to control activities such as queries, data storage, and data retrieval; or to provide services such as data security, a data dictionary, and data integration.

The dialogue capabilities provided by this DSS generally are very limited. The model oriented DSS has access to a dialogue management system, but typically does not provide capabilities such as tracking dialogue usage, multiple dialogue styles, and easy access to other components within the DSS. Not surprisingly, most users of the model oriented DSS consider the dialogue component to be inflexible.

## 5.2. A data oriented DSS

The data oriented DSS typically supports mid and upper management in relatively unstructured decisions, such as identifying possible problems and/or potential opportunities. As a result, the data oriented DSS exhibits strong database management capabilities, moderate dialogue support, and a weak model management component. The database component generally provides access to a number of organizational databases, and these databases support multiple users within the organization. This type of DSS has a well-developed database management system to control database activities such as queries, data storage, and data retrieval; or to provide services such as data security, a data dictionary, and data integration.

The dialogue capabilities provided by the data oriented DSS generally are limited. Interaction between the user and the DSS is controlled by a dialogue management system, but provides only basic capabilities to the DSS user. The dialogue management system does not, however, typically support more advanced dialogue capabilities such as tracking dialogue usage, multiple dialogue styles, and the analysis of dialogue usage patterns. Interestingly, many users of the data oriented DSS indicated they consider the dialogue component to be flexible.

The data oriented DSS typically does not include a well-developed model management system, and therefore offers limited modeling capabilities to its users. This DSS generally does not support a model directory, access to multiple models, the ability to integrate models, nor the ability to use model building blocks and/or sub-routines to develop complex models. Capabilities provided to the user would include simple model building tools, easy access to existing models, and extraction of required data from the DSS database.

## 5.3. A generic DSS

The generic DSS is the least developed of the five DSS categories identified in this study. Users of this DSS generally were not satisfied with the overall DSS performance and typically could not clearly identify the type of task supported. As a result, the generic DSS provides weak modeling, weak dialogue, and weak database capabilities to the DSS user. The modeling component of this DSS generally does not include a formal model management system, and offers very limited modeling capabilities to its users. This DSS would not support capabilities such as a model directory, access to multiple models, the integration of models, nor the ability to build complex models through model building blocks and/or subroutines.

The database component of the generic DSS typically provides access to a limited number of databases, and these generally are exclusive to the DSS. Although the DSS supports a limited number of databases, there typically is no formal database management system to control activities such as queries, data storage, and data retrieval; or to provide services such as data security, a data dictionary, and data integration.

The dialogue capabilities provided by this DSS were the weakest of the five DSS categories identified in this study. This component typically does not have access to a dialogue management system, and does not support capabilities such as tracking dialogue usage, multiple dialogue styles, and easy access to other components within the DSS. Not surprisingly, most users of the generic DSS consider the user interface to be inflexible.

## 5.4. A data / model oriented DSS

The data/model oriented DSS generally supports mid-level managers in the identification of problems and opportunities, and the analysis and selection between different alternatives. As a result, this type of DSS provides its user with strong database capabilities, strong modeling capabilities, and moderate dialogue capabilities. The data/model oriented DSS typically provides access to several organizational databases, and these databases support multiple users within the organization. This type of DSS has a well-developed database management system to control database activities such as queries, data storage, and data retrieval; or to provide services such as data security, a data dictionary, and data integration.

The modeling component of the data/model oriented DSS generally provides access to several models through a well-developed model management system. This model management system supports the development of new models through subroutines and/or model building tools, integration of existing models, and access to a model directory. This system also supports interaction between the modeling and database components of their DSS.

The dialogue capabilities provided by this DSS are typically less developed than the capabilities provided by the data and modeling components. The data/model oriented DSS generally has access to a dialogue management system, but does not support capabilities such as tracking dialogue usage and/or multiple dialogue styles. This dialogue management system does, however, provide the DSS user with easy access to the other components within the DSS. Surprisingly, many users of the data/model oriented DSS consider the dialogue component to be very flexible.

## 5.5. A fully developed DSS

The fully developed DSS supports lower and mid-level managers in semi-structured tasks involving problem/opportunity recognition and the evaluation of different alternatives. All three components are well-developed and provide the user with many of the capabilities commonly associated with DSS. This DSS typically provides access to several models through a well-developed model management system. The model management system supports the development of new models via subroutines and model building tools, integration of new and existing models, interaction with DSS databases, and access to a model directory. Users of the fully developed DSS indicated the models supported operational, tactical, and strategic decisions.

## 6. Comparison of proposed taxonomy to alter's DSS taxonomy

The third objective of this study was to determine if the DSS taxonomy could be mapped to the DSS taxonomy proposed by Alter [2]. Respondents were asked to classify their DSS according to seven statements which identified the main operational characteristics of Alter's seven categories. Each respondent could indicate that their DSS could perform one or more of characteristics listed. Table 5 provides a summary of the responses.

Analysis indicates that the five (5) DSS categories mapped cleanly onto the taxonomy proposed by Alter [2]. Respondents within each DSS category were generally able to identify one or possibly two categories within the taxonomy that best described the functions performed by their DSS. This would appear to indicate the DSS categories identified in this study fit the taxonomy proposed by Alter [2].

Huff et al. [16] tried to determine if there were any unnecessary categories within the Alter taxonomy. They concluded that, based on their study, the taxonomy did not contain any unnecessary categories. The results of this study tend to support these findings. Examination of Table 5 indicates that all seven categories within Alter's taxonomy have a meaningful number of respondents from the various DSS categories.

Another interesting fact that can be observed from Table 5 is the degree to which the seven categories are mutually exclusive. Most of the respondents (82.9%) indicated only one category as describing the function their DSS performed. Respondents who did indicate their DSS belonged to more than one category typically selected a second category that was very similar in the task performed. For example, the model oriented DSS users who classified their DSS into more than one category, typically selected the accounting models category and the representational models category as best describing the functions performed by their DSS. In Alter's taxonomy, both of these categories are described as model oriented in the task(s) they perform. Huff et al. [16] found that 83 of the 131 DSS studied could be classified into a single category and the rest were described by two or more of the categories. They explained this by suggesting that the DSS studied may indeed be multi-use systems rather the result of category ambiguity. The results of this study tend to support their explanation and that Alter's categories are indeed mutually exclusive.

## 7. Conclusions and implications

This study had three major objectives. The first objective was to determine if specific combinations of DSS capabilities could be identified. A clustering technique (Ward's minimum variance) was used to group DSS according to the capabilities provided by the database, model base, and dialogue components of the DSS. Analysis indicated that five DSS categories could be identified and that each category provided its DSS user with a unique set of capabilities.

The second objective was to develop a taxonomy based on the capabilities identified in the first part of the study. The proposed taxonomy identifies five different types of DSS that have been developed in response to problems faced by

DSS users. The categories identified range from the “generic” DSS which provides very little in the way of capabilities to its user(s) to the “fully developed” DSS that provides its user(s) with strong database, modeling, and dialogue capabilities.

The third objective was to determine if a relationship could be established between the taxonomy proposed in this study and the DSS taxonomy suggested by Alter [2]. The results indicate that the five DSS categories mapped easily onto Alter's taxonomy. Each of the categories could typically be classified into one or two of the seven groups within Alter's DSS taxonomy. Since the taxonomy proposed in this study is based on DSS capabilities and Alter's taxonomy is based on the task performed, it is not surprising that a specific category of the capabilities based DSS taxonomy could be developed in response to more than one type of task oriented problem. The successful mapping of the DSS categories onto Alter's taxonomy provides additional support to the existence of these categories.

An additional issue that was investigated was the appropriateness of the taxonomy proposed by Alter [2]. Huff et al. [16] found that there were no unnecessary categories within the taxonomy and that the categories were generally mutually exclusive. Based on the results obtained in this study, it appears that Alter's taxonomy still provides a useful mechanism for classifying DSS used by decision-makers. This implies that the types of tasks supported by today's DSS have not changed significantly from the tasks supported when Alter first proposed his taxonomy.

The results of this study should be useful to DSS developers and academicians alike. DSS developers can use the results of this study as a guideline for developing future DSS. By clearly identifying the operations that are to be supported and categorizing these into Alter's taxonomy, the DSS developer would be provided a starting point as to the type of capabilities that should be included in each component of the DSS. It should be noted that this study did not attempt to identify specific capabilities (i.e., a linear programming model), but rather to determine the relative strengths of the different DSS

components. Another benefit of this study is that the proposed taxonomy could provide insights into the different types of DSS that are currently being used by today's decision-makers. This study was able to identify five major types of DSS that have been developed to solve the problems faced by today's decision-makers. Four of the five categories provided a set of capabilities that were well-suited for the type of problems they supported. Only the generic DSS did not provide the capabilities that needed to support the task that was actually performed (File Drawer operations). This is important because this is the category where the users could not clearly identify the type of tasks the DSS supported, and suggests that identification of the tasks to be supported is possibly the critical part of DSS design. This also implies that DSS developers who use a prototyping methodology must be sure to clearly identify the type of tasks that are to be supported before they begin building the initial DSS prototype.

Finally, the findings of this study should provide a framework for future research. Additional studies should be undertaken to verify the existence of the DSS categories identified in the proposed taxonomy and also to expand and elaborate on the capabilities provided by each DSS category. Also, as suggested by Ariav and Ginzberg [4], future DSS research should attempt to identify the decision-making environment in which each of these DSS evolved. The development of DSS would be improved significantly if DSS developers better understood the relationship between the decision-making environment and the DSS that developed in response to that particular environment.
