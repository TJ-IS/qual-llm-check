Case Study

# A Korean group decision support system

Milam Aiken \*, Daeryong Kim, Chi Hwang, Long-Chuan Lu

Department of Management and Marketing, School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677 USA

## Abstract

A Group Decision Support System (GDSS) can be used to improve communication in many languages. Heretofore, the vast majority of research with these systems has been conducted on American groups using English. Here, we demonstrate how groups of Korean students used a GDSS developed at the University of Mississippi to exchange comments in Korean and English anonymously and simultaneously. The study found no significant differences between the English and Korean systems in terms of self-assessed ratings of evaluation apprehension, production blocking, and process satisfaction. Participants rated both systems favourably, supporting our hypothesis that Korean groups can benefit from the use of a GDSS.

Keywords: Group decision support systems; Electronic meeting systems; Electronic brainstorming; Korean

## 1. Introduction

The group meeting is one of the most frequent and important activities in organizations. However, they are often inefficient and ineffective because of the limitations of speech as a medium of communication $[12,17,28,34,35]$ . In addition, research has demonstrated that problems with verbal meetings vary tremendously among groups with different cultures and languages. For example, East Asians are more uncomfortable and hesitant to speak than are Westerners in a verbal meeting because of Confucian principles $[24,25,30]$ .

Group Decision Support Systems (GDSSs) have been developed to partially overcome the problems of verbal group meetings [4,20,29,31,33]. However, most of the GDSS research to date has focused on groups of American students (e.g., [8,9]) and businessmen (e.g., [2,15]) using English as the only language of communication. There is a need to test the use of GDSSs with groups from different cultures and speaking other languages to determine how these factors affect the efficiency and effectiveness of meetings.

This paper has two objectives. The first is to demonstrate that groups using a language other than English can use a GDSS to improve meeting productivity. We chose native Korean students because they have a non-Western culture and their language uses a different written representation. The second is to examine how groups evaluate a GDSS when using two different languages (English and Korean).

## 2. Meetings using the Korean language

Communication is a fundamentally social process and subject to the influence of culture [11,32]. Therefore, before we introduce a new communication technology to a community of a particular culture, we need to understand the communication patterns that can be traced to the philosophical roots and value systems in that culture. First, we identify the major communication patterns of the Korean culture and their impacts on verbal meetings. We also discuss how a Korean GDSS can be used to improve the process and outcomes of meetings.

## 2.1. The Confucian philosophy

Confucianism has been the most influential force in the culture of Korea since it was adopted as the official philosophy of the Yi dynasty [23]. The principal underlying Confucianism is that social order and stability can be achieved by inculcating four cardinal principles: in (humanism), ei (faithfulness), ye (propriety), and ji (wisdom). The Confucian philosophy is also used to regulate five basic human relationships (o-ryoon): loyalty between king and subject, closeness between father and son, distinction in duty between husband and wife, obedience between elders and youth, and faithfulness among friends [38]. As the foundation of Korean social relationships, the philosophy has become the code of behaviour that governs the way people in this society communicate with one another.

Because the main focus of Confucian philosophy is on the maintenance of social relationships, Koreans have a strong tendency to develop the kind of communication patterns that promote them. Koreans regulate their relationships according to the status of the persons involved and the particular context of their encounters, and they distinguish between group members and non-group members by different linguistic codes. Korean is a complex language that uses highly-stratified codes according to social status, degree of intimacy, age, sex, education, level of formality, and nature of relationships [36]. To preserve proper relationships, one needs to choose the correct communication form for speaking to a person and one must be extremely sensitive of proper word choice, perhaps distracting attention away from the content of the idea.

One of the most important characteristics of

Confucian society is its formalism. Socialization in these societies consists largely of teaching the stylized responses appropriate to every situation. Individuals are judged entirely by the way they fit into the prescribed patterns for interpersonal relations. Being polite means adhering to the prescribed formal model and it is the sole measure of one's virtue.

Reciprocity plays another essential role in Korean interpersonal communication patterns. For example, “defending face” is one of the main factors influencing Korean communication patterns. There are many concrete mechanisms for defending face, such as mediated communication (asking someone else to transmit the message), refracted communication (talking to a third person in the presence of the hearer), and acting as a delegate (conveying one’s message as being from someone else), which are all indirect forms of communication. In a verbal meeting, participants must constantly filter through their memory of a list of giving/taking history before they comment on another’s opinions to prevent offense. At the same time, the receiver must have sensitivity and the ability to discern implicit meaning.

Along with the five basic human relationships (o-ryoon), In-hwa (the key principle of Korean business behaviour) has made Korean human relationships more complex [3]. In-hwa is defined as harmony based on respect of hierarchical relationships, including obedience to authority, and this therefore links people who are unequal in rank, prestige, and power. However, the In-hwa relationship is intrinsically an unequal one, and each person has the responsibility to support the other person and make him or her happy. Consequently, Koreans seldom criticize or provide negative information outright. In a verbal meeting, In-hwa can enforce subordinates' silence, eliminating their potentially valuable input. Group members' attention in a verbal meeting is diverted from concentration on the topic by efforts of maintaining In-hwa relationships.

An emphasis on group uniformity is another important element of Confucianism. The Korean community recognizes each person primarily as a member of the whole, giving precedence to the benefit of the group over the individual [37].

Thus, Korean society has often been labelled as “collective” or “group-oriented” [5,18,19] and this cultural orientation has made a significant impact on Korean personality development and communication patterns. In this group-oriented society, individual members are expected to submerge selfish goals in favour of the group’s. The member then takes profit only after the group has profited from its activity. For example, Korean people tend to ignore the importance of individual uniqueness and psychological construction of self. In verbal communication, a Korean has a tendency to devalue himself or herself and to show no aggression or assertiveness in speech. In a society or an organization in which collectivism is the norm, group pressure can be applied to ensure conformity. Consequently, in a verbal meeting, group members are not likely to state their own opinions under this pressure but will yield to group consensus.

In Korean society, proper human relationships are dictated by social classes or roles, which in turn determine speaking order among participants in a formal, verbal meeting. On the basis of the Confucian teaching of o-ryoon, a subordinate person usually waits to speak. By the time a junior person has a chance to speak, his or her comment may no longer be relevant. When the cycle of speaking order gets too complicated, certain meeting rules or a mediator is needed to control the discussion. Although social harmony is essential to organizational cooperation, intricate speaking order arrangements often make meetings inefficient.

Evaluation apprehension (being afraid of what others may think of you and your comment) and related speech anxiety may be the most pervasive verbal communication problem in Western society [26]. However, this problem may be even more serious in Korean society. Because Korean culture is strongly influenced by the paradigm of collective thought, a Korean is reluctant to state what he or she thinks, is hesitant in voicing opinions, and is not likely to talk unless forced. The emphasis on memorization in education also produces poor communication skills and makes Koreans more hesitant. In a verbal meeting, a Korean is more reluctant to speak than a Westerner is, and this might make a verbal meeting inefficient.

## 2.2. Benefits of a Korean GDSS

We believe that many problems with verbal Korean meetings may be reduced or eliminated by using a Group Decision Support System $[1,14]$ . Although GDSSs can include a wide variety of technology including teleconferencing, bulletin boards, and even electronic mail, this computer-based system for the support of collaborative group work typically provides group members with anonymity (no one knows who wrote a particular comment) and parallel communication (all group members are able to type and submit comments simultaneously) $[6,7,10]$ . The provision of anonymity may enhance subordinates' credibility, because ideas and comments are not associated with any particular person, but instead belong to the group $[16,21]$ . A group using a GDSS is therefore much less concerned with face maintenance, and a group member may feel free to criticize an idea without worrying about causing another to lose face. Parallel communication allows subordinates to participate equally with superiors, and nobody needs to wait for a turn to speak $[22,27]$ .

## 3. The Korean / English GDSS case study

## 3.1. Subjects

Twelve Korean students who were enrolled at the University of Mississippi participated in the study (this sample of convenience was limited due to the relatively small number of Koreans at the University). Their ages ranged from 21 to 38 with a mean of 30.4, and most of the subjects knew each other through frequent social contacts and student activities. On average, the group had 2.8 years of working, 3.2 years of computer, and 0.6 years of GDSS experience (see Table 1). Approximately 58% of the participants had never used a GDSS and 42% had never used a Korean word-processor before.

## 3.2. The GDSS software

The Korean subjects used two versions of electronic brainstorming software developed at the

University of Mississippi. The English version allowed subjects to submit comments and view others' comments simultaneously and is similar to other brainstorming programs in VisionQuest, GroupSystems, and TeamFocus [9]. The Korean version is similar to the English brainstorming software but allows users to type characters in the Korean alphabet (14 consonants and 10 vowels) through a standard QWERTY keyboard. Because only a few subjects had used a Korean wordprocessor before, the text-entry rate was slow. All subjects were able to write the characters by hand much faster than they were able to type the characters on the keyboard.

## 3.4. Case study procedures

The subjects were given 10–15 minutes of introduction to the case study and instruction on how to use each version of the brainstorming software. Instruction for the use of the Korean software took a little longer because the subjects were unfamiliar with the QWERTY/Korean keyboard mapping. The subjects were then given time to practice using each system.

After the instruction session, the subjects were asked the question “What are possible solutions for the parking problem on campus?” to discuss electronically for 15 minutes using Korean and then were asked the question “How can international students make friends on campus?” to discuss electronically for 15 minutes using English. Fig. 1 shows a partial transcript of the discussion in Korean. After the meeting, a questionnaire was administered to assess the subjects’ perceptions of production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and satisfaction with the meeting process (see Appendix). The subjects were also asked to assess the usefulness of a Korean GDSS and to state which language (Korean or English) they preferred in a verbal or GDSS meeting.

## 3.5. Case study results

In general, the subjects gave high ratings for both the Korean and the English GDSS (see Table 2). In the English meeting, 75% said it was easy to communicate, 83% said they experienced little or no evaluation apprehension, and 67% said they were satisfied with the meeting process. In the Korean GDSS meeting, 75% said it was easy to communicate, 92% said they experienced little or no evaluation apprehension, and 58% said they were satisfied with the meeting process. A matched-pairs T-test (shown in Table 3) shows there is no statistically significant difference between the ratings of the Korean and English GDSS for these three measures. Finally, no significant correlations were found among the demographic data and the dependent variables, with the exception that subject age was negatively correlated with process satisfaction in the Korean meeting (R = -0.71, p = 0.009) and in the English meeting (R = -0.61, p = 0.04).

Most of the participants stated that a Korean GDSS was a good idea (58% agreed). The vast majority (92%) of the Korean students showed a preference for using their native language in oral meetings, but only 25% preferred using Korean in a GDSS meeting (50% preferred the English system while 25% were neutral).

## 3.6. Discussion

The Korean subjects did not experience difficulty in expressing themselves in both the English and Korean GDSS meetings (shown by the high ratings on production blocking); they experienced very little evaluation apprehension and were satisfied with the meeting processes. Further, there was no significant difference between their ratings for the two systems. Therefore, these findings support our belief that a GDSS can reduce many of the negative effects of verbal meetings conducted in languages other than English. However, further studies must be conducted on groups using other languages in verbal and GDSS meetings to generalize these findings.

The study also found that only 25% of the subjects preferred using Korean for a GDSS meeting, perhaps due to the relative difficulty of typing Korean characters on an English keyboard. However, many Koreans may not know English well enough to have the option. Also, more practice or a customized keyboard may increase this percentage.

Finally, process satisfaction was negatively correlated with age; perhaps the older Koreans felt that they were losing “authority” while the younger Koreans might have enjoyed the chance to contribute more because of parallel communication and anonymity. In fact, the provision of anonymity may be the most important factor in improving Korean meetings because of the need to preserve face and go along with the group.

The case study has two major limitations. First, the small sample size dictated by the number of Koreans at the University of Mississippi may limit generalizations to other groups of Koreans. For example, are Koreans studying in America different from Koreans in their native country? We do not believe that the location of the study or influence from American culture affected the results. Second, the subjects' slow typing speeds using the Korean character set may have biased the results toward the use of an English GDSS. Koreans in their native country also may type slowly, but they are probably less likely to know English, and thus would be forced to use a Korean GDSS.

## 4. Conclusions

A Group Decision Support System can be used to enhance Korean meetings. A GDSS may be especially beneficial for Korean and other groups whose cultures emphasize conformity, respect, deference to superiors, and preservation of face, because such a system allows individuals to participate more without violating their cultural beliefs. Further case studies and experiments are planned on Korean groups as well as groups of Japanese, Chinese, and Malaysian students using their native languages.
